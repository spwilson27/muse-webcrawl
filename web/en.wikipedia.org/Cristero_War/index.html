Cristero War

The Cristero War (1926–1929), also known as La Cristiada, was a widespread popular uprising in west-central Mexico against the anti-clerical provisions of the Mexican Constitution of 1917 and their enforcement by President Plutarco Elías Calles. The government's attempt to suppress the Catholic Church through the "Calles Law" led the Mexican episcopate to suspend public worship; in response, Catholic peasants and townspeople — the Cristeros, named for their battle cry ¡Viva Cristo Rey! ("Long live Christ the King!") — took up arms. Tens of thousands died before a negotiated settlement in 1929 restored public worship.

Background

The Constitution of 1917 contained strongly anti-clerical articles: it restricted the property and political rights of the Church, outlawed religious orders and public worship outside churches, and limited the number of priests. Enforcement varied until Calles took office in 1924 and began applying the provisions rigorously. The Calles Law of 1926 imposed penalties for violations, prompting the bishops to suspend church services from 1 August 1926 and Catholics to organise an economic boycott.

The uprising

Armed revolts began in late 1926 and spread across Jalisco, Michoacán, Guanajuato, Colima, and neighbouring states. The rebels, initially scattered local bands, were eventually unified under the professional soldier Enrique Gorostieta, a former federal general hired by the National League for the Defence of Religious Liberty. Cristero forces fought the Federal Army in guerrilla warfare, holding substantial rural territory but never capturing a major city. The government campaign was marked by atrocities on both sides, including executions of priests — among them Miguel Pro, shot in 1927 — and reprisals against rebel supporters.

Settlement

Through the mediation of the United States ambassador Dwight Morrow, negotiations between the government and the Church produced the arreglos (agreements) of June 1929: churches reopened, worship resumed, and the Cristeros laid down their arms under an amnesty. Many former Cristeros were nevertheless killed in the following years, and the anti-clerical laws remained on the books, though loosely enforced. A smaller second uprising, La Segunda, flickered in the 1930s.

Legacy

The war left deep marks on Mexican politics and on relations between church and state, which were formally normalised only with constitutional reforms in 1992. A number of Cristero martyrs, including Miguel Pro and the young José Sánchez del Río, were canonised by the Catholic Church. The war is the setting of Graham Greene's novel The Power and the Glory (1940).
