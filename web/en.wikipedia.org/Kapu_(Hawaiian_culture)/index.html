Kapu (Hawaiian culture)

Kapu is the ancient Hawaiian code of conduct of laws and regulations. The kapu system was universal in lifestyle, gender roles, politics and religion. An offense that was kapu was often a capital offense, but also often denoted a threat to spiritual power, or theft of mana. Kapus were strictly enforced. Breaking one, even unintentionally, often meant immediate death (Koʻo kūʻauhau). The concept is related to the tapu or taboo found in other Polynesian cultures.

The word "kapu" is usually translated to "forbidden" or "sacred". In contrast, noa means "common" or "free". The kapu system was used in Hawaiʻi until 1819, when King Kamehameha II, acting with his mother Keōpūolani and his father's queen Kaʻahumanu, abolished it by the symbolic act of sharing a meal of forbidden foods with the women of his court, an event known as ʻAi Noa.

Kapu system

The kapu system was pervasive in Hawaiian culture. Kapus were imposed by chiefs and priests and covered many aspects of daily life, including what foods could be eaten, where people could go, and how men and women interacted. A well-known restriction was the ʻai kapu, the eating restrictions that separated men and women at meals and forbade women from eating certain foods, such as pork, bananas, and certain kinds of fish, which were considered offerings to the gods.

Places of refuge, known as puʻuhonua, allowed a person who had broken a kapu to escape punishment if they could reach the sanctuary before being caught.

Abolition

After the death of Kamehameha I in 1819, his successor Liholiho (Kamehameha II) abolished the kapu system. The abolition, sometimes called the Free Eating (ʻAi Noa), occurred shortly before the arrival of Christian missionaries in 1820 and profoundly reshaped Hawaiian society and religion.
