Moral luck

Moral luck

Field: Moral philosophy, ethics
Key figures: Bernard Williams, Thomas Nagel
Introduced: 1976 (Williams; Nagel 1979)

Moral luck describes circumstances in which a moral agent is assigned moral blame or praise for an action or its consequences, even though a significant part of what happened was beyond the agent's control. The concept became prominent through essays by Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel, first presented at a 1976 symposium of the Aristotelian Society and published in subsequent years.

Kinds of moral luck

Thomas Nagel distinguished four kinds of moral luck: resultant luck, in how one's actions turn out (for example, two equally reckless drivers, one of whom happens to hit a pedestrian); circumstantial luck, in the situations and moral tests one happens to face; constitutive luck, in one's character, temperament, and inclinations; and causal luck, in being determined by antecedent circumstances beyond one's control.

The problem

Moral luck appears to conflict with the "control principle"—the intuition that people should be judged only for what lies within their control. Yet ordinary moral and legal practice routinely judges outcomes: a successful attempt at wrongdoing is typically punished more severely than an identical failed attempt. Williams used cases such as the painter Gauguin abandoning his family for art to argue that moral justification can depend on outcomes beyond the agent's control, challenging Kantian views that morality is immune to luck.

Responses

Philosophers have responded by denying that moral luck undermines moral responsibility, by restricting moral assessment to intentions and character, or by accepting that luck pervades moral life. The debate connects to wider discussions of free will, desert, and the law's treatment of attempts versus completed crimes.
