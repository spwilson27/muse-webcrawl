Inertial measurement unit


A micro-electromechanical inertial measurement unit.

An inertial measurement unit (IMU) is an electronic device that measures and reports a body's specific force, angular rate, and sometimes its orientation, using a combination of accelerometers, gyroscopes and occasionally magnetometers. IMUs are the core sensors of inertial navigation systems, which compute position, velocity and attitude by dead reckoning from a known starting point, without needing external references such as GPS.

Construction

A basic IMU contains three orthogonally mounted accelerometers, measuring linear acceleration along three axes, and three orthogonally mounted gyroscopes, measuring angular velocity about those axes. Such a device is said to have six degrees of freedom. Adding a three-axis magnetometer gives nine-axis devices that can reference magnetic north. Early IMUs used spinning-mass gyroscopes and gimballed or strapdown platforms; modern units overwhelmingly use MEMS sensors, and high-performance systems use ring laser gyroscopes or fibre-optic gyroscopes.

Applications

IMUs guide aircraft, spacecraft, missiles, ships and submarines; stabilise drones and cameras; track motion in smartphones, game controllers and virtual-reality headsets; and monitor vehicle dynamics in automotive safety systems. Because integration of acceleration signals causes errors to accumulate over time — a manifestation of sensor drift and bias — IMU data is commonly fused with GPS, barometric or visual measurements using filters such as the Kalman filter.
