Time-domain astronomy

Time-domain astronomy is the study of how astronomical objects change with time. Though the study may be said to begin with Galileo's telescope observations of the Moon and sunspots, the field has expanded greatly with modern wide-field survey telescopes that repeatedly image large areas of sky, allowing astronomers to detect and characterise variable and transient phenomena.

Phenomena

Time-domain astronomy covers objects varying on timescales from milliseconds to centuries, including supernovae, gamma-ray bursts, tidal disruption events, kilonovae, variable stars, pulsars, active galactic nuclei, exoplanet transits, and moving solar system objects such as asteroids and comets. It also includes multi-messenger events, where electromagnetic observations are combined with gravitational-wave, neutrino or cosmic-ray detections.

Surveys

Dedicated surveys such as the Palomar Transient Factory, Pan-STARRS, the Catalina Real-Time Transient Survey, ASAS-SN and the Zwicky Transient Facility have discovered thousands of transients per year. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) is expected to issue millions of alerts per night, requiring automated classification and rapid follow-up by robotic telescopes. Radio facilities and space missions likewise conduct time-domain programmes across the electromagnetic spectrum.

Methods

The field relies on difference imaging to detect changes against reference images, machine-learning classification of alert streams, and coordinated target-of-opportunity observations. Light curves — plots of brightness against time — are the primary data product, encoding information about explosions, rotation, orbital motion and accretion.
