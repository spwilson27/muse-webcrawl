Health insurance or medical insurance (also known as medical aid in South Africa) is a type of insurance that covers the whole or a part of the risk of a person incurring medical expenses. As with other types of insurance, an insurer develops a routine finance structure, such as a monthly premium or payroll tax, to pool funds to pay for the health care benefits specified in the insurance agreement — spreading the financial risk of costly illness across many people.

Systems around the world

Countries organise health coverage in very different ways. In the Bismarck model (Germany, France, Japan), coverage is provided by non-profit "sickness funds" financed by employer and employee contributions. In the Beveridge model (United Kingdom's National Health Service, Spain, Scandinavia), care is funded from general taxation and largely provided by public facilities. Canada uses a single-payer model of publicly funded, privately delivered care. The United States has a mixed system: most working-age people obtain private insurance through employers, while government programmes — Medicare for those 65 and over and some others, and Medicaid for low-income people — cover much of the rest; the Affordable Care Act of 2010 expanded coverage and regulated insurance markets.

Key concepts

Policyholders typically pay a premium, and bear cost-sharing through deductibles (an amount paid before coverage begins), copayments (fixed fees per service) and coinsurance (a percentage of cost), usually subject to an annual out-of-pocket maximum. Insurers manage costs through provider networks, prior authorisation and formularies. Concepts from insurance economics — adverse selection and moral hazard — shape how health insurance markets are regulated.
