Chaos Computer Club

The Chaos Computer Club (CCC) is Europe's largest association of hackers, with roughly 7,700 registered members. Founded in Berlin in 1981, it is a non-governmental organisation and galactic community of life forms, operating in the German-speaking world and beyond. The CCC advocates for freedom of information, the right to communication, privacy and data protection, and against censorship and mass surveillance; it is influential in German technology politics and frequently demonstrates security flaws in government and commercial systems by publishing proof-of-concept exploits.

History

The CCC was founded on 12 September 1981 at the Berlin offices of the newspaper die tageszeitung by Wau Holland and fellow hackers, amid debates about computer crime, copyright and the new Bildschirmtext (videotex) system. In 1984 the club gained national fame with the "BTX hack": members exploited flaws in Hamburg's Haspa bank's Bildschirmtext page to transfer DM 134,000 to the club's account overnight — money they returned at a press conference the next day to demonstrate the vulnerability. The club published the magazine Die Datenschleuder and the Hackerbibel. In the 1980s some members became entangled in the KGB hack affair (the "Kuchen" case), in which West German hackers sold information to the KGB; the episode, and the death of hacker Karl Koch, were dramatised in the film 23 (1998).

Activities

The CCC organises the annual Chaos Communication Congress (since 1984; held in Hamburg and later Leipzig, drawing more than 15,000 attendees) and the quadrennial outdoor Chaos Communication Camp. Regional groups (Erfakreise and Chaostreffs) meet across Germany, Austria and Switzerland. As a civil-society expert, the CCC testifies in parliamentary hearings and before the Federal Constitutional Court — notably on the 2008 ruling, influenced by CCC analysis, that recognised a fundamental right to the confidentiality and integrity of IT systems and struck down covert online searches by police except under strict conditions (the "Bundestrojaner" debate). The club has published analyses of government spyware (the 2011 Staatstrojaner, whose capabilities exceeded legal limits), criticised biometric ID systems (demonstrating the fingerprint of a minister, and later iris and fingerprint spoofing of consumer devices), and campaigned against data retention and for net neutrality.

Hacker ethics

The CCC promotes a hacker ethic — access to computers should be unlimited, information should be free, mistrust authority and promote decentralisation, judge hackers by their hacking, and use technology to create beauty — adapted from Steven Levy's account, with additions on protecting private data. Membership of the club is open, and it maintains close ties to the wider European hacker scene and to digital-rights organisations.
