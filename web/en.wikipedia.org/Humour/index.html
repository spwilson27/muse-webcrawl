Humour

Humour
Related concepts: Laughter, comedy, wit, irony, satire
Theories: Superiority, relief, incongruity, benign violation

Humour (British English) or humor (American English) is the tendency of experiences to provoke laughter and provide amusement. People of all ages and cultures respond to humour, although what is considered funny varies widely. The term derives from the humoral theory of ancient medicine, in which bodily fluids called humours were thought to govern temperament.

Theories

The superiority theory, associated with Plato and Hobbes, holds that laughter expresses a feeling of superiority over others' misfortunes. Relief theory, developed by Spencer and Freud, treats laughter as a release of nervous energy. Incongruity theory — the most influential modern account — says humour arises when expectations are violated in a surprising but resolvable way. Benign violation theory proposes that humour occurs when something is perceived as both a violation and benign.

Forms

Humour takes many forms: jokes and wordplay, slapstick and physical comedy, satire and parody, irony and sarcasm, observational comedy, self-deprecation and gallows humour. It appears in literature, theatre, film, stand-up comedy, cartoons and internet memes.

Psychology and health

Humour serves social functions: bonding, easing tension, signalling affiliation and softening criticism. Coping humour is associated with resilience, and laughter has short-term physiological effects including muscle relaxation and stress reduction. Developmental studies show infants respond to playful incongruity before they can speak.

Culture

Humour is deeply culture-bound; translation often fails because wordplay and shared references do not transfer. National stereotypes about humour — British irony, Jewish self-deprecation, deadpan Nordic styles — reflect real traditions as well as clichés.
