Nazi plunder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nazi plunder was the organised looting of artwork and other valuables that occurred as a result of the organised plundering of European countries during the Third Reich by agents acting on behalf of the Nazi Party and senior figures such as Adolf Hitler and Hermann Göring. Beyond art, the regime systematically seized gold, currency, furniture, books, and religious objects, including the property of murdered Jewish citizens channelled through institutions like the Einsatzstab Reichsleiter Rosenberg (ERR).

Scale and victims

Millions of cultural objects were taken from museums, churches, libraries, and private collections across occupied Europe. Jewish collectors and dealers — among them the Rothschilds, Paul Rosenberg, and Jacques Goudstikker — were prime targets, their property "aryanised," sold abroad for foreign exchange, or reserved for Hitler's planned Führermuseum in Linz. Entire national collections in Poland and the Soviet Union were devastated; many works were destroyed as "degenerate."

Recovery and restitution

After the war, the Allied "Monuments Men" of the MFAA recovered vast caches from salt mines and castles, returning much of it to countries of origin. Restitution to individual owners and heirs continues into the 21st century, guided in part by the 1998 Washington Principles; famous disputes include the Klimt Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I case and the 2012 discovery of the Gurlitt trove in Munich.
