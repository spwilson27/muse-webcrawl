Joseph Rudyard Kipling (30 December 1865 – 18 January 1936) was an English novelist, short-story writer, poet, and journalist. He was born in British India, which inspired much of his work. He is seen as an innovator in the art of the short story, and his children's books are classics.

Life

Kipling was born in Bombay and taken to England at the age of five. After school at the United Services College in Devon, he returned to India in 1882 to work as a journalist in Lahore and Allahabad, publishing stories drawing on Anglo-Indian life. Returning to England in 1889, he quickly became one of the most popular writers of his day.

Works

His fiction includes The Jungle Book (1894), the novel Kim (1901), the Just So Stories (1902) and many short stories, including "The Man Who Would Be King" (1888). His poems include "Mandalay" (1890), "Gunga Din" (1890), "The White Man's Burden" (1899) and "If—" (1910). In 1907 he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature, the first English-language writer to receive it, and at 41 the youngest recipient to date.

Reputation

Kipling's reputation has shifted with political attitudes toward empire: celebrated in his lifetime, he fell from favour after the First World War, but his technical mastery and storytelling have continued to be admired, and his major works remain in print. He lived from 1902 at Bateman's in Burwash, Sussex, now preserved by the National Trust.
