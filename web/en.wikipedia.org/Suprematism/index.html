Suprematism

Suprematism (Russian: супрематизм) is an early 20th-century avant-garde art movement founded by the Russian artist Kazimir Malevich around 1913. It consists of basic geometric shapes — squares, circles, rectangles, lines — in a limited range of colors, arranged in abstract compositions.

History

Malevich unveiled Suprematism at the 0,10 Exhibition in Petrograd in December 1915, where his Black Square (1915) was hung across the corner of the room, the place traditionally reserved for religious icons. In his manifesto From Cubism to Suprematism he proclaimed the "supremacy of pure feeling" over the depiction of objects, seeking to free painting from the burden of the visible world.

Followers and collaborators included El Lissitzky, Olga Rozanova, Lyubov Popova, and members of the UNOVIS group in Vitebsk, where Malevich taught from 1919. Suprematism influenced the Bauhaus and modern design through Lissitzky's work, and later the "white on white" series (1918) pushed abstraction toward its limit. In the later 1920s, Stalinist cultural policy suppressed the avant-garde in favor of Socialist Realism, and Malevich died in obscurity in 1935.
