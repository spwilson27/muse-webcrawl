Naval strategy

Naval strategy is the planning and conduct of war at sea, the naval equivalent of military strategy on land. It encompasses the formulation of a state's maritime objectives and the employment of naval forces to achieve them, in peace and in war. Naval strategy is part of the broader field of maritime strategy, which also addresses the political, economic, and diplomatic uses of the sea. Key concepts include sea control, sea denial, power projection, protection of sea lines of communication, and naval diplomacy.

Classical theorists

Alfred Thayer Mahan, in The Influence of Sea Power upon History, 1660–1783 (1890), argued that command of the sea — achieved through a battle fleet able to defeat the enemy in decisive battle and sustain maritime trade — was decisive to national greatness. Julian Corbett, in Some Principles of Maritime Strategy (1911), nuanced this view: command of the sea is normally relative and local, and naval power matters chiefly for its effect on events ashore — securing communications, supporting armies, and applying economic pressure through blockade. The French Jeune École advocateded commerce raiding (guerre de course) by torpedo craft and cruisers as an asymmetric answer to a superior battle fleet.

Core elements

Naval strategies typically address fleet structure and basing, chokepoint control, convoy and anti-submarine warfare, amphibious operations, naval blockade, deterrence (including ballistic missile submarines), and presence missions. In the nuclear age, sea-based deterrent patrols became a central strategic function of major navies. Modern debate includes the role of aircraft carriers, anti-access/area-denial systems, uncrewed vessels, and the protection of undersea infrastructure.
