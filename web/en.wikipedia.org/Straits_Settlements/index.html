The Straits Settlements were a group of British territories on and around the Strait of Malacca in Southeast Asia. Originally established in 1826 under the British East India Company, they comprised the ports of Singapore, Penang (Prince of Wales Island) and Malacca, with Singapore as capital from 1832.

History

Penang was founded by Francis Light in 1786; Singapore was established by Stamford Raffles in 1819; Malacca passed to Britain under the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824. In 1826 the three were united as a presidency of British India. Booming trade in tin, rubber, spices and opium, and their role as entrepôts between China, India and Europe, made them prosperous; in 1867, after lobbying by merchants dissatisfied with rule from Calcutta, they became a Crown colony administered from the Colonial Office in London. Labuan (off Borneo) joined in 1906–07, along with dependencies including Christmas Island and the Cocos (Keeling) Islands.

The colony was shattered by the Japanese occupation (1942–45), which began with the fall of Singapore in February 1942. On the British return, the Straits Settlements were dissolved in 1946: Singapore became a separate Crown colony (and eventually an independent republic), while Penang and Malacca joined the Malayan Union, later Malaysia.

Legacy

The settlements created the multi-ethnic port cities of modern Malaysia and Singapore, with their distinctive Peranakan (Straits Chinese) culture, common-law institutions, and English-language education, and laid out enduring urban cores such as George Town and Singapore's civic district.
