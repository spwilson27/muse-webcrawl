Toxoplasmosis is a parasitic disease caused by Toxoplasma gondii, an obligate intracellular protozoan. Cats and other felids are the definitive hosts in which the parasite reproduces sexually; virtually all warm-blooded animals, including humans, serve as intermediate hosts. Up to a third of the world's human population is estimated to carry the parasite in latent tissue cysts, usually without symptoms.

Transmission and life cycle

Humans acquire infection by eating undercooked meat containing tissue cysts, ingesting oocysts shed in cat faeces (for example via contaminated soil, water, or unwashed vegetables), or congenitally from an infected mother. After ingestion, fast-multiplying tachyzoites spread through the body before the immune system drives the parasite into slow-growing bradyzoite cysts in muscle and brain.

Clinical features

In immunocompetent people, acute infection is usually asymptomatic or causes a mild flu-like illness with lymphadenopathy. Serious disease occurs in two settings: congenital infection, which can cause miscarriage, hydrocephalus, intracranial calcifications, and chorioretinitis; and immunosuppression, especially advanced HIV/AIDS, where reactivation causes toxoplasmic encephalitis with ring-enhancing brain lesions. Ocular toxoplasmosis can scar the retina in otherwise healthy people.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is chiefly serological (IgG and IgM antibodies), supplemented by PCR in pregnancy and immunocompromised patients. Healthy people usually need no treatment; when required, the standard regimen combines pyrimethamine with sulfadiazine and folinic acid. Prevention focuses on cooking meat thoroughly, washing produce, and care with cat litter during pregnancy.

Behavioural research

The parasite famously alters rodent behaviour, reducing rats' aversion to cat odour — an adaptation thought to promote transmission to felids. Proposed links between latent infection and human behaviour or psychiatric illness remain an active and contested research topic.
