Puyallup River

The Puyallup River (pronounced pew-AL-əp) is a river in the U.S. state of Washington, about 45 miles (72 km) long. It flows northwest from glaciers on the western slopes of Mount Rainier to Commencement Bay in Puget Sound at Tacoma. Its major tributaries are the Carbon River and the White River. The river is named for the Puyallup Tribe, whose reservation lies along its lower reaches.

Course

The river begins at the Puyallup and Tahoma glaciers on Mount Rainier and runs through the northwest corner of Mount Rainier National Park before joining the Mowich River. Near Orting it is joined by the Carbon River, and at Sumner it receives the White River, whose flow was diverted into the Puyallup system in the early twentieth century. From there it flows through Puyallup and Fife across a broad alluvial plain to its mouth on Commencement Bay in Tacoma's tideflats.

Geology and hazards

Because it is fed by glacial meltwater, the river carries a heavy load of silt and often appears milky white. The Puyallup valley is built of sediment from ancient lahars (volcanic mudflows) from Mount Rainier, most notably the Osceola Mudflow about 5,600 years ago and the Electron Mudflow about 500 years ago. Cities such as Orting lie within the inundation zone of future lahars, and the valley is equipped with lahar warning sirens. The river itself is prone to flooding, and much of its lower course is confined by levees and channelized.

Biology and use

The Puyallup supports runs of Chinook, coho, chum, and pink salmon and steelhead trout, with hatcheries on the river and its tributaries. Salmon fishing rights reserved by the Puyallup Tribe under the Medicine Creek Treaty of 1854 were affirmed in the Boldt Decision of 1974. The Electron Hydroelectric Project on the upper river has generated power since 1904, diverting water into a flume along the valley wall.
