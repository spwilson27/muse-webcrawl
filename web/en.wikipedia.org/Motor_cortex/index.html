Motor cortex

[Image: The primary motor cortex (Brodmann area 4) in the frontal lobe]

The motor cortex comprises regions of the cerebral cortex in the posterior frontal lobe that are involved in the planning, control and execution of voluntary movements. It is conventionally divided into the primary motor cortex (Brodmann area 4), lying on the precentral gyrus, and two higher-order areas lying just in front of it: the premotor cortex and the supplementary motor area.

Organization

The primary motor cortex is organized somatotopically as the "motor homunculus," first mapped by Wilder Penfield through electrical stimulation in awake patients: body parts with fine control - the face, tongue and hands - occupy disproportionately large territories. Giant pyramidal neurons called Betz cells, found only in area 4, originate part of the corticospinal tract, which descends through the internal capsule, brainstem and spinal cord to drive motor neurons. The premotor cortex helps select movements based on sensory cues and learned associations, while the supplementary motor area is important for internally generated and bimanual sequences.

Clinical significance

Damage to the motor cortex or its projections - for example from stroke - causes weakness or paralysis on the opposite side of the body. Motor cortex activity underlies motor learning, and its representation reorganizes with practice and after injury, a form of plasticity exploited by rehabilitation and brain-computer interface research.
