Structural biology

Structural biology is a branch of molecular biology, biochemistry, and biophysics concerned with the molecular structure of biological macromolecules (especially proteins, made up of amino acids, and RNA or DNA, made up of nucleotides), how they acquire the structures they have, and how alterations in their structures affect their function.

Methods

The principal techniques of structural biology are:

  • X-ray crystallography — historically the dominant method, used to determine the structures of DNA, ribosomes, and thousands of proteins
  • NMR spectroscopy — used for smaller proteins and for studying dynamics in solution
  • Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) — which underwent a "resolution revolution" in the 2010s, enabling near-atomic structures of large complexes
  • Mass spectrometry and small-angle scattering for lower-resolution and dynamic information
  • Computational methods, notably AlphaFold, which predicted structures for nearly all catalogued proteins

History

The field traces its origins to the X-ray diffraction work of the early twentieth century, the determination of the DNA double helix in 1953, and the first protein structures — myoglobin and haemoglobin — solved by John Kendrew and Max Perutz around 1960. The Protein Data Bank, founded in 1971, now archives hundreds of thousands of experimentally determined structures.

Applications

Structural knowledge underpins structure-based drug design, allowing medicinal chemists to design molecules that fit the binding sites of target proteins, and is fundamental to understanding enzyme mechanisms, signalling, and the molecular basis of disease.
