Dissolution of parliament

The dissolution of parliament is the dispensation or abolition of a sitting parliament, terminating all legislative functions and typically triggering a general election. It is a feature of many parliamentary systems, in contrast to the fixed terms common in presidential systems.

Practice

In Westminster-style systems, dissolution is formally carried out by the head of state (a monarch or governor-general), usually on the advice of the prime minister. Dissolution ends the terms of all members of the lower house; in some countries the upper house is permanent and is not dissolved. A parliament may also dissolve automatically when its maximum term expires, or early dissolution may be restricted by law — for example, the United Kingdom's Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 removed the prerogative power of dissolution before being repealed by the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022, which revived it.

Notable and controversial dissolutions

Dissolution can be politically contentious when a head of state refuses a prime minister's request, or grants one in controversial circumstances — such as the 1975 Australian constitutional crisis, in which the Governor-General dismissed the prime minister, or Canada's 1926 King–Byng affair. Some constitutions, such as Norway's, do not allow dissolution at all, while Germany's permits it only under narrow conditions following a failed confidence vote.
