Generic he

In English, the generic he or masculine generic is the use of the masculine pronouns he, him and his to refer to a person of unspecified sex, as in "Each student should bring his book." This usage was prescribed by prescriptive grammarians from the eighteenth century onward and was long standard in formal writing.

History

Before the rise of the generic he, the singular they was commonly used for a referent of unknown sex, appearing in authors such as Chaucer, Shakespeare and Jane Austen. Grammarians such as John Kirby (1746) argued that the masculine gender was "more worthy" and should comprehend the feminine; an Act of the British Parliament in 1850 (Lord Brougham's Act) declared that in legislation words importing the masculine gender should include females. This gave the generic he official sanction in legal English.

Decline and debate

From the 1970s, feminist linguists argued that the generic he is not psychologically neutral, citing studies showing that readers more often picture males when the pronoun is used. Most major style guides now recommend alternatives: recasting into the plural, writing he or she, or using the singular they, which has been endorsed by the Associated Press, Chicago Manual of Style (with reservations) and the APA Publication Manual, whose seventh edition (2019) fully accepts singular they as a generic and as a personal pronoun.
