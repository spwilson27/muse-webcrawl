Glycolysis

Glycolysis is the metabolic pathway that converts glucose into pyruvate, and can occur with or without the presence of oxygen. The free energy released in this process is used to form the high-energy molecules ATP (adenosine triphosphate) and NADH (reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide). Glycolysis is a sequence of ten reactions catalyzed by enzymes, occurring in the cytosol of nearly all organisms.

Overview

The pathway is divided into an energy-investment (preparatory) phase, in which glucose is phosphorylated by hexokinase and phosphofructokinase consumes a second ATP, and an energy-payoff phase, in which the two three-carbon sugars produced are oxidized to yield four ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation and two NADH. Overall, glycolysis of one glucose molecule yields a net gain of two ATP, two NADH, and two pyruvate molecules.

Steps

The ten steps proceed: glucose → glucose-6-phosphate → fructose-6-phosphate → fructose-1,6-bisphosphate → (split into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and dihydroxyacetone phosphate) → 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate → 3-phosphoglycerate → 2-phosphoglycerate → phosphoenolpyruvate → pyruvate. The key regulatory, essentially irreversible steps are catalyzed by hexokinase, phosphofructokinase-1 (the principal control point, inhibited by ATP and activated by fructose-2,6-bisphosphate), and pyruvate kinase.

Fates of pyruvate

In aerobic conditions, pyruvate enters the mitochondria and is oxidized through the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, the citric acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation. In anaerobic conditions, pyruvate is fermented — to lactate in animals and many microorganisms, or to ethanol and carbon dioxide in yeast — regenerating the NAD+ needed to keep glycolysis running.

History and significance

Glycolysis, also known as the Embden–Meyerhof–Parnas pathway after its principal elucidators, is thought to be one of the most ancient metabolic pathways, predating the oxygenation of Earth's atmosphere. It is central to medicine: its upregulation in cancer cells (the Warburg effect) is exploited in PET imaging, and defects in its enzymes cause rare anemias and metabolic diseases.
