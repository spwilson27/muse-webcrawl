National City Lines

National City Lines, Inc. (NCL) was a public transportation company formed in 1936 in the United States, controlled with investment by General Motors, Firestone Tire, Standard Oil of California, Phillips Petroleum and Mack Trucks. Through subsidiaries it acquired streetcar and bus systems in dozens of American cities and converted many electric streetcar lines to bus operation — the episode at the centre of the "General Motors streetcar conspiracy" controversy.

Operations

NCL and its subsidiaries Pacific City Lines and American City Lines operated transit in some 46 cities including Los Angeles (through Los Angeles Railway), Oakland (Key System), St. Louis, Baltimore, Salt Lake City and Tulsa. In most properties the companies replaced streetcars with buses supplied by their investors, and agreed to purchase exclusively from those suppliers.

Antitrust case

In 1947 the companies and their supplier-investors were indicted; in United States v. National City Lines (1949) they were convicted of conspiring to monopolise the sale of buses and supplies to the operating companies — though acquitted of conspiring to monopolise transit itself. The corporations were fined $5,000 each and one executive $1. Historians debate how decisive NCL was in the broader decline of American streetcars, which also faced suburbanisation, fare regulation, ageing infrastructure and the economics of private operation — but in popular memory the case remains the emblem of that decline.
