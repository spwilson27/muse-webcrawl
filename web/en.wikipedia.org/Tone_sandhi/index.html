Tone sandhi

Tone sandhi is the change that occurs in the pronunciation of a tone when it appears in a particular phonological or grammatical context, most typically when adjacent to another tone. It is a type of sandhi — sound change at word or morpheme boundaries — found in tonal languages such as Mandarin Chinese, Cantonese, Thai, Vietnamese and many languages of West Africa and the Americas. The underlying (citation) tone of a syllable may thus be pronounced quite differently in connected speech.

Mandarin Chinese

The best-known example is the Mandarin third-tone sandhi: a third tone (low-dipping, 214) followed by another third tone is pronounced as a rising second tone. Thus nǐ hǎo ("hello") is pronounced approximately ní hǎo, though written tone marks in pinyin normally keep the original third tone. Sequences of three or more third tones change according to the word's internal structure. Two grammatical words have their own sandhi rules: bù "not" (fourth tone) becomes second tone bú before another fourth tone, and yī "one" shifts between first, second and fourth tone depending on context. Half-third sandhi reduces a third tone before any non-third tone to a low falling contour without the rise.

Other languages

Min languages such as Taiwanese Hokkien have far more extensive systems: nearly every tone changes when a syllable is not in phrase-final position, governed by a regular cycle of changes sometimes called the "tone circle", and long utterances must be parsed into phrase-final and non-final syllables to be pronounced correctly. Cantonese has a lexical "changed tone" (pin yam) that marks familiarity or diminutive meaning. In Thai, sandhi effects are mostly limited, while many African languages show downdrift and tone terracing that can be analysed in sandhi terms.

Analysis

Tone sandhi was a testing ground for theories of phonology: the opacity of Mandarin third-tone chains (where the structure of a word determines which syllables change) motivated prosodic and optimality-theoretic analyses, and the distinction between underlying and surface tones parallels other alternations in sound patterns. Written forms — pinyin, characters or dictionaries — usually record the underlying tone, so learners must learn sandhi rules to pronounce words correctly in context.
