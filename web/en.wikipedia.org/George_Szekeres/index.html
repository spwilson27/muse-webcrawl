George Szekeres

George Szekeres
Born: 29 May 1911, Budapest, Hungary
Died: 28 August 2005, Adelaide, Australia
Fields: Mathematics, theoretical physics
Known for: Erdős–Szekeres theorem; Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates

George Szekeres (Hungarian: Szekeres György; 29 May 1911 – 28 August 2005) was a Hungarian–Australian mathematician. He is known for fundamental results in combinatorics — most famously the Erdős–Szekeres theorem on monotone subsequences and the "happy ending problem" — and for the Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates used in general relativity.

Early life in Hungary

Szekeres was trained as a chemist at the Technical University of Budapest and worked for six years as an analytical chemist, but mathematics was his passion. In Budapest he belonged to a circle of brilliant young mathematicians including Paul Erdős and Paul Turán. With Erdős he proved in 1935 that any sufficiently long sequence of distinct real numbers contains a long monotone subsequence — the Erdős–Szekeres theorem — and the pair also tackled the problem of points in general position forming convex polygons. Esther Klein observed that among any five points in the plane some four form a convex quadrilateral; the ensuing investigation, which Erdős and Szekeres solved for larger cases, was nicknamed the "happy ending problem" because it led to the marriage of George Szekeres and Esther Klein in 1937.

Shanghai and Australia

Escaping the rising persecution of Jews in Europe, the Szekeres family fled in 1939 to Shanghai, where they lived through the Second World War and the Chinese civil war in the Hongkew ghetto community of refugees. In 1948 Szekeres took up a lectureship at the University of Adelaide, beginning a distinguished Australian career; in 1963 he became the inaugural professor of pure mathematics at the University of New South Wales in Sydney, where he built a leading school of combinatorics. In relativity, his 1960 paper introduced the coordinate system independently developed by Martin Kruskal that removes the coordinate singularity at the Schwarzschild horizon, clarifying the maximal extension of the Schwarzschild black hole solution.

Later life

Szekeres was a foundation fellow of the Australian Academy of Science and was made a Member of the Order of Australia in 2002. He was deeply committed to mathematics education and problem-solving competitions. In 2004 he and Esther celebrated their 67th wedding anniversary; remarkably, they died within an hour of each other in Adelaide on 28 August 2005, both aged 94.
