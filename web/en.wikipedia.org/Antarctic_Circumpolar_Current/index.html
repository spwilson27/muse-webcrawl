Antarctic Circumpolar Current
TypeOcean current
LocationSouthern Ocean, encircling Antarctica
DirectionWest to east (clockwise seen from the South Pole)
TransportAbout 100–150 million cubic metres per second (Sverdrups) — the largest of any current

The Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC) is the world's largest ocean current. It flows clockwise (as seen from the South Pole) from west to east around Antarctica, through the Southern Ocean, driven by the strong westerly winds of the sub-Antarctic latitudes — the "roaring forties" and "furious fifties". It is the only major current that flows completely around the globe, unimpeded by continents except where it squeezes through the Drake Passage between South America and the Antarctic Peninsula.

Characteristics

The ACC transports on the order of 100–150 Sverdrups (millions of cubic metres per second), far more than any other current, and connects the Atlantic, Pacific and Indian ocean basins, exchanging water among them. Its flow is concentrated in several sharp fronts — the Subantarctic Front and the Antarctic Polar Front (the historical "Antarctic Convergence") — across which temperature and salinity change rapidly. South of the current, wind-driven upwelling brings deep, nutrient-rich water to the surface, supporting the productivity of the Southern Ocean ecosystem, including Antarctic krill.

Role in climate

By isolating Antarctica thermally from warmer subtropical waters, the ACC helps maintain the Antarctic ice sheet. Its establishment — after the opening of the Drake Passage and the Tasmanian gateway as Gondwana broke up, roughly 34 million years ago around the Eocene–Oligocene transition — is widely linked to the onset of Antarctic glaciation. The ACC also plays a central role in the global overturning circulation and in the ocean's uptake of heat and carbon dioxide; changes in the westerly winds under climate change are observed to be shifting and intensifying the current, with implications for ice-shelf melt and sea-level rise.
