John Donne

[Image placeholder: portrait of John Donne]

John Donne (c. 1571–1572 – 31 March 1631) was an English poet, scholar, soldier, secretary, and cleric in the Church of England. He is considered the pre-eminent representative of the metaphysical poets. His poetic works are noted for their metaphorical and sensual style and include sonnets, love poems, religious poems, Latin translations, epigrams, elegies, songs, and satires.

Born into a recusant Catholic family in London, Donne studied at Oxford and Cambridge without taking a degree (to avoid the required oath), and read law at Lincoln's Inn. He sailed with the Earl of Essex's expeditions against Spain and became secretary to Sir Thomas Egerton, Lord Keeper of the Great Seal. In 1601 he secretly married Egerton's niece, Anne More; the marriage wrecked his secular career and landed him briefly in prison.

Cleric and preacher

After years of poverty and patronage, Donne took holy orders in 1615 at the urging of James I, and in 1621 became Dean of St Paul's Cathedral, a post he held until his death. His sermons ranked among the most celebrated of the age. His prose Devotions upon Emergent Occasions (1624), written during a serious illness, contains the famous meditations "No man is an island" and "for whom the bell tolls; it tolls for thee".

Poetry and legacy

Donne's secular verse — the Songs and Sonnets, including "The Flea", "The Sun Rising", "A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning", and "The Canonization" — is famed for its bold conceits and argumentative wit; his religious verse includes the Holy Sonnets ("Death, be not proud"). Published posthumously, his work fell from favour in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries but was revived by T. S. Eliot and modern criticism, which proclaimed him one of the greatest love poets in English.
