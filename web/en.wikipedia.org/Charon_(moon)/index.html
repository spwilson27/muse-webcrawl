Charon is the largest moon of the dwarf planet Pluto and the largest known satellite relative to its primary body in the Solar System. With half Pluto's diameter and one-eighth its mass, Charon is so large that the pair are often considered a double dwarf planet: the system's barycentre lies in space above Pluto's surface. Charon was discovered in 1978 by astronomer James W. Christy at the U.S. Naval Observatory, as a periodic bulge in photographic images of Pluto.

Name

Christy named the moon after Charon, the ferryman of the dead in Greek mythology who carries souls across the river Styx — a fitting companion to Pluto, god of the underworld — while noting the name also echoed his wife's name, Charlene ("Char"). The name was formally adopted by the International Astronomical Union in 1986.

Orbit and rotation

Charon and Pluto are locked in mutual tidal synchrony: Charon orbits in 6.387 days, exactly Pluto's rotation period, so each body keeps the same face toward the other forever. Charon is the innermost and by far the largest of Pluto's five known moons; the smaller moons Styx, Nix, Kerberos, and Hydra orbit beyond it.

Surface and composition

NASA's New Horizons spacecraft flew through the Pluto system on 14 July 2015, returning the first close images of Charon. They revealed a surprisingly varied world: smooth, apparently young plains in the south (Vulcan Planitia) likely resurfaced by cryovolcanism or tectonic relaxation; a fractured chasm system (Serenity Chasma) among the largest canyons known; and a dark reddish north polar cap, informally named Mordor Macula, coloured by tholins — organic compounds thought to form from methane escaping Pluto and frozen onto Charon's winter pole. Charon's surface is dominated by water ice, unlike Pluto's nitrogen and methane ices, and its density (about 1.70 g/cm³) implies a mostly rocky interior with a substantial ice fraction. No significant atmosphere was detected.

Formation

The leading hypothesis holds that Charon formed from debris of a giant impact between Pluto and another Kuiper belt object early in Solar System history, analogous to the formation of Earth's Moon.
