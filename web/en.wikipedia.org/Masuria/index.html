Masuria (Polish: Mazury; German: Masuren) is a historical region in northeastern Poland, famous for its more than 2,000 lakes. Together with Warmia it forms the Warmian-Masurian Voivodeship. The region is one of Poland's most popular tourist destinations, renowned for sailing, canoeing, angling and unspoiled nature, and it was a finalist in the New7Wonders of Nature competition.

Geography

The Masurian Lake District stretches across the southeastern part of the former East Prussia. Its landscape was shaped by the Pleistocene ice age: terminal moraines, drumlins and thousands of post-glacial lakes, the largest being Śniardwy (113.8 km²) and Mamry. The Great Masurian Lakes are connected by rivers and canals, forming a sailing route of about 200 km. Extensive forests, including the Piska Forest, and the Augustów Primeval Forest fringe the region.

History

The area was originally inhabited by the Old Prussians, a Baltic people conquered by the Teutonic Order in the 13th century. From the 14th century, settlers from Mazovia — Polish-speaking peasants known as Masurians — moved into the southern part of the region, giving it its name. After the secularisation of the Teutonic Order in 1525, Masuria became part of Ducal Prussia and later the Kingdom of Prussia and the German Empire. The Masurians, though Polish-speaking, were mostly Lutheran and identified strongly with Prussia; in the 1920 East Prussian plebiscite they voted overwhelmingly to remain in Germany. After the Second World War the region was transferred to Poland, the remaining German population was expelled, and the Masurians largely emigrated to West Germany in later decades.

Culture and tourism

Today Masuria markets itself as the "Land of a Thousand Lakes." Towns such as Giżycko, Mikołajki and Ruciane-Nida are centres of yacht tourism, and the region is crossed by the Elbląg Canal to the west. The Wolf's Lair, Hitler's wartime headquarters near Kętrzyn, is a major historical attraction.
