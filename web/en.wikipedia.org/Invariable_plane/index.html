Invariable plane

The invariable plane of a planetary system, also called the Laplacian plane after its discoverer Pierre-Simon Laplace, is the plane passing through its barycenter (center of mass) perpendicular to its angular momentum vector. In the Solar System, about 98% of the angular momentum is contributed by the orbital angular momenta of the four giant planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune), so the invariable plane lies close to Jupiter's orbital plane.

Properties

The invariable plane is unaffected by perturbations within the system: because total angular momentum is conserved, the plane perpendicular to it, passing through the barycenter, is fixed in inertial space. This makes it a natural reference plane for describing the Solar System as a whole, more fundamental in some respects than either the ecliptic (Earth's orbital plane) or the Sun's equator.

The invariable plane is inclined by about 0.5° to Jupiter's orbit, about 1.57° to the ecliptic, and about 6–7° to the Sun's equator. All planets' orbits are inclined to it by less than about 6°, Mercury being the most inclined after Pluto among the dwarf planets.

History

Laplace developed the concept in his work on celestial mechanics (Mécanique céleste, published 1799–1825), in which he showed that the plane of maximum total moment of rotation could serve as a fundamental reference. The concept generalizes to any isolated gravitating system, such as a galaxy or a binary star system.

Use as a reference

Although the ecliptic remains the usual reference for Solar System coordinates, the invariable plane is sometimes preferred for long-term dynamical studies because it does not drift under planetary perturbations. Positions measured relative to it are stable over very long timescales, limited only by external effects such as passing stars and the galactic tide.
