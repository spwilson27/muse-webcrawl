The House of Commons of Canada (French: Chambre des communes du Canada) is the lower house of the Parliament of Canada, together with the Senate and the Crown. Members are elected by popular vote in single-member districts called ridings, and are known as members of Parliament (MPs).

Although the Senate must also approve legislation, the Commons is the dominant chamber: the government is formed by the party or coalition able to command its confidence, and the prime minister sits in it by modern convention.

History

The House of Commons was created by the Constitution Act, 1867, which established the Dominion of Canada and modeled its Parliament on that of the United Kingdom. The number of seats has grown with Canada's population and territory; a redistribution following the 2021 census set the chamber at 343 seats from the 2025 general election.

Composition and elections

MPs are elected using the first-past-the-post system for terms of up to five years, though elections are commonly called after about four years. Seats are distributed among the provinces roughly in proportion to population, subject to constitutional minimums, with the three territories receiving one seat each.

Functions

The Commons introduces and debates most bills, including all money bills, scrutinizes the government through question period, and can force an election by passing a motion of no confidence. Its committees study legislation and issues in detail.
