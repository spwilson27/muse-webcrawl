Amyloid plaque

Amyloid plaques (also called senile plaques or neuritic plaques) are extracellular deposits of the amyloid-beta (Aβ) peptide found in the brains of people with Alzheimer's disease. Along with neurofibrillary tangles of tau protein inside neurons, they are one of the two defining pathological features of the disease, first described by Alois Alzheimer in 1906.

Formation

Amyloid-beta is produced when the amyloid precursor protein (APP) is cleaved by beta- and gamma-secretase enzymes. The resulting peptides, particularly Aβ42, are prone to aggregate: soluble oligomers assemble into fibrils with a cross-beta sheet structure, which accumulate in dense-core plaques surrounded by dystrophic neurites, activated microglia and astrocytes. Diffuse plaques lacking the dense core are also common, especially in early stages and in cognitively normal older people.

Role in Alzheimer's disease

The amyloid cascade hypothesis proposes that abnormal Aβ accumulation initiates the disease process, driving tau pathology, synaptic loss and dementia. Plaque burden can be measured in life using PET imaging with amyloid-binding tracers, and its presence helps define Alzheimer's disease biologically. Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies that clear plaques have been developed as disease-modifying treatments, though their clinical benefit and risks remain debated.
