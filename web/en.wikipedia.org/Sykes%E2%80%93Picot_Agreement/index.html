Sykes–Picot Agreement

Sykes–Picot Agreement
Signed: 16 May 1916
Parties: United Kingdom, France (with Russian assent)
Subject: Partition of Ottoman Arab provinces
Negotiators: Mark Sykes, François Georges-Picot

The Sykes–Picot Agreement was a secret treaty concluded in 1916 between the United Kingdom and France, with the assent of the Russian Empire, defining their proposed spheres of influence and control in the Middle East should the Ottoman Empire be defeated in the First World War.

Terms

Negotiated by diplomats Mark Sykes and François Georges-Picot, the agreement allotted Britain control of southern Mesopotamia and the ports of Haifa and Acre, France control of Lebanon, coastal Syria, and southeastern Anatolia, with other Arab lands divided into British and French zones of influence and Palestine placed under international administration. A large area was nominally reserved for an Arab state or confederation under European tutelage.

Disclosure and legacy

The Bolsheviks published the secret text in November 1917, embarrassing Britain, whose promises in the Hussein–McMahon correspondence to support Arab independence appeared contradicted by the agreement. The post-war settlement substantially altered the map, but "Sykes–Picot" remains a byword for imperial duplicity and is frequently blamed for the artificial borders and later conflicts of the modern Middle East.
