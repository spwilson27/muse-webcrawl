Demutualization

Demutualization is the process by which a mutually owned organisation — one owned by its members or customers rather than by shareholders — converts into a publicly traded company owned by shareholders. Demutualization has been most common among life insurance companies, building societies, savings banks, and stock exchanges. The process is also known as "privatisation" of a mutual in some contexts, and its reverse, converting a shareholder-owned firm into a mutual, is rare but not unknown.

Process and motives

In a full demutualization, members exchange their ownership rights for shares in the new company, cash, or enhanced policy benefits, and the company typically raises additional capital through an initial public offering. In a sponsored or partial demutualization, a mutual holding company may retain control while a minority stake is sold. Motives commonly cited include gaining access to capital markets for growth and acquisitions, the ability to use shares as acquisition currency or employee incentives, and escaping regulatory constraints that apply to mutuals. Critics argue that demutualization transfers accumulated value from generations of members to current members and managers, weakens the customer-oriented ethos of mutuals, and exposes former mutuals to short-term market pressures.

Examples

A wave of demutualizations swept the insurance industry in the 1990s and early 2000s: in the United States, companies such as MetLife, Prudential, and John Hancock converted, while Canada's Manulife and Sun Life did the same. In the United Kingdom, many building societies converted to banks in the 1980s and 1990s — Abbey National, Halifax, Woolwich, and Northern Rock among them — a trend that drew criticism after several of the converted institutions failed or required rescue in the financial crisis of 2008. Stock exchanges, including the New York Stock Exchange's parent in 2006, also demutualized to become for-profit listed companies.
