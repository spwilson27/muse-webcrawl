Marcus Tullius Cicero (3 January 106 BC – 7 December 43 BC) was a Roman statesman, lawyer, scholar, philosopher and Academic sceptic who tried, during the political crises that led to the end of the Roman Republic, to uphold republican principles. He is regarded as one of Rome's greatest orators and prose stylists, and his Latin was long taken as the model of classical style.

Born at Arpinum to a wealthy municipal family of equestrian rank, Cicero was a "new man" (novus homo) in Roman politics. After studying law, rhetoric and philosophy at Rome and in Greece, he rose through the cursus honorum, becoming quaestor in Sicily in 75 BC and winning fame by prosecuting the corrupt governor Verres. As consul in 63 BC he suppressed the conspiracy of Catiline, executing five conspirators without trial — an act that later drove him into exile in 58–57 BC.

Civil wars and death

Caught between Pompey and Julius Caesar, Cicero reluctantly sided with Pompey; after Caesar's assassination in 44 BC he emerged as leader of the senatorial cause, delivering the Philippics against Mark Antony. When the Second Triumvirate was formed, Cicero was proscribed and murdered on 7 December 43 BC; his head and hands were displayed on the Rostra in the Forum.

Works and influence

Cicero's surviving writings include 58 speeches, treatises on rhetoric, philosophy and politics, and more than 900 letters, which give an unmatched picture of late-republican Rome. He introduced Greek philosophy to Roman readers, coining Latin terms such as qualitas. Rediscovered by Petrarch, his works shaped Renaissance humanism and the political thought of the Enlightenment.
