Liver cancer is cancer that begins in the cells of the liver (primary liver cancer), as distinct from cancer that spreads to the liver from elsewhere (liver metastases, which are considerably more common). The most frequent primary type in adults is hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), which accounts for roughly 75–85% of cases. Other primary types include intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (cancer of the bile ducts within the liver) and, in young children, hepatoblastoma.

Causes and risk factors

Most hepatocellular carcinoma develops in a liver damaged by cirrhosis. Worldwide, chronic infection with hepatitis B virus is the single largest cause; hepatitis C, heavy alcohol use, and increasingly metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (fatty liver related to obesity and diabetes) are major contributors. Dietary exposure to aflatoxins—poisons produced by Aspergillus moulds on stored grains and nuts—synergizes strongly with hepatitis B. Vaccination against hepatitis B has sharply reduced HCC incidence in countries with universal childhood immunization programs.

Signs and diagnosis

Early liver cancer usually causes no symptoms. Later features can include pain in the upper right abdomen, unexplained weight loss, loss of appetite, jaundice, fatigue, and fluid accumulation in the abdomen (ascites). People with known cirrhosis are often monitored with periodic ultrasound, sometimes combined with the blood marker alpha-fetoprotein (AFP). Diagnosis is usually made by characteristic appearances on multiphasic CT or MRI scans; biopsy is reserved for uncertain cases.

Treatment and prognosis

Management depends on tumour stage, liver function, and overall health, often summarized by staging systems such as the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer classification. Potentially curative options for early disease include surgical resection, liver transplantation, and local ablation (such as radiofrequency or microwave ablation). For intermediate disease, transarterial chemoembolization is used; for advanced disease, systemic therapies including targeted agents (e.g., sorafenib, lenvatinib) and immunotherapy combinations (such as atezolizumab with bevacizumab) have improved survival. Overall prognosis remains guarded because many cases are detected late and arise in already diseased livers.

Epidemiology

Liver cancer is among the top causes of cancer death globally, with the highest incidence in East Asia, Southeast Asia, and parts of Africa, reflecting the distribution of chronic hepatitis B. Incidence in Western countries has been rising with the obesity epidemic.
