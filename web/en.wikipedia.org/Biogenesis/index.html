Biogenesis

Biogenesis is the production of new living organisms or organelles. The law of biogenesis, attributed to Louis Pasteur, states that living things can only arise from other living things by reproduction — for example, a spider lays eggs that develop into spiders. The assumption is that life cannot arise spontaneously from non-living matter, a position contrasted with abiogenesis and with the older doctrine of spontaneous generation.

History

The term "biogenesis" was coined by Henry Charlton Bastian, while Thomas Henry Huxley preferred the related term in his 1870 address to the British Association. Through the nineteenth century, experiments by Francesco Redi, Lazzaro Spallanzani and, decisively, Pasteur dismantled the idea that organisms such as maggots or microbes arose spontaneously in broth or meat. Pasteur's swan-neck flask experiments of 1859–1861 showed that sterile broth remained sterile unless exposed to airborne particles.

Biogenesis and abiogenesis

Biogenesis describes the ordinary reproduction of life from life observed in the present day. It does not by itself explain the ultimate origin of life on Earth; the study of how life first arose from non-living chemistry is the field of abiogenesis, which concerns events billions of years ago under very different conditions.
