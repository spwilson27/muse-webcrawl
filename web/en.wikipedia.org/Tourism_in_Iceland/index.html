Tourism in Iceland

Tourism in Iceland has grown considerably in economic significance in the past 15 years. The tourism industry is one of Iceland's three main sources of foreign exchange revenue, alongside marine products and aluminium. The number of tourists visiting Iceland each year grew from under 500,000 in 2010 to around 2.3 million in 2018, several times the country's population of roughly 360,000.

History

Iceland's tourism boom was spurred by the publicity surrounding the 2010 eruption of Eyjafjallajökull, the depreciation of the Icelandic króna after the 2008 financial crisis, and national marketing campaigns such as "Inspired by Iceland". Low-cost airlines, notably Icelandair and WOW air (until its 2019 collapse), introduced stopover programmes that made Iceland an easy addition to transatlantic trips.

Attractions

Most visitors tour the Golden Circle — Þingvellir National Park, the Geysir geothermal area, and Gullfoss waterfall — from Reykjavík. Other major draws include the South Coast waterfalls Seljalandsfoss and Skógafoss, the black sand beach at Reynisfjara, the Jökulsárlón glacier lagoon, whale watching from Húsavík and Reykjavík, geothermal bathing at the Blue Lagoon, and northern lights viewing in winter. Vatnajökull National Park covers much of the southeast and central highlands.

Impact and challenges

Tourism created jobs and regional income but also raised concerns about pressure on fragile natural sites, housing costs in Reykjavík, and road safety for visitors unaccustomed to Icelandic conditions. The COVID-19 pandemic caused a sharp collapse in arrivals in 2020, followed by a strong recovery in subsequent years.
