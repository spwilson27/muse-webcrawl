Monsoon of South Asia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The monsoon of South Asia is the world's most powerful monsoon system, a seasonal reversal of winds that brings the great majority of annual rainfall to India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Sri Lanka. It is driven by the differential heating of the Asian landmass and the Indian Ocean, together with the seasonal migration of the Intertropical Convergence Zone and the elevated heat source of the Tibetan Plateau.

The southwest (summer) monsoon

As the subcontinent heats in April and May, a low-pressure zone over northern India draws in moist air from the Indian Ocean. The southwest monsoon sets in over Kerala around 1 June and advances across India by mid-July, splitting into an Arabian Sea branch — which drenches the Western Ghats — and a Bay of Bengal branch, which heads northeast and then westward up the Gangetic plain. From June to September the system delivers roughly 70–80% of India's annual rainfall, on which its agriculture and water supply depend; failures or excesses cause droughts and floods affecting hundreds of millions of people.

The northeast (winter) monsoon

From October, cooling land reverses the flow: dry northeasterly winds blow offshore, but where they cross the Bay of Bengal they bring the main rainy season to southeastern India — Tamil Nadu and coastal Andhra Pradesh — and to Sri Lanka, from October to December.

Variability

Though its seasonal advance is regular, the monsoon varies greatly within and between years, modulated by the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, the Indian Ocean Dipole, and intraseasonal oscillations that produce "active" and "break" spells. The India Meteorological Department issues long-range forecasts each spring; the monsoon's performance is a matter of national economic significance and a focus of climate-change research.
