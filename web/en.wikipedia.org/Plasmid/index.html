Plasmid

A plasmid is a small, circular DNA molecule separate from the chromosome

A plasmid is a small, extrachromosomal DNA molecule within a cell that is physically separated from chromosomal DNA and can replicate independently. They are most commonly found as small circular, double-stranded DNA molecules in bacteria; however, plasmids are sometimes present in other organisms, including archaea and eukaryotes such as yeast. The term was introduced by Joshua Lederberg in 1952.

Properties and significance

Plasmid sizes range from about one thousand to several hundred thousand base pairs, and a single cell may carry from one copy to hundreds of copies of a given plasmid. Plasmid genes are usually not essential for survival under ordinary conditions, but they often confer advantages such as antibiotic resistance, the ability to metabolise unusual compounds, production of toxins or bacteriocins, and virulence factors. Because plasmids can be transferred between bacteria by conjugation, they are major agents of horizontal gene transfer and of the rapid spread of antibiotic resistance among pathogens.

Use in biotechnology

Engineered plasmids were foundational tools of recombinant DNA technology. A gene of interest can be inserted into a plasmid vector containing an origin of replication and a selectable marker such as an antibiotic-resistance gene, allowing the plasmid to be propagated in bacteria and used to produce proteins — the route by which recombinant human insulin was first manufactured — or to deliver genes in molecular biology experiments. Famous laboratory plasmids include the pBR322 and pUC series. Plasmid-based DNA vaccines and gene-therapy vectors are active areas of research.
