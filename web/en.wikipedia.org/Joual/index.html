Joual

Region: Montreal and working-class Quebec
Language family: Indo-European → Romance → French → Quebec French
Named from: Local pronunciation of cheval ("horse")

Joual is the name given to the working-class dialect of French spoken in Montreal, long stigmatized as a debased patois but reclaimed during Quebec's Quiet Revolution as a badge of Québécois identity. The word derives from the local pronunciation of cheval ("horse"), said as joual.

Features

Joual is characterized by distinctive phonology — including affrication of /t/ and /d/ before high vowels, diphthongization of long vowels, and reductions such as chu for je suis ("I am") — together with archaisms preserved from colonial French, borrowings from English (char for car, fun, chum), and religious profanity, the sacres, drawn from Catholic vocabulary such as tabarnac (tabernacle) and câlice (chalice).

The joual debate

The term entered public debate in 1959–1960, notably through an exchange in Le Devoir in which André Laurendeau and the pseudonymous "Frère Untel" (Jean-Paul Desbiens) lamented the state of French among Quebec students. During the 1960s, however, writers of the Parti pris movement embraced joual as the authentic language of a colonized people. Michel Tremblay's play Les Belles-sœurs (1968), written entirely in joual, was a landmark of Quebec theatre, arguing that the people's real speech belonged on stage.

Status today

Linguists generally treat joual as a sociolect of Quebec French rather than a separate language, and debate continues over the divide between popular speech and the standardized Quebec French promoted by institutions. Many of its features have spread socially and regionally, and the word itself now often denotes Montreal working-class French in general.
