FARC

The FARC operated for decades from Colombia's rural regions.

The Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia—People's Army (Spanish: Fuerzas Armadas Revolucionarias de Colombia—Ejército del Pueblo, FARC–EP or FARC) was a Marxist–Leninist guerrilla movement that fought the Colombian government from 1964 until its demobilization in 2017, the longest-running insurgency in the Western Hemisphere.

Origins and growth

The FARC emerged from peasant self-defence groups and the Colombian Communist Party during La Violencia and the military assault on the "independent republic" of Marquetalia in 1964. Under leaders including Manuel Marulanda ("Tirofijo") and Jacobo Arenas, it adopted a strategy of prolonged guerrilla war aimed at seizing power. Numbering only a few hundred fighters initially, it grew to an estimated 15,000–18,000 combatants by the late 1990s, financed by "taxes" on the coca economy, kidnapping for ransom, extortion, and illegal mining—a shift that led many governments to designate it a terrorist organization and narco-trafficking group.

Conflict

The insurgency featured attacks on military and civilian targets, mass kidnappings (including presidential candidate Íngrid Betancourt, rescued in 2008), and control of large rural zones. A demilitarized zone granted for peace talks in 1998–2002 collapsed amid continued violence. Under President Álvaro Uribe's "democratic security" policy and U.S.-backed Plan Colombia, the FARC was gravely weakened, losing senior commanders including Raúl Reyes and Mono Jojoy.

Peace accord

Negotiations in Havana under President Juan Manuel Santos produced a peace accord signed in 2016; after an initial version was narrowly rejected in a plebiscite, a revised accord was ratified by Congress. In 2017 the FARC handed over its weapons to the United Nations and reorganized as a political party (later renamed Comunes). Some dissident factions rejected the accord and rearmed. The conflict overall caused hundreds of thousands of deaths and displaced millions of Colombians.
