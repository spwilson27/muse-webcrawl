Problem of induction

The problem of induction is a philosophical problem that questions the rationality of predictions about unobserved things based on previous observations. These inferences from the observed to the unobserved — for example, inferring that the sun will rise tomorrow because it has always risen in the past — characterise inductive reasoning. The problem was classically formulated by the Scottish philosopher David Hume in the eighteenth century: any attempt to justify induction, he argued, either appeals to deduction (which cannot guarantee conclusions about unobserved matters) or appeals to induction itself, which is circular.

Hume's formulation

In A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40) and the Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748), Hume divided all reasoning into relations of ideas (demonstrable, necessary) and matters of fact (contingent). Inferences about unobserved matters of fact presuppose that the future will resemble the past — the "uniformity of nature" — but this principle can itself only be supported by experience, which presupposes it. Hume's own "sceptical solution" held that inductive inference rests not on reason but on custom and habit, a natural instinct shared with animals.

Famous illustrations

Bertrand Russell illustrated the problem with a chicken that, fed every day of its life, one day has its neck wrung — "more refined views of the uniformity of nature would have been useful to the chicken". The black swan problem — Europeans believed all swans white until black swans were found in Australia — became a byword for the fragility of inductive generalisations, later developed by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.

Responses

Karl Popper's falsificationism claimed science avoids induction entirely, proceeding by conjecture and refutation — an account many philosophers consider descriptively incomplete. Nelson Goodman posed a "new riddle of induction" with his predicate grue, asking why some generalisations are projectible and others not. Bayesian approaches justify updating beliefs via conditionalisation without claiming certainty. Other responses include reliabilism, pragmatic vindications (Reichenbach), and Humean naturalism, which accepts Hume's conclusion that belief here outruns reason.
