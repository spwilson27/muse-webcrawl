Fiber-optic communication

Fiber-optic communication is a method of transmitting information from one place to another by sending pulses of light through an optical fiber. The light forms an electromagnetic carrier wave that is modulated to carry information. Fiber is preferred over electrical cabling when high bandwidth, long transmission distances, or immunity to electromagnetic interference are required.

Principle

An optical fiber consists of a core of highly transparent glass or plastic surrounded by a cladding of lower refractive index, so light is confined by total internal reflection. Modern single-mode fibers have attenuation as low as 0.2 decibels per kilometre at the 1550 nm wavelength window, allowing signals to travel many tens of kilometres before amplification is needed.

System components

A fiber-optic link requires an optical transmitter, usually a laser diode or LED, the fiber itself, and a receiver using a photodiode. Long-haul systems use erbium-doped fiber amplifiers and wavelength-division multiplexing, in which many colours of light carry independent channels over the same fiber, to reach capacities of many terabits per second.

History

The theoretical basis was laid by Charles Kao in the 1960s, who proposed that purified glass fibers could carry light over long distances; he later received the Nobel Prize in Physics for this work. Corning produced the first low-loss fibers in 1970, and commercial systems spread rapidly through telephone networks in the 1980s.

Applications

Optical fibers form the backbone of the Internet, including transoceanic submarine cables, and are increasingly used for direct connections to homes and businesses (fiber to the home). They are also used in medicine, sensing and industrial inspection.
