New Jerusalem

The New Jerusalem descending from heaven, as described in Revelation 21.

In the Book of Ezekiel in the Hebrew Bible, Ezekiel's vision of a restored temple and city is a prototype of the New Jerusalem, which is described in detail in the Book of Revelation in the New Testament as the heavenly city that descends from God at the end of history. John of Patmos describes a vast cubical city, 12,000 stadia in each dimension, with walls of jasper, foundations adorned with precious stones, twelve gates of pearl named for the tribes of Israel, and streets of pure gold; within it God dwells directly with humanity, there is no temple, sun, death, or sorrow, and the river and tree of life stand at its centre (Revelation 21–22).

Interpretations

Interpretations differ widely. Many Christians understand the city literally as a future dwelling place of the redeemed on a renewed earth; others read it symbolically as the Church, the people of God, or the consummation of God's kingdom. Jewish tradition anticipates a rebuilt Jerusalem and Third Temple in the messianic age. The phrase has been applied metaphorically to earthly projects — from Puritan settlements in New England to William Blake's poem "And did those feet in ancient time," which vows to build Jerusalem "in England's green and pleasant land."

In other traditions

In the Latter Day Saint movement, the New Jerusalem is a city to be built in America, identified with Independence, Missouri. Various intentional communities and denominations have taken the name. The concept links to related eschatological ideas such as the Kingdom of God and the new heavens and new earth of Isaiah.
