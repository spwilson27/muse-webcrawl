Italian War of 1551–1559

Warfare in Italy during the 1550s

The Italian War of 1551–1559, sometimes called the Habsburg–Valois War, was the final conflict of the Italian Wars (1494–1559), fought between the Kingdom of France under Henry II and the Habsburg empire of Charles V and later Philip II of Spain, with shifting involvement of the Papal States, the Republic of Siena, the Ottoman Empire and England.

Course of the war

The war began in 1551 when Henry II allied with German Protestant princes (Treaty of Chambord, 1552) and the Ottomans, seizing the Three Bishoprics of Metz, Toul and Verdun. Fighting spread to Italy, where France supported Siena against Imperial-Florentine forces; Siena fell in 1555 after a gruelling siege, ending its republic. In 1556 Charles V abdicated, dividing his realms between Ferdinand I and Philip II. Spanish forces under the Duke of Alba invaded the Papal States, while in the north the Duke of Guise's failed expedition to Naples (1557) was offset by the French capture of Calais from England in January 1558 — England's last continental possession. Spain's decisive victories at St Quentin (1557) and Gravelines (1558) nevertheless pushed France toward peace.

Peace of Cateau-Cambrésis

The war ended with the Treaties of Cateau-Cambrésis (April 1559) between France, Spain and England. France retained the Three Bishoprics and Calais but renounced its claims in Italy (apart from the marquisate of Saluzzo) and restored Savoy and Piedmont. The settlement confirmed Spanish dominance in Italy — Milan, Naples, Sicily, Sardinia and the Presidi — for over a century.
