False memory syndrome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

False memory syndrome (FMS) describes a condition in which a person's identity and relationships are affected by memories that are factually incorrect but strongly believed — most controversially, recovered memories of childhood abuse that are alleged to have been created through suggestive therapeutic techniques. The term was coined by Peter J. Freyd and popularised by the False Memory Syndrome Foundation (FMSF), established in the United States in 1992 by parents accused of abuse on the basis of such memories.

Background

The concept arose during the "memory wars" of the 1980s and 1990s, when some psychotherapeutic practices — hypnosis, guided imagery, dream interpretation and pressure to "recover" repressed memories of abuse — were accused of implanting vivid false recollections in patients. Research by psychologists such as Elizabeth Loftus demonstrated that memory is reconstructive and that false memories of entire events could be created in experimental settings (the "lost in the mall" paradigm).

Status and debate

False memory syndrome is not recognised as a diagnosis in the DSM-5 or ICD-11. Critics of the concept, including some clinicians and abuse-survivor advocates, argue that the term was never validated scientifically and that it has been used to discredit genuine victims. Mainstream psychological bodies take a middle position: delayed recall of genuine abuse is documented, but memories recovered through highly suggestive techniques should be treated with caution and require corroboration. The False Memory Syndrome Foundation dissolved in 2019.
