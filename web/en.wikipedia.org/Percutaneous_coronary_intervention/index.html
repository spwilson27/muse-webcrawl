Percutaneous coronary intervention

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), commonly known as coronary angioplasty or simply angioplasty, is a minimally invasive procedure used to open narrowed coronary arteries and restore blood flow to the heart muscle. It is performed by an interventional cardiologist in a cardiac catheterisation laboratory.

Procedure

A catheter is threaded to the coronary arteries, usually from the radial artery at the wrist or the femoral artery in the groin, under X-ray (fluoroscopic) guidance and contrast imaging. A guidewire crosses the narrowed segment, a balloon is inflated to compress the atherosclerotic plaque (balloon angioplasty), and in most cases a stent — a small metal mesh tube, nowadays usually drug-eluting — is left in place to keep the artery open. Patients receive dual antiplatelet therapy afterwards to prevent stent thrombosis.

Uses

PCI performed urgently ("primary PCI") is the preferred treatment for ST-elevation myocardial infarction (heart attack) when available promptly. It is also used for other acute coronary syndromes and for stable coronary artery disease with limiting angina; for some patterns of disease, coronary artery bypass grafting remains superior. Andreas Gruentzig performed the first successful coronary balloon angioplasty in 1977.
