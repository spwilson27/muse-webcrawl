The anterior interosseous nerve (also called the volar interosseous nerve) is a branch of the median nerve that supplies the deep muscles on the front of the forearm. It arises from the median nerve in the proximal forearm, just below the elbow, and descends on the front surface of the interosseous membrane between the radius and ulna, accompanied by the anterior interosseous artery.

Course and supply

The nerve typically branches from the median nerve about 5–8 cm below the lateral epicondyle, passing deep to the superficial flexor muscles. It innervates three muscles: the flexor pollicis longus (flexor of the thumb), the lateral (radial) half of the flexor digitorum profundus serving the index and middle fingers, and the pronator quadratus near the wrist. It also gives articular branches to the wrist joint. Unusually for a named nerve, it has no cutaneous (skin) distribution, though it carries some proprioceptive fibres.

Clinical significance

Injury or entrapment produces anterior interosseous nerve syndrome, a pure motor palsy: patients cannot flex the tip of the thumb or the distal joint of the index finger, so they cannot make the "OK" sign, producing instead a flattened pinch. Causes include compression by fibrous bands or accessory muscles, trauma and fractures, and neuritis (including as part of Parsonage–Turner syndrome). Many neuritic cases recover spontaneously over months; persistent compression may be treated surgically.
