The laboratory rat (or lab rat) is a strain of the brown rat, Rattus norvegicus domestica, bred and kept for scientific research. Although less commonly used than the laboratory mouse, rats have been an important model organism for studies in medicine, genetics, psychology, and other fields, and they remain the second most used vertebrate in research after mice.

History

Rats were among the first animals domesticated specifically for research. Albino brown rats were used in experiments in France and England in the early 19th century, arising from the fancy-rat breeding associated with rat-catchers such as Jack Black in Victorian London. The first purposefully inbred rat colony was established at the Wistar Institute in Philadelphia in the early 20th century under Henry Herbert Donaldson, and the Wistar rat became the ancestor of many modern strains. Rats were central to early nutrition science — including the discovery of vitamins — and were flown in early space research programmes.

Strains

Laboratory rats are maintained as outbred stocks (such as Wistar and Sprague Dawley), which preserve genetic variation, and as highly inbred strains (such as Fischer 344 and Lewis), in which individuals are nearly genetically identical. Specialised models include the spontaneously hypertensive rat (a model of human hypertension), the Zucker rat (obesity and diabetes), and transgenic and knockout rats developed more readily since the sequencing of the rat genome in 2004 and the advent of efficient gene-editing.

Uses in research

Rats are used in toxicology and drug safety testing, cancer research, neuroscience (their larger brains facilitate surgery and imaging), cardiovascular and metabolic research, transplantation studies, and behavioural psychology — where maze learning, operant conditioning and addiction models are classic rat paradigms. Their size makes them better than mice for repeated blood sampling, physiological monitoring and many surgical procedures.

Biology and husbandry

Laboratory rats are social, docile animals, generally easy to handle and rarely aggressive toward humans. Adults weigh roughly 250–500 g. They are nocturnal omnivores, typically fed standardised pelleted diets under controlled conditions. Modern animal-welfare regulations, including the principles of the "3Rs" (replacement, reduction, refinement), govern their use in most jurisdictions.
