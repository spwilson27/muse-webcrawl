Spiral

In mathematics, a spiral is a curve which emanates from a point, moving farther away as it revolves around the point. Spirals occur widely in nature — in shells, galaxies, hurricanes, and plant growth — and in art and architecture.

Two-dimensional spirals

Important types include the Archimedean spiral, in which the distance between successive turns is constant; the logarithmic spiral, whose size increases while its shape remains unchanged (self-similar); the Fermat spiral; and the hyperbolic spiral. The logarithmic spiral was called the spira mirabilis by Jacob Bernoulli.

Three-dimensional spirals

A curve that winds around an axis at a constant distance is a helix; conical helices wind around a cone. Spherical spirals trace paths on spheres. The double helix structure of DNA is a famous natural example.

In nature and culture

Spiral patterns appear in nautilus shells, the arrangement of seeds in sunflowers (phyllotaxis, related to the Fibonacci sequence and golden ratio), spiral galaxies, and weather systems. Spirals are among the oldest decorative motifs in human art, from Neolithic carvings at Newgrange to Celtic art.
