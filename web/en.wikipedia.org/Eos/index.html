Eos (Ancient Greek: Ἠώς, Ēṓs) is the Greek Titan-goddess of the dawn. Each morning she rises from her home at the edge of the Oceanus and opens the gates of heaven so that Helios, the sun god, may ride his chariot across the sky. In Homer's epics she is described with the formulaic epithet "rosy-fingered" (rhododaktylos), and she is often depicted wearing a saffron-coloured robe woven with flowers.

Mythology

Eos is the daughter of the Titans Hyperion and Theia, and the sister of Helios and Selene, the moon. By the Titan Astraeus she bore the winds (the Anemoi) and the stars. Her mortal lovers were a recurring theme: Orion, Cephalus, and the Trojan prince Tithonus, for whom she begged Zeus to grant immortality but forgot to ask for eternal youth, so that he shrivelled with age. Her son Memnon, king of the Ethiopians, was killed by Achilles in the Trojan War, and her tears of grief are said to form the morning dew.

Worship and art

Eos received little independent cult worship in Greece, but she was a popular subject in Attic vase painting of the fifth century BC, often shown pursuing her lovers or mourning Memnon. Her Roman counterpart is Aurora.
