Zither

The zither (German: [ˈtsɪtɐ]) is a class of stringed instruments in which the strings are stretched over a resonating body without a projecting neck, and, in organological usage, the term covers a large family of related instruments found worldwide. The word zither is a German rendering of the Greek kithara, from which the guitar family also derives its name.

Types

The best-known form is the Alpine concert zither, developed in 19th-century Bavaria and Austria: a flat wooden box bearing five melody strings over a fretted fingerboard, played with a plectrum on the thumb, plus dozens of open accompaniment and bass strings plucked by the left hand. Instrument makers such as Johann Georg Stauffer and Johann Petzmayer shaped its design. The related chord zither or autoharp, fretless psalteries, and the hammered dulcimer belong to the wider zither family, as do the Chinese guzheng, the Japanese koto and the Korean geomungo in the broad organological sense.

Cultural presence

The zither is strongly associated with Alpine folk music, and it achieved international fame through Anton Karas's score for the 1949 film The Third Man, whose "Harry Lime Theme", played on a Viennese zither, became a worldwide hit.
