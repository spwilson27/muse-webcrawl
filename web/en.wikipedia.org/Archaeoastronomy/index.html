Archaeoastronomy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Archaeoastronomy (also spelled archaeoastronomy) is the interdisciplinary or multidisciplinary study of how people in the past "have understood the phenomena in the sky, how they used these phenomena and what role the sky played in their cultures". It combines astronomy, archaeology, anthropology, and ethnohistory, drawing on written records where they exist (sometimes distinguished as ethnoastronomy for living traditions) and on the alignments and iconography of material remains where they do not.

History of the field

Interest in the astronomical functions of ancient monuments dates to antiquarian work on Stonehenge in the 18th and 19th centuries. The field gained modern form in the 1960s and 1970s with Gerald Hawkins's Stonehenge Decoded (1963) and the work of Alexander Thom on British megalithic sites, and with studies of Maya calendrics and the astronomical codices of Mesoamerica. Although early claims were sometimes speculative and met scholarly scepticism, the field matured with stricter statistical and contextual methods, and with dedicated journals and societies (the European Society for Astronomy in Culture, SEAC, founded 1992, and the International Society for Archaeoastronomy and Astronomy in Culture, ISAAC).

Methods and evidence

Archaeoastronomers survey the orientation of buildings, tombs, and monuments against the horizon positions of the sun at solstices and equinoxes, the moon's standstill extremes, and rising points of bright stars and constellations. Because chance alignments are common, rigorous work requires statistical testing across groups of similar sites, corroboration from iconography, myth, and texts, and care for the changing sky — precession shifts star positions over millennia. Well-documented cases include the solar alignments of Newgrange in Ireland, whose roof-box admits the winter solstice sunrise; the Egyptian temples oriented to solstices and stellar risings; Maya "E-group" complexes marking seasonal sunrises; and the Chankillo solar observatory in Peru (c. 300 BC), inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Criticism and debates

Critics within archaeology have warned against reducing ritual architecture to astronomical function, against cherry-picking alignments, and against projecting modern constellations onto past cultures. The modern discipline answers by insisting on cultural context and by treating astronomical knowledge as one strand of ancient cosmology, ritual, and calendrical practice.
