FieldPalaeontology, phylogenetics

A ghost lineage is a hypothesised phylogenetic lineage that is inferred to have existed but has no fossil record. The term was coined by palaeontologist Mark Norell in 1992. Ghost lineages arise when a phylogenetic tree implies that a group must have originated earlier than its oldest known fossil, because its sister group is known from older rocks; the unrecorded interval between the inferred origin and the first fossil appearance is the ghost lineage.

Detection and significance

Ghost lineages are detected by comparing cladograms with the stratigraphic record. Measures such as the stratigraphic consistency index and the gap excess ratio quantify how much of a tree consists of ghost lineages. Long ghost lineages may indicate an incomplete fossil record, a genuinely rapid radiation, or problems with the phylogeny or with the dating of rocks. They are common, for example, in discussions of the early evolution of modern mammal orders, many of which appear to diverge in the Cretaceous while their fossils appear only after the Cretaceous–Palaeogene boundary.

Related concepts

The term should not be confused with a "ghost population" in population genetics, an unsampled population inferred from genetic admixture patterns, or with a Lazarus taxon, a group that disappears from the fossil record and later reappears. A gap in the record of a single taxon is sometimes called a ghost range.
