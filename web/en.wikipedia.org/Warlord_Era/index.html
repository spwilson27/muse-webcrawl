Warlord Era

The Warlord Era (Chinese: 軍閥時代) was a period in the history of the Republic of China when control of the country was divided among former military cliques of the Beiyang Army and other regional factions, which were listed by name as warlords. It lasted from 1916, following the death of Yuan Shikai, until 1928, when the Nationalist government in Nanjing nominally reunified China through the Northern Expedition.

Origins

The military power of the Beiyang Army, built up under Yuan Shikai, outlived its creator. After Yuan’s failed attempt to restore the monarchy and his death in June 1916, no figure commanded nationwide authority, and provincial military governors became effectively independent rulers who raised their own armies, taxes and foreign loans.

The cliques

The dominant factions were the Anhui clique of Duan Qirui, the Zhili clique of Feng Guozhang and later Wu Peifu and Cao Kun, and the Fengtian clique of Zhang Zuolin in Manchuria. In the south and west, semi-independent regimes included the Guangxi, Yunnan, Shanxi (under Yan Xishan) and Guominjun forces, as well as Sun Yat-sen’s rival government in Guangzhou, later the base of the Kuomintang. Shifting alliances produced repeated wars, notably the Zhili–Anhui War (1920), the two Zhili–Fengtian wars (1922 and 1924) and the Central Plains War at the era’s close.

Effects

The fragmentation weakened China internationally, enabling expanding Japanese influence in Manchuria and Shandong. Warfare, conscription, requisitioning and warlord taxation devastated many regions, while opium cultivation was promoted by many regimes as revenue. Yet the era also saw cultural ferment — the May Fourth Movement and New Culture Movement — and considerable regional variation, with some warlords promoting modernisation in their territories.

End

Chiang Kai-shek’s Northern Expedition (1926–1928), conducted with Soviet assistance in its early phase, defeated or co-opted the major northern cliques. The Northeast Flag Replacement of December 1928, in which Zhang Xueliang accepted Nanjing’s authority, formally reunified China, though in practice powerful militarists retained regional autonomy well into the 1930s.
