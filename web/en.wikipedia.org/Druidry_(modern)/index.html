Druidry (modern)

Druidry, also called Druidism, is a modern spiritual or religious movement that generally promotes harmony and connection with the natural world. It draws inspiration from the druids, the priestly class of the ancient Celtic peoples, though modern Druidry has no direct continuity with Iron Age religion and is most accurately described as a form of modern Paganism or a nature-based spirituality.

History

Interest in druids revived during the 17th and 18th centuries, when antiquarians such as John Aubrey and William Stukeley linked them (incorrectly) with Stonehenge and Avebury. Fraternal and cultural druid orders appeared from the late 18th century, including the Ancient Order of Druids (1781) and Welsh cultural institutions associated with Iolo Morganwg, who invented many "druidic" traditions of the Welsh Eisteddfod. A more explicitly spiritual Druidry emerged in the mid-20th century with groups such as the Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids (OBOD), founded in 1964 by Ross Nichols, and in North America the Reformed Druids of North America (1963) and Ár nDraíocht Féin (1983).

Beliefs and practices

Modern Druids commonly celebrate eight seasonal festivals — the solstices, equinoxes, and the Celtic fire festivals of Samhain, Imbolc, Beltane, and Lughnasadh — often at stone circles or in groves of trees. Reverence for nature, ancestors, and the spirits of place, creative arts such as poetry and music, and concepts such as awen (flowing inspiration) feature widely. Beliefs vary greatly: adherents may be polytheists, animists, pantheists, or simply spiritual seekers, and many groups emphasise personal experience over doctrine.

Recognition

In 2010, the Charity Commission for England and Wales recognised the Druid Network as a charity promoting a religion, a milestone widely reported as official recognition of Druidry as a religion in the United Kingdom. Modern Druid gatherings at Stonehenge for the summer solstice draw thousands of participants.
