Guaíra Falls

The Guaíra Falls (Portuguese: Salto das Sete Quedas; Spanish: Saltos del Guairá) were a series of immense waterfalls on the Paraná River on the border between Brazil and Paraguay. By volume of water they were among the largest waterfalls on Earth, with a flow roughly twice that of Niagara Falls and significantly greater than that of Iguazu Falls.

Description

The falls consisted of a series of 18 cataracts grouped in seven sets, where the Paraná River was forced through a narrow gorge. The roar of the water could reportedly be heard many kilometres away, and the site was a major tourist attraction and a proposed national park area.

Submergence

In 1982 the falls were submerged by the reservoir of the Itaipu Dam, then the largest hydroelectric project in the world. As the waters rose, the Brazilian military dynamited the submerged rock face in part to aid navigation. The flooding, carried out under an agreement between the military governments of Brazil and Paraguay, ended public protests and eliminated one of South America's great natural wonders in exchange for hydroelectric power.

Legacy

The disappearance of Guaíra Falls is frequently cited in debates over the environmental and cultural costs of mega-dams. The Guaraní people, for whom the falls held significance, and local communities on both sides of the border lost a landmark; the nearby city of Guaíra, Paraná, preserves the memory in its name.
