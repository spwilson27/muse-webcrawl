Negative-feedback amplifier

A negative-feedback amplifier (or feedback amplifier) is an electronic amplifier in which a fraction of the output signal is fed back to the input so as to oppose, or subtract from, the input signal. The principle was invented by Harold Stephen Black at Bell Laboratories in 1927 and patented in 1937.

Advantages and trade-offs

Applying negative feedback trades raw gain for major improvements: the overall gain becomes stable and predictable, set mainly by the feedback network; bandwidth increases; distortion and noise generated inside the amplifier are reduced; and input and output impedances can be controlled. The cost is reduced gain and the risk of instability (oscillation) if phase shift in the loop becomes large at high frequencies.

Analysis

For a forward gain A and feedback factor β, the closed-loop gain is approximately A/(1+Aβ), approaching 1/β when the loop gain is large. Feedback topology is classified by whether the output is sampled as voltage or current and whether the feedback is mixed with the input in series or shunt, giving four configurations.

Applications

Negative feedback is fundamental to operational amplifiers, audio amplification, control systems, and regulated power supplies — nearly all modern analogue electronics.
