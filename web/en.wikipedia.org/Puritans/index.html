Puritans

The Puritans were English Protestants in the 16th and 17th centuries who sought to purify the Church of England of Roman Catholic practices, maintaining that the Church of England had not been fully reformed and should become more Calvinist. Puritanism was founded after the accession of Elizabeth I and played a central role in English history during the English Civil War and in the founding of the New England colonies in America.

Beliefs

Puritans held that the Bible was the sole authority for religious practice, opposed set forms of prayer and episcopal church government retained from Catholicism, and emphasized preaching, Sabbath observance, personal conversion, and a disciplined moral life. Most were Calvinists who believed in predestination and in a covenant between God and the elect. Within the movement there was diversity: some sought reform from within the established church, while separatists such as the Pilgrims of Plymouth Colony broke with it entirely.

History in England

Under Elizabeth I and the early Stuart kings, Puritans faced intermittent persecution, and many emigrated. During the Civil War, Puritan-aligned Parliamentarians, led by figures such as Oliver Cromwell, defeated the Royalists, executed Charles I in 1649, and established the Commonwealth. The Restoration of 1660 ended Puritan political ascendancy, and the Act of Uniformity of 1662 expelled some two thousand Puritan clergy from the Church of England, giving rise to English Nonconformity.

New England

Between 1630 and 1640, in the Great Migration, roughly twenty thousand Puritans settled in New England, founding the Massachusetts Bay Colony and towns governed as self-governing congregational communities. Leaders such as John Winthrop envisioned the colony as a "city upon a hill". Puritan institutions — Harvard College (founded 1636), town meetings, and an emphasis on literacy so that all could read the Bible — left a lasting mark on American culture, as did tensions with dissenters such as Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson and events such as the Salem witch trials of 1692.

Legacy

Puritan ideas about covenant, calling, and moral discipline have been credited with shaping English and American literature, education, and work ethics. The word "Puritan" survives in the pejorative adjective "puritanical", denoting strictness about pleasure and morality — a connotation the historian Perry Miller and others have argued oversimplifies a movement that was intellectually rigorous and communally oriented.
