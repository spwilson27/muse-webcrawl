Misinformation

Misinformation is incorrect or misleading information presented as fact, whether or not it is intended to deceive. It is distinguished from disinformation, which is deliberately deceptive information spread to influence opinion or obscure the truth, and from malinformation, genuine information shared to cause harm. Misinformation can arise from honest errors, faulty memory, satire taken seriously, outdated material, or rumours, and is spread both by people who believe it and by those who knowingly amplify it.

Related termsDisinformation, malinformation, fake news, rumour, propaganda
Key distinctionMisinformation lacks deceptive intent; disinformation is deliberate
CountermeasuresFact-checking, media literacy, prebunking, corrections, content moderation
Research fieldsCommunication, psychology, information science

Causes and spread

Misinformation spreads through word of mouth, news media errors, and especially social media, where emotionally charged and novel content attracts engagement; a widely cited 2018 study of Twitter by Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral found that false stories spread farther, faster, and deeper than true ones. Cognitive factors such as confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, and the illusory truth effect (repeated exposure increasing perceived truth) facilitate belief and sharing. Research also shows that simply prompting people to consider accuracy before sharing can reduce the spread of falsehoods.

Effects

Misinformation can distort elections, hinder public-health responses (as with vaccine misinformation and false claims during the COVID-19 pandemic), fuel financial panics and scams, and damage reputations. Exposure to corrections generally updates beliefs, though a "continued influence effect" means retracted claims can still shape reasoning.

Countermeasures

Responses include professional fact-checking organisations, platform labels and ranking changes, media-literacy education, and "prebunking" — psychological inoculation that warns people about manipulation techniques in advance. Experts caution that the label "misinformation" can itself be contested and sometimes misapplied to genuine debate, making transparent standards important.
