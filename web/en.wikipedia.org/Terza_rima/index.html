Terza rima

Terza rima (Italian: [ˈtɛrtsa ˈriːma], "third rhyme") is a rhyme scheme used in poetry, consisting of tercets (three-line stanzas) with an interlocking rhyme pattern: the second line of each tercet rhymes with the first and third lines of the next, giving the scheme ABA BCB CDC DED and so on. The poem may end with a single line or a couplet that resolves the final rhyme.

History

Terza rima was invented by Dante Alighieri for his Divine Comedy (c. 1308–1321), where its forward-driving interlock suited the pilgrim's journey and its threefold structure echoed the Holy Trinity. The form was taken up in Italian by Petrarch and Boccaccio, and adapted into other languages despite the relative scarcity of rhymes in English compared with Italian.

In English

Chaucer used terza rima in parts of the Complaint unto Pity. Notable English examples include Shelley's Ode to the West Wind and The Triumph of Life, Byron's The Prophecy of Dante, and Robert Browning's The Statue and the Bust. In the twentieth century the form appears in works by W. H. Auden, T. S. Eliot (the "Little Gidding" airman passage of Four Quartets is in a modified terza rima), and many translators of Dante, including Dorothy L. Sayers and John Ciardi, who sought to reproduce the original scheme.
