Nemo iudex in causa sua

Nemo iudex in causa sua (also rendered nemo iudex in sua causa) is a Latin legal phrase meaning "no one should be a judge in their own cause". It is one of the two foundational rules of natural justice in common-law systems, the other being audi alteram partem ("hear the other side"). The maxim gives rise to the rule against bias in judicial and administrative decision-making.

Application

The rule disqualifies a decision-maker who has a personal interest in the outcome, whether financial or otherwise. In English law its classic statement came in Dr Bonham's Case (1610) and it was forcefully applied in Dimes v Grand Junction Canal (1852), where a decree of the Lord Chancellor was set aside because he held shares in the canal company. Modern law distinguishes automatic disqualification (for direct pecuniary interests), presumed bias, and apparent bias, where the question is whether a fair-minded and informed observer would conclude there was a real possibility of bias — the test refined in Porter v Magill (2001). The 1999 House of Lords decision concerning Senator Pinochet, set aside because of Lord Hoffmann's links to Amnesty International, is a celebrated modern application.

Related principles

The maxim works alongside rights to a fair hearing in instruments such as Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights and underlies recusal practice in courts around the world. Its rationale is both that actual justice be done and that justice must manifestly be seen to be done, in the words of Lord Hewart in R v Sussex Justices, ex parte McCarthy (1924).
