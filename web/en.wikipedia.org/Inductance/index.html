Inductance

Inductance is the property of an electrical conductor by which a change in the electric current flowing through it induces an electromotive force (voltage) in the conductor itself (self-inductance) or in nearby conductors (mutual inductance). It arises from the magnetic field generated by the current: any change in current changes the magnetic flux, and by Faraday's law of induction this induces an opposing voltage, expressed by Lenz's law. The SI unit of inductance is the henry (H), named after Joseph Henry.

Definition

The self-inductance L of a circuit is defined by the relation between induced voltage v and the rate of change of current i:

v(t) = L · di(t)/dt

Equivalently, inductance links the magnetic flux linkage λ to the current: L = λ/i. For a solenoid with N turns, cross-sectional area A, length l, and core permeability μ, L ≈ μN²A/l. The energy stored in an inductor's magnetic field is U = ½·L·i².

Mutual inductance and coupling

When two circuits share magnetic flux, a changing current in one induces a voltage in the other, quantified by the mutual inductance M. This is the operating principle of the transformer. The coupling coefficient k = M/√(L₁L₂) ranges from 0 (no coupling) to 1 (perfect coupling). Real inductors also exhibit parasitic resistance and capacitance, which limit their behaviour at high frequencies.

Applications

Inductors — typically coils of wire, often wound on ferrite or iron cores — are fundamental components of electronics. Together with capacitors they form tuned LC circuits used in radio receivers; they serve as chokes and filters in power supplies, energy-storage elements in switch-mode converters, and, in the form of motor and generator windings, the basis of most electric machinery. The phenomenon was discovered independently by Michael Faraday and Joseph Henry in the 1830s.
