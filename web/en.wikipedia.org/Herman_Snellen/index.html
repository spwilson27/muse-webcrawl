Herman Snellen (19 February 1834 – 18 January 1908) was a Dutch ophthalmologist who introduced the standardised eye chart that bears his name for measuring visual acuity. His Snellen chart, first published in 1862, remains in use worldwide today.

Life and career

Snellen was born in Zeist and studied medicine at Utrecht University, where he was a pupil of the pioneering ophthalmologist Franciscus Donders. He became associated with the Netherlands Hospital for Eye Patients (Nederlandsch Gasthuis voor Ooglijders) in Utrecht, succeeding Donders as its director, and in 1877 he was appointed professor of ophthalmology at Utrecht. He was a leading figure in Dutch and international ophthalmology, known also for his work on the surgical treatment of glaucoma and other eye conditions, and he helped found the international congresses of the specialty.

The Snellen chart

Before Snellen, tests of vision used ordinary printed text of inconsistent size, making results hard to compare. Snellen designed letters — optotypes — drawn on a 5 × 5 grid so that the whole letter subtends five minutes of arc and its strokes one minute of arc at the intended viewing distance. Each line of the chart is labelled by the distance at which a normal eye can read it, giving the familiar Snellen fraction (such as 20/20 in feet, or 6/6 in metres): the test distance divided by the distance at which the smallest line read is legible to standard vision. This rational, reproducible system spread rapidly through Europe and America and became the universal clinical standard, later supplemented by charts such as the Landolt C and the logMAR/ETDRS charts used in research.

Death and legacy

Snellen died in Utrecht in 1908. His name endures daily in eye clinics, opticians' practices, school screenings and driving tests wherever the Snellen chart hangs on the wall.
