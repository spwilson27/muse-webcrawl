Holy Lance

The Holy Lance, also known as the Lance of Longinus, the Spear of Destiny and the Holy Spear, is the lance that is alleged to have pierced the side of Jesus as he hung on the cross, during the Crucifixion. The Gospel of John (19:31–37) relates that a Roman soldier thrust a spear into Christ's side and that blood and water flowed out; later tradition gave the soldier the name Longinus. Several objects have been claimed over the centuries to be this relic, the most famous being the lance preserved in the Imperial Treasury at the Hofburg in Vienna.

Biblical account

Only the Gospel of John mentions the piercing: because it was the eve of the Sabbath, the soldiers broke the legs of the two men crucified with Jesus to hasten their deaths, but finding Jesus already dead, one soldier instead pierced his side with a spear, producing a flow of blood and water, interpreted by Christians as a fulfilment of prophecy and a symbol of the sacraments. The apocryphal Gospel of Nicodemus names the soldier Longinus.

History of the relics

A lance relic was venerated in Jerusalem by the 6th century and later at Constantinople. During the First Crusade, crusaders besieged in Antioch in 1098 claimed to discover a Holy Lance buried beneath the cathedral of St Peter after visions reported by the pilgrim Peter Bartholomew; the "discovery" raised morale before a victory over Kerbogha, though sceptics, then and since, doubted its authenticity, and the Antioch lance's fate is unclear. Lance relics were also kept in Paris (brought by Louis IX with the Crown of Thorns, and lost in the Revolution), Rome and elsewhere.

The Vienna lance

The lance in Vienna — part of the Imperial Regalia of the Holy Roman Empire — is a winged lance (a Carolingian-type spearhead) with a nail said to be from the True Cross bound into its blade with gold, silver and copper wire, and later gold cuffs bearing inscriptions. It was used in imperial coronations and carried into battle by emperors. Scientific examination in 2003 dated the spearhead to around the 8th century AD. The regalia were taken to Nuremberg for centuries, moved to Vienna in 1796 ahead of Napoleon's armies, seized by Nazi Germany in 1938 and returned by the United States after the Second World War; they are displayed in the Imperial Treasury (Schatzkammer) of the Hofburg Palace.

Other claimants

An Armenian lance at Echmiadzin Cathedral (the "Geghard" lance) is venerated as the spear that pierced Christ, brought, according to tradition, by the apostle Thaddeus. A lance fragment in St Peter's Basilica in the Vatican was sent by the Ottoman sultan Bayezid II to Pope Innocent VIII in 1492. The claims cannot all be true, and the proliferation of relics was already mocked in the Middle Ages.

In legend and culture

The Holy Lance gathered rich legendary associations: it was said to confer invincibility on its possessor, inspiring rulers from Charlemagne to, in popular occult literature, Adolf Hitler — a myth amplified by Trevor Ravenscroft's speculative bestseller The Spear of Destiny (1972). The lance appears in Arthurian legend as the bleeding lance of the Grail procession, in Wagner's Parsifal, and in modern films, comics and games, generally under the name "Spear of Destiny".
