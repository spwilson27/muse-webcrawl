Environmentalism

Environmentalism or environmental rights is a broad philosophy, ideology, and social movement about supporting life, habitats, and surroundings. While environmentalism focuses more on the environmental and nature-related aspects of green ideology and politics, ecologism combines the ideology of social ecology and environmentalism. Environmentalism advocates the preservation, restoration and improvement of the natural environment and critical earth system elements or processes such as the climate, and may be referred to as a movement to control pollution or protect plant and animal diversity.

History

Early environmental concerns appear in the conservation movement of the late 19th century, with figures such as John Muir, founder of the Sierra Club (1892), and the preservation-versus-conservation debate against utilitarian foresters like Gifford Pinchot. The modern movement is usually dated from Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962), which exposed the dangers of DDT and other pesticides. The first Earth Day in 1970 mobilised some 20 million Americans and preceded a wave of landmark legislation — clean air and water acts and endangered-species protection — and the creation of environmental agencies in many countries.

Branches and ideas

The movement spans mainstream conservation organisations (WWF, The Nature Conservancy), direct-action groups (Greenpeace, founded 1971; Earth First!), green political parties, and schools of thought including deep ecology, ecofeminism, social ecology, environmental justice — which highlights the unequal burden of pollution on poor and minority communities — and market-based approaches such as carbon trading. From the 1990s onward, anthropogenic climate change became the central issue, driving international frameworks from the Kyoto Protocol to the Paris Agreement of 2015 and youth movements such as Fridays for Future.

Criticism

Environmentalism is criticised from various directions: by proponents of economic growth who stress costs and development needs in poorer countries; by "ecomodernists" who argue technology and intensification can decouple prosperity from impact; and internally over nuclear power, population, and the balance between local and global priorities.
