Ghee

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ghee (Sanskrit: घृत, ghṛta) is a type of clarified butter that originated in India. It is prepared by simmering butter — traditionally made from the milk of cows or buffalo — until its water content evaporates and the milk solids separate and lightly brown, giving ghee its characteristic nutty aroma and golden colour. Ghee is commonly used in the cuisines of the Indian subcontinent, in Hindu religious rituals, and in the traditional Ayurvedic system of medicine.

Preparation

Traditional Indian methods begin with curd rather than cream: milk is fermented into curd, churned into butter, and the butter is then heated slowly in a heavy vessel. As it simmers, foam is skimmed or allowed to subside, the water boils off, and the casein and lactose sink and begin to caramelise. When the fat is clear and fragrant, it is strained and cooled; good ghee solidifies into a slightly granular mass. Because nearly all water and milk solids are removed, ghee keeps for months without refrigeration and has a high smoke point (about 250 °C / 482 °F), higher than that of ordinary butter.

Uses

Culinary

Ghee is used for frying and tempering spices (tadka), for brushing onto flatbreads such as roti and naan, for dressing cooked rice and dal, and as the essential ingredient of many sweets, including Mysore pak and ladoo. Regional cuisines use it distinctly: it is the preferred frying medium in much of North India, while in Bengal it flavours rice dishes and khichuri. Related products exist elsewhere — samna in Egypt, smen in Morocco, and clarified butter in European cooking — though European clarified butter is usually cooked for a shorter time and lacks ghee's browned notes.

Religious

In Hinduism, ghee is sacred: it is the principal offering poured into the fire in yajna sacrifices, fuels the lamps (deepa) placed before deities, and is one of the five ingredients of panchamrita. Cow ghee in particular is regarded as pure and auspicious.

Nutrition

Ghee is almost entirely fat, roughly 60 percent of it saturated, and is rich in fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K, along with small amounts of butyric acid and conjugated linoleic acid. It contains essentially no lactose or casein, so it is often tolerated by people sensitive to dairy proteins. Ayurvedic tradition esteems ghee as a rejuvenative food, while modern dietary guidance generally treats it like other saturated fats, to be used in moderation. Much commercial "ghee" sold cheaply is in fact vanaspati, hydrogenated vegetable fat, a different product.
