The Orders in Council were a series of decrees in 1807 that restricted neutral trade and enforced a system partly designed to limit the commercial activities of the United States, issued by the British government during the Napoleonic Wars. They formed part of Britain's economic warfare against Napoleonic France, answering the Berlin Decree of November 1806, by which Napoleon declared a blockade of the British Isles and forbade trade with Britain (the Continental System).

Under the Orders — principally those of January 1807 and the more sweeping orders of 11 November 1807 — France and any ports controlled by France or its allies were placed under blockade. Neutral ships wishing to trade with enemy ports were required first to call at a British port, submit to search, and take out a British licence (in effect paying British duties).

Provisions

The January 1807 order prohibited neutral coasting trade between ports controlled by France. The November orders extended the system: all ports from which British ships were excluded were subjected to the same restrictions as if actually blockaded, and goods from enemy colonies had to pass through Britain. France answered with the Milan Decree (December 1807), which declared that neutral ships submitting to British search or taxation were lawful prizes — placing American shipping in an impossible position between the two systems.

Relations with the United States

The Orders were deeply resented in the United States, whose merchant marine was seized in large numbers by both sides; combined with impressment of American sailors by the Royal Navy, they were a leading grievance behind President Madison's road to war. Facing domestic economic pressure from British manufacturers hurt by American countermeasures, the British government suspended the Orders on 23 June 1812 — too late, as Congress had already declared war on 18 June, opening the War of 1812.
