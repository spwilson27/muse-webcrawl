The unification of Italy (Italian: Unità d'Italia), also known as the Risorgimento ("Resurgence"), was the 19th-century political and social movement that in 1861 resulted in the consolidation of different states of the Italian Peninsula into a single state, the Kingdom of Italy.

Background

After the fall of the Roman Empire, Italy remained divided for centuries among foreign powers and local states, including the Papal States, the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, Austrian-ruled Lombardy–Venetia, and the Kingdom of Sardinia. The revolutions of 1848 and the First Italian War of Independence failed to unite the peninsula, but they established Piedmont-Sardinia and its constitutional monarchy as the nucleus of a future Italian state.

Course of unification

Under Prime Minister Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour, Piedmont allied with France and defeated Austria in 1859, annexing Lombardy. In 1860 Giuseppe Garibaldi led the Expedition of the Thousand, conquering Sicily and Naples, which were handed over to King Victor Emmanuel II. On 17 March 1861 the Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed. Venetia was gained in 1866 through alliance with Prussia, and Rome was captured in 1870 after French troops withdrew during the Franco-Prussian War; Rome became the capital in 1871.

Legacy

Unification created a single Italian state but left deep regional divides, particularly between the industrial north and the agrarian south — the "Southern Question" — and unresolved claims to Italian-speaking territories under Austria, known as Italia irredenta, which fed into Italy's entry into World War I. National heroes of the Risorgimento include Giuseppe Mazzini, the movement's prophet, and Giuseppe Garibaldi, its most famous soldier.
