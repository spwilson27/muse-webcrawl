Reservoir simulation

Reservoir simulation is an area of reservoir engineering in which computer models are used to predict the flow of fluids — typically oil, water, and gas — through porous media in a hydrocarbon reservoir. It is a central tool for developing field development plans, estimating reserves, optimising well placement, and evaluating recovery strategies such as waterflooding and enhanced oil recovery.

Approach

A simulation study begins with a geological model describing the reservoir's structure, porosity, and permeability, which is upscaled into a grid of cells. Fluid properties are described by PVT data, and flow within the grid is computed by numerically solving the mass-conservation equations together with Darcy's law for multiphase flow. Simulator types include black-oil models (tracking oil, water, and dissolved/free gas phases) and compositional models (tracking individual hydrocarbon components), as well as thermal simulators for steam-based recovery.

History matching and forecasting

Models are calibrated by history matching: adjusting uncertain parameters so the simulation reproduces observed production and pressure data. The matched model is then run forward to forecast production under different development scenarios. Because of subsurface uncertainty, modern practice often uses ensembles of model realisations and assisted history-matching methods.

Software and history

Reservoir simulation developed from the 1950s–60s alongside digital computing. Widely used commercial simulators include Schlumberger's ECLIPSE and INTERSECT, Computer Modelling Group's IMEX/GEM/STARS, and Halliburton Landmark's Nexus; open-source options include OPM Flow. Simulations routinely involve millions of grid cells and run on high-performance computing clusters.
