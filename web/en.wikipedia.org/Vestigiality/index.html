Vestigiality

Vestigiality is the retention, during the process of evolution, of genetically determined structures or attributes that have lost some or all of their ancestral function in a given species. Examples include the wings of flightless birds, the pelvic bones of whales and snakes, the eyes of blind cave-dwelling animals, and, in humans, the appendix, wisdom teeth, the coccyx, and the arrector pili muscles that produce goose bumps.

History

Vestigial structures were noted by thinkers from Aristotle onward and were catalogued extensively by naturalists in the 19th century. Charles Darwin used them in On the Origin of Species as evidence for descent with modification: structures reduced and useless in one species are inexplicable except as inheritances from ancestors in which they were functional. In 1893 the German anatomist Robert Wiedersheim listed some 86 human vestigial organs.

Interpretation

A structure is vestigial relative to a lost ancestral function; it may still retain minor or secondary uses (an exaptation), as the appendix may serve as a reservoir for gut bacteria. At the molecular level, pseudogenes — disabled copies of once-functional genes, such as the human GULO gene for vitamin C synthesis — are considered vestigial DNA. Vestigiality at all levels is a key line of evidence for common descent.
