The Euler–Mascheroni constant (also called Euler's constant, but not to be confused with the number e) is a mathematical constant usually denoted by the lowercase Greek letter gamma (γ). It is defined as the limiting difference between the harmonic series and the natural logarithm: γ = limn→∞ (Hn − ln n), where Hn is the n-th harmonic number.

Properties

Euler introduced the constant in 1734 and computed it to several decimal places; Lorenzo Mascheroni extended the computation and popularised the symbol γ. The constant appears throughout analysis and number theory — in the digamma function, the Gamma function's expansion, integrals involving the exponential integral, and estimates of divisor sums and prime-counting errors.

Remarkably, it is still unknown whether γ is rational or irrational, let alone transcendental, making it one of the most prominent open problems about a classical constant. Its value has been computed to hundreds of billions of digits.
