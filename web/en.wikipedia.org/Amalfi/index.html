Amalfi is a town in the province of Salerno, Campania, Italy, lying at the mouth of a deep ravine on the Gulf of Salerno. It gives its name to the famous Amalfi Coast. In the Middle Ages Amalfi was one of Italy's great maritime republics, rivalling Pisa, Genoa, and Venice.

History

Amalfi became an independent duchy in the 9th century and grew rich on trade with the Byzantine Empire and the Islamic world; its merchants were active from Constantinople to Egypt. Its maritime code, the Tabula Amalphitana, was a standard of Mediterranean sea law, and Amalfitan sailors are traditionally credited with an early use of the compass in the West — the town's tradition honours Flavio Gioia. Amalfi's power declined after Norman conquest in 1131, a Pisan sack in 1135, and a catastrophic storm and sea-quake in 1343.

Today

The town's landmarks include the cathedral (Duomo) of Saint Andrew, with its Arab-Norman cloister (Chiostro del Paradiso), and the museum of Amalfi's ancient paper-making industry. Tourism and the production of limoncello from local lemons dominate the modern economy.
