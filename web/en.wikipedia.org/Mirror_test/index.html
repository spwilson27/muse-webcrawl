Mirror test

The mirror test, sometimes called the mark test, mirror self-recognition (MSR) test, red spot technique, or rouge test, is a behavioural technique developed in 1970 by American psychologist Gordon Gallup Jr. to investigate self-awareness in animals, including humans. In the test, an animal is anesthetised (or otherwise distracted) and a mark is placed on a part of its body it cannot normally see; the animal is then given access to a mirror. If it touches or investigates the mark on its own body, this is taken as evidence that it recognises the reflection as itself rather than another animal.

Procedure

Gallup's original experiments exposed chimpanzees to mirrors for several days and then marked their foreheads and ears with an odourless red dye under anaesthesia. On regaining access to the mirror, the chimpanzees touched the marks on their own bodies far more often than control animals, and used the mirror to inspect otherwise invisible body parts.

Results across species

Great apes (chimpanzees, bonobos, orangutans, and some gorillas) consistently pass. Bottlenose dolphins and Asian elephants have passed modified versions; Eurasian magpies passed a sticker-based version, the first non-mammal to do so. A 2019 study reported that cleaner wrasse fish scraped marked throats against surfaces after seeing them in mirrors, a result still debated. Dogs and cats fail the visual version — a fact sometimes attributed to their greater reliance on smell; olfactory analogues of the test have been proposed.

Human development and criticism

Most children pass the rouge test by about 18–24 months, although cross-cultural studies show wide variation, prompting criticism that the test measures familiarity with mirrors and cultural norms about touching one's face as much as self-awareness. Critics also caution that passing indicates a specific form of visual self-recognition, not necessarily a full concept of self, and that failure does not prove its absence.
