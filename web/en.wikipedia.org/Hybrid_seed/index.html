Hybrid seed

Summary

Field: Agriculture, plant breeding

Produced by: Crossing two inbred parent lines

Key advantage: Heterosis (hybrid vigour)

Common crops: Maize, sorghum, sunflower, rice, many vegetables

Hybrid seed is seed produced by cross-pollinating two genetically distinct, usually highly inbred, parent lines of a plant. The resulting first-generation (F1) offspring typically exhibit heterosis, or hybrid vigour — greater yield, uniformity, and resistance to stress than either parent — making hybrid seed a cornerstone of modern commercial agriculture.

Production

Producing hybrid seed is labour-intensive. Breeders first develop inbred lines by self-pollinating plants for several generations. The two parent lines are then planted together and cross-pollinated under controlled conditions. In maize, the female plants are detasselled so they can only receive pollen from the male line; in other crops, male sterility systems are used to prevent self-pollination. Seed is harvested only from the female parent.

History and impact

Hybrid maize, developed in the United States in the early 20th century from the research of George Harrison Shull, Edward East, and others and commercialised by pioneers such as Henry A. Wallace, spread rapidly through the American Corn Belt in the 1930s and 1940s, dramatically increasing yields. Hybrid varieties later transformed sorghum, sunflower, rice, and vegetable production worldwide.

Considerations

Because the desirable traits of F1 hybrids segregate in the next generation, seed saved from a hybrid crop does not breed true, so farmers typically purchase fresh seed each season — a point of economic and political debate. Critics also raise concerns about the narrowing of crop genetic diversity, while supporters credit hybrids with major gains in food production.
