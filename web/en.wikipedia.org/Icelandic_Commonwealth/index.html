The Icelandic Commonwealth (Icelandic: Þjóðveldið), also known as the Icelandic Free State, was the political structure in Iceland from the establishment of the Althing in 930 until the signing of the Old Covenant (Gamli sáttmáli) in 1262–1264, which brought Iceland under the Norwegian crown. Unusually for medieval Europe, it had no king or central executive authority; power rested with local chieftains called goðar.

Settlement and founding

Norse settlers (along with Celtic slaves and settlers) colonised Iceland from about 870 AD. In 930 the leading chieftains established a general assembly, the Althing, at Þingvellir, with a lawspeaker (lögsögumaðr) who recited the law from memory, and a law council (lögrétta) that amended laws and settled disputes. Regional assemblies and quarter courts handled local cases, and around 1005 a Fifth Court was added as a court of appeal.

Government and society

Authority was organised around chieftaincies (goðorð), which were personal, transferable and even inheritable property rather than territorial jurisdictions. Free farmers (bændr) chose which chieftain to support and could change allegiance. The Commonwealth had laws, courts and a sophisticated legal procedure — famously described in the law code Grágás and depicted in the sagas — but no executive arm to enforce judgements; enforcement fell to the successful litigant, which fed the culture of feud.

Around the year 1000, under pressure from the Norwegian king, the Althing adopted Christianity as the official religion by arbitration of the lawspeaker Þorgeir Þorkelsson, while permitting private pagan practice for a time.

Decline: Age of the Sturlungs

During the 13th century (the Age of the Sturlungs, c. 1220–1264), chieftaincies concentrated in a few great families, and violent feuds escalated into civil war, dramatised in Sturlunga saga. Norwegian kings intervened increasingly in Icelandic affairs, and exhausted Icelanders accepted the rule of Haakon IV of Norway under the Old Covenant of 1262–1264, ending the Commonwealth while retaining the Althing and Icelandic law for centuries.
