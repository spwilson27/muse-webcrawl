Zhiguai xiaoshuo

Zhiguai xiaoshuo (Chinese: 志怪小說; pinyin: zhìguài xiǎoshuō), translated as "tales of the strange" or "records of anomalies", is a genre of Chinese fiction that flourished during the Six Dynasties period (220–589 AD) and continued in later dynasties. Zhiguai are short accounts — often only a paragraph or a few lines long, written in classical Chinese — describing supernatural events, ghosts, spirits, fox fairies, portents, and other marvels, presented in a matter-of-fact, quasi-historical style as if they were factual records.

Characteristics

The name combines zhi ("record") and guai ("strange" or "anomalous"). Unlike the longer, more elaborate wonder tales (chuanqi) of the Tang dynasty, zhiguai entries are terse and annalistic, typically naming a person, place, and date and reporting the strange occurrence without narrative embellishment. Their compilers often claimed simply to be transmitting accounts they had heard or read, reflecting both popular religion and the early Chinese belief that anomalies were meaningful omens within a cosmos governed by correlative forces.

Major works

Important zhiguai collections include Gan Bao's Soushen Ji (搜神記, "In Search of the Supernatural", 4th century), the most famous work of the genre, compiled to demonstrate that the spirit world was real; the Shuyi Ji ("Records of Strange Things"); Liu Yiqing's Youming Lu ("Records of the Hidden and the Visible"); and the Shiyi Ji attributed to Wang Jia. In the Tang dynasty, Duan Chengshi's Youyang Zazu continued the tradition in miscellany form. The genre profoundly influenced later Chinese fiction, culminating in Pu Songling's Qing-dynasty masterpiece Liaozhai Zhiyi ("Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio"), and it echoes today in Chinese fantasy literature and film.

Scholarship

Modern scholars, notably Robert Ford Campany, stress that zhiguai authors were not naive: the genre's documentary posture was a deliberate literary and religious strategy, and the tales are key sources for early medieval Chinese religion, folklore, and everyday life.
