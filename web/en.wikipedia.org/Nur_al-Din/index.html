Nur al-Din (full name: al-Malik al-Adil Nur al-Din Abu al-Qasim Mahmud ibn Imad al-Din Zengi; February 1118 – 15 May 1174), sometimes spelled Nureddin or Nuruddin, was a Turkoman member of the Zengid dynasty who ruled the Syrian province of the Seljuk Empire. He is regarded as an important figure of the counter-Crusade against the Crusader states, and he reigned from 1146 to 1174.

Early life and rise

Nur al-Din was the second son of Imad al-Din Zengi, the atabeg of Mosul and Aleppo. After his father's assassination in 1146, the Zengid lands were divided: Nur al-Din took Aleppo, while his elder brother Saif al-Din Ghazi took Mosul. He soon began campaigning against the Crusader Principality of Antioch and inflicted a crushing defeat on the Franks at the Battle of Inab in 1149, where Prince Raymond of Poitiers was killed.

Unification of Muslim Syria

Nur al-Din's overriding goal was the unification of the Muslim states of Syria and northern Iraq as a precondition for expelling the Crusaders. In 1154 he took Damascus, bringing most of Muslim Syria under a single ruler. He promoted Sunni orthodoxy, founding madrasas and hospitals (including the famous Nuri hospital in Damascus) and presenting himself as a pious ruler waging jihad.

Egypt

During the 1160s Nur al-Din competed with King Amalric of Jerusalem for control of the declining Fatimid Caliphate in Egypt. His Kurdish general Shirkuh led several expeditions there, and Shirkuh's nephew Saladin eventually took power in Egypt in 1169. Although Egypt nominally acknowledged Nur al-Din's suzerainty, the relationship between Nur al-Din and Saladin grew tense, and Nur al-Din was preparing to invade Egypt to discipline his ambitious subordinate when he fell ill.

Death and legacy

Nur al-Din died in Damascus on 15 May 1174, reportedly of complications from a throat infection. He was succeeded by his young son As-Salih Ismail al-Malik, but Saladin soon took control of both Egypt and Syria, uniting them under the Ayyubid dynasty. Nur al-Din's reputation for justice, piety, and patronage of learning made him a celebrated figure in later Islamic tradition, and his mausoleum and madrasa in Damascus survive as notable monuments.
