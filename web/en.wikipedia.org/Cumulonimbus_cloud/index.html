Cumulonimbus cloud

A cumulonimbus is a dense, towering vertical cloud associated with thunderstorms and atmospheric instability. Formed from water vapor carried upward by powerful air currents, cumulonimbus clouds can reach heights of 12–18 km, into the upper troposphere and lower stratosphere, and are the only clouds that produce hail, thunder, and lightning.

Formation and structure

Cumulonimbus develops from cumulus congestus when warm, moist air rises in an unstable atmosphere. At the tropopause the top spreads into a characteristic anvil shape (incus). A mature cell contains strong updrafts and downdrafts; rain-cooled downdraft air can produce gust fronts and microbursts hazardous to aviation.

Types and severe weather

Single-cell storms are brief, while multicell clusters and supercells — cumulonimbus with a persistent rotating updraft called a mesocyclone — can produce large hail, flash floods, and tornadoes. In the tropics, cumulonimbus organized into mesoscale convective systems delivers much of the rainfall and drives the global circulation through latent heat release.

Classification

The World Meteorological Organization lists species including cumulonimbus calvus (smooth top) and capillatus (fibrous, anvil-bearing top). Supplementary features include mammatus, arcus, and tuba.
