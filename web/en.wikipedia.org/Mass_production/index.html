Mass production

Ford's moving assembly line, a landmark of mass production.

Mass production, also known as flow production or continuous production, is the production of substantial amounts of standardised products in a constant flow, including and especially on assembly lines. It relies on the principles of specialisation of labour, interchangeable parts, and mechanisation to achieve high volume and low unit cost.

History

Precursors included the Venetian Arsenal, the Portsmouth Block Mills, and the American system of manufactures with interchangeable parts developed in the 19th century. Henry Ford's Highland Park plant introduced the moving assembly line in 1913, cutting the time to build a Model T chassis from over twelve hours to about ninety minutes and enabling dramatic price reductions.

Characteristics

Mass production typically involves dedicated machinery, standardised designs, division of labour into narrow repetitive tasks, and large production runs to amortise tooling costs. It contrasts with craft production and with later flexible and lean production systems, such as the Toyota Production System, which sought smaller batches and rapid changeover.

Effects

Mass production made consumer goods such as automobiles, radios, and appliances affordable to a mass market, reshaped industrial labour and urban life, and underpinned the Allied war effort in the Second World War. It has also been criticised for deskilling work, environmental impact, and product uniformity.
