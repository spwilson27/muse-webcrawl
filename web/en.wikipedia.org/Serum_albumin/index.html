Serum albumin

Human serum albumin, the most abundant protein in blood plasma.

Serum albumin is the main protein in human blood plasma, normally present at about 35–50 grams per litre and accounting for roughly half of plasma protein content. It is produced by the liver as preproalbumin and secreted as a single-chain protein of 585 amino acids. Related albumins occur in the blood of other vertebrates and, in different forms, in plants and egg white.

Functions

Albumin maintains oncotic pressure, the osmotic force that keeps fluid within the circulation. It is also a versatile carrier: it binds and transports fatty acids, bilirubin, hormones such as thyroxine, metal ions, and many drugs including warfarin and penicillin, influencing their distribution and half-life. It contributes to plasma buffering capacity and acts as an antioxidant through its free thiol group.

Clinical significance

Low serum albumin (hypoalbuminaemia) occurs in liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition, chronic inflammation and severe illness, and is a marker of disease severity rather than a diagnosis in itself. Albumin lost in the urine (albuminuria) is an early sign of kidney damage. Purified human albumin is used therapeutically as a plasma-volume expander in selected settings such as large-volume paracentesis and some forms of shock, and laboratory measurement of albumin forms part of standard liver function tests.

Structure

Human serum albumin is a heart-shaped, predominantly alpha-helical protein of three homologous domains, each split into two subdomains. Its multiple binding pockets explain its ability to carry chemically diverse ligands, a property exploited in drug design when predicting plasma protein binding.
