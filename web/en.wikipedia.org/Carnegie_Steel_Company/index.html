The Carnegie Steel Company was a steel-producing company created by the Scottish-American industrialist Andrew Carnegie to consolidate his steel and coke enterprises in the Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, area. Launched in 1892, it became the largest and most profitable steel company in the world before Carnegie sold it to financier J. P. Morgan in 1901, when it became the core of the newly formed U.S. Steel Corporation.

Origins

Carnegie began building his steel empire in the 1870s with the Edgar Thomson Steel Works in Braddock, Pennsylvania, which adopted the Bessemer process and rail production on a large scale. Through vertical integration — owning iron ore fields in the Lake Superior region, coke works at Connellsville developed by Henry Clay Frick, railroads and steamships — Carnegie Steel controlled every stage of production from raw material to finished rail and structural steel. Its works included the Homestead Steel Works, the Duquesne Works and the Edgar Thomson plant.

Homestead strike

In 1892, the year of the company's consolidation, Carnegie Steel's manager Henry Clay Frick provoked the Homestead strike by cutting wages and refusing to renew the contract of the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers. A gun battle between locked-out workers and Pinkerton detectives on 6 July 1892 killed at least ten people, and the Pennsylvania militia was deployed to reopen the plant with strikebreakers. The strike collapsed in November, seriously weakening unionization in the American steel industry for decades; Carnegie, who was in Scotland, bore lasting reputational damage.

Sale to U.S. Steel

By 1900 Carnegie Steel earned profits of some $40 million a year and produced more steel than all of Great Britain. In 1901 Carnegie sold the company to J. P. Morgan for about $480 million — Carnegie's personal share was roughly $225 million in gold bonds — making him the richest man in the world. Carnegie Steel became a principal subsidiary of U.S. Steel, the first billion-dollar corporation, and Carnegie devoted the rest of his life to philanthropy, funding libraries, universities and foundations.
