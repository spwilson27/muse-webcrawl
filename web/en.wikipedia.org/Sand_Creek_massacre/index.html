The Sand Creek massacre (also the Chivington massacre) was a massacre of Cheyenne and Arapaho people by the U.S. Army in the American Indian Wars that occurred on 29 November 1864. A 700-man force of the Colorado Territorial Militia, commanded by Colonel John Chivington, attacked and destroyed a village of about 500 Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho along Big Sandy Creek in southeastern Colorado Territory, despite the village flying a United States flag and a white flag of surrender.

Background

Tensions during the Colorado War of 1864 escalated after the Hungate massacre and raids along the Platte trails. Chiefs Black Kettle and White Antelope had sought peace and reported to Fort Lyon, where they were directed to camp at Sand Creek under assurances of safety. Most of the village's fighting-age men were away hunting buffalo when Chivington's force arrived at dawn.

The massacre

The militia attacked the camp, killing indiscriminately; two-thirds of the dead were women and children, and many bodies were mutilated, with trophies later displayed in Denver. Captain Silas Soule refused to order his men to fire and later testified against Chivington. Estimates of the Native dead range from about 70 to over 160; Chivington's force lost about two dozen men, several to friendly fire.

Aftermath

Congressional and military investigations condemned the attack as a massacre; Chivington resigned but was never prosecuted. The killings intensified warfare on the Plains. The site is now preserved as the Sand Creek Massacre National Historic Site (authorised 2000, established 2007). In 2014 Colorado's governor formally apologised to the Cheyenne and Arapaho descendants.
