Brown bear

Scientific nameUrsus arctos
FamilyUrsidae
Mass130–600 kg (males vary by population)
Conservation statusLeast Concern (IUCN)

The brown bear (Ursus arctos) is a large bear species found across Eurasia and North America. In North America, the populations of brown bears are called grizzly bears, while the subspecies that inhabits the Kodiak Islands of Alaska is known as the Kodiak bear. It is one of the largest living terrestrial members of the order Carnivora, rivalled in size only by its closest relative, the polar bear.

Description

Brown bears have long, thick fur with a moderately long mane at the back of the head, and colour varies from very light blond through reddish and dark brown. They have a large hump of muscle over the shoulders, powerful forelimbs with long claws adapted for digging, and a dished facial profile. Adults typically weigh from 130 to 600 kg depending on population and season.

Distribution and habitat

The species ranges from Western Europe and Russia across Asia to Japan, and in North America from Alaska to northern Mexico (historically). Brown bears occupy diverse habitats including tundra, alpine meadows, steppe, and dense forests. The global population is estimated at around 200,000, with the largest numbers in Russia, Canada, and Alaska.

Behaviour and diet

Brown bears are omnivorous, eating grasses, sedges, roots, berries, insects, fish such as salmon, and mammals. They accumulate fat in summer and autumn and hibernate in dens through the winter. Females give birth during hibernation, usually to two cubs, which remain with their mother for about two and a half years.

Relationship with humans

Brown bears have featured in the folklore and symbolism of many cultures and are the national animal of Finland and a symbol of Russia. Conflicts arise over livestock, crops, and garbage, and bear attacks, though rare, occur when bears are surprised or defending food or cubs.
