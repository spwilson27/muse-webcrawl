Henry VIII


Henry VIII after Holbein

Henry VIII (28 June 1491 – 28 January 1547) was King of England from 1509 until his death. He is widely known for his six marriages and for his efforts to have the first of them, to Catherine of Aragon, annulled. His disagreement with Pope Clement VII about the annulment led Henry to initiate the English Reformation, separating the Church of England from papal authority. He appointed himself Supreme Head of the Church of England and dissolved the monasteries, confiscating their wealth.

Reign

The second Tudor monarch, Henry inherited a full treasury from his father Henry VII but spent it on wars with France and Scotland and on a lavish court. His chief ministers included Cardinal Thomas Wolsey, Thomas More, and Thomas Cromwell, the architect of the Reformation statutes and the dissolution of the monasteries (1536–1541). Henry's government passed the Laws in Wales Acts, legally uniting Wales with England, and Henry was proclaimed King of Ireland in 1542.

The six wives

Henry's six marriages are summarised by the rhyme "divorced, beheaded, died; divorced, beheaded, survived": Catherine of Aragon (annulled), Anne Boleyn (executed 1536), Jane Seymour (died after giving birth to the future Edward VI), Anne of Cleves (annulled), Catherine Howard (executed 1542), and Catherine Parr, who outlived him. His desire for a male heir drove much of his policy; he was succeeded by Edward VI, then Mary I—daughter of Catherine of Aragon—then Elizabeth I, daughter of Anne Boleyn.

Later years and legacy

Injuries from a 1536 jousting accident contributed to Henry's obesity and ill health in later life; by his death he weighed about 178 kg (392 lb). His reign strengthened royal power and the navy, but also saw thousands executed for treason and heresy. Henry remains one of England's most famous and controversial monarchs.
