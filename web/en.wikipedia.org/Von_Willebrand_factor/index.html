Von Willebrand factor

Von Willebrand factor (VWF) is a blood glycoprotein involved in hemostasis (blood clotting). It is a large multimeric protein produced by endothelial cells and megakaryocytes, stored in Weibel–Palade bodies and platelet α-granules, and released into the blood plasma.

VWF performs two main functions. First, it mediates the adhesion of platelets to exposed collagen at sites of vascular injury, acting as a molecular bridge under the high shear conditions of flowing blood. Second, it binds and stabilizes coagulation factor VIII, protecting it from premature degradation and thereby prolonging its half-life in circulation.

Clinical significance

Deficiency or dysfunction of VWF causes von Willebrand disease, the most common inherited bleeding disorder, first described by Finnish physician Erik Adolf von Willebrand in 1926. Conversely, excessively large VWF multimers — normally cleaved by the enzyme ADAMTS13 — accumulate when ADAMTS13 is deficient, leading to the platelet-rich microthrombi of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). VWF levels in the blood are influenced by ABO blood group, with type O individuals having lower levels on average.
