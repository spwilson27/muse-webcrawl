Tristan and Iseult

Tristan and Iseult (also spelled Isolde) is a medieval romance, originating in Celtic legend and retold by many authors, about the adulterous love between the Cornish knight Tristan and the Irish princess Iseult. The story predates and influenced the Arthurian romance of Lancelot and Guinevere, and has had a lasting impact on Western literature and art.

The story

Tristan, nephew of King Mark of Cornwall, is sent to Ireland to fetch Iseult as a bride for his uncle. On the return voyage the two accidentally drink a love potion intended for Iseult and Mark, binding them in an irresistible love. Despite marriages, exile and repeated discovery and forgiveness, the lovers cannot renounce their passion. In many versions Tristan later marries another Iseult — Iseult of the White Hands — and, dying of a wound, sends for the first Iseult; deceived by a lie about the colour of the returning ship's sail, he dies before she arrives, and she dies of grief beside him. A hazel and honeysuckle, or rose and vine, grow from their graves and intertwine.

Versions

The earliest surviving verse versions are those of the 12th-century poets Béroul and Thomas of Britain, whose texts survive in fragments; Gottfried von Strassburg's German version (c. 1210), also unfinished, is considered a masterpiece of medieval literature. The tale was incorporated into the Prose Tristan and into Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur.

Legacy

The legend inspired many later works, most famously Richard Wagner's opera Tristan und Isolde (1865), which transformed the story into a metaphysical drama of love and death and revolutionised Western music.
