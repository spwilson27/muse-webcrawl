Controlled digital lending

Controlled digital lending (CDL) is a practice in which a library lends a digital copy of a book it physically owns, in place of the physical item, while ensuring that the number of copies in circulation never exceeds the number the library lawfully owns. The model is frequently summarised by the phrase "own to lend": for every physical copy acquired, the library may lend one digital copy at a time, and the physical copy is sequestered while the digital one is on loan.

The practice gained prominence in the 2010s as libraries digitised their print collections and sought a framework for lending e-books under terms closer to traditional print lending than to commercial e-book licensing, which typically charges libraries recurring fees and imposes restrictive conditions.

Principles

Advocates describe CDL as resting on several technical and legal controls. The library must own a physical copy of the work; it must maintain a strict one-to-one owned-to-loaned ratio; it must use digital rights management or equivalent technical measures to prevent copying and redistribution; and loans must be time-limited, expiring automatically at the end of the lending period.

Supporters ground the practice in the first-sale doctrine of copyright law, which permits the owner of a lawfully made copy to lend or resell that copy, and in fair use, arguing that format-shifting a copy for lending replicates the function of print lending without enlarging the total number of circulating copies.

History

The concept was articulated in a 2011 paper by David Hansen and Kyle Courtney and was developed further in a 2018 position statement on controlled digital lending issued by a group of library copyright specialists. The Internet Archive's Open Library programme became the most visible implementation, lending scanned copies of books in its collection.

Legal challenges

Publishers have contested the practice. In 2020, four major publishers sued the Internet Archive, initially over its National Emergency Library, which had temporarily suspended the waitlist controls of CDL during the COVID-19 pandemic. In Hachette v. Internet Archive, the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York ruled in 2023 that the Archive's controlled digital lending programme infringed the publishers' copyrights, and the Second Circuit affirmed the judgment in 2024. The litigation significantly chilled adoption of CDL by libraries in the United States.
