Kabir (c. 1440 – c. 1518) was a fifteenth-century Indian mystic poet and saint whose verses helped shape the Bhakti movement. A weaver by trade, raised in a Muslim family in Varanasi, he became — by tradition — a disciple of the Hindu teacher Ramananda, and his poetry bridged Hindu and Muslim vocabularies while attacking the orthodoxies of both.

Life and legend

Little about Kabir's life is certain; much comes from hagiographies written by his followers. Legend holds that he was found as an infant by the Muslim weavers Niru and Nima, and that on his death at Maghar, Hindus and Muslims who disputed over his body found only flowers beneath his shroud. His choice of Maghar — a town proverbially considered an inauspicious place to die — expressed his teaching that liberation depends on one's inner state, not on dying in a holy city.

Poetry and philosophy

Kabir composed oral poems in a vernacular Hindi idiom (the sant bhasha), full of paradox, reversal (ulatbansi), and images drawn from weaving and daily labour. His verses are preserved in three main streams: the Bijak of the Kabir-panth, the Guru Granth Sahib of the Sikhs — which includes over 500 of his verses, the largest contribution by any non-Guru author — and the Dadu-panth manuscripts of Rajasthan. Kabir preached a formless God (Ram, in his usage, not simply the epic hero), rejected caste, idolatry, and empty ritual, and insisted on direct experience over scripture. Rabindranath Tagore's English translations, Songs of Kabir (1915), introduced him to a global readership.
