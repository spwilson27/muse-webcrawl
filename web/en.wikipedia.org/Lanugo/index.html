Lanugo

Lanugo
DefinitionFine, soft, unpigmented hair covering the fetal body
Appears~16 weeks of gestation
Usually shedBy ~36–40 weeks (before or shortly after birth)

Lanugo is very thin, soft, usually unpigmented hair that covers the body of a human fetus and is also found on many other mammals. It begins to develop at about four to five months of gestation and is normally shed in utero by around the seventh or eighth month, although it is sometimes present at birth — especially in premature infants — and disappears on its own within a few weeks.

Function and development

Lanugo is thought to help bind the vernix caseosa — the waxy, protective coating of the fetal skin — to the body, and it may play a role in regulating the fetus's temperature before subcutaneous fat accumulates. Swallowed lanugo shed into the amniotic fluid contributes to the meconium. It is the first hair produced by the fetal hair follicles and is distinct from the coarser vellus hair and terminal hair that develop later.

Clinical associations

In adults, new growth of lanugo-like hair can be a sign of severe malnutrition, most notably in anorexia nervosa, where it is interpreted as the body's attempt to insulate itself after the loss of fat. It can also occur in certain cancers and other conditions (hypertrichosis lanuginosa).
