Scramjet

A scramjet (supersonic combustion ramjet) is a variant of a ramjet airbreathing jet engine in which combustion takes place in supersonic airflow. As in ramjets, a scramjet relies on high vehicle speed to compress the incoming air forcefully before combustion (hence "ram"), but unlike in a ramjet, which decelerates the air to subsonic velocities before combustion, a scramjet slows the air only slightly, keeping it supersonic throughout the engine.

Principles

Scramjets operate most efficiently at very high speeds, roughly Mach 5 and above. Because they carry no oxidiser and have no rotating compressor machinery, they promise a higher specific impulse than rockets for atmospheric flight. Key challenges include mixing fuel with the supersonic airstream in milliseconds, managing extreme aerodynamic heating, and maintaining stable combustion — often described as "lighting a match in a hurricane".

History and flight tests

Theoretical work on supersonic combustion dates to the 1950s. The first successful flight test of a scramjet was achieved by the Australian HyShot program in 2002. NASA's X-43A set speed records for an airbreathing aircraft in 2004, reaching nearly Mach 10. The Boeing X-51 Waverider flew a sustained scramjet-powered flight of about 210 seconds in 2013. Several countries are developing scramjet technology for hypersonic cruise missiles and reusable launch vehicles.

Applications

Proposed applications include hypersonic missiles, very-high-speed reconnaissance aircraft, and the upper stages of two-stage-to-orbit spaceplanes. No scramjet vehicle has yet entered operational service.
