Trachea

The trachea, commonly called the windpipe, is a cartilaginous tube that connects the larynx to the bronchi of the lungs, allowing the passage of air, and is present in almost all air-breathing animals with lungs. In humans it is roughly 10–11 cm long and about 2.5 cm in diameter, extending from the cricoid cartilage at the level of the sixth cervical vertebra to the carina, where it divides into the two main bronchi.

Structure

The trachea is reinforced by 16 to 20 C-shaped rings of hyaline cartilage, open at the back where the trachealis muscle spans the gap against the esophagus; this arrangement keeps the airway open while allowing the esophagus room to expand during swallowing. Its inner lining is a ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium rich in mucus-secreting goblet cells. The beating cilia and mucus form the mucociliary escalator, which carries trapped particles and pathogens up and out of the airway.

Function and clinical significance

Beyond conducting air, the trachea warms, moistens, and cleans inhaled air. Obstruction of the trachea is life-threatening; emergency procedures such as intubation, cricothyrotomy, and tracheostomy bypass blockages above or within it. Diseases of the trachea include tracheitis, tracheomalacia (softening of the cartilage), stenosis after prolonged intubation, and rare tumours. In 2008 the trachea became a landmark of regenerative medicine when a tissue-engineered trachea, grown from a donor scaffold seeded with the patient's own stem cells, was transplanted.

Other animals

In insects and other arthropods the term trachea refers to an entirely different system: a network of fine tubes delivering oxygen directly to tissues through openings called spiracles.
