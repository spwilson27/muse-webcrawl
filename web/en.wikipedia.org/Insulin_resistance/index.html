Insulin resistance

Insulin resistance (IR) is a pathological condition in which cells in insulin-sensitive tissues — chiefly muscle, liver, and adipose tissue — fail to respond normally to the hormone insulin. In the early stages, the pancreas compensates by secreting more insulin (hyperinsulinemia), so blood glucose may remain normal; over time, if pancreatic beta-cell function declines, impaired glucose tolerance and type 2 diabetes can develop. Insulin resistance is the central feature of the metabolic syndrome.

Pathophysiology

When insulin binds its receptor on a cell surface, it triggers a signalling cascade through insulin receptor substrates and PI3K/Akt that moves the glucose transporter GLUT4 to the membrane in muscle and fat, suppresses hepatic glucose output, and promotes fat storage. In insulin resistance — associated with excess visceral and ectopic fat, lipid intermediates such as diacylglycerol and ceramides, inflammation, and endoplasmic-reticulum stress — these signals are blunted. The liver continues to release glucose despite high insulin, muscle takes up less glucose after meals, and adipose tissue releases more free fatty acids, worsening the cycle.

Causes and associations

The most common drivers are obesity (especially abdominal), physical inactivity, and genetic predisposition; other causes include pregnancy, puberty, severe illness, glucocorticoid treatment, and rare genetic syndromes of severe insulin resistance such as lipodystrophy and insulin-receptor mutations. Insulin resistance is associated with polycystic ovary syndrome, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and increased cardiovascular risk. The skin change acanthosis nigricans is a visible marker.

Diagnosis and management

The research gold standard is the hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp; in practice, fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, and triglyceride-to-HDL ratios serve as surrogates, alongside tests of blood glucose. Management centres on weight loss, aerobic and resistance exercise, and dietary change, all of which can substantially restore sensitivity; metformin and GLP-1 receptor agonists are among the pharmacologic options used for associated conditions.
