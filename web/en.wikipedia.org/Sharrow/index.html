Sharrow

A shared lane marking (sharrow) painted on a city street.

A sharrow or shared lane marking is a street marking consisting of a bicycle symbol with two chevrons (arrows) above it, painted on a travel lane that is shared by bicycles and motor vehicles. The word is a portmanteau of "shared" and "arrow". Sharrows are used to indicate a shared environment for bicycles and automobiles, to remind motorists to expect cyclists, and to guide cyclists to ride far enough into the lane to avoid the "door zone" of parked cars.

The marking originated in Denver, Colorado, in the 1990s (where it was developed by planner James Mackay and was originally called a "bike in a house" in earlier variants elsewhere), and was formally adopted into the US Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD) in its 2009 edition. Similar markings are used in Canada, Australia, Japan, and several European countries.

Purpose and effectiveness

Sharrows do not create an exclusive bicycle facility and confer no special right-of-way; bicyclists and motorists continue to share the lane under ordinary traffic law. Studies of their effectiveness have been mixed: some research finds modest increases in the distance motorists give cyclists and reductions in sidewalk riding and wrong-way riding, while cycling advocates often criticise sharrows as inadequate compared with dedicated bicycle lanes or protected infrastructure.
