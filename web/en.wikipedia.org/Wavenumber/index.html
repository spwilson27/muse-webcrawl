A wave: wavenumber counts cycles per unit distance

In the physical sciences, the wavenumber (also wave number or repetency) is the spatial frequency of a wave, measured in cycles per unit distance (ordinary wavenumber) or radians per unit distance (angular wavenumber). It is analogous to temporal frequency, which is the number of wave cycles per unit time.

Definitions

The angular wavenumber, k, is defined as k = 2π/λ, where λ is the wavelength; it equals the magnitude of the wave vector and appears in the standard form of wave equations, e.g. a plane wave exp[i(kx − ωt)]. The spectroscopic wavenumber, ν̃, is defined as the reciprocal of the wavelength in vacuum, ν̃ = 1/λ = ν/c, where ν is frequency and c is the speed of light. It is conventionally expressed in reciprocal centimetres (cm⁻¹), a unit sometimes called the kayser. Because energy is proportional to frequency, spectroscopic wavenumber is directly proportional to energy, making it convenient for infrared and Raman spectroscopy: molecular vibrations typically appear between about 200 and 4,000 cm⁻¹.

Applications

Wavenumber is used throughout spectroscopy (where spectra are plotted against cm⁻¹), in the physics of wave propagation and electromagnetic theory, in atmospheric science (zonal wavenumber counts waves around a latitude circle), and in quantum mechanics, where the wave vector ħk gives particle momentum via the de Broglie relation. In dispersive media the wavenumber depends on frequency through the dispersion relation ω(k).
