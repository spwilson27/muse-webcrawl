Georges de Rham (10 September 1903 – 9 October 1990) was a Swiss mathematician, known for his contributions to differential topology and differential geometry. His work on differential forms and cohomology created a bridge between analysis and topology that remains central to modern geometry.

Biography

De Rham was born in Roche, in the canton of Vaud. He studied at the University of Lausanne and then in Paris, where he completed his doctorate in 1931 under the supervision of Élie Cartan and Henri Lebesgue. His thesis established the result now known as de Rham's theorem. He taught at the universities of Lausanne and Geneva, and served as president of the Swiss Mathematical Society. An avid mountaineer, he was also known for his love of the Swiss Alps.

Mathematical work

De Rham's theorem states that the de Rham cohomology of a smooth manifold, defined using differential forms and exterior derivatives, is isomorphic to its singular cohomology with real coefficients. This result connected the analytic theory of differential forms with algebraic topology. He also developed the theory of currents, a generalisation of differential forms and distributions that allows integration over chains and singular objects, and studied harmonic forms and the decomposition of manifolds, including the splitting theorem associated with his name.

Legacy

De Rham cohomology is a standard tool in differential geometry, used in the study of characteristic classes, index theory and mathematical physics. De Rham received the Prize of the City of Lausanne in 1979 and was a member of several academies, including the French Academy of Sciences.
