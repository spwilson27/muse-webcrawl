Film studio

A film studio (also known as a movie studio or motion picture studio) is a major entertainment company or motion picture company that has its own privately owned studio facility or facilities for the production of films. Studios also distribute the films they produce, and the largest of them control much of the global film industry.

History

The earliest film studios were built in the 1890s and 1900s, when producers sought controlled environments with abundant natural light. Glass-roofed stages in places such as Fort Lee, New Jersey, and later Hollywood, California, allowed filming regardless of weather. By the 1920s the major Hollywood studios — among them Paramount Pictures, Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, Warner Bros., RKO, and 20th Century Fox — operated as vertically integrated companies controlling production, distribution, and exhibition, a system known as the studio system.

The 1948 United States Supreme Court decision in United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc. forced the majors to divest their theatre chains, weakening the studio system. In the decades that followed, independent production grew, and many historic studio lots became rental facilities serving producers of all sizes.

Facilities

A modern film studio complex typically includes sound stages, backlots with standing exterior sets, production offices, workshops for set construction and costume, editing and post-production suites, and screening rooms. Large complexes such as Pinewood Studios in England, Cinecittà in Rome, and the studio lots of Burbank and Hollywood remain centres of film and television production.

Major studios

The companies commonly called the "major film studios" today include Walt Disney Pictures, Warner Bros. Pictures, Universal Pictures, Paramount Pictures, and Sony's Columbia Pictures, together with large mini-majors and streaming-era producers such as Amazon MGM Studios and Netflix.
