Drunkard's walk

A simulated two-dimensional random walk (placeholder image)

The drunkard's walk is a popular name for a random walk: a mathematical process in which an object moves in a sequence of steps, each step taken in a random direction. The name evokes the image of an intoxicated person staggering unpredictably from a lamppost, taking each step without regard to the last.

One-dimensional walk

In the simplest case, a walker on the integer line starts at 0 and at each step moves one unit left or right with equal probability. After n steps, the expected position is still 0, but the expected distance from the origin grows proportionally to the square root of n. A celebrated result, Pólya's theorem, states that in one and two dimensions the walker returns to the origin with probability 1, while in three or more dimensions there is a positive probability of wandering away forever.

Applications

Random walks model Brownian motion and diffusion in physics, stock-price movements in finance, animal foraging paths in ecology, and polymer shapes in chemistry. The concept also appears in computer science, in randomized algorithms and in Google's PageRank, which models a "random surfer" following links.

In popular culture

The phrase was popularized by Karl Pearson in 1905 and appears in Leonard Mlodinow's 2008 book The Drunkard's Walk: How Randomness Rules Our Lives, which explores the role of chance in everyday outcomes.
