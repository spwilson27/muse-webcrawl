History of science and technology in China

The history of science and technology in China records one of the world's great inventive traditions. Ancient and imperial China produced the Four Great Inventions — papermaking, printing, gunpowder, and the compass — along with major advances in astronomy, mathematics, medicine, and engineering. Joseph Needham's monumental Science and Civilisation in China project documented this heritage and posed "Needham's question": why modern science arose in Europe rather than China.

Ancient foundations

Chinese astronomers kept meticulous records of eclipses, comets, and "guest stars" (novae) from the Bronze Age. Zhang Heng built a seismoscope in AD 132. Mohist texts explored optics and logic; the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art systematized practical mathematics. Traditional Chinese medicine developed acupuncture and pharmacology over two millennia.

Song dynasty flourishing

The Song dynasty (960–1279) saw a technological zenith: Bi Sheng's movable type, gunpowder weapons, Su Song's astronomical clock tower (1092), advances in metallurgy and shipbuilding, and Shen Kuo's polymathic Dream Pool Essays, which noted magnetic declination. Papermaking (attributed to Cai Lun, AD 105) and woodblock printing had already spread Chinese texts widely, and maritime compasses guided navigation from the 11th century.

Ming to modern

Later dynasties saw continued craft innovation but relative stagnation in theoretical science; Jesuit missionaries from the 17th century transmitted European astronomy and mathematics. After the upheavals of the 19th and 20th centuries, the People's Republic invested heavily in science; contemporary China leads or competes in fields from spaceflight and quantum communications to artificial intelligence and high-speed rail.
