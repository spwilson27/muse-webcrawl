Civil parish

In England, a civil parish is a type of administrative parish used for local government. It is a territorial designation which is the lowest tier of local government. Civil parishes can range in size from a tiny hamlet with a few dozen residents to a large town of over 100,000 people. A civil parish may be served by a parish council (which may style itself a town or city council) or, in the smallest cases, by a parish meeting.

History

Civil parishes originated in the ecclesiastical parishes of the Church of England, which took on secular functions such as poor relief under Tudor legislation. The Local Government Act 1894 formally separated civil from ecclesiastical functions, creating elected parish councils and transferring civil functions away from church vestries.

Coverage

There are more than 10,000 civil parishes in England, covering much of the country; historically unparished areas survived in parts of London and other cities, though the Local Government and Public Involvement in Health Act 2007 made it possible to create parishes everywhere, including London. Scotland and Wales have analogous but distinct units (community councils).

Functions

Parish councils maintain amenities such as village halls, allotments, cemeteries, footpaths and war memorials, and are consulted on planning applications. They raise funds through a precept added to council tax.
