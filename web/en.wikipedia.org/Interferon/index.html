Interferon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Interferons (IFNs) are a group of signaling proteins made and released by host cells in response to the presence of several viruses. In a typical scenario, a virus-infected cell releases interferons, causing nearby cells to heighten their anti-viral defences. Interferons belong to the large class of proteins known as cytokines, molecules used for communication between cells to trigger the protective defences of the immune system that help eradicate pathogens.

Types

Interferons are classified by the receptor they signal through. Type I interferons include IFN-α (produced by leukocytes) and IFN-β (produced by fibroblasts); type II consists of IFN-γ, secreted mainly by T cells and natural killer cells, which primarily regulates immune responses; type III interferons (IFN-λ) act at mucosal surfaces. Signalling proceeds through the JAK-STAT pathway, inducing hundreds of interferon-stimulated genes whose products inhibit viral replication, degrade viral nucleic acids, and increase antigen presentation via MHC molecules.

Discovery

Interferons were discovered in 1957 by Alick Isaacs and Jean Lindenmann at the National Institute for Medical Research in London, who observed that chick cells treated with inactivated influenza virus produced a substance that "interfered" with viral growth — hence the name.

Medical use

Recombinant interferons are used as drugs: interferon beta for multiple sclerosis, pegylated interferon alpha (historically) for hepatitis B and C, and interferon alpha for certain cancers such as hairy cell leukaemia and Kaposi's sarcoma. Common side effects include flu-like symptoms, fatigue, mood changes, and cytopenias. Defects in interferon production or signalling cause susceptibility to severe viral infections, while overactive interferon responses characterize autoimmune "interferonopathies" such as Aicardi–Goutières syndrome.
