George Boole (2 November 1815 – 8 December 1864) was a largely self-taught English mathematician, philosopher and logician, most of whose short career was spent as the first professor of mathematics at Queen's College, Cork in Ireland. He worked in the fields of differential equations and algebraic logic, and is best known as the author of The Laws of Thought (1854) which contains Boolean algebra. Boolean logic is credited with laying the foundations for the information age. Boole maintained that:

"No general method for the solution of questions in the theory of probabilities can be established which does not explicitly recognise, not only the special numerical bases of the science, but also those universal laws of thought which are the basis of all reasoning, and which, whatever they may be as to their essence, are at least mathematical as to their form."

Early life

Boole was born in Lincoln, Lincolnshire, England, the son of John Boole (1779–1848), a shoemaker, and Mary Ann Joyce. He had a primary school education and learned Latin from a local bookseller. At age 16 he became the breadwinner for his parents and three younger siblings, taking up a junior teaching position in Doncaster. Largely self-educated in mathematics, he began publishing in the Cambridge Mathematical Journal in his late twenties; his 1844 paper on a general method in analysis won the Royal Society's first gold medal for mathematics.

Career

In 1849 Boole was appointed the first professor of mathematics at Queen's College, Cork (now University College Cork), despite having no university degree. He remained there for the rest of his life. In 1855 he married Mary Everest, niece of Sir George Everest, after whom the mountain is named; they had five daughters. Boole died in 1864 of pneumonia, aged 49, after walking in heavy rain and lecturing in wet clothes.

Work

Boole's first published work was in invariant theory and differential equations, including the operator methods underlying what are now called Boole's rule in numerical integration and, indirectly, the concept of the Boolean satisfiability problem. His lasting fame rests on two works on logic: The Mathematical Analysis of Logic (1847), prompted by the public dispute between Augustus De Morgan and William Hamilton over the quantification of the predicate, and An Investigation of the Laws of Thought (1854), which set out an algebra of logic in which variables take the values true and false (represented 1 and 0) and the operations correspond to conjunction, disjunction, and negation.

Legacy

Boolean algebra was little used until the 20th century, when Claude Shannon's 1937 master's thesis showed that it could optimise electromechanical relay circuits; it became the foundation of digital circuit design and, ultimately, of all modern computing. The Boolean data type, Boolean operators in search and programming, and the lunar crater Boole are named after him. University College Cork, where he worked, preserves his legacy in the Boole Library and in extensive celebrations of the bicentenary of his birth in 2015, during which he was described as one of the "forefathers of the information age".
