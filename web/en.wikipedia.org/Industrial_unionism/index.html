Industrial unionism

Industrial unionism is a trade union organising method through which all workers in the same industry are organised into the same union, regardless of skill or trade, thus giving workers in one industry, or in all industries whose employers have interlocking interests, more leverage in bargaining. It contrasts with craft unionism, which organises workers by their specific skilled trade.

History

Industrial unionism arose in the late 19th and early 20th centuries as mass production created large workforces of semi-skilled workers whom the craft unions of the American Federation of Labor (AFL) largely declined to organise. Early advocates included Eugene V. Debs's American Railway Union and the Western Federation of Miners. The Industrial Workers of the World (IWW, founded 1905) carried the idea further into revolutionary industrial unionism — "One Big Union" of all workers. In 1935 the Committee for Industrial Organization (later the Congress of Industrial Organizations, CIO) was formed within the AFL and expelled the next year; CIO unions such as the United Auto Workers and United Steelworkers organised the mass-production industries through campaigns and sit-down strikes (notably Flint, 1936–37), transforming American labour before the AFL–CIO merger of 1955.

Examples

Industrial unions exist worldwide: in Germany and Scandinavia industry-wide bargaining is the norm; South Africa's NUMSA and COSATU federations, Australia's industry unions after the 1980s amalgamations, and Britain's large general and industry unions also embody the model. Advocates argue industry-wide organisation prevents employers from whipsawing crafts against one another; critics have cited reduced craft autonomy and, in the IWW's case, revolutionary aims.
