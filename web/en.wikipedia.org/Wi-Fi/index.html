Wi-Fi

Wi-Fi is a family of wireless network protocols based on the IEEE 802.11 standards, used for local area networking of devices and Internet access. The name, a trademark of the Wi-Fi Alliance, is popularly thought to be short for "wireless fidelity", but it was coined as a pun on "hi-fi" and has no official expansion.

History

The 802.11 standard was released in 1997; the 802.11b amendment of 1999, marketed by Apple as AirPort, brought Wi-Fi into homes. Successive generations greatly increased speed and capacity: 802.11g (2003), 802.11n (Wi-Fi 4, 2009), 802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5, 2014), 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6, 2019) and 802.11be (Wi-Fi 7, 2024).

How it works

Devices communicate with a wireless access point, often integrated into a home router, which connects them to each other and to the wider Internet. Wi-Fi operates mainly in the 2.4 GHz band (longer range, slower), the 5 GHz band (faster, shorter range) and, for Wi-Fi 6E and 7, the 6 GHz band. Networks are identified by an SSID and are usually secured with WPA2 or WPA3 encryption; earlier WEP security is obsolete and insecure.

Uses

Wi-Fi is the dominant means of connecting phones, computers, televisions and smart-home devices, and is offered in cafés, airports, hotels and public spaces as "hotspots". Alternatives for longer ranges include cellular networks (4G/5G).
