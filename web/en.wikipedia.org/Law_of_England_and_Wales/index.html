Law of England and Wales

English law is the common law legal system of England and Wales, comprising mainly criminal law and civil law, each branch having its own courts and procedures. It is one of the three distinct legal jurisdictions of the United Kingdom, the others being Scots law and Northern Ireland law. Historically, English common law became the basis of legal systems throughout the Commonwealth and influenced the law of the United States and many other countries.

Sources of law

The principal sources are legislation (Acts of Parliament and delegated legislation), the common law developed through judicial precedent under the doctrine of stare decisis, and, historically, equity. Courts are bound by decisions of higher courts in the hierarchy. Since devolution, the Welsh Senedd may legislate on devolved matters, so Welsh law increasingly diverges within the single jurisdiction.

Courts

Civil cases are heard in the County Court and the High Court, with appeals to the Court of Appeal (Civil Division) and ultimately the Supreme Court. Criminal cases begin in magistrates' courts; serious offences are tried in the Crown Court before a judge and jury, with appeals to the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division). Specialist tribunals handle areas such as employment, immigration and tax.

Legal profession

The profession is divided between solicitors, who deal directly with clients and are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, and barristers, self-employed advocates regulated by the Bar Standards Board. Judges are appointed by the Judicial Appointments Commission.

History

The common law developed after the Norman Conquest as royal courts standardised custom across England. Magna Carta (1215) limited royal power, and the Judicature Acts of 1873–75 fused the administration of common law and equity. The Constitutional Reform Act 2005 created the Supreme Court, which opened in 2009.
