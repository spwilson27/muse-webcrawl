Clientelism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Clientelism is a political or social system in which power and support are exchanged through personal, reciprocal relationships: a patron distributes goods, services, jobs or other favours to clients, who in return offer political support, votes, loyalty or labour. The term derives from the ancient Roman clientela, the system of patronage between patrons and their clients, but it describes practices found across history and in many modern democracies.

Definition and forms

Political scientists typically distinguish:

  • Traditional clientelism — durable, hierarchical relationships, often in agrarian societies (e.g. landlord–peasant ties, the Italian South, or coronelismo in Brazil).
  • Modern or mass clientelism — party-based machines distributing state resources: public-sector jobs, welfare benefits, infrastructure or permits in exchange for votes. The urban political machines of the United States (Tammany Hall), the Christian Democrats and Socialists of post-war Italy, and the PRI of Mexico are classic examples.
  • Vote buying — the most transactional, short-term form, involving cash or gifts immediately around elections.
  • Relational clientelism — ongoing, repeated exchanges that create norms of reciprocity and personal loyalty over time.

Clientelism is closely related to but distinct from corruption: corruption involves the illicit use of office for private gain, whereas clientelism can operate partly within legal channels, though the two frequently overlap.

Consequences

Clientelism can provide welfare to poor constituencies excluded from formal channels, but it typically distorts public policy, misallocates resources, undermines programmatic party competition, and weakens bureaucratic autonomy and the rule of law. Because benefits are conditional on support, it discourages collective action for universal public goods. Scholars debate whether economic development, urbanisation and stronger institutions reduce clientelism or merely transform it.
