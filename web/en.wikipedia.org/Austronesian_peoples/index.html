The Austronesian expansion, from Taiwan to Madagascar, Polynesia and Rapa Nui.

The Austronesian peoples, sometimes referred to as Austronesian-speaking peoples, are a large group of peoples in Taiwan, Maritime Southeast Asia, Micronesia, coastal New Guinea, Island Melanesia, Polynesia, and Madagascar that speak the Austronesian languages. Major Austronesian groups include the Malays, Javanese, Sundanese, Filipinos, Māori, Hawaiians, Malagasy, and the Indigenous peoples of Taiwan.

Origins and expansion

According to the prevailing "Out of Taiwan" model, supported by linguistics, archaeology, and genetics, the expansion began around 3000–1500 BC as Neolithic seafarers from Taiwan — itself settled earlier from the coast of mainland Asia — spread south into the Philippines and then through island Southeast Asia. Mastering sophisticated outrigger and lashed-lug sailing technology, Austronesian voyagers achieved the widest pre-modern maritime dispersal of any language family: westward to Madagascar by the first millennium AD, and eastward across the Pacific, reaching Rapa Nui (Easter Island) and Hawai'i, and eventually New Zealand by around AD 1250–1300. Genetic studies have also suggested contact with South America, evidenced by the presence of the sweet potato in Polynesia.

Culture

Shared cultural traits carried across the expansion include tattooing, stilt houses, jade carving, wet-rice agriculture (in the western range), outrigger boats, and the domestication of pigs, chickens, and dogs. The Lapita culture of the western Pacific (c. 1500–500 BC), known for its distinctive dentate-stamped pottery, is associated with the ancestors of the Polynesians.
