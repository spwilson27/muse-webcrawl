Curd

Curd is the solid portion of milk that separates from the liquid whey when milk coagulates. Coagulation can be brought about by adding rennet (containing the enzyme chymosin), by acid such as lemon juice or vinegar, by bacterial cultures that ferment lactose to lactic acid, or by heat. Curd is the starting point of cheese, and in various forms is eaten directly in many food cultures.

Production and cheesemaking

In cheesemaking, milk is warmed and inoculated with rennet and starter culture; within about half an hour it sets into a gel that is cut into cubes to release whey, then stirred, heated, salted, and pressed according to the cheese style. The way curds are handled — milled for cheddar, stretched (pasta filata) for mozzarella, washed for Gouda — determines texture and flavor. Fresh, squeaky cheese curds are a specialty of Quebec (where they top poutine) and the upper midwestern United States.

Other culinary uses

Acid-set curds form the basis of paneer in South Asia, queso fresco in Latin America, and chhena used in Bengali sweets. In Indian English, "curd" commonly refers to yogurt (dahi), a distinct fermented product. Fruit curds — lemon, lime, or passion-fruit curd — are unrelated sweet spreads made from eggs, sugar, butter, and citrus juice. The word also survives in the nursery rhyme "Little Miss Muffet," who sat eating her curds and whey.

Chemistry

Coagulation centers on casein, milk's main protein, which forms micelles stabilized by κ-casein. Rennet cleaves κ-casein, and acid lowers the pH toward casein's isoelectric point (~4.6); in both cases the micelles aggregate into a network trapping fat and water — the curd.
