American philosophy

American philosophy is the activity, corpus, and tradition of philosophers affiliated with the United States. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that American philosophy has lacked a single unifying feature, but that its practitioners have reflected and shaped American national identity through intellectual activity.

History

Colonial philosophy was dominated by Puritan theologians such as Jonathan Edwards, whose writings combined Calvinism with Lockean empiricism and Newtonian science. The Enlightenment thought of the Founding Fathers, drawing on Locke and Montesquieu, shaped the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution. In the nineteenth century, transcendentalism, led by Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau, gave American letters a distinctive spiritual and literary philosophy.

Pragmatism

The most original American school is pragmatism, founded by Charles Sanders Peirce and developed by William James, John Dewey, and George Herbert Mead. Pragmatism judges the meaning and truth of ideas by their practical consequences, and influenced education, law, and democratic theory. In the twentieth century, universities in the United States also became a world centre for analytic philosophy, with figures such as W. V. O. Quine, Donald Davidson, Saul Kripke, and John Rawls, whose A Theory of Justice (1971) reshaped political philosophy. Pragmatism was later revived by Richard Rorty and others.
