Milton Friedman

Born: July 31, 1912, Brooklyn, New York, U.S.

Died: November 16, 2006, San Francisco, California

Field: Economics

Known for: Monetarism, permanent income hypothesis

Awards: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences (1976)

Milton Friedman (1912–2006) was an American economist and statistician who received the 1976 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for his research on consumption analysis, monetary history and theory, and the complexity of stabilisation policy. He was a leading figure of the Chicago school of economics.

Contributions

With Anna Schwartz, Friedman wrote A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960 (1963), which argued that the Great Depression was largely caused by the Federal Reserve's contraction of the money supply. His permanent income hypothesis reshaped the theory of consumption, and he championed monetarism — the view that the supply of money is the principal determinant of nominal income — against the reigning Keynesian orthodoxy.

Public advocacy

In works such as Capitalism and Freedom (1962) and the television series Free to Choose (1980), Friedman advocated for free markets, school vouchers, a negative income tax, floating exchange rates, and the abolition of conscription. He advised several governments, and economists on the University of Chicago model influenced economic reforms in Chile in the 1970s.
