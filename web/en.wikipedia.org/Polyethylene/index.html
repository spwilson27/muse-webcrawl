Polyethylene

Polyethylene or polythene (abbreviated PE; IUPAC name polyethene or poly(methylene)) is the most common plastic in use today. It is a polymer, produced by the polymerization of ethylene (ethene), and consists primarily of long chains of the monomer –CH2–CH2–. Global production exceeds 100 million tonnes annually, accounting for roughly a third of the entire plastics market.

History

Polyethylene was first synthesized accidentally by the German chemist Hans von Pechmann in 1898, who noticed a white, waxy substance while heating diazomethane. It was first industrially synthesized by Eric Fawcett and Reginald Gibson at Imperial Chemical Industries (ICI) in England in 1933, again by accident, when ethylene gas was subjected to very high pressure. Michael Perrin at ICI developed the first commercially viable high-pressure process in 1939. Karl Ziegler's catalysts in the 1950s enabled low-pressure production of high-density polyethylene, for which Ziegler and Giulio Natta shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Classification

Polyethylene is classified by density and branching. High-density polyethylene (HDPE) has minimal branching, high strength, and is used for bottles, pipes, and milk jugs. Low-density polyethylene (LDPE) is highly branched, flexible, and used mainly for plastic bags and films. Linear low-density polyethylene (LLDPE) has short branches and is used for stretch film. Ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) is exceptionally tough and is used in body armor and artificial joints.

Properties and uses

Polyethylene is a thermoplastic, chemically resistant to most acids, alkalis, and solvents, and an excellent electrical insulator. Its low cost and versatility make it ubiquitous in packaging, containers, toys, geomembranes, and agricultural film. Because of its durability and resistance to biodegradation, polyethylene waste is a significant contributor to plastic pollution in the environment.
