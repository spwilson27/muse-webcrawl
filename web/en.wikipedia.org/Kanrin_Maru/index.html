Kanrin Maru

The Kanrin Maru (咸臨丸) was a screw-driven sloop-of-war of the Tokugawa shogunate, best known as the first Japanese warship to cross the Pacific Ocean, travelling to San Francisco, California, in 1860. Built in the Netherlands, she symbolised Japan's rapid adoption of Western naval technology in the final decades of the Edo period.

Construction

The ship was ordered by the Tokugawa shogunate from the Dutch shipyard of Fop Smit at Kinderdijk and launched in 1857 under the name Japan. She was a wooden-hulled, screw-propelled sloop of roughly 300 tons, barque-rigged and fitted with a small auxiliary steam engine, giving her both sail and steam propulsion. On delivery to Japan she was renamed Kanrin Maru and assigned to the shogunate's naval training establishment at Nagasaki, where Dutch officers instructed Japanese sailors in modern seamanship.

The 1860 voyage to America

In 1860 the shogunate sent the Kanrin Maru across the Pacific as an escort to the delegation travelling aboard the American warship USS Powhatan to ratify the Treaty of Amity and Commerce in Washington, D.C. Commanded by Katsu Kaishū, with a crew of Japanese officers and sailors advised by the American officer John Mercer Brooke, the ship left Uraga on 9 February 1860 and reached San Francisco on 17 March. The crossing, made largely under Japanese command in heavy weather, was celebrated in Japan as proof that the country's seamen could master Western navigation.

Among those who crossed on the voyage was Fukuzawa Yukichi, who later became a leading educator and writer of the Meiji era; a famous photograph taken in San Francisco shows him with a young American girl.

Later career

After her return, the Kanrin Maru served the shogunate navy during the Boshin War. In 1868 she was damaged in stormy weather while being transferred, and she sank off the coast of present-day Shizuoka Prefecture. Her crewmen who died were buried at清水 (Shimizu), where memorials to the ship and her crew are maintained today.

Legacy

The voyage of the Kanrin Maru is remembered as a milestone in Japan's modernisation. A full-size replica of the ship was later built and displayed in Japan, and the centenary of the 1860 voyage was marked by joint Japanese-American commemorations.
