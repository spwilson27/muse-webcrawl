Indulgence

The sale of indulgences in the early 16th century helped spark the Reformation.

TraditionRoman Catholic theology
DefinitionRemission of temporal punishment for sins already forgiven

In the teaching of the Catholic Church, an indulgence (Latin: indulgentia) is "a remission before God of the temporal punishment due to sins whose guilt has already been forgiven, which the faithful Christian who is duly disposed gains under certain prescribed conditions through the action of the Church." Indulgences may be plenary (remitting all temporal punishment) or partial, and may be applied to the living or, by way of suffrage, to the dead.

History

Indulgences developed from the early Church's practices of public penance and the commutation of penances. From the 11th century, indulgences were granted to crusaders, and the theory of the "treasury of merit" — the superabundant merits of Christ and the saints — was formalised by scholastic theologians. By the late Middle Ages indulgences were frequently granted in return for alms, and professional pardoners such as Johann Tetzel sold them to fund projects including the rebuilding of St Peter's Basilica in Rome.

Reformation and reform

Martin Luther's Ninety-five Theses of 1517, written against the preaching of indulgences, became a catalyst of the Protestant Reformation. The Council of Trent (1545–1563) upheld the doctrine but condemned abuses, and in 1567 Pope Pius V abolished the sale of indulgences and the office of pardoner. Modern practice, restated by Pope Paul VI in Indulgentiarum Doctrina (1967), ties indulgences to prayer, works of charity, pilgrimage and holy years rather than money.
