Soil, also commonly referred to as earth or dirt, is a mixture of organic matter, minerals, gases, liquids, and organisms that together support the life of plants and other organisms. Soil forms at the surface of the land through the weathering of rock and the accumulation and decay of organic material, and is organized into layers called horizons.

Composition and horizons

A typical soil is roughly half mineral matter — particles graded as sand, silt, and clay, whose proportions determine soil texture — and half pore space filled with water and air, with a few percent of organic matter in most topsoils. From the surface down, a profile typically shows an organic O horizon, a mineral A horizon (topsoil), an eluviated E horizon in some soils, a B horizon of accumulated clay and oxides (subsoil), a C horizon of weathered parent material, and bedrock (R).

Formation

Soil formation (pedogenesis) is governed by five factors summarized by Hans Jenny: parent material, climate, living organisms, topography, and time. Formation is slow — often centuries to a millennium for a centimetre of topsoil — which makes soil effectively non-renewable on human timescales.

Functions and importance

Soils anchor and nourish nearly all terrestrial plant life and hence agriculture; store and filter water; harbour immense biodiversity (a gram of soil may hold billions of microorganisms); cycle nutrients; and store more carbon than the atmosphere and all vegetation combined. Soil degradation through erosion, compaction, salinization, and contamination threatens food security worldwide.

Classification

Systems such as the USDA Soil Taxonomy and the international World Reference Base classify soils into orders and groups — including Mollisols of the grasslands, Oxisols of the humid tropics, and Gelisols of permafrost regions — based on observable properties and formation processes.
