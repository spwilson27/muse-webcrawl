Messenger RNA

Messenger RNA (mRNA) is a single-stranded molecule of RNA that corresponds to the genetic sequence of a gene, and is read by a ribosome in the process of synthesizing a protein. mRNA is created during the process of transcription, where an enzyme (RNA polymerase) converts the gene into primary transcript mRNA (pre-mRNA). This pre-mRNA usually still contains introns, which are removed by splicing before the mature mRNA is exported from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, where ribosomes translate it.

Structure and lifecycle

A mature eukaryotic mRNA consists of a 5′ cap, a 5′ untranslated region, a coding sequence, a 3′ untranslated region, and a poly(A) tail. The molecule is inherently unstable compared with DNA, with a lifetime from minutes to hours — allowing cells to change protein production quickly. Degradation is carried out by exonucleases and decapping enzymes.

Discovery

mRNA was proposed in 1961 by François Jacob and Jacques Monod and demonstrated by Sydney Brenner, Jacob and Matthew Meselson: an unstable RNA intermediate carries information from genes to ribosomes, confirming the "central dogma" flow DNA → RNA → protein.

Medical applications

Synthetic mRNA, delivered in lipid nanoparticles, is used in mRNA vaccines — most famously the Pfizer–BioNTech and Moderna COVID-19 vaccines authorized in 2020 — and is being investigated for cancer immunotherapy and rare diseases. Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman received the 2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the nucleoside base modifications that made mRNA vaccines feasible.
