Southern Gothic is a subgenre of Gothic fiction set in the American South. It transfers the haunted castles and aristocratic decay of European Gothic to decaying plantations, small towns and swamps, and focuses on grotesque characters, violence, madness, poverty and the region's history of slavery and defeat. Rather than relying on the supernatural, Southern Gothic writers often locate horror in social realities and moral corruption, mixed with dark humour and a strong sense of place.

Decaying grandeur is a hallmark setting of Southern Gothic fiction.

Characteristics

Common elements include dilapidated plantation houses and faded aristocracy; eccentric, damaged or grotesque characters (Flannery O'Connor's fiction is famous for them); an oppressive atmosphere of heat and decay; themes of racism, fundamentalist religion, violence and the inescapability of the past — summed up in Faulkner's line that in the South "the past is never dead. It's not even past."

Major works

William Faulkner's novels (Absalom, Absalom!, The Sound and the Fury) anchor the mode. Others include Carson McCullers's The Heart Is a Lonely Hunter, Flannery O'Connor's Wise Blood and "A Good Man Is Hard to Find", Eudora Welty's stories, Harper Lee's To Kill a Mockingbird, and the plays of Tennessee Williams, including A Streetcar Named Desire. The mode extends to film and television (for example True Detective season one) and to music.

Origins

The genre grew out of nineteenth-century Southern writing (including elements in Poe, who was raised in Virginia, and in Mark Twain) and flowered after the First World War as Southern writers confronted modernity, poverty and the legacy of the Civil War and slavery.
