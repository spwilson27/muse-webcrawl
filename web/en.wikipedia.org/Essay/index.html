An essay is, generally, a piece of writing that gives the author's own argument, but the definition is vague, overlapping with those of the letter, the paper, the article, the pamphlet, and the short story. Essays have been sub-classified as formal and informal: formal essays are characterised by "serious purpose, dignity, logical organisation, length", whereas the informal essay is characterised by "the personal element (self-revelation, individual tastes and experiences), humour, graceful style", and fluid structure.

History

The modern essay begins with Michel de Montaigne, whose Essais (1580) — the word means "attempts" — explored his own thoughts on subjects from thumbs to cannibals in a deliberately provisional, self-questioning style. Francis Bacon adapted the form to English in his terse, aphoristic Essayes (1597–1625). In the eighteenth century Joseph Addison and Richard Steele made the periodical essay a vehicle of manners and ideas in The Spectator. The nineteenth century produced great practitioners such as Charles Lamb, William Hazlitt, and Ralph Waldo Emerson; the twentieth added George Orwell, Virginia Woolf, and James Baldwin, and the form continues in literary journalism and online newsletters.

Forms and uses

Essays serve many purposes: the argumentative essay advances a thesis with evidence; the expository essay explains; the descriptive essay evokes; the narrative essay tells a story; and the personal or familiar essay reflects on experience in a distinctive voice. The essay film applies the same reflective mode to cinema. In education, the essay is a standard assessment format, and in North America the five-paragraph essay is a common school template. Longer related forms include the treatise and the review essay.
