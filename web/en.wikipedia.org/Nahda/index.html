Nahda

The Nahda (Arabic: النهضة, an-nahḍa, "the awakening" or "renaissance") was a cultural, literary and intellectual revival in the Arab world during the 19th and early 20th centuries, centred chiefly on Egypt and Greater Syria. It involved the modernisation of the Arabic language, the growth of journalism and publishing, and new engagement with science, education and political thought.

Origins

The movement is often dated from Napoleon's invasion of Egypt in 1798 and the reforms of Muhammad Ali Pasha, who sent educational missions to Europe and founded schools and printing presses. Scholars such as Rifa'a al-Tahtawi, who led a student mission to Paris in 1826, translated European works and promoted educational reform.

Developments

In Beirut and Cairo, Christian and Muslim intellectuals alike founded newspapers and journals — such as Al-Jinan, edited by Butrus al-Bustani — and produced dictionaries, encyclopaedias and the first modern Arabic novels and plays. Figures including al-Bustani, Nasif al-Yaziji, Ahmad Faris al-Shidyaq and later Jurji Zaydan reshaped Arabic prose and helped lay the cultural foundations of modern Arab nationalism.
