Salvarsan

Salvarsan, known chemically as arsphenamine and marketed under the trade name "606", was the first effective treatment for syphilis and the first modern chemotherapeutic agent. It was developed in 1909 by Paul Ehrlich and his assistant Sahachiro Hata in Frankfurt, Germany, and introduced into clinical practice in 1910.

Development

Ehrlich hypothesized that a chemical could be found that would kill pathogens without seriously harming the patient — his concept of a "magic bullet" (Zauberkugel). Building on the arsenic compound atoxyl, his laboratory systematically synthesized and tested hundreds of organoarsenic derivatives. The 606th preparation tested, arsphenamine, proved powerfully active against Treponema pallidum, the spiral bacterium causing syphilis, when Hata tested it in infected rabbits. The drug was manufactured by Hoechst and distributed worldwide, initially free to physicians in exchange for clinical reports.

Use and legacy

Salvarsan had to be dissolved in sterile water and injected intravenously, a demanding procedure, and it carried significant side effects owing to its arsenic content. A more soluble derivative, Neosalvarsan ("914"), followed in 1912. Nevertheless, Salvarsan remained the standard syphilis treatment for more than three decades, until penicillin displaced it in the 1940s. Its success founded the discipline of chemotherapy and remains a landmark in the history of drug discovery.
