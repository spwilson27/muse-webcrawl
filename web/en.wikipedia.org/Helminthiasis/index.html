Helminthiasis

Helminthiasis

Specialty: Infectious disease, parasitology

Causes: Parasitic worms (helminths)

Treatment: Anthelmintic drugs such as albendazole, mebendazole, praziquantel

Helminth eggs, used in diagnosis by stool microscopy

Helminthiasis, also known as worm infection, is any macroparasitic disease of humans and other animals in which a part of the body is infected with parasitic worms, known as helminths. There are numerous species of these parasites, broadly classified into tapeworms (cestodes), flukes (trematodes), and roundworms (nematodes).

Types and transmission

The most common helminthiases are those caused by infection with intestinal helminths: ascariasis, trichuriasis, and hookworm infection, together classed as soil-transmitted helminthiases. Other important forms include schistosomiasis, lymphatic filariasis, onchocerciasis, and food-borne trematodiases. Transmission occurs through contaminated soil, water, food, or insect vectors, depending on the species.

Signs and symptoms

Many infections are light and asymptomatic. Heavier burdens can cause abdominal pain, diarrhoea, malnutrition, anaemia, impaired growth and cognitive development in children, and, in specific infections, organ damage such as liver fibrosis, bladder pathology, or lymphatic obstruction.

Prevention and treatment

Control relies on improved sanitation, clean water, hygiene education, and preventive chemotherapy — periodic mass administration of anthelmintic medicines such as albendazole, mebendazole, ivermectin, and praziquantel to at-risk populations, a strategy promoted by the World Health Organization against neglected tropical diseases.

Epidemiology

Helminthiases are among the most common infections worldwide, affecting well over a billion people, with the greatest burdens in sub-Saharan Africa, Asia, and Latin America, particularly in communities without adequate water and sanitation.
