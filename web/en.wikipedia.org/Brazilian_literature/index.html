Brazilian literature

Brazilian literature is the literature written by Brazilians, principally in the Portuguese language, from the colonial period to the present. It developed largely in dialogue with Portuguese letters but progressively asserted a distinct national identity — in subject matter (the land, Indigenous peoples, slavery, the sertão), in language (absorbing Indigenous and African vocabulary and rhythms) and in movements such as Romantic Indianism, Realism, and the Modernism launched by São Paulo's Week of Modern Art in 1922. Its most universally acclaimed author is Machado de Assis (1839–1908), regarded as one of the greatest novelists in world literature.

Colonial period

The founding document is the letter of Pero Vaz de Caminha (1500) describing the landfall of Pedro Álvares Cabral's fleet. Baroque poetry flourished with Gregório de Matos, the satirical "Boca do Inferno", and the epic Prosopopeia (1601) of Bento Teixeira; Jesuit writers such as José de Anchieta wrote in Portuguese, Tupi and Latin. In the 18th century, the Arcadian poets of Minas Gerais — Tomás António Gonzaga (Marília de Dirceu) and Cláudio Manuel da Costa — combined classical forms with local landscape, and several were implicated in the Inconfidência Mineira conspiracy of 1789.

Nineteenth century

Independence (1822) spurred Romanticism: Gonçalves Dias gave voice to Indianism in poems like "Canção do exílio" (1843), and José de Alencar's novels O Guarani (1857) and Iracema (1865) mythologised the Indigenous past. Castro Alves became the poet of abolition. Machado de Assis transformed Brazilian prose: after a Romantic first phase, The Posthumous Memoirs of Brás Cubas (1881) and Dom Casmurro (1899) inaugurated a biting, philosophically sceptical realism unmatched in the language. Aluísio Azevedo's O Cortiço (1890) exemplifies Naturalism, and Euclides da Cunha's Os Sertões (1902) — part reportage, part epic — anatomised the Canudos war and the backlands.

Modernism and after

The Week of Modern Art (São Paulo, February 1922) — with Mário de Andrade, Oswald de Andrade, Anita Malfatti and others — broke with academicism; Oswald's Cannibalist Manifesto (1928) proposed devouring European culture to make it Brazilian. Mário's novel Macunaíma (1928) remains the movement's emblem. The 1930s "novel of the Northeast" — Graciliano Ramos (Vidas Secas, 1938), Jorge Amado (Capitães da Areia, 1937), Rachel de Queiroz, José Lins do Rego — mapped regional life and drought. Postwar writing reached new heights: João Guimarães Rosa's Grande Sertão: Veredas (1956) reinvented Portuguese in the voice of the sertão, and Clarice Lispector's introspective fiction (Near to the Wild Heart, 1943; The Hour of the Star, 1977) won international recognition, as did the poetry of Carlos Drummond de Andrade and João Cabral de Melo Neto.

Contemporary literature

Late-20th and 21st-century figures include Rubem Fonseca's urban crime fiction, Raduan Nassar, Milton Hatoum (The Brothers), Paulo Coelho — whose The Alchemist (1988) is among the best-selling books ever by a Brazilian — and newer voices such as Geovani Martins and Itamar Vieira Junior (Torto Arado, 2019). The Brazilian Academy of Letters, founded in 1897 with Machado de Assis as its first president, remains the language's august institution.
