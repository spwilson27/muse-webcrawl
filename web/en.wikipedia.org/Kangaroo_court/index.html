Kangaroo court

A kangaroo court is a court or tribunal that disregards recognised standards of law and justice, carrying on a mock or sham legal proceeding. The term implies that the outcome is predetermined, that the accused has little or no chance of a fair hearing, and that the body may have no genuine legal authority. It is often applied to bodies convened by non-judicial authorities — rebel movements, prison inmates, workplaces or other groups — and sometimes to official courts accused of flagrant bias or summary procedure.

Etymology

The phrase originated in the United States in the mid-19th century, in the era of the California Gold Rush, where itinerant "courts" among miners and settlers dispensed rough justice. The earliest printed citation dates to 1853 in Texas. Why "kangaroo" was chosen is uncertain: suggestions include the image of proceedings "jumping" to conclusions, jumping over the law altogether, or the pockets of a kangaroo (an empty form). Contrary to popular belief, the term is not Australian.

Characteristics

Common features attributed to kangaroo courts include disregard for the defendant's rights, coerced or absent defence, predetermined verdicts, trivial or invented charges, and punishments out of proportion to any offence. The expression is frequently used rhetorically against show trials and against institutions criticised as rubber stamps.

Related usage

Within prisons, "kangaroo courts" are informal inmate tribunals enforcing prisoner codes. In sport and the military, the term is used playfully for team-run mock fines sessions for minor misdemeanours.
