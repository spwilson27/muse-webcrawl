Steam cracking

Steam cracking is a petrochemical process in which saturated hydrocarbons are broken down into smaller, often unsaturated, hydrocarbons — principally ethylene and propylene, the most important building blocks of the chemical industry. It is the principal industrial method for producing light olefins (alkenes).

Process

Feedstocks such as ethane, propane, butane, naphtha or gas oil are mixed with steam and rapidly heated in tubular furnaces to 750–950 °C in the absence of oxygen. Residence times are very short — often well under a second — to maximise olefin yields and minimise secondary reactions. The hot effluent is quenched to stop the reactions, then compressed and separated by fractional distillation and selective hydrogenation.

The steam diluent lowers the partial pressure of the hydrocarbons, favouring the formation of olefins, and reduces coke deposition on the reactor tubes, although periodic decoking with steam and air is still required.

Products

  • Ethylene — used for polyethylene, ethylene oxide and many other chemicals.
  • Propylene — used for polypropylene, acrylonitrile and propylene oxide.
  • Butadiene and butenes — for synthetic rubber and polymers.
  • Pyrolysis gasoline — rich in benzene, toluene and xylenes.
  • Hydrogen, methane and fuel oil by-products.

Significance

Steam crackers are among the largest units in petrochemical complexes; world-scale plants produce over a million tonnes of ethylene per year. Because the process is highly energy-intensive, it is also a significant source of industrial carbon dioxide emissions, motivating research into electrically heated furnaces and alternative feedstocks.
