Large Hadron Collider

The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) is the world's largest and highest-energy particle collider. It was built by the European Organization for Nuclear Research (CERN) between 1998 and 2008 in collaboration with over 10,000 scientists and hundreds of universities and laboratories from more than 100 countries. It lies in a tunnel 26.7 kilometres (16.6 mi) in circumference, as deep as 175 metres (574 ft) beneath the France–Switzerland border near Geneva.

Design

The collider accelerates two counter-rotating beams of protons (or heavy ions) in a ring of superconducting magnets cooled to −271.3 °C, colder than outer space. The beams collide at four crossing points, where the detectors ATLAS, CMS, ALICE, and LHCb are located. In its second run the proton beams reached energies of 6.5 TeV each, giving a centre-of-mass energy of 13 TeV; Run 3, begun in 2022, operates at 13.6 TeV.

Physics goals and discoveries

The LHC was built to test predictions of particle physics, including the existence of the Higgs boson, supersymmetry, extra dimensions, and the nature of dark matter. On 4 July 2012, ATLAS and CMS announced the discovery of a new particle consistent with the Higgs boson, a finding that led to the 2013 Nobel Prize in Physics for François Englert and Peter Higgs. The experiments have also produced measurements of rare decays, CP violation, and quark–gluon plasma in heavy-ion collisions.

History

First beams circulated on 10 September 2008. Nine days later a faulty electrical connection caused a magnet quench and the release of liquid helium, delaying operations for over a year. Collisions at increasing energies followed through Runs 1 and 2, long shutdowns for upgrades, and the start of Run 3 in July 2022. A major upgrade, the High Luminosity LHC, is planned to increase collision rates substantially in the 2030s.
