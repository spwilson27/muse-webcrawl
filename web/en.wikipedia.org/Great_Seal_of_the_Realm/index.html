The Great Seal of the Realm is a seal used by the monarch of the United Kingdom to signify approval of state documents. It is one of the oldest symbols of the authority of the Crown, with a lineage stretching back to the reign of Edward the Confessor in the eleventh century, whose seal is regarded as the origin of the tradition.

Use

The Great Seal is affixed to documents of the highest importance, including letters patent, royal proclamations, writs for the election of Members of Parliament, and the instruments ratifying treaties. The process of sealing, known as "passing under the Great Seal", is the final formal step giving such documents legal effect. Authority to use the seal is granted by the monarch through a warrant countersigned by the responsible minister.

The seal is kept by the Lord Chancellor, who holds the office of Keeper of the Great Seal of England — a responsibility dating to the medieval Chancery. Day-to-day custody rests with the Clerk of the Crown in Chancery.

Design

The seal is double-sided. The obverse traditionally shows the sovereign enthroned in coronation robes; the reverse shows the sovereign on horseback in armour. The design changes with each reign. The seal introduced on the accession of Elizabeth II was designed by Gilbert Ledward; a new seal designed by Timothy Noad came into use in 2001, notably struck in silver rather than the traditional base-metal alloy, and smaller than its predecessors.

Sealing wax was historically used, but modern practice employs a wafer of coloured material moulded between the seal matrices. Different colours of wax denote different classes of document.

History

Edward the Confessor introduced the practice of authenticating royal grants with a large double-sided seal. William the Conqueror retained the custom, and successive monarchs — with the exceptions of Stephen and the Commonwealth period — maintained it. During the Commonwealth, Parliament commissioned its own Great Seal, and after the Restoration the royal seal was restored. A famous episode occurred in 1646 when Parliament's possession of a seal became a central symbol in the struggle with Charles I.

Under the Acts of Union, separate arrangements persisted for Scotland, which has its own Great Seal used for Scottish documents following devolution.
