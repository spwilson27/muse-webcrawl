The Emergency Alert System (EAS) is a national warning system in the United States that allows authorised officials to broadcast emergency alerts and warning messages to the public via cable, satellite and broadcast television and radio. Put into place in 1997, it replaced the earlier Emergency Broadcast System and forms part of the Integrated Public Alert and Warning System (IPAWS), alongside Wireless Emergency Alerts sent to mobile phones.

Operation

Alerts are originated by federal, state and local authorities — most frequently by the National Weather Service for severe weather warnings. Messages use the Specific Area Message Encoding (SAME) protocol: a digitally encoded header identifies the type of event, the areas affected and the duration, followed by an attention tone and a voice or text message. Broadcasters' decoding equipment automatically decides whether to relay an alert, and all participants must conduct weekly and monthly tests.

National activations

The system allows the President to address the nation in a national emergency, although this capability has never been used for an actual event. A first nationwide test of the EAS was conducted on 9 November 2011, and periodic national tests have followed. In January 2018, a false ballistic missile alert transmitted in Hawaii — sent through a related state system during a drill — caused widespread alarm and led to procedural reforms.

Criticism and security

Commentators have raised concerns about alert fatigue, inconsistent local activation, and the security of EAS encoder/decoder equipment, after researchers demonstrated vulnerabilities that could allow false alerts to be injected. Regulators have responded with requirements for software updates and reporting of false activations.
