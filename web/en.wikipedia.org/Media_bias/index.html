Media bias

Media bias is the bias of journalists and news producers within the mass media, in the selection of stories that are reported and how they are covered. The term implies a pervasive or institutional bias that contravenes the standards of professional journalism, rather than the perspective of an individual journalist. Types commonly discussed include political bias, corporate bias, bias arising from advertising pressures, and bias in story selection and framing.

Forms of bias

  • Selection bias — choosing which events are newsworthy and which are ignored.
  • Framing bias — presenting facts within a particular interpretation or vocabulary.
  • Partisan bias — favouring one political party, ideology or candidate.
  • Corporate and advertising bias — shaping coverage to the interests of owners or advertisers.
  • Mainstream bias — the tendency to report what other outlets report, reinforcing a shared conventional account.

Explanations and research

Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky's propaganda model, set out in Manufacturing Consent (1988), argues that structural filters — ownership, advertising, sourcing from official institutions, flak, and ideology — systematically shape news without need for conspiracy. Other scholars emphasise commercial incentives toward sensationalism and horse-race election coverage, professional norms, audience demand, and journalists' personal backgrounds. Studies of audience perception describe the hostile media effect: partisans on both sides tend to see the same coverage as biased against their own side.

Public opinion and consequences

Surveys in many countries have recorded declining trust in news media, often polarised along partisan lines. Perceptions of bias can contribute to audience fragmentation, the growth of openly partisan outlets and alternative media, and demands for regulation or public-service broadcasting. Media-literacy education and transparency about sourcing are frequently proposed remedies.
