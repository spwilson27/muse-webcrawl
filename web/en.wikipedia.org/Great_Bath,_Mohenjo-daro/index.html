The Great Bath is a large public water tank found in the ruined Bronze Age city of Mohenjo-daro in Sindh, Pakistan. It was one of the largest structures of the ancient Indus Valley civilisation and is often described as the earliest public water tank in human history. Archaeologists generally interpret it as a place for ritual bathing, given the importance of ritual purity in later South Asian religions.

Description

The bath is a sunken rectangular tank measuring approximately 11.88 metres (39.0 ft) north–south by 7.01 metres (23.0 ft) east–west, with a maximum depth of about 2.43 metres (8.0 ft). Two wide staircases, one at the northern end and one at the southern end, lead down into the tank. The floor was made watertight by finely fitted bricks laid on edge and sealed with a thick layer of natural tar (bitumen), one of the earliest known uses of waterproofing in construction. A small hole at one corner of the floor would have allowed the bath to be drained for cleaning.

The tank sat at the centre of a courtyard surrounded by a colonnade and a series of small rooms, some of which may have been changing rooms. The surrounding buildings included what some scholars identify as a large granary and a complex sometimes called the "College of Priests."

Discovery and excavation

Mohenjo-daro was rediscovered in 1922 by R. D. Banerji of the Archaeological Survey of India, and large-scale excavations followed under John Marshall and later Ernest Mackay during the 1920s and 1930s. The Great Bath was among the most prominent structures uncovered. The site, including the Great Bath, was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1980, the first such designation in South Asia.

Purpose

No temple or palace has been conclusively identified at Mohenjo-daro, and the Great Bath is frequently cited as evidence that water and ritual purity played a central role in Indus religious life, much as ritual bathing later became important in Hinduism. Some scholars suggest it was used for special religious functions where water was thought to cleanse and renew. Others note it may simply have served as an elite or communal bathing facility.

Conservation

The exposed brickwork is vulnerable to waterlogging, salinity and rising damp from the nearby Indus River. The catastrophic Pakistan floods of 2022 damaged parts of the Mohenjo-daro site, prompting emergency conservation work on the Great Bath and surrounding structures.
