Supreme Soviet

The Supreme Soviet (Russian: Верховный Совет, "Supreme Council") was the highest body of state authority in the Soviet Union from 1936 until the country's dissolution in 1991, and nominally its legislature. In practice, real power lay with the Communist Party of the Soviet Union, and the Supreme Soviet functioned largely to approve decisions already taken by the party leadership, until competitive elections in 1989 made the new Congress of People's Deputies a genuine arena of debate.

Structure

Under the 1936 and 1977 constitutions, the Supreme Soviet had two chambers of equal rights: the Soviet of the Union, elected by population, and the Soviet of Nationalities, representing the union republics and autonomous regions. Deputies were elected for four (later five) year terms in uncontested single-candidate elections for most of the Soviet period. Between its short sessions, its Presidium exercised its functions, issuing decrees (ukazy) and formally acting as the collective head of state; the Chairman of the Presidium was often described abroad as the Soviet "president".

Union republics

Each union republic and autonomous republic also had its own supreme soviet, mirroring the all-union body, as did the Russian SFSR — whose Supreme Soviet and its chairman, Boris Yeltsin, became a rival centre of power in 1990–91.

End of the USSR

Gorbachev's reforms created the Congress of People's Deputies in 1989, which elected a reconstituted, permanently sitting Supreme Soviet. Following the failed August 1991 coup, the Supreme Soviet presided over the dissolution of the USSR, formally ending its existence in December 1991.
