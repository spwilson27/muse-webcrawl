Sherman Antitrust Act

Enacted2 July 1890, by the 51st United States Congress
Named forSenator John Sherman of Ohio
Citation15 U.S.C. §§ 1–7
Signed byPresident Benjamin Harrison

The Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 is a United States antitrust law that prescribes the rule of free competition among those engaged in commerce. It was the first federal statute to limit cartels and monopolies, and it remains the core of American antitrust policy. The Act is named after its principal author, Senator John Sherman.

Provisions

Section 1 outlaws every contract, combination or conspiracy in restraint of trade or commerce among the states or with foreign nations. Section 2 makes it a felony to monopolise, or attempt to monopolise, any part of such trade or commerce. Violations carry criminal penalties for individuals and corporations, and injured private parties may sue for treble (threefold) damages.

Enforcement and landmarks

Early enforcement was limited, and the Act was even used against labour unions, but landmark cases defined its reach: Northern Securities Co. v. United States (1904), and the 1911 decisions in Standard Oil Co. v. United States and the American Tobacco case, which broke up two great trusts and established the "rule of reason". The Act was later supplemented by the Clayton Antitrust Act and the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914. Major modern applications include the breakup of AT&T in 1982 and cases against IBM and Microsoft.
