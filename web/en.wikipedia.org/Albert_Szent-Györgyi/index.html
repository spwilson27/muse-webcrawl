Albert Imre Szent-Györgyi (1893–1986) was a Hungarian biochemist who won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1937. He is credited with discovering vitamin C and, partly, the components and reactions of the citric acid cycle, as well as discoveries about the proteins of muscle contraction.

Vitamin C

Working at the University of Szeged in the early 1930s, Szent-Györgyi isolated a substance he called "hexuronic acid" from adrenal glands and in large quantities from Hungarian paprika, for which Szeged was famous. With Norman Haworth, he showed it was identical to the antiscorbutic factor, vitamin C, and it was renamed ascorbic acid. His work also clarified steps of cellular respiration, including the role of fumaric acid, contributing to the understanding later formalised in the Krebs cycle.

Later life

Active in the Hungarian resistance during the Second World War, Szent-Györgyi emigrated to the United States in 1947 and worked at the Marine Biological Laboratory in Woods Hole, Massachusetts, studying muscle proteins (actin and myosin) and, later, his controversial "bioelectronics" theory of cancer. He became a prominent critic of the Vietnam War and nuclear arms.
