Hall–Héroult process

The Hall–Héroult process is the major industrial process for smelting aluminium. It involves dissolving aluminium oxide (alumina), most often obtained from bauxite through the Bayer process, in molten cryolite, and electrolysing the molten salt bath, typically in a purpose-built cell. The process was invented independently and nearly simultaneously in 1886 by the American chemist Charles Martin Hall and the Frenchman Paul Héroult — both born in 1863 and both dying in 1914.

Process

Pure alumina melts at over 2,000 °C, but dissolved in cryolite (Na3AlF6) it electrolyses at about 950–980 °C. The carbon-lined cell acts as the cathode; a direct current passed through the bath reduces aluminium ions to molten metal, which sinks to the bottom of the cell and is periodically siphoned off. Carbon anodes are consumed in the process, reacting with liberated oxygen to form carbon dioxide, and must be regularly replaced. Depending on the configuration, the cells are called prebake or Søderberg types.

Significance

Before Hall–Héroult, aluminium was more precious than gold; the process cut its price dramatically and enabled the modern aluminium industry. The process is highly energy-intensive — smelters are typically located where cheap electricity, often hydroelectric, is available — and its greenhouse gas emissions, including perfluorocarbons from "anode effects", remain a subject of technological improvement.
