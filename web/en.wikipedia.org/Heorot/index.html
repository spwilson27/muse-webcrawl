Heorot (Old English: "Hall of the Hart") is a mead hall described in the Old English epic poem Beowulf as the centre of the kingdom of the Danish king Hrothgar. It is one of the principal settings of the first part of the poem and a symbol of the order, generosity and civilisation that the monster Grendel attacks.

In the poem

Hrothgar, prosperous in war, decides to build a hall "greater than the children of men had ever heard of", a place where he can distribute treasure and hold feasts with his retainers. The building of Heorot, its gables steep and horn-tipped, is described as a triumph of the heroic world. The noise of feasting in the hall, however, enrages Grendel, a monstrous descendant of Cain who lives in the nearby fens. For twelve winters Grendel raids Heorot at night, killing and devouring the sleeping thanes, and the hall stands empty and useless. The Geatish hero Beowulf comes to Hrothgar's aid, waits in Heorot by night, and tears off Grendel's arm in unarmed combat; the arm is hung in the hall as a trophy. After a further attack by Grendel's mother, Heorot is restored to festivity, though the poem foreshadows that it will one day be destroyed by fire in fighting between Danes and Heathobards.

Historical background

Great timber halls were the political and social centres of early medieval Scandinavian and Anglo-Saxon society, and archaeologists have excavated hall complexes of the period, notably at Lejre in Denmark — traditionally associated with Hrothgar's court — and at Yeavering in Northumbria, described by Bede. Heorot itself is usually read as the literary ideal of the institution: a place of gift-giving, oath-taking, song and mutual loyalty, whose vulnerability dramatises the fragility of heroic civilisation.
