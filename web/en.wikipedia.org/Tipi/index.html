Tipi

A tipi (also tepee) is a cone-shaped tent, traditionally made of animal skins upon wooden poles, used by the Indigenous peoples of the Great Plains and Canadian Prairies of North America. Distinct from other conical tents, the tipi includes adjustable smoke flaps at the top that allow a fire to burn inside in all weather.

Construction

A tripod of long poles (often lodgepole pine) is raised and bound, additional poles leaned against it, and a cover of sewn buffalo hides — later canvas — wrapped around, leaving an opening at the apex. Two smoke flaps, controlled by exterior poles, direct smoke out while keeping rain from the central fire. An inner liner improves insulation and draught control. A typical family tipi stood about 4–5 metres high and could be pitched or struck in under an hour, suiting the mobile life of Plains peoples such as the Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho and Crow.

Use and symbolism

Tipis were typically owned and pitched by women. Covers were sometimes painted with records of battles, hunts or spiritual designs, and their orientation and decoration could carry ceremonial meaning. After the destruction of the bison herds in the 19th century, canvas replaced hides. Tipis remain in use today for ceremonial and cultural gatherings such as powwows, and in modern recreational forms.
