Skaz

Skaz Origin: Russian literature
Key theorists: Boris Eichenbaum; Mikhail Bakhtin
Notable practitioners: Nikolai Leskov; Mikhail Zoshchenko; Pavel Bazhov

Skaz (Russian: сказ) is a Russian literary term for a narrative technique in which the story is told in the voice of a narrator-persona, often a folk or socially distinct character whose speech patterns — vocabulary, syntax, dialect and intonation — differ markedly from standard literary language. The word itself is related to skazat', "to tell", and to skazka, a folk tale.

Development of the concept

The term was developed by the Russian Formalists, above all Boris Eichenbaum, in essays of 1918–19 on Nikolai Gogol's "The Overcoat" and on the storyteller's art. For Eichenbaum, skaz oriented a text toward oral performance: the written prose imitates the gestures, pauses and intonations of spoken narration. Mikhail Bakhtin later treated skaz within his theory of double-voiced discourse, analysing how the author's intentions interact with and refract through the narrator's alien speech.

Practitioners

The classic master of skaz is Nikolai Leskov, whose tales such as "The Tale of Cross-eyed Lefty from Tula and the Steel Flea" (1881) are narrated in a rich, punning folk idiom. In the Soviet period Mikhail Zoshchenko used a semi-literate petty-bourgeois narrator to satirical effect, while Pavel Bazhov's Ural folk tales employed the voice of an old miner-storyteller. Skaz-like narration appears in many other literatures wherever authors adopt a distinctive oral persona, but the term remains tied to the Russian tradition.
