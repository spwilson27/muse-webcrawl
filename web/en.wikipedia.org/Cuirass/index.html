Cuirass

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cuirass is a piece of armour consisting of a breastplate and backplate fastened together, worn to protect the torso. The word comes from the Old French cuirace, ultimately from the Latin coriaceus, "of leather" — early cuirasses were made of hardened leather before bronze and steel became standard.

History

Bronze cuirasses shaped to the musculature of the chest (the "muscle cuirass") were worn by Greek hoplites and Roman officers. Medieval plate armour incorporated cuirasses of articulated steel plates, and by the 16th and 17th centuries heavy cavalry — cuirassiers — wore substantial three-quarter armour built around a strong, sometimes bullet-proofed cuirass. Napoleonic France, Britain and several German states fielded cuirassier regiments in polished steel cuirasses well into the era of rifled firearms.

Survival and ceremonial use

Although battlefield use declined in the 19th century, cuirasses survive in ceremonial dress: the British Household Cavalry still wears them on state occasions, as do the French Republican Guard's cavalry and the Italian Corazzieri. The cuirass also gave its name to the ironclad warship protection scheme and to the cuirass ventilator in medicine.
