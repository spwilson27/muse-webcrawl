Charismatic movement

Origins1960s, within mainline Protestant and later Catholic churches
Key practicesSpeaking in tongues, prophecy, faith healing, contemporary worship
Related movementPentecostalism
AdherentsHundreds of millions worldwide (with Pentecostalism)

The charismatic movement is an interdenominational Christian renewal movement that emphasizes the work of the Holy Spirit, spiritual gifts (charismata), and the contemporary experience of the miraculous within established, historic churches. Unlike classical Pentecostals, who formed separate denominations, charismatics generally remained within their existing traditions — Protestant, Roman Catholic, and Orthodox — while adopting practices such as speaking in tongues, prophecy, and healing prayer.

History

The movement is conventionally dated to 1960, when Dennis Bennett, rector of St. Mark's Episcopal Church in Van Nuys, California, publicly announced that he had received the baptism in the Holy Spirit and spoken in tongues. Similar revivals spread through Lutheran, Presbyterian, and Methodist congregations. In 1967 the renewal reached the Roman Catholic Church at Duquesne University in Pittsburgh, and the Catholic Charismatic Renewal grew into a worldwide movement recognized by the Vatican. The charismatic renewal gave rise in turn to independent charismatic churches and networks, sometimes called the "Third Wave", and influenced worship styles — raised hands, spontaneous prayer, and contemporary music — across much of global Christianity.

Beliefs and practices

Charismatics affirm the continuing availability of the gifts listed in 1 Corinthians 12–14, including tongues, interpretation, prophecy, healing, and words of knowledge. Most do not teach, as classical Pentecostals often do, that tongues are the necessary evidence of Spirit baptism. Worship tends to be expressive and participatory, and many charismatic communities emphasize small prayer groups, personal testimony, and evangelism.

Criticism

Critics from cessationist traditions argue that the miraculous gifts ceased with the apostolic age, while others have questioned emotionalism, the prosperity gospel found in some charismatic circles, and accountability structures in independent networks.
