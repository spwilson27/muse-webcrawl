Record sleeve

A record sleeve protects the disc and carries the album's artwork and information.

A record sleeve, also called an album cover, record jacket or album sleeve, is the outer packaging of a phonograph record. It protects the disc from dust, scratches and handling, and serves as the principal surface for artwork, photography, credits and liner notes. The sleeve may refer to the printed outer jacket, the plain inner sleeve (paper or poly-lined) that directly encloses the record, or to both as a unit.

History

Early shellac 78 rpm records were sold in plain paper or company-branded sleeves, or bound into book-like "albums" of sleeves — the origin of the word album for a collection of recordings. Commercial art covers began in the late 1930s; Alex Steinweiss, art director at Columbia Records from 1939, is widely credited with inventing the illustrated album cover, and sales of records so packaged rose sharply. The 12-inch LP, introduced by Columbia in 1948, made the sleeve a large square canvas, and from the 1950s through the 1970s album covers became a major field of graphic design and photography, producing celebrated work by designers such as Reid Miles, and studios such as Hipgnosis.

Types

Common forms include the single-pocket sleeve; the gatefold, which opens like a book and doubles the display area; sleeves with die-cut shapes or windows; and box sets. Singles were often issued in generic company sleeves or in picture sleeves reproducing the artist's image. Inside, anti-static rice-paper or plastic-lined inner sleeves are preferred by collectors to reduce static and dust. With the shift to CDs the sleeve shrank to the jewel-case booklet, and in the streaming era its role passed to digital cover art thumbnails — though the vinyl revival has revived elaborate sleeve design.

Collecting and preservation

Record collectors grade both disc and sleeve, and condition of the cover significantly affects value. Wear patterns such as ring wear, seam splits and writing reduce grade; sleeves are stored upright, away from heat, and often protected in outer plastic covers.
