Gambler's fallacy

TypeCognitive bias / logical fallacy
Also known asMonte Carlo fallacy; fallacy of the maturity of chances
Related conceptsHot-hand fallacy; regression to the mean; law of large numbers

The gambler's fallacy, also known as the Monte Carlo fallacy or the fallacy of the maturity of chances, is the incorrect belief that, if a particular event occurs more frequently than normal during the past, it is less likely to happen in the future (or vice versa), when it has otherwise been established that the probability of such events does not depend on what has happened in the past. Such events, having the quality of historical independence, are referred to as statistically independent.

Examples

The classic illustration is coin tossing: after a run of heads, a person prone to the fallacy believes tails is "due," even though each toss of a fair coin remains 50/50. The fallacy is named for an incident at the Monte Carlo Casino on August 18, 1913, when the roulette ball landed on black 26 times in a row; gamblers lost millions betting against black, reasoning that red was overdue.

Related phenomena

The inverse error, expecting a streak to continue, underlies the hot-hand fallacy in sports. Psychologists attribute the gambler's fallacy partly to a belief in the "law of small numbers" — that even short sequences must be representative of the underlying probability. The fallacy has been documented in real-world settings including lottery play, judicial asylum decisions, and baseball umpiring.
