Mississippi River

The Mississippi River is the chief river of the second-largest drainage system in North America, second only to the Hudson Bay drainage system. From its traditional source of Lake Itasca in northern Minnesota, it flows generally south for 2,340 miles (3,770 km) to the Mississippi River Delta in the Gulf of Mexico. With its many tributaries, the Mississippi's watershed drains all or parts of 32 U.S. states and two Canadian provinces.

Course

The Upper Mississippi runs from the headwaters past Minneapolis–Saint Paul, where it meets the Minnesota River, to St. Louis, where it joins the Missouri River. The Middle Mississippi continues to Cairo, Illinois, where the Ohio River — which contributes more water than the Missouri — joins from the east. The Lower Mississippi then winds through broad floodplains past Memphis, Vicksburg and Natchez to New Orleans and the bird's-foot delta below it.

History

Indigenous peoples lived along the river for millennia, from the mound-building Mississippian culture to the peoples for whom its name, derived from the Ojibwe misi-ziibi ("great river"), was adopted. Hernando de Soto reached the river in 1541; French explorers Marquette and Jolliet descended it in 1673, and La Salle claimed the whole basin for France in 1682. Steamboats made it the great commercial artery of the 19th century, celebrated in Mark Twain's Life on the Mississippi.

Navigation and floods

A system of locks and dams on the upper river and levees on the lower river maintains a 9-foot navigation channel for barge traffic. Catastrophic floods in 1927 and 1993 led to successive expansions of federal flood control.
