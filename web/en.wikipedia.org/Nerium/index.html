Nerium oleander, commonly known as oleander, is a flowering shrub or small tree in the dogbane family Apocynaceae and the sole species in the genus Nerium. Native to a broad region from the Mediterranean basin eastward to southern Asia, it has been cultivated for so long that its exact origin is uncertain. It is among the most widely grown ornamental plants in warm climates worldwide — and among the most poisonous garden plants.

Description

Oleander grows 2–6 m tall, with erect stems, thick leathery lance-shaped leaves arranged in whorls, and showy terminal clusters of flowers in shades of pink, white, red, or yellow, blooming through the summer. The fruit is a long, narrow paired follicle releasing numerous downy seeds. The plant is highly tolerant of drought, poor soils, salt spray, and heat, which has made it a staple of highway median plantings in warm regions such as California, Texas, and the Mediterranean.

Toxicity

All parts of the plant contain potent cardiac glycosides, chiefly oleandrin, which affect the heart like an overdose of digoxin. Ingestion of even a small amount can cause nausea, arrhythmia, and death in humans and livestock; smoke from burning oleander is also hazardous. Despite this, the plant's beauty and toughness keep it ubiquitous in ornamental horticulture, and compounds from it have been investigated (so far without established clinical use) for antiviral and anticancer properties.
