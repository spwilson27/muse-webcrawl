Caciquismo

Caciquismo is a political system in Spain and in the former Spanish colonies, based on the rule of local bosses called caciques. It is most associated with the Restoration period in Spain (1874–1931), during which elections were managed and manipulated through networks of local political bosses who controlled votes in rural constituencies.

The Restoration system

Under the Restoration settlement, the two dynastic parties — the Conservatives of Antonio Cánovas del Castillo and the Liberals of Práxedes Mateo Sagasta — alternated in power in the so-called turno pacífico. Elections were effectively decided in advance: the Ministry of the Interior drew up the desired result (the encasillado), civil governors transmitted it to the provinces, and caciques delivered the local vote through patronage, coercion, and fraud, including the manipulation of electoral rolls (the pucherazo).

Caciques

A cacique was typically a wealthy landowner, lawyer, or notable whose influence rested on control of jobs, credit, administrative favours, and protection. The system weakened genuine party competition and was denounced by regenerationist critics such as Joaquín Costa. Though the classic system declined with urbanisation and the crises of the early 20th century, the term caciquismo remains a byword in Spanish political life for boss rule and clientelism, and cognate practices existed across Latin America.
