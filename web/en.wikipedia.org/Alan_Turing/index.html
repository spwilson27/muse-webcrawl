Alan Turing

Born: 23 June 1912, Maida Vale, London
Died: 7 June 1954, Wilmslow, Cheshire
Fields: Mathematics, logic, computer science, cryptanalysis

Alan Mathison Turing (23 June 1912 – 7 June 1954) was an English mathematician, computer scientist, logician, cryptanalyst, philosopher, and theoretical biologist. He was highly influential in the development of theoretical computer science, providing a formalisation of the concepts of algorithm and computation with the Turing machine, which can be considered a model of a general-purpose computer. Turing is widely considered to be the father of theoretical computer science and artificial intelligence.

Early life and education

Turing was born in Maida Vale, London, and educated at Sherborne School and King's College, Cambridge, where he was elected a fellow in 1935. His 1936 paper "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem" introduced the universal machine and proved that the halting problem is undecidable. He subsequently studied at Princeton University under Alonzo Church, earning his PhD in 1938.

Codebreaking

During the Second World War, Turing worked at Bletchley Park, Britain's codebreaking centre. He devised the Bombe, an electromechanical device that helped decipher German Enigma-encrypted messages, building on earlier Polish work. His contributions are estimated to have shortened the war in Europe. In 1945 he was appointed an OBE for his wartime services.

Later work

After the war Turing designed the Automatic Computing Engine at the National Physical Laboratory and later worked at the University of Manchester on early stored-program computers. His 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" proposed the Turing test as a criterion of machine intelligence. He also made pioneering contributions to mathematical biology, modelling morphogenesis in a landmark 1952 paper.

Prosecution and legacy

In 1952 Turing was prosecuted for homosexual acts, then illegal in Britain, and subjected to chemical castration treatment. He died in 1954 from cyanide poisoning, ruled a suicide at inquest. In 2009 the British government issued a public apology, and in 2013 Turing received a royal pardon. The Turing Award, often called the "Nobel Prize of Computing", bears his name, and his portrait appears on the Bank of England £50 note issued in 2021.
