The Saussurean model of the sign as a two-sided entity.

In semiotics, a sign is anything that communicates a meaning that is not the sign itself to the interpreter of the sign. The meaning can be intentional, as when a word is spoken, or unintentional, as when a symptom is taken as a sign of a medical condition. Signs can communicate through any of the senses: visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, or gustatory.

Saussure's model

The Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure (1857–1913), a founder of semiotics, defined the sign as a two-sided entity consisting of a signifier — the form the sign takes, such as a sound pattern or written word — and a signified — the concept it represents. Saussure stressed that the relationship between signifier and signified is arbitrary: there is no natural connection between the word "tree" and the concept it evokes.

Peirce's model

The American philosopher Charles Sanders Peirce (1839–1914) proposed a triadic model: a representamen (the form of the sign), an object (that to which the sign refers), and an interpretant (the sense made of the sign). Peirce classified signs into three types: icons, which resemble their object; indexes (or indices), which are causally or physically connected to their object, like smoke to fire; and symbols, which relate to their object by convention, like words.

Semiosis

The process by which a sign produces meaning is called semiosis. Because an interpretant can itself become a sign for a further interpretant, semiosis is in principle unlimited — a chain of interpretation that Peirce called "unlimited semiosis".

Applications

The concept of the sign is foundational not only in linguistics but also in literary theory, anthropology, communication studies, and the study of visual media, where images, gestures, clothing, and architecture are all analyzed as sign systems.
