Relevance logic
Developed byAlan Ross Anderson, Nuel Belnap, J. Michael Dunn and others
Key requirementVariable sharing: premises and conclusion must share propositional content
Main systemsE (entailment), R, T

Relevance logic (also called relevant logic) is a family of non-classical logics designed to avoid the paradoxes of material and strict implication — classically valid inferences such as "the moon is made of cheese, therefore anything" or "a true statement is implied by anything at all". Relevance logicians require that the premises of a valid argument be genuinely relevant to its conclusion.

The variable-sharing property

In the best-known systems, R and E, a theorem of the form A → B must have A and B sharing at least one propositional variable. This simple constraint blocks both the paradoxes of implication and the principle of explosion: from P ∧ ¬P one cannot derive an unrelated Q, because Q shares no content with the premises. Relevance logics are therefore paraconsistent, though their founders' motivation was relevance rather than contradiction-tolerance.

History

Ideas trace to work by Ivan Orlov and to C. I. Lewis's dissatisfaction with material implication; the field took shape in the 1950s–1970s with Anderson and Belnap's system E of entailment and the stronger system R. Semantics using the Routley–Meyer ternary relation made the logics technically respectable and connected them to substructural logic generally.
