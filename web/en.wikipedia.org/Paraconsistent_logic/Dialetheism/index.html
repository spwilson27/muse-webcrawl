Dialetheism

Dialetheism
ClaimSome contradictions are true
Key proponentsGraham Priest, Jc Beall
Logic requiredA paraconsistent logic (to block explosion)
ExamplesLiar paradox, Russell's paradox (on some treatments)

Dialetheism is the view that some statements are both true and false — that there exist true contradictions, called dialetheia. It is the most radical position in the family of paraconsistent approaches to logic: where most paraconsistent logicians merely hold that reasoning can be non-trivial in the presence of contradictions, dialetheists argue that some contradictions describe reality correctly.

Motivation

The standard examples are the semantic paradoxes. The liar sentence — "this sentence is false" — appears to be true if and only if it is false, and dialetheists take this equivalence at face value: the sentence is both. Similar treatments are offered for Russell's paradox in set theory and for paradoxes of motion and change descending from Zeno and Heraclitus. Buddhist philosophy, especially the Madhyamaka use of the catuskoti (four corners: true, false, both, neither), is often cited as a historical antecedent.

Objections

Critics object that dialetheism makes negation and disagreement unintelligible — if a claim can be both true and false, denying it seems to say nothing — and that dialetheists must still somehow exclude the vast majority of contradictions. Graham Priest, the view's leading defender, replies within his Logic of Paradox (LP), a paraconsistent system in which the principle of explosion fails, so accepting a few contradictions does not entail everything.
