Non-classical logic

Non-classical logic
DefinitionAny formal system differing from classical logic in its assumptions
ExamplesIntuitionistic, modal, many-valued, fuzzy, paraconsistent, relevance logics
Principles questionedExcluded middle, non-contradiction, bivalence, explosion

Non-classical logics are formal systems that depart from the assumptions of classical propositional and predicate logic — for example by rejecting the law of the excluded middle, the principle of bivalence (every statement is either true or false), monotonicity, or the principle of explosion, according to which anything follows from a contradiction.

Main families

  • Intuitionistic logic, developed by L. E. J. Brouwer and Arend Heyting, refuses to assert a statement or its negation without a constructive proof.
  • Modal logics extend classical logic with operators for necessity and possibility, and their kin for time, knowledge and obligation.
  • Many-valued and fuzzy logics (Łukasiewicz, Zadeh) admit truth values beyond true and false, modelling vagueness.
  • Paraconsistent logics tolerate contradictions without collapse into triviality; their most committed philosophers are dialetheists.
  • Relevance logic requires premises to be genuinely relevant to conclusions, rejecting paradoxes of material implication.
  • Substructural logics, including linear logic, restrict the structural rules governing premises.

Uses

Non-classical logics are applied in computer science (program verification, databases with inconsistent information), linguistics, mathematics and philosophy. The label covers both extensions of classical logic and rivals that restrict it.
