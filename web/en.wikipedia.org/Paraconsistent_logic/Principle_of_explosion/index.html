Principle of explosion

Principle of explosion
Latin nameEx falso quodlibet ("from falsehood, anything")
StatementFrom a contradiction, any statement follows
Holds inClassical and intuitionistic logic
Fails inParaconsistent and relevance logics

The principle of explosion (Latin: ex falso quodlibet, "from falsehood, anything [follows]") is a rule of classical logic stating that from contradictory premises — a statement and its negation — any conclusion whatsoever can be derived. A logic or theory in which every sentence is provable is called trivial; explosion is why classical theories must avoid inconsistency at all costs.

Proof sketch

From P and ¬P: by disjunction introduction, P ∨ Q holds for arbitrary Q; then by disjunctive syllogism with ¬P, Q follows. This derivation shows why paraconsistent systems reject one of its steps — usually disjunctive syllogism or the behaviour of negation.

Responses

Paraconsistent logics are designed so that explosion fails, allowing inconsistent but non-trivial reasoning about imperfect databases, legal codes and paradoxes. Relevance logic blocks it by requiring premises to be relevant to the conclusion. Dialetheists accept some contradictions as true and therefore regard the failure of explosion as essential, not optional. The principle is jokingly remembered by the claim that Bertrand Russell, challenged to prove he was the Pope from a false arithmetic premise, obliged: if 2 + 2 = 5, then 4 = 5, subtract 3 and 1 = 2 — so Russell and the Pope are two, hence one.
