Al-Andalus (Arabic: الأندلس) was the Muslim-ruled area of the Iberian Peninsula. The term is used by modern historians for the successive Muslim states in Iberia from the Umayyad conquest of 711 until the fall of the Nasrid Emirate of Granada to the Catholic Monarchs in 1492. At its greatest extent in the eighth century, al-Andalus covered most of the peninsula and part of southern France.

History

Muslim forces under Tariq ibn Ziyad landed in 711 and rapidly overthrew the Visigothic kingdom. After a period as a province of the Umayyad Caliphate, Abd al-Rahman I founded an independent emirate at Córdoba in 756; his descendant Abd al-Rahman III proclaimed the Caliphate of Córdoba in 929, making it one of the wealthiest and most advanced states in Europe. The caliphate collapsed in 1031 into rival taifa kingdoms, which were successively absorbed by the Almoravid and Almohad empires from North Africa. Christian kingdoms expanded southward in the process known as the Reconquista; after the fall of Seville in 1248 only Granada survived, as a tributary emirate, until its surrender in 1492.

Society and culture

Al-Andalus was a multi-religious society of Muslims, Christians, and Jews, famous for scholarship, philosophy, medicine, mathematics, and poetry: figures such as Averroes, Maimonides, and Ibn Arabi are associated with its intellectual world. Córdoba's Great Mosque, the Alhambra of Granada, and the Giralda of Seville are among its enduring monuments. Its legacy persists in Spanish language vocabulary, agriculture, architecture, and music.
