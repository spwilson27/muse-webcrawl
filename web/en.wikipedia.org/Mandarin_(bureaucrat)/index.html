A mandarin (Chinese: 官; pinyin: guān) was a bureaucrat scholar in the government of imperial China, as well as in the Ryukyu Kingdom, and, by adoption of the system, in Korea and Vietnam. The word "mandarin" derives from the Portuguese mandarim, which in turn comes from the Malay menteri ("minister") and Sanskrit mantrin ("minister" or "counsellor"); by association it became linked in European usage with the Chinese word for officials. The standard language spoken by officials — "Mandarin" (Guanhua, "officials' speech") — takes its name from them.

The examination system

From the Sui dynasty onward, and in mature form under the Tang and Song, mandarins were recruited predominantly through the imperial examination system, a rigorous set of tests centred on the Confucian classics and literary composition. Candidates progressed through local, provincial, and metropolitan examinations to earn degrees such as jinshi. The system — which survived, with interruptions and reforms, until its abolition in 1905 — made imperial China in principle one of history's great meritocracies, though in practice success demanded years of costly education, favouring wealthy families.

Ranks and insignia

Officials were graded in nine ranks (jiupin), each with upper and lower grades, divided into civil and military branches. Under the Ming and Qing dynasties, rank was displayed by the buzi, an embroidered rank badge worn on the robe — birds (such as cranes) for civil officials and animals (such as qilin and lions) for military officers — and by the colour of the button on the official hat. A clear glass or crystal button marked the highest rank, a red coral button the first rank, and so on.

Role and legacy

Mandarins staffed the ministries of the central government in the capital and governed provinces, prefectures, and counties across the empire, combining administrative, judicial, and fiscal functions. The county magistrate, the lowest-ranking territorial official, was styled "father-and-mother official" for his paternal authority. The mandarin ideal — the scholar-official (shidafu) devoted to Confucian learning and public service — profoundly shaped Chinese political culture and the bureaucratic traditions of East and Southeast Asia. In modern English, "mandarin" is also used figuratively for powerful civil servants, and for an elite, standardised form of a language.
