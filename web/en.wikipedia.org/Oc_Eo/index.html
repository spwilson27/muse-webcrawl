Óc Eo

Óc Eo (Vietnamese: Óc Eo) is an archaeological site in the Mekong Delta of southern Vietnam, in present-day An Giang Province, near the town of Óc Eo in Thoại Sơn District. It is generally identified as one of the principal trading ports of the ancient kingdom of Funan, flourishing from roughly the 1st to the 7th century CE.

History

Funan was a network of trading states in the lower Mekong region. Óc Eo, located some distance inland from the coast near Mount Ba Thê, was connected to the sea by a system of canals and is thought to have served as an entrepôt linking trade routes between India, China, and the Roman world. Chinese records describe Funan as a wealthy maritime power, and Óc Eo's wealth of imported goods supports this picture.

Excavations

The site was investigated by the French archaeologist Louis Malleret of the École française d'Extrême-Orient beginning in 1942. Excavations revealed foundations of brick structures, canals, workshops for goldsmiths and jewellers, and a remarkable assemblage of artefacts: Roman coins of Marcus Aurelius and Antoninus Pius, Indian seals with Sanskrit inscriptions, Chinese mirrors, jewellery of gold, carnelian, agate, and glass, and local pottery. These finds define the so-called Óc Eo culture of the early Mekong Delta.

Decline

Óc Eo declined along with Funan in the 6th–7th centuries, as maritime trade routes shifted and the successor state of Chenla, centred further inland, rose to prominence. The silting of canals and the shift of trade toward the Malay Peninsula contributed to the port's abandonment.
