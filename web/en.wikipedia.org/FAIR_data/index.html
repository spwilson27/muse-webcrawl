FAIR data

Introduced2016 (Wilkinson et al., Scientific Data)
PrinciplesFindable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable
ScopeResearch data, metadata, and digital objects
RelatedOpen data, data stewardship, research data management

FAIR data are data which meet principles of findability, accessibility, interoperability, and reusability (FAIR). The FAIR principles were published in 2016 by a consortium of scientists and organizations, led by Mark D. Wilkinson, in the journal Scientific Data, under the title "The FAIR Guiding Principles for scientific data management and stewardship". They were designed to guide data producers and publishers in making research outputs machine-actionable, so that humans and computers alike can discover, access, combine, and reuse them.

The principles

Findable: data and metadata are assigned globally unique, persistent identifiers (such as DOIs), described with rich metadata, and registered in searchable resources. Accessible: data can be retrieved by their identifier using standardized, open protocols, with authentication and authorization where necessary; metadata remain accessible even when the data are no longer available. Interoperable: data use formal, broadly applicable languages for knowledge representation, shared vocabularies that themselves follow FAIR principles, and qualified references to other data. Reusable: data are richly described with accurate and relevant attributes, released under a clear usage license, and associated with detailed provenance.

Adoption

The FAIR principles have been endorsed by funders and institutions worldwide, including the European Commission (which made FAIR a requirement under Horizon Europe and the European Open Science Cloud), the U.S. National Institutes of Health, and the G7 and G20. FAIR is often summarized by the phrase "as open as possible, as closed as necessary": FAIR data are not necessarily open data, since accessibility may be restricted for privacy or security reasons while the metadata and access conditions remain transparent.

Implementation

Implementation tools include FAIR maturity indicators, the FAIR Data Point specification, community standards such as the GO FAIR initiative, and metrics frameworks developed by the Research Data Alliance and FAIRsFAIR projects. Related movements, notably the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance, complement FAIR by addressing collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, and ethics.
