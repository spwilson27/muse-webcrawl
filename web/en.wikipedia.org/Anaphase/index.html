Anaphase

Anaphase is a stage of mitosis (and of meiosis) in which replicated chromosomes are split and the newly copied chromosomes (daughter chromatids) are moved to opposite poles of the cell. It follows metaphase, during which chromosomes align at the cell's equator, and precedes telophase, when the two new nuclei form.

Process

Anaphase begins abruptly when the anaphase-promoting complex (APC/C) triggers the breakdown of cohesin, the protein complex holding sister chromatids together, via the enzyme separase. In anaphase A, the kinetochore microtubules of the mitotic spindle shorten, pulling the chromatids poleward. In anaphase B, the spindle poles themselves move apart, elongating the cell, driven by motor proteins acting on polar microtubules. Together the movements ensure each daughter cell receives an identical set of chromosomes.

Regulation

Entry into anaphase is guarded by the spindle assembly checkpoint, which delays APC/C activation until every chromosome is properly attached to the spindle. Premature or erroneous separation can produce aneuploid daughter cells with missing or extra chromosomes.
