Submarine landslide

Submarine landslides are marine landslides that transport sediment across the continental shelf, down the continental slope, and onto the deep ocean floor. They range from small slumps to enormous collapses involving hundreds of cubic kilometres of material, and are a significant mechanism of sediment transport in the oceans, as well as a geohazard.

Causes

Submarine landslides can be triggered by earthquakes, rapid sediment loading, storm-wave pressure, slope oversteepening, gas hydrate dissociation, and changes in pore-fluid pressure. Weak sediment layers on very gentle slopes — sometimes less than one degree — can fail over wide areas.

Tsunamis and hazards

By displacing large volumes of water, submarine landslides can generate tsunamis. The ~8,100-year-old Storegga Slide off Norway produced a tsunami that inundated coastlines around the North Atlantic. The 1929 Grand Banks earthquake triggered a slide and turbidity current that severed transatlantic telegraph cables in sequence and generated a deadly tsunami in Newfoundland. Slides also threaten seabed infrastructure such as pipelines, cables, and offshore platforms.

Deposits

Sliding masses may disintegrate into debris flows and turbidity currents, depositing sediment as turbidites and submarine fans on the abyssal plain.
