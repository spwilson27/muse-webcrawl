The Battle of Leyte Gulf (23–26 October 1944) was the largest naval battle of World War II and, by some criteria, the largest naval battle in history. Fought in the waters around the Philippine island of Leyte, it was the Imperial Japanese Navy's attempt to repel the Allied invasion of Leyte — the first step in the liberation of the Philippines. Instead, the battle effectively destroyed Japan's remaining carrier forces and much of its battle fleet, leaving the Japanese navy unable to mount another major operation.

Background

Under the Japanese Shō-Gō ("Victory") plan, a northern force of carriers under Vice Admiral Jisaburō Ozawa — with very few aircraft left — would lure Admiral Halsey's fast carrier fleet away, while two surface forces converged on the Leyte landing beaches: Vice Admiral Takeo Kurita's powerful Centre Force through the San Bernardino Strait, and a Southern Force through Surigao Strait.

The engagements

The battle comprised several distinct actions. In the Battle of the Sibuyan Sea (24 October), American carrier aircraft sank the super-battleship Musashi. That night, in the Battle of Surigao Strait, Rear Admiral Jesse Oldendorf's battleships and cruisers annihilated the Southern Force in the last battleship-versus-battleship action in history, "crossing the T" of the Japanese column. Meanwhile Ozawa's decoy succeeded: Halsey raced north and crushed the carriers in the Battle off Cape Engaño (25 October), sinking four, including Zuikaku, the last survivor of the Pearl Harbor attack force.

But Kurita's Centre Force had slipped through the unguarded San Bernardino Strait and fell upon a small force of escort carriers and destroyers off Samar. In the Battle off Samar, the vastly outgunned "Taffy 3" fought so fiercely — with destroyers charging battleships — that Kurita, believing he faced a much stronger force, withdrew. The battle also saw the first organized kamikaze attacks, which sank the escort carrier St. Lo.

Outcome

Japan lost four carriers, three battleships, numerous cruisers and destroyers, and some 12,500 men; the United States lost light and escort carriers, destroyers, and about 3,000 men. The Imperial Japanese Navy never again fought as a coordinated fleet, and the Philippines campaign proceeded.
