Cholera

Cholera is an infection of the small intestine by some strains of the bacterium Vibrio cholerae. Symptoms may range from none, to mild, to severe. The classic symptom is large amounts of watery diarrhea that lasts a few days. Vomiting and muscle cramps may also occur. Diarrhea can be so severe that it leads within hours to severe dehydration and electrolyte imbalance, and untreated cholera can be fatal within hours to days.

Cause and transmission

Cholera is caused by ingesting food or water contaminated with Vibrio cholerae, typically from the feces of an infected person. The bacterium produces an enterotoxin that causes intestinal cells to secrete massive amounts of water and electrolytes. Outbreaks occur mainly in places with inadequate water treatment, poor sanitation, and crowding — conditions often created by war, famine and natural disasters.

History

Cholera spread from its endemic home in the Ganges delta of India in seven global pandemics during the 19th and 20th centuries. During the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak in London, physician John Snow mapped cases to a single water pump, founding modern epidemiology. Filippo Pacini identified the bacterium in 1854, and Robert Koch isolated it in 1884. The seventh pandemic, caused by the El Tor biotype, began in 1961 and continues; major recent outbreaks include Zimbabwe (2008), Haiti (2010, introduced after the earthquake) and Yemen (2016 onwards), one of the largest in recorded history.

Treatment and prevention

The mainstay of treatment is rapid rehydration: oral rehydration salts for most patients, intravenous fluids for the severely dehydrated, plus zinc supplements in children and antibiotics in severe cases. With prompt treatment the fatality rate falls below 1%, compared with up to 50% untreated. Prevention rests on clean water, sanitation and hygiene; oral cholera vaccines provide protection for several years and are used in outbreak responses.
