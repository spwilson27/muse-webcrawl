Oblique order

The oblique order is a maneuver in military tactics, in which an attacking army concentrates its forces to attack a single enemy flank while refusing (holding back) the rest of its line, creating a progressively stronger echelon. The maneuver allows a locally superior force to defeat the enemy in detail while the refused flank delays contact with the rest.

History

The earliest famous use was by the Theban general Epaminondas at the Battle of Leuctra in 371 BC, where a phalanx deepened to fifty ranks on the left crushed the Spartan right, and again at Mantinea in 362 BC. Philip II and Alexander of Macedon adapted the idea in their combined-arms armies. In the 18th century, Frederick the Great of Prussia perfected the oblique order, most famously at the Battles of Rossbach and Leuthen in 1757, where his concentrated wing rolled up much larger Austrian and allied forces.

Requirements

Success demands well-drilled troops able to maneuver in echelon, terrain or speed that conceals the weak refused flank, and an enemy that does not react in time.
