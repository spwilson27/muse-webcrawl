How to Lie with Statistics is a book written by Darrell Huff and illustrated by Irving Geis, first published in 1954. It is a short, humorous introduction to the ways statistics can be misused — through biased sampling, misleading averages, distorted graphs and spurious correlation — and is one of the best-selling statistics books of the twentieth century.

Contents

Chapters include "The Sample with the Built-in Bias", "The Well-Chosen Average", "The Gee-Whiz Graph" and "How to Talk Back to a Statistic". Huff shows how a truncated y-axis can make a modest rise look dramatic, how an "average" income can conceal vast inequality depending on whether mean, median or mode is used, and how advertisers and pollsters can reach desired conclusions with small or self-selected samples. The closing chapter gives readers five questions to ask of any statistic: who says so, how do they know, what's missing, did somebody change the subject, and does it make sense?

Reception and influence

The book has remained in print for decades and has sold over a million and a half copies in English alone, with translations into many languages. It is widely assigned in introductory statistics and critical-thinking courses. Statisticians have praised its accessibility while noting that Huff — who later testified for the tobacco industry questioning the link between smoking and cancer — wrote as an advocate of healthy scepticism rather than as a working statistician.
