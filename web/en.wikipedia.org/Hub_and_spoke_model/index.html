Hub and spoke model

Hub and spoke model
TypeNetwork / distribution topology
Used inAirlines, freight, computing, logistics
ContrastPoint-to-point model

The hub and spoke model is a system of connections arranged like a wire wheel, in which traffic moves between outlying points ("spokes") through one or more central interchange points ("hubs"). It is widely used in transport, freight distribution and telecommunications, where it is contrasted with the point-to-point model, in which each destination is connected directly.

Air transportation

Following the deregulation of the United States airline industry in 1978, carriers such as Delta Air Lines (Atlanta) and American Airlines (Dallas/Fort Worth) built networks around hubs, funnelling passengers from many small cities through a hub where they change aircraft. The model lets an airline serve far more city pairs with the same fleet and raises load factors, but lengthens journeys, concentrates delays at hubs and requires carefully timed "banks" of arrivals and departures. Package carriers such as FedEx (Memphis) and UPS (Louisville) apply the same logic to freight.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include economies of scale, fewer routes to operate, and efficient sorting at a central facility. Disadvantages include vulnerability to disruption at the hub, congestion, longer travel times, and additional handling costs. Variants include multi-hub networks and "rolling hubs" with continuous rather than banked schedules.
