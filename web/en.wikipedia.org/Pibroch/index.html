Pibroch

Pibroch, or pìobaireachd (pronounced roughly "pee-broch"), is a form of music of the Great Highland bagpipe. Known in Gaelic as ceòl mòr ("great music"), it is characterised by a slow, stately ground (the ùrlar) followed by a series of increasingly elaborate variations, and is regarded as the classical repertoire of the Highland pipes, in contrast to the lighter ceòl beag of marches, strathspeys, and reels.

A lone piper playing a pibroch on the Great Highland bagpipe.

Structure

A pibroch begins with the ùrlar, the ground or theme, played slowly. Variations follow — typically a singling and doubling of the ground (called siubhal), then the taorluath and crùnluath movements, which ornament the melody with rapid grace-note figures. Many pibrochs conclude by returning to the ground. A full pibroch may last from five to fifteen minutes.

History

The repertoire developed between roughly 1600 and 1850, composed for clan occasions: laments for chiefs, salutes, gatherings, and battle tunes. Great piping families, such as the MacCrimmons of Skye — hereditary pipers to the MacLeods — shaped the tradition. Music was transmitted orally using a sung notation called canntaireachd, in which vocables represent notes and ornaments.

Notation and competition

The first published collection appeared in the early 19th century. Today pibroch is central to solo piping competitions, including events at the Argyllshire Gathering and the Northern Meeting, where gold medals are awarded for pibroch performance.
