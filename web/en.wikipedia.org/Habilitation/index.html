Habilitation


A habilitation defence

Habilitation is the highest university degree or the procedure by which it is achieved, conferring the formal qualification to supervise doctoral research and to teach at university level in a given field. It is required for appointment to full professorships in a number of European countries, notably Germany, Austria, Switzerland, France, Poland, and others. The term derives from the Latin habilis ("fit, suitable") and the medieval conferral of the venia legendi, the right to lecture.

Procedure

A candidate—typically already holding a doctorate—submits a major scholarly work, the Habilitationsschrift, which may be a monograph or a "cumulative habilitation" of published papers, together with evidence of teaching. The work is evaluated by the faculty, and the candidate usually gives a public trial lecture and undergoes an oral disputation. Success grants the title of Privatdozent (in Germany) and the venia legendi for a specific subject.

Variations and reforms

In France, the equivalent qualification is the habilitation à diriger des recherches (HDR), introduced in 1984. Germany introduced the Juniorprofessur in 2002 as an alternative route to a chair that does not require habilitation, reflecting criticism that the traditional system delayed independence and academic security into a scholar's forties. In some countries the habilitation has been abolished or made optional, while in others it remains a near-mandatory step for professorial careers.
