Honey

Honey is a sweet, viscous food substance made by honey bees from the nectar of flowering plants. Bees store it in wax honeycombs inside the hive, where its low water activity and acidity let it keep almost indefinitely; edible honey has been found in ancient Egyptian tombs.

Production

Forager bees collect nectar and carry it in a specialised stomach, where enzymes begin breaking sucrose into glucose and fructose. Back in the hive the nectar is regurgitated, passed between bees, and fanned to evaporate water until the moisture content falls below about 20 percent. A colony must visit millions of flowers to make a kilogram of honey.

Composition and properties

Honey is roughly 38 percent fructose, 31 percent glucose, and 17 percent water, with small amounts of other sugars, enzymes, minerals, and pollen. Its flavour, colour, and tendency to crystallise depend on the floral source — from pale acacia to dark buckwheat honey. Honey has mild antibacterial properties and has been used in wound care since antiquity.

Cuisine and culture

Honey is used as a sweetener, in baking, and fermented into mead. Cave paintings in Spain suggest humans gathered honey at least 8,000 years ago. Because of a risk of infant botulism from Clostridium botulinum spores, honey is not recommended for children under one year old.
