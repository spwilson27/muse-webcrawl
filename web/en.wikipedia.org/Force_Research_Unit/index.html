Force Research Unit

BranchBritish Army (Intelligence Corps)

The Force Research Unit (FRU) was a covert British Army unit that ran agents and informers within paramilitary organizations in Northern Ireland during the Troubles. Operating from the early 1980s (with roots in earlier agent-handling organizations such as the Military Reaction Force), the FRU recruited and handled agents inside both republican groups such as the Provisional IRA and loyalist groups such as the Ulster Defence Association (UDA). The unit was later renamed the Joint Support Group (JSG).

Activities

The FRU's best-known agent was Brian Nelson, a former soldier who became the UDA's senior intelligence officer while reporting to the FRU. Nelson supplied information that, the Army argued, saved many lives by allowing security forces to anticipate attacks; but he also participated in targeting individuals for loyalist assassination, including republicans and uninvolved civilians. Another FRU-linked agent in the IRA, codenamed "Stakeknife" — widely reported to be Freddie Scappaticci, head of the IRA's internal security unit — was the subject of the long-running Operation Kenova investigation.

Controversy and collusion inquiries

The unit became central to allegations of collusion between the security forces and loyalist paramilitaries, particularly after the murder of Belfast solicitor Pat Finucane in 1989 by UDA gunmen. The Stevens Inquiries, led by Sir John Stevens, investigated collusion and concluded that elements of the security forces had colluded in loyalist murders. Stevens's teams arrested FRU personnel, and the unit's commander, Brigadier Gordon Kerr, was investigated though not charged. In 2003 Stevens confirmed that collusion had occurred and that the murders of Finucane and student Adam Lambert could have been prevented.

The de Silva review (2012) into the Finucane case found "shocking" levels of state collusion while concluding there was no overarching state conspiracy to kill him. Former FRU operators who spoke publicly, under pseudonyms such as "Martin Ingram", alleged that handlers allowed agents to commit crimes to protect their cover. The FRU's history remains one of the most contested legacies of British intelligence operations in Northern Ireland.
