Homestead Acts

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Homestead Acts were several laws in the United States by which an applicant could acquire ownership of government land or the public domain, typically called a homestead. In all, more than 160 million acres (650,000 km²) of public land, or nearly 10 percent of the total area of the United States, was given away free to 1.6 million homesteaders; most homesteads were found west of the Mississippi River.

The Homestead Act of 1862, signed by President Abraham Lincoln on 20 May 1862 during the American Civil War, granted an adult citizen, or intended citizen, who had never borne arms against the United States, 160 acres (65 hectares) of surveyed government land, provided the claimant lived on and cultivated the claim for five years ("proving up"), after which title passed for a nominal filing fee. Unusually for the era, the act applied equally to men and women and to immigrants intending to become citizens.

Related legislation

Later statutes adjusted the system. The Southern Homestead Act of 1866 opened public lands in five southern states, intended in part to help freedmen and loyal whites after the Civil War, though it had limited success. The Timber Culture Act of 1873 and Desert Land Act of 1877 addressed tree planting and irrigation in arid regions. The Enlarged Homestead Act of 1909 doubled the allotment to 320 acres in dry western states, and the Stock-Raising Homestead Act of 1916 raised it to 640 acres for ranching land. The acts were repealed in 1976 by the Federal Land Policy and Management Act, with a ten-year extension in Alaska lasting until 1986.

Impact

Homesteading shaped the settlement of the Great Plains and the West, drawing millions of settlers, including large numbers of European immigrants and single women, who made up a notable share of claimants in some states. It also accelerated the dispossession of Native American peoples, since the "public domain" distributed was largely land ceded — often under coercion — by Indigenous nations. Fraud by speculators and railroads was widespread, and many claims failed because 160 acres was too little to farm profitably in arid country west of the 100th meridian. Nevertheless, the homestead principle remains a defining element of American land policy and mythology.
