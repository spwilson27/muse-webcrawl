Delta hedging

Delta hedging is an options strategy that aims to reduce, or hedge, the directional risk associated with price movements in the underlying asset. It involves offsetting long and short positions so that the overall delta — the sensitivity of the portfolio value to small changes in the price of the underlying — is zero, or close to zero.

Method

A trader who has sold a call option, for example, is exposed to losses if the underlying rises. By buying a quantity of the underlying equal to the option's delta multiplied by the number of shares controlled by the option, the trader creates a position whose value is, for small price moves, approximately unchanged. Because delta changes as the underlying price and time change (an effect measured by gamma), the hedge must be rebalanced continually, a practice known as dynamic hedging.

Theory

Delta hedging is central to the Black–Scholes model: by continuously adjusting the hedge, an option can be replicated by a self-financing portfolio of the underlying and a risk-free asset, which leads to the Black–Scholes pricing formula. In practice, discrete rebalancing, transaction costs, jumps in price and volatility changes mean the hedge is imperfect, and traders also monitor higher-order Greeks such as gamma and vega.
