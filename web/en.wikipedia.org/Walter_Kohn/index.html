Walter Kohn

Walter Kohn (March 9, 1923 – April 19, 2016) was an Austrian-American theoretical physicist and theoretical chemist. He was awarded, with John Pople, the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1998. The award recognized their contributions to the understanding of the electronic properties of materials. In particular, Kohn played the leading role in the development of density functional theory, which revolutionized scientists' approach to the electronic structure of atoms, molecules and solid materials.

Early life

Kohn was born in Vienna, Austria, in 1923 into a Jewish family. He escaped Nazi persecution through the Kindertransport to England, and later emigrated to Canada, where he studied at the University of Toronto. Unable to enter the chemistry building during the war because he was a German national, he switched to physics and mathematics, earning his bachelor's degree in 1945. He received his PhD from Harvard University in 1948 under Julian Schwinger.

Career

Kohn taught at Carnegie Mellon University and at the University of California, San Diego, before moving to the University of California, Santa Barbara, in 1979, where he became the founding director of the Institute for Theoretical Physics (now the Kavli Institute for Theoretical Physics).

Density functional theory

With Pierre Hohenberg, Kohn proved the Hohenberg–Kohn theorems (1964), establishing that the ground-state properties of an electron system are uniquely determined by its electron density. With Lu Jeu Sham, he developed the Kohn–Sham equations (1965), which recast the problem in terms of non-interacting particles moving in an effective potential. Density functional theory became one of the most widely used methods in computational chemistry and condensed-matter physics.
