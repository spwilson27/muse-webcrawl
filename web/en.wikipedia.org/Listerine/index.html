Listerine is a brand of antiseptic mouthwash. It was developed in 1879 by Joseph Lawrence, a chemist in St. Louis, Missouri, and named in honour of Joseph Lister, the pioneer of antiseptic surgery. Originally sold as a surgical antiseptic and floor cleaner, it was marketed to dentists from the 1890s and became the first over-the-counter mouthwash sold in the United States in 1914.

Composition

The original formula combines essential oils — menthol, thymol, eucalyptol and methyl salicylate — in an alcohol base (about 21–26% ethanol in the original variant). Alcohol-free versions are also sold. The active ingredients give Listerine its characteristic strong taste and its antiseptic action against oral bacteria.

History and marketing

Listerine was marketed by the Lambert Pharmacal Company and famously advertised against "halitosis" (bad breath) in the 1920s, a campaign credited with greatly expanding the mouthwash market. The brand passed through Warner-Lambert and Pfizer before becoming part of Johnson & Johnson's consumer health business (now Kenvue).

Efficacy and safety

Clinical studies support essential-oil mouthwashes as an adjunct to brushing and flossing for reducing plaque and gingivitis. The alcohol content has prompted debate about dry mouth and a possible link to oral cancer, though reviews by dental bodies have not established a causal association.
