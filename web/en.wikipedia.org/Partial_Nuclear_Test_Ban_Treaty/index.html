The Partial Test Ban Treaty (PTBT), formally the Treaty Banning Nuclear Weapon Tests in the Atmosphere, in Outer Space and Under Water, prohibits all test detonations of nuclear weapons except for those conducted underground. It was signed in Moscow on 5 August 1963 by the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom and the United States, and entered into force on 10 October 1963.

The treaty was driven by public concern over radioactive fallout from atmospheric tests, which had contaminated the environment and entered the food chain, notably after large thermonuclear tests such as Castle Bravo in 1954. Negotiations had begun in the late 1950s, and a moratorium on testing held from 1958 to 1961 before the Soviet Union resumed testing. The Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962 renewed the momentum for an agreement.

Terms

Parties agree to prohibit, prevent and not carry out any nuclear weapon test explosion, or any other nuclear explosion, in the atmosphere, beyond its limits including outer space, or under water, including territorial waters or high seas. Underground tests remain permitted provided they do not cause radioactive debris to be present outside the testing state's territory.

Signatories and non-signatories

More than one hundred states eventually joined the treaty. France and China, both then developing nuclear arsenals, refused to sign and continued atmospheric testing — France until 1974 and China until 1980.

Legacy

The PTBT was an early landmark of Cold War arms control and a step toward the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty of 1968. Its prohibition on above-ground testing was extended to all environments by the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty of 1996, which has been signed but has not entered into force.
