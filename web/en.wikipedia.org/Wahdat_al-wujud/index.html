Waḥdat al-wujūd (Arabic: وحدة الوجود), meaning the "Unity of Existence" or "Unity of Being", is a Sufi philosophy that existence is ultimately identical with God — that is, that there is no true existence except God's existence, and created things exist only as manifestations or self-disclosures (tajallī) of the one Being. The doctrine is most closely associated with the Andalusian mystic and philosopher Ibn Arabi (1165–1240), although the term itself was coined by later commentators on his work rather than by Ibn Arabi personally.

Doctrine

According to the doctrine, "there is no god but God" (lā ilāha illā Allāh) is interpreted to mean that there is nothing in existence except God, and that the world of multiplicity is the theatre of God's self-manifestation through His names and attributes. The relationship of God to the world is compared to that of the sun to its rays, or of a mirror to the images reflected in it: the cosmos is neither identical with God in a crude pantheistic sense, nor wholly separate from Him.

Ibn Arabi's metaphysics, elaborated in works such as the Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam ("The Bezels of Wisdom") and the vast al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya ("The Meccan Revelations"), was systematized by his stepson and foremost disciple Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī and developed by later commentators including ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Kāshānī and ʿAbd al-Karīm al-Jīlī, author of al-Insān al-kāmil ("The Perfect Man").

Criticism and influence

The doctrine was controversial. Critics such as Ibn Taymiyya denounced it as pantheism and a denial of the distinction between Creator and creature, while its defenders argued it safeguarded absolute divine unity (tawḥīd). A later rival formulation, waḥdat al-shuhūd ("unity of witnessing"), was advanced by the Indian Naqshbandi shaykh Aḥmad Sirhindī, who held that the mystic's experience of unity is subjective. Wahdat al-wujud profoundly influenced Sufi thought and poetry across the Ottoman, Persian and Indian worlds, including the works of Rumi, Jami and the Chishti and Qadiri orders.
