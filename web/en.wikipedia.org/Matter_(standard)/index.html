Matter (standard)

In classical physics and general chemistry, matter is any substance that has mass and takes up space by having volume. All everyday objects that can be touched are ultimately composed of atoms, which are made of interacting subatomic particles. In everyday as well as scientific usage, "matter" generally includes atoms and anything made of them, and any particles (or combination of particles) that act as if they have both rest mass and volume. It excludes massless particles such as photons, and other energy phenomena or waves such as light.


Atoms are the building blocks of ordinary matter.

Matter exists in different states (also known as phases) defined by various physical properties, such as state of matter, phase, shape, and density. The atomic theory describes matter as consisting of discrete particles, an idea first proposed by ancient Greek philosophers such as Democritus and developed by scientists including John Dalton in the nineteenth century.

Definition

There is no single universally agreed scientific definition of matter. In ordinary usage, matter is anything with mass and volume. In modern physics, matter is sometimes distinguished from antimatter and from fields: under the standard model of particle physics, matter particles are fermions — quarks and leptons — which combine to form protons, neutrons, and electrons, and hence atoms. Energy, while related to matter through Einstein's relation E = mc2, is not itself usually classed as matter.

States of matter

The most familiar states of matter are solid, liquid, and gas, distinguished by the arrangement and energy of their constituent particles. Solids maintain a fixed shape and volume; liquids maintain volume but take the shape of their container; gases expand to fill their container entirely. At high temperatures, matter becomes a plasma of ionized particles — the most abundant state in the visible universe, found in stars. At extremely low temperatures near absolute zero, matter forms exotic quantum states such as Bose–Einstein condensates, first created in the laboratory in 1995.

Structure

Atoms consist of a dense nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by a cloud of electrons. The number of protons determines the chemical element. Atoms combine into molecules and extended structures through chemical bonds. The observable properties of bulk matter — its colour, hardness, melting point, and conductivity — emerge from these microscopic arrangements, a relationship studied by materials science and condensed matter physics.

Antimatter and dark matter

Antimatter consists of particles with the same mass as ordinary matter particles but opposite charge and quantum numbers; when matter and antimatter meet, they annihilate into energy. The universe contains far more matter than antimatter — an asymmetry that remains an open problem in cosmology. Dark matter, inferred from gravitational effects on galaxies, appears to make up about 27 percent of the universe's mass–energy content, while ordinary matter accounts for only about 5 percent. Its composition is unknown and it is not described by the standard model.

History of the concept

Greek philosophers debated the fundamental substance of the world: Thales proposed water, Heraclitus fire, and Empedocles four elements — earth, air, fire, and water — a scheme adopted by Aristotle. The atomists Democritus and Leucippus held that matter consisted of indivisible particles moving in a void. Alchemists and early chemists replaced these ideas with experimental elemental theory, culminating in Dalton's atomic theory (1803–1808) and Mendeleev's periodic table (1869), which organized the elements by atomic weight and chemical behaviour.
