Observable universe

The observable universe is a ball-shaped region of the universe comprising all matter that can be observed from Earth, because the electromagnetic radiation from those objects has had time to reach Earth since the beginning of cosmic expansion. Its radius is about 46.5 billion light-years, giving a diameter of roughly 93 billion light-years — much larger than the 13.8-billion-year age of the universe multiplied by the speed of light, because space itself has expanded while the light travelled.

Contents

Estimates put the number of galaxies in the observable universe at around 200 billion to 2 trillion. It contains on the order of 1080 atoms, mostly hydrogen and helium. The oldest light observable is the cosmic microwave background, emitted about 380,000 years after the Big Bang; beyond that "surface of last scattering" the early universe was opaque.

Structure and horizons

Observed matter is arranged in a cosmic web of filaments and voids, but on scales above a few hundred million light-years the observable universe appears homogeneous and isotropic, as expected from the cosmological principle. The observable universe is not the whole universe: regions beyond the particle horizon are causally disconnected from us, and the entire universe may be vastly larger or infinite. As expansion accelerates, an event horizon limits the regions that will ever become observable.

Observations

Deep-field images from the Hubble and James Webb telescopes reveal galaxies as they were within a few hundred million years of the Big Bang, pushing observation close to the theoretical edge of the observable volume.
