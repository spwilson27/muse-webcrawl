Stand-up comedy

MediumLive performance; recorded specials
OriginsVaudeville, music hall, minstrelsy (19th–20th c.)
VenuesComedy clubs, theatres, arenas
Notable figuresLenny Bruce, Richard Pryor, George Carlin, Joan Rivers

Stand-up comedy is a form of comedic performance in which a comedian addresses a live audience directly, usually delivering a scripted or semi-scripted monologue of humorous stories, jokes and one-liners, often with a microphone on a bare stage.

History

Stand-up grew out of the monologues of vaudeville and British music hall, and the chalk-talk and minstrel traditions of the 19th century. Masters of ceremonies in burlesque and variety houses gradually became featured acts. In the 1950s and 1960s performers such as Mort Sahl, Lenny Bruce and Joan Rivers reshaped the form into topical, personal and confrontational commentary, paving the way for the comedy-club boom of the 1970s and 1980s led by Richard Pryor, George Carlin, Steve Martin and Robin Williams.

Form and technique

A stand-up set is built from "bits" — short routines with set-ups and punchlines — honed through repeated performance at open-mic nights and club spots. Performers develop a stage persona, work on timing and delivery, handle hecklers, and use callbacks to earlier jokes. "Bombing" (failing to get laughs) and "killing" (a triumphant set) are the form's poles.

Modern era

Television specials, the Edinburgh Festival Fringe, and later streaming platforms turned leading stand-ups into arena acts, while podcasts and social media clips created new routes to audiences bypassing traditional clubs and television gatekeepers. The form is now practised worldwide in many languages.
