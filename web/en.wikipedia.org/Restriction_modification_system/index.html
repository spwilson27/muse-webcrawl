Restriction modification system

A restriction modification system is a bacterial and archaeal defence mechanism that distinguishes the cell's own DNA from foreign DNA. It pairs a restriction endonuclease, which cuts DNA at or near specific sequences, with a methyltransferase that modifies the same sequences in the host genome and protects them from cleavage.

Mechanism

When bacteriophage DNA enters a cell, its recognition sites are usually unmethylated and can be cut by the restriction enzyme. Host DNA acquires the protective methyl pattern as it is replicated. Newly synthesised strands are briefly hemimethylated but are generally recognised as self and methylated fully before restriction occurs.

Types

Type I systems are large multisubunit complexes that recognise asymmetric sequences, translocate DNA, and cut at variable distances. Type II systems usually have separate restriction and modification enzymes and cut at precise positions within or near palindromic recognition sites; these became standard tools in molecular cloning. Type III systems require two inversely oriented sites and cut a short distance away. Type IV systems differ by targeting DNA that is already modified.

Biological roles

Restriction modification limits horizontal gene transfer and parasitism by phages and plasmids, but successful invaders may evolve mimics of host methylation, anti-restriction proteins, or loss of recognition sites. The systems can behave as selfish genetic elements because a cell that loses the modification activity before the longer-lived restriction enzyme risks lethal cutting of its own chromosome.

Applications

Type II restriction enzymes enabled recombinant DNA technology by allowing predictable cutting and joining of DNA fragments. Their specificity underlies cloning, mapping, genotyping, and many diagnostic assays.
