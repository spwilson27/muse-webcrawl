Song cycle


Franz Schubert, whose Winterreise is among the most famous song cycles

A song cycle (German: Liederkreis or Liederzyklus) is a group, or cycle, of individually complete songs designed to be performed in sequence as a unit. The songs are either for solo voice or a group, and are usually accompanied by piano, though sometimes by an ensemble or orchestra. The songs may be linked by a common narrative, theme, poet, or musical device such as recurring motifs or a unifying key scheme.

History

The genre flourished in the German-speaking world during the Romantic era. Ludwig van Beethoven's An die ferne Geliebte (1816) is generally considered the first true song cycle. Franz Schubert composed two of the most celebrated examples: Die schöne Müllerin (1823) and Winterreise (1827), both settings of poems by Wilhelm Müller.

Notable examples

Robert Schumann's Dichterliebe and Frauen-Liebe und Leben (both 1840), Johannes Brahms's Die schöne Magelone, Hugo Wolf's Mörike and Goethe settings, and Gustav Mahler's Das Lied von der Erde are landmarks of the genre. In England, major cycles include Ralph Vaughan Williams's Songs of Travel and Benjamin Britten's Les Illuminations and Winter Words. Later composers such as Samuel Barber (Hermit Songs) extended the tradition.

Characteristics

Although each song in a cycle is a complete musical statement, the cycle as a whole is designed to be greater than the sum of its parts, with connections of text, narrative, and musical structure giving it coherence across the set.
