Central bank

A central bank, reserve bank, or monetary authority is an institution that manages the currency and monetary policy of a country or monetary union. In contrast to a commercial bank, a central bank possesses a monopoly on increasing the monetary base. Many central banks also have supervisory and regulatory powers to ensure the stability of member institutions, to prevent bank runs, and in some cases also to enforce policies on financial consumer protection and against bank fraud, money laundering, or terrorism financing. Central banks in most developed nations are institutionally independent from political interference, even though governments typically have governance rights and central banks conduct policy within mandates set by legislatures.

Functions

The primary function of a central bank is monetary policy: controlling the money supply and short-term interest rates to achieve objectives such as price stability (low, stable inflation), full employment and stable growth. Tools include setting the policy (discount) rate, open market operations in government securities, reserve requirements, and, since the 2008 financial crisis, quantitative easing — large-scale asset purchases. Central banks issue banknotes and coin (often through a state mint), act as banker to the government and to commercial banks, manage foreign-exchange and gold reserves, and operate or oversee payment systems.

Lender of last resort

Articulated by Walter Bagehot in Lombard Street (1873), the doctrine holds that in a panic a central bank should lend freely, at a penalty rate, against good collateral, to solvent but illiquid institutions. This function underpins deposit insurance and modern crisis management.

History

The Swedish Sveriges Riksbank (founded 1668) and the Bank of England (1694) are the oldest surviving central banks. The Bank of England developed, during the 19th century, into the model of a modern central bank, acquiring a de facto monopoly of note issue. The United States created the Federal Reserve System in 1913 after recurring banking panics. Many countries established central banks in the 20th century, often at or after independence. Since the 1990s, operational independence combined with inflation targeting — pioneered by the Reserve Bank of New Zealand in 1990 — has become the dominant institutional model. The European Central Bank (1998) conducts policy for the euro area as a supranational central bank.

Independence

Empirical studies associate central-bank independence with lower average inflation without obvious cost to growth, though independence is debated: critics argue unelected technocrats wield great power, and after 2008 the expansion of central-bank mandates into financial stability and asset purchases raised questions of accountability and distributional effects.
