Sir Nicholas Harold Lloyd Ridley (10 July 1906 – 25 May 2001) was a British ophthalmologist who invented the intraocular lens (IOL) and pioneered intraocular lens surgery for patients with cataracts, one of the most common and consequential operations in modern medicine. His insight grew out of wartime observations of Royal Air Force pilots whose eyes tolerated fragments of acrylic canopy plastic without inflammation, leading him to implant the first artificial lens in 1949.

Early life and education

Ridley was born in Kibworth Harcourt, Leicestershire, the son of Nicholas Charles Ridley, an ophthalmologist, and Margaret (née Parker). He was educated at Charterhouse School and Pembroke College, Cambridge, before qualifying in medicine at St Thomas' Hospital, London, in 1930. He trained in ophthalmology at Moorfields Eye Hospital and St Thomas'.

The wartime observation

During the Second World War, Ridley treated injured aircrew, including Gordon "Mouse" Cleaver, a Hurricane pilot shot down in August 1940 whose eyes were penetrated by fragments of the aircraft's Perspex (poly(methyl methacrylate), PMMA) canopy. Ridley noted that the acrylic splinters produced no significant inflammatory reaction in the eye — unlike glass or metal — an observation he remembered after the war. A medical student, Steve Parry, is also reported to have suggested to Ridley that an artificial lens might be implanted to replace a cataractous one, an idea Ridley developed into practice.

The first intraocular lens

Working with John Pike of Rayner and the plastics division of Imperial Chemical Industries, Ridley had a lens manufactured from Perspex CQ, a clinical-grade PMMA. On 29 November 1949, at St Thomas' Hospital, he inserted the first artificial lens into the eye of a 45-year-old woman following extracapsular cataract extraction; the permanent implant was completed on 8 February 1950. The lens, a biconvex disc mimicking the shape and refractive power of the natural crystalline lens, restored useful vision.

Opposition and vindication

Ridley's innovation met fierce professional hostility: critics feared infection, dislocation and unforeseen complications, and he faced years of ostracism, including from leading figures such as Sir Stewart Duke-Elder. Nevertheless, he persisted, implanting thousands of lenses and refining the technique; by the 1970s improved designs, sterilisation and microsurgery had made lens implantation the standard treatment for cataract worldwide. Today tens of millions of IOLs are implanted annually.

Later career and honours

Ridley practised in London and wrote on tropical ophthalmology, co-founding the International Intra-Ocular Implant Club in 1966 to promote the technique. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1986 and was knighted in the 2000 New Year Honours for his pioneering work. The Ridley Foundation and the Harold Ridley Foundation support ophthalmic research and training. He retired to Stapleford, near Salisbury, Wiltshire, where he died in 2001.
