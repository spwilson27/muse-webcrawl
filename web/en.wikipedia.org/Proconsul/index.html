Proconsul

A proconsul was an official of ancient Rome who acted on behalf of a consul (pro consule). Under the Roman Republic, a proconsul was a consul whose imperium had been extended (prorogued) beyond his term, usually so that he could govern a province or command an army; later the title was given directly to provincial governors who held consular rank.

Republic

As Rome's overseas territories grew, elections could not supply enough magistrates, so consuls and praetors had their commands extended. Proconsuls held essentially the same imperium as consuls within their provinces — commanding armies, administering justice, and supervising taxation — but were barred from exercising power within Italy itself. Famous proconsuls include Julius Caesar in Gaul and Pompey in the East.

Empire

Under Augustus, provinces were divided between imperial provinces, governed by the emperor's legates, and senatorial provinces, governed by proconsuls chosen by lot from ex-consuls and ex-praetors. The proconsuls of Asia and Africa were the most prestigious senatorial posts. The title survived in later usage for various viceregal and colonial offices, and "proconsul" is sometimes used metaphorically for powerful regional administrators.
