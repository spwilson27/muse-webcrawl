Interoperability

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Interoperability is a characteristic of a product or system to work with other products or systems. In computing and telecommunications, interoperability describes the ability of different information systems, devices and applications to access, exchange, integrate and cooperatively use data in a coordinated manner, within and across organisational boundaries. The term is also used in defence, healthcare, government, software engineering and many other fields where diverse systems must function together.

Types

Interoperability is often described in layers:

  • Technical interoperability — the hardware and software components, protocols and infrastructures that allow machines to communicate, such as network protocols and data formats.
  • Syntactic interoperability — the structure and format of exchanged data, enabling systems to parse information, for example through shared schemas such as XML or JSON.
  • Semantic interoperability — the ability of systems to interpret exchanged information unambiguously, using shared vocabularies, ontologies and terminologies.
  • Organisational interoperability — the alignment of business processes, responsibilities and expectations that lets organisations work together effectively.

Standards

Interoperability is usually achieved through adherence to open standards developed by standards organisations such as the ISO, IEC, IEEE, W3C and IETF. Widely adopted standards — including TCP/IP, HTTP, HTML and Unicode — underpin the interoperability of the Internet and the Web. In healthcare, standards such as HL7 and FHIR support the exchange of clinical data between hospitals, laboratories and insurers.

Software interoperability

In software, interoperability is enabled by application programming interfaces (APIs), common file formats and middleware. Operating systems and platforms may support interoperability through compatibility layers or virtualisation. Conversely, proprietary formats and undocumented protocols can produce "vendor lock-in", in which users cannot easily move data between competing products. Competition policy in the European Union and elsewhere has at times mandated interoperability information, notably in cases involving dominant software platforms.

Semantic interoperability

Semantic interoperability goes beyond exchanging data to ensuring the meaning of the data is preserved and understood. It relies on shared reference models and terminologies; in medicine, for example, SNOMED CT and ICD classifications allow records created in one system to be interpreted correctly in another. The semantic web programme proposed machine-readable metadata and ontologies, expressed in RDF and OWL, to achieve similar goals on the Web.
