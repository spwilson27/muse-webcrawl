The Charter of the French Language (French: Charte de la langue française), commonly known as Bill 101, is a law of the province of Quebec, Canada, enacted in 1977 by the Parti Québécois government of René Lévesque. It defines French as the official language of Quebec and makes it the normal language of work, instruction, communications, commerce and business in the province.

Provisions

The Charter requires that commercial signage be predominantly in French, that most children of immigrants attend French-language schools, and that businesses with a set number of employees operate internally in French ("francization"). It established the Office québécois de la langue française to oversee implementation. Amendments include Bill 178 (1988), Bill 86 (1993) and the major strengthening Bill 96 (2022), which extended French requirements to smaller businesses and federal undertakings.

Legal challenges and impact

Several provisions have been tested in court; in Ford v Quebec (AG) (1988) the Supreme Court of Canada struck down a French-only sign rule, prompting Quebec to invoke the notwithstanding clause. The law is credited with reversing the assimilation of francophones and integrating newcomers into French-speaking society, though it remains debated by the anglophone minority and business groups.
