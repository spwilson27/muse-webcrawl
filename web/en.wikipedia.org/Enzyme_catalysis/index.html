Enzyme catalysis

Enzyme catalysis is the increase in the rate of a process by a biological molecule, an "enzyme". Most enzymes are proteins, and most such processes are chemical reactions. Within the enzyme, generally catalysis occurs at a localised site, called the active site.

Most enzymes are made predominantly of proteins, either a single protein chain or many such chains in a multi-subunit complex. Enzymes often also incorporate non-protein components, such as metal ions or specialised organic molecules known as cofactors (e.g. adenosine triphosphate). Many cofactors are vitamins, and their role as vitamins is linked directly to their use in the catalysis of biological processes within metabolism. Catalysis of biochemical reactions in the cell is vital since many but not all metabolically essential reactions have very low rates when uncatalysed.

Mechanisms

Enzymes accelerate reactions — often by factors of millions or more — principally by binding the transition state more tightly than the substrate, thereby lowering the activation energy. Contributing strategies include:

  • Proximity and orientation: bringing reactants together in the correct alignment;
  • Acid–base catalysis: amino-acid side chains donate or accept protons;
  • Covalent catalysis: a transient covalent intermediate forms (as in the serine proteases);
  • Metal ion catalysis: bound metals stabilise charges or generate nucleophiles;
  • Strain and induced fit: substrate binding reshapes the enzyme (Koshland's induced-fit model, succeeding Fischer's lock-and-key picture).

Kinetics

Enzyme rates are classically described by Michaelis–Menten kinetics, characterised by the maximum rate Vmax and the Michaelis constant Km. The catalytic efficiency kcat/Km of the fastest enzymes approaches the diffusion limit (~108–109 M−1s−1) — "catalytic perfection".

Regulation and inhibition

Cells regulate enzymes through allosteric effectors, covalent modification such as phosphorylation, and feedback inhibition. Competitive, non-competitive and uncompetitive inhibitors are the basis of many drugs, from statins to HIV protease inhibitors.
