Ultramarine is a deep blue colour pigment which was originally made by grinding lapis lazuli into a powder. The name comes from the Latin ultramarinus, literally "beyond the sea", because the pigment was imported to Europe from mines in Afghanistan by way of Venice.

History

Lapis lazuli was mined in the Sar-e Sang valley of Badakhshan, Afghanistan, for more than 6,000 years. The extraction of pure blue pigment from the rock was developed by the 11th–14th centuries and was laborious, making natural ultramarine more expensive than gold. In Renaissance painting it was reserved for the most important elements of a picture, above all the robes of the Virgin Mary, and contracts sometimes specified its use. Johannes Vermeer used it extensively.

Synthetic ultramarine

In 1824 a French society offered a prize for a synthetic process; Jean-Baptiste Guimet produced artificial ultramarine in 1826, and Christian Gmelin published a similar process in 1828. Synthetic ultramarine, chemically identical in essence to the natural pigment, is cheap and widely used in paints, plastics, inks, cosmetics and as a laundry bluing agent to counteract yellowing of white fabrics.

Chemistry

The pigment is a sulfur-containing sodium aluminium silicate with a sodalite cage structure; its blue colour arises from the trisulfur radical anion (S3−) trapped within the crystal lattice.
