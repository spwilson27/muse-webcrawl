Unit cell

In geometry, biology, mineralogy and solid state physics, a unit cell is a repeating unit that, when stacked together without rotation or reflection and with no gaps, fills the entire space of a crystal lattice or other periodic structure. In three dimensions the unit cell is a parallelepiped defined by three edge vectors of lengths a, b and c and the angles α, β and γ between them; these six quantities are the lattice parameters of the crystal.

Primitive and conventional cells

A primitive cell contains lattice points only at its corners and has the smallest possible volume — one lattice point per cell. Conventional cells are chosen to display the symmetry of the lattice and may contain additional lattice points on faces or in the interior, as in the body-centred and face-centred cubic cells. The Wigner–Seitz cell is a primitive cell constructed from the perpendicular bisectors of the vectors to neighbouring lattice points; in reciprocal space the corresponding construction gives the first Brillouin zone.

Crystal systems

Unit cells are classified into seven crystal systems by the constraints on their edge lengths and angles: cubic (a = b = c, all angles 90°), tetragonal, orthorhombic, hexagonal, trigonal (rhombohedral), monoclinic and triclinic. Combining these with the possible centreings yields the 14 Bravais lattices. The arrangement of atoms within the cell — the basis — together with the lattice determines the full crystal structure, conventionally reported with fractional coordinates and a space group symbol.

Determination

Unit cell dimensions are measured by X-ray, neutron or electron diffraction: the positions of diffraction maxima fix the reciprocal lattice, and hence the real-space cell, while intensities reveal the atomic contents. Cell parameters are fundamental data in crystallographic databases and materials identification.
