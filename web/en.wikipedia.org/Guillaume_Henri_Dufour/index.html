Guillaume Henri Dufour

Guillaume Henri Dufour (15 September 1787 – 14 July 1875) was a Swiss army officer, engineer, cartographer and politician. He served as general of the Swiss federal army on four occasions, most famously commanding the federal forces in the Sonderbund War of 1847, which he won quickly and with notable restraint, helping to lay the ground for the modern Swiss federal state. He directed the creation of the first complete topographic map of Switzerland, presided over the First Geneva Convention of 1864, and was a co-founder and first president of the International Committee of the Red Cross.

Military career

Born to Genevan parents in exile, Dufour was educated at the École Polytechnique in Paris and served as an engineer officer under Napoleon before returning to Geneva. He taught mathematics and fortification, designed fortifications and bridges (including early suspension bridges in Geneva), and became Switzerland's quartermaster general, overseeing the federal topographic survey from 1832. Appointed general in 1847, he defeated the separatist Sonderbund cantons in a campaign of less than a month with minimal bloodshed, ordering humane treatment of the defeated.

Cartography and humanitarian work

The Topographische Karte der Schweiz (Dufour map), published at 1:100,000 between 1845 and 1865, set new standards for relief shading and accuracy. In 1863 Dufour joined Henry Dunant and others in the Committee of Five that founded the Red Cross, and he chaired the 1864 diplomatic conference that produced the First Geneva Convention. The highest peak in Switzerland, Dufourspitze in the Monte Rosa massif, is named after him.
