Tibetan Plateau

Tibetan Plateau
Area: about 2.5 million km²
Average elevation: over 4,500 m
Nickname: "Roof of the World"; "Third Pole"
Surrounding ranges: Himalayas (S), Kunlun (N), Karakoram (W), Hengduan (E)

The Tibetan Plateau is a vast elevated plateau in Central and East Asia, covering most of the Tibet Autonomous Region, Qinghai, and parts of neighbouring provinces of China, as well as Ladakh in India. It is the world's highest and largest plateau, with an area of about 2.5 million square kilometres and an average elevation exceeding 4,500 metres — hence its nickname, the "Roof of the World". Formed by the ongoing collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates that began about 50 million years ago, the plateau's thick crust (up to 70–80 km) continues to be uplifted and deformed today.

Geography and climate

The plateau is bounded by the Himalayas to the south, the Kunlun Mountains to the north, the Karakoram to the west, and the Hengduan Mountains to the east. Its interior contains vast high plains (such as the Changtang), brackish lakes including Namtso and Siling Lake, and the Qiangtang nature reserve, home to chiru (Tibetan antelope), wild yak, and kiang. The climate is cold and dry, with steppe and alpine desert vegetation, intense solar radiation, and large daily temperature swings.

The "Third Pole" and Asia's rivers

The Tibetan Plateau and surrounding ranges hold the largest store of ice outside the polar regions — some 100,000 km² of glaciers — earning it the name "Third Pole". It is the source of many of Asia's great rivers, including the Yangtze, Yellow River, Mekong, Salween, Brahmaputra, Indus, Ganges, and Tarim, on which well over a billion people depend. Glaciers on the plateau have been retreating in recent decades, and warming there is proceeding faster than the global average, with consequences for permafrost, lakes, and downstream water supply.

People and ecology

The plateau has been inhabited for tens of thousands of years; Tibetans and other high-altitude populations show genetic adaptations to low oxygen, including variants in the EPAS1 gene inherited from Denisovan ancestors. Traditional livelihoods centre on yak and sheep pastoralism and barley farming in the valleys. The plateau's uplift strongly influences the Asian monsoon and global atmospheric circulation.
