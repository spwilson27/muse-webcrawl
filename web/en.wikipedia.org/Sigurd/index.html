Sigurd (Old Norse: Sigurðr), known in German tradition as Siegfried, is a legendary hero of Norse and Germanic mythology, the central figure of the Völsung cycle. He is famed as the slayer of the dragon Fafnir, whose blood gave him the ability to understand the speech of birds and whose heart he roasted and tasted. His story — of heroism, betrayal, and an early death — is among the most influential legends of medieval Europe.

Legend

Sigurd was the posthumous son of Sigmund, a hero of the Völsung line, and was raised at the court of King Hjalprek, where the dwarf-smith Regin forged for him the sword Gram from the shards of his father's blade. Urged on by Regin, Sigurd killed Fafnir, Regin's brother, who had turned into a dragon to guard a cursed hoard of gold. Warned by the birds he could now understand, Sigurd killed the treacherous Regin as well.

He then rode through a ring of fire to awaken the valkyrie Brynhild, to whom he pledged himself. Later, bewitched by a potion of forgetfulness at the court of the Burgundian Gjúkungs, he married their sister Gudrun and, disguised as his brother-in-law Gunnar, won Brynhild for him. When the deception was revealed, Brynhild engineered Sigurd's murder — in the Norse version he is slain in his bed by Gunnar's brother Guttorm.

Legacy

The legend is preserved in the Icelandic Völsunga saga and the poems of the Poetic Edda, and in Germany in the Nibelungenlied (c. 1200). Richard Wagner drew on both traditions for Siegfried and Götterdämmerung in the Ring cycle. Carvings of the Sigurd story appear on Scandinavian runestones and on church doors in Norway and the Isle of Man. See also Fafnir, Brynhild, and Nibelungenlied.
