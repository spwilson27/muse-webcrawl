United States occupation of Nicaragua

The United States occupation of Nicaragua from 1912 to 1933 was part of the Banana Wars, a series of U.S. military interventions in Latin America conducted to protect American commercial interests. U.S. Marines landed in Nicaragua in 1912 and remained, in varying numbers, for over two decades, shaping the country's politics and laying the groundwork for the later Somoza dynasty.

U.S. Marines served in Nicaragua for most of the period from 1912 to 1933.

Background

American intervention followed instability after the fall of President José Santos Zelaya in 1909 and reflected "dollar diplomacy": Washington supported Conservative governments willing to protect U.S. banking and fruit interests and to exclude European influence near a possible canal route. Marines landed in 1912 to suppress a Liberal revolt, and the Bryan–Chamorro Treaty of 1916 granted the United States canal rights in Nicaragua.

Guerrilla resistance

In 1927, after Marines returned during the Constitutionalist War, Liberal general Augusto César Sandino refused to accept a U.S.-brokered settlement and waged a guerrilla campaign from the northern mountains. The Marines organized and trained the Nicaraguan National Guard as a supposedly nonpartisan constabulary. Sandino's resistance made him a lasting symbol of anti-imperialism in Latin America.

Withdrawal and legacy

Under President Franklin D. Roosevelt's Good Neighbor policy, the last Marines departed on January 2, 1933. The National Guard, commanded by Anastasio Somoza García, became the dominant force in the country; Sandino was murdered by guardsmen in 1934 after signing a peace agreement, and Somoza seized the presidency in 1937, beginning a family dictatorship that lasted until the Sandinista revolution of 1979.
