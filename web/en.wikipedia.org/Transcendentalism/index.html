Transcendentalism

Walden Pond, site of Thoreau's cabin

Transcendentalism is a philosophical, spiritual and literary movement that developed in the late 1820s and 1830s in the New England region of the United States. A core belief is in the inherent goodness of people and nature, and that society and its institutions have corrupted the purity of the individual. Transcendentalists believe that individuals are at their best when truly self-reliant and independent, and that divine experience is available in everyday life rather than through distant institutions.

Origins and beliefs

The movement grew out of liberal Unitarianism and was shaped by German idealist philosophy (especially Kant and, through Coleridge and Carlyle, Schelling), the writings of Emanuel Swedenborg, and translations of Hindu and other Asian scriptures. Its informal nucleus was the Transcendental Club, founded in Boston in 1836 and sometimes called the Hedge Club, whose members included Ralph Waldo Emerson, Margaret Fuller, Bronson Alcott, George Ripley and Henry David Thoreau. Emerson's essay Nature (1836) served as a founding statement, followed by his addresses "The American Scholar" (1837) and the "Divinity School Address" (1838). The journal The Dial (1840–1844), edited by Fuller and Emerson, carried the movement's essays, poems and reviews; the utopian community at Brook Farm (1841–1847) attempted to put its ideals into practice.

Major figures and legacy

Emerson's essays on self-reliance, the Over-Soul and the correspondence between mind and nature, Thoreau's Walden (1854) and "Resistance to Civil Government" (1849), Fuller's Woman in the Nineteenth Century (1845) and Whitman's Leaves of Grass (1855) are the movement's enduring monuments. Transcendentalism influenced American literature, abolitionism, feminism and later environmental thought, and its emphases on intuition, individual conscience and the divinity of nature remain influential in American culture.
