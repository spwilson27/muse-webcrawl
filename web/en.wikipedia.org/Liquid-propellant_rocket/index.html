Liquid-propellant rocket

A liquid-propellant rocket or liquid rocket utilizes a rocket engine that uses liquid propellants. Liquids are desirable because they have a reasonably high density and high specific impulse (Isp), allowing the volume of the propellant tanks to be relatively low. It is also possible to throttle, shut down, and restart liquid-propellant engines, which is difficult or impossible with solid rockets.

History

The first liquid-propellant rocket was launched by Robert H. Goddard on 16 March 1926 in Auburn, Massachusetts, using liquid oxygen and gasoline. During the Second World War, Germany deployed the V-2 rocket, powered by liquid oxygen and ethanol, under Wernher von Braun. After the war, liquid rockets powered the space race: the Soviet R-7 and American Atlas, Titan and Saturn families all used liquid propellants.

Propellants and engine cycles

Common propellant combinations include liquid oxygen with kerosene (RP-1) or liquid hydrogen, hypergolic pairs such as nitrogen tetroxide and hydrazine derivatives that ignite on contact, and methane/liquid oxygen combinations pursued for reusability. Propellants are fed to the thrust chamber by pressure-fed systems or turbopump cycles including gas-generator, staged combustion and expander cycles. Cooling is often regenerative, circulating fuel through channels in the chamber walls.
