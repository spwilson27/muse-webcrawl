Blood plasma is a light amber-coloured liquid component of blood in which blood cells are suspended. It makes up about 55% of the body's total blood volume. It is the intravascular part of extracellular fluid (all body fluid outside cells). It is mostly water (up to 95% by volume), and contains important dissolved proteins (6–8%) — serum albumins, globulins, and fibrinogen — glucose, clotting factors, electrolytes (Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+, HCO3−, Cl−, etc.), hormones, carbon dioxide (plasma being the main medium for excretory product transportation), and oxygen.

Function

Plasma plays a vital role in maintaining blood pressure and volume, supplying critical proteins for blood clotting and immunity, and serving as the medium for exchange of vital minerals. It also helps maintain proper pH balance in the body and transports nutrients, waste products, and cells.

Plasma and serum

Serum is blood plasma without clotting factors; plasma, by contrast, retains fibrinogen and other coagulation proteins. Plasma separation is done by spinning a tube of fresh blood containing an anticoagulant in a centrifuge until the blood cells fall to the bottom.

Clinical use

Donated plasma is separated from whole blood or collected by plasmapheresis and frozen as fresh frozen plasma (FFP) for transfusion to patients with clotting disorders, liver disease, or massive haemorrhage. Plasma is also fractionated into products such as albumin, immunoglobulins, and clotting factor concentrates. The large-scale plasma-for-medicine programme of the Second World War, associated with Charles R. Drew, pioneered modern blood banking. Convalescent plasma has been used experimentally during epidemics of infectious disease.
