Print capitalism

Coined byBenedict Anderson
Key workImagined Communities (1983)
FieldNationalism studies; media history

Print capitalism is a concept developed by the political scientist Benedict Anderson in his book Imagined Communities (1983) to describe the convergence of capitalism and printing technology that, in his account, made modern national consciousness possible. As publishers sought ever larger markets, printed materials were produced in vernacular languages rather than Latin, creating unified fields of communication below the level of the old sacred languages and above the level of local spoken dialects.

Mechanisms

Anderson identified three effects of print capitalism: it fixed languages in stable printed forms, lending them a new permanence; it created "print-languages" that standardised dialects and enabled strangers to imagine themselves part of one reading public; and it elevated some vernaculars into languages of administration and power. The daily newspaper and the novel were emblematic forms, letting readers picture thousands of unknown others performing the same act of reading simultaneously — the essence of the nation as an "imagined community."

Legacy

The concept has been widely adopted, debated, and extended in nationalism studies, book history, and media studies, including to later media such as radio, television, and the internet.
