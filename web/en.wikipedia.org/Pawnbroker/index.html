Pawnbroker

A pawnbroker is an individual or business (a pawnshop or pawn shop) that offers secured loans to people, with items of personal property used as collateral. The items pawned are called pledges or pawns. If the borrower repays the loan plus interest within an agreed period, the item is returned; if the loan defaults, the pawnbroker may sell the pledged item to recover the money. Pawnbrokers also typically buy and sell second-hand goods outright.

How pawn lending works

A customer brings in an item—commonly jewellery, musical instruments, electronics, tools, or firearms—and the pawnbroker appraises its resale value, offering a loan of a fraction of that value. The agreement is recorded on a pawn ticket stating the amount, interest and fees, and the redemption deadline, often one to four months with possible extensions. Because the loan is fully collateralised, no credit check is required, and defaulting does not harm the borrower's credit record; the borrower simply forfeits the item. In practice, a majority of pawn loans are redeemed.

History

Pawnbroking is among the oldest forms of lending, attested in ancient Greece and Rome and in China for over two thousand years. In medieval Europe, where Christian prohibitions on usury constrained lending, pawnbroking was practised by Jewish moneylenders, the Lombards of northern Italy, and the charitable monts-de-piété founded by Franciscan friars in the 15th century to offer low-interest loans to the poor. The trade's enduring symbol—three golden balls suspended from a bar—is traditionally linked to the Medici family of Florence and to the legend of St. Nicholas, said to have given three bags of gold as dowries. In Qing and Republican China, pawnshops were a pillar of local credit; their distinctive tall counters and the character 當 (dang) sign remain cultural symbols in Hong Kong and Taiwan.

Regulation

Pawnbroking is licensed and regulated in most jurisdictions. Laws typically cap interest rates, require records of transactions and identification of sellers (to deter trade in stolen goods), mandate holding periods during which police can check pledged items against theft reports, and require unredeemed goods to be sold by auction or retail. In the United States pawnshops are regulated at state level and are also subject to federal laws covering firearms, money laundering, and consumer credit disclosures.
