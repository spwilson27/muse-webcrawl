Repossession

Repossession is a legal process in which a lender or creditor retakes possession of property that was used as collateral for a loan, after the borrower defaults — typically by missing payments. The term most often applies to motor vehicles financed by auto loans, but repossession can involve any tangible collateral, such as boats, equipment, or household goods bought on credit. For real property, the analogous process is foreclosure.

Vehicle repossession is commonly carried out by recovery agents using tow trucks.

United States

Under Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code, a secured creditor may repossess collateral after default without a court order, provided it can do so without breaching the peace — meaning, broadly, without force, threats, or entering a closed garage or home. The creditor may then sell the collateral in a commercially reasonable manner and apply the proceeds to the debt. If the sale does not cover the balance, the borrower may owe a deficiency judgment; if it exceeds the debt and costs, the surplus goes to the borrower. State laws vary: some require notice and a right to cure the default before repossession, and special protections apply to active-duty servicemembers under the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act. Repossession is typically reported to credit bureaus and can seriously damage a borrower's credit history.

Other jurisdictions

In the United Kingdom, repossession of vehicles and goods is governed by the Consumer Credit Act 1974 and related law; goods bought on hire purchase generally cannot be repossessed without a court order once the borrower has paid one-third of the total price. Mortgage repossession (possession proceedings) involves the courts and is regulated to ensure borrowers are treated fairly.
