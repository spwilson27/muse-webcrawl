Clear and present danger

Clear and present danger was a doctrine adopted by the Supreme Court of the United States to determine under which circumstances the First Amendment free speech protections could be limited. The phrase was introduced by Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. in Schenck v. United States (1919): the question was "whether the words used are used in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger that they will bring about the substantive evils that Congress has a right to prevent."

History

In Schenck, the Court upheld the conviction of a socialist who distributed leaflets urging resistance to the wartime draft; Holmes's opinion also produced the famous analogy that the First Amendment does not protect "falsely shouting fire in a theatre". Holmes soon adopted a more speech-protective reading, dissenting in Abrams v. United States (1919), and later courts, particularly in the McCarthy-era cases such as Dennis v. United States (1951), qualified the test in ways critics said weakened it.

Replacement

In Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), the Court replaced the doctrine with the stricter imminent lawless action test: advocacy of force or lawbreaking may be punished only where it is directed to inciting imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action. The older phrase survives in common usage and gave its name to the 1989 Tom Clancy novel and 1994 film Clear and Present Danger.
