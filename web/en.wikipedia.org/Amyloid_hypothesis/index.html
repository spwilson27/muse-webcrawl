Amyloid hypothesis

The amyloid hypothesis (or amyloid cascade hypothesis) is the prevailing theory of the cause of Alzheimer's disease. It proposes that the accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) peptides — fragments cleaved from the amyloid precursor protein (APP) — in the brain is the primary driver of the disease, triggering tau pathology, synaptic dysfunction, neuronal death, and ultimately dementia (see amyloidosis for amyloid disease elsewhere in the body).

Evidence

The hypothesis draws strong support from genetics: mutations in APP and in the presenilin genes, which affect Aβ production, cause early-onset familial Alzheimer's disease, while people with Down syndrome, who carry an extra copy of the APP gene, develop Alzheimer's pathology early. The protective A673T variant in APP, which reduces Aβ production, lowers Alzheimer's risk.

History

The hypothesis was articulated in its modern form by John Hardy and David Allsop in 1991 and by Hardy and Gerald Higgins in 1992. For three decades it directed most drug development toward clearing Aβ or blocking its production.

Criticism and controversy

Numerous anti-amyloid drugs failed in clinical trials, leading critics to question the hypothesis. Controversy intensified in 2022 when a Science investigation reported apparent image manipulation in an influential 2006 Nature paper on a specific Aβ oligomer (Aβ*56), prompting re-examination of parts of the literature.

Anti-amyloid therapies

The monoclonal antibodies aducanumab (approved controversially by the FDA in 2021, later discontinued), lecanemab (2023), and donanemab (2024) demonstrably remove amyloid plaques and modestly slow cognitive decline in early Alzheimer's, providing the first clinical evidence supporting amyloid as a therapeutic target, though debate continues about the size and significance of the benefit.
