Haplogroup

A haplogroup (from the Greek haploûs, "single, simple") is a group of similar haplotypes that share a common ancestor with a single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) mutation. In human genetics, the most studied haplogroups are those of the Y chromosome (Y-DNA haplogroups), inherited strictly along the paternal line, and of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA haplogroups), inherited strictly along the maternal line. Because these DNA segments do not recombine, their mutations accumulate over generations, allowing researchers to trace ancient migrations and ancestry.

Formation and naming

As mutations occur randomly over time, each new SNP defines a branch on a phylogenetic tree of descent. Haplogroups are named with letters (and alternating numbers and letters for sub-branches, called subclades), such as Y-DNA haplogroups R1b, I1, J2, or mtDNA haplogroups H, U, T and L. All living humans' maternal lines ultimately converge on "Mitochondrial Eve", and paternal lines on "Y-chromosomal Adam", the most recent common ancestors of each system.

Geographic distribution

Different haplogroups predominate in different regions because of founder effects, genetic drift and historical migrations. For example, Y-DNA haplogroup R1b is common in western Europe, Q in Indigenous peoples of the Americas, O in East Asia, and E in much of Africa. Mitochondrial haplogroup L is the most basal and diverse lineage, found mainly in sub-Saharan Africa, while haplogroup X occurs in both Europe and the Americas.

Uses

Haplogroups are used in population genetics to reconstruct prehistoric migration routes, in archaeology and ancient-DNA studies to understand the ancestry of past populations, and in commercial genetic genealogy, where customers learn the deep ancestral origins of their direct maternal and paternal lines. Haplogroups do not correspond to ethnicities or "races", since they trace only one line of descent among thousands of ancestors, and they generally reveal little about an individual's overall genome or traits.

Deep ancestry and other species

The haplogroup concept also applies to other organisms and to non-human lineages; researchers use equivalent marker systems in studies of animals, plants and even viruses to track lineage diversification and spread.
