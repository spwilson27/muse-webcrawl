Dust explosion

A dust explosion is the rapid combustion of fine particles suspended in the air, often but not always in an enclosed location. Dust explosions can occur where any concentrated dust clouds of coal, sawdust, wood particles or other powdered materials capable of burning are present in high enough concentrations. Dust explosions are a frequent hazard in coal mines, grain elevators, and other industrial environments. They are also commonly used in special effects at concerts, sporting arenas and in other pyrotechnics.

Conditions required

A dust explosion requires five elements, sometimes called the dust explosion pentagon: a combustible dust, an oxidant (usually atmospheric oxygen), a dispersion of the dust into the air forming a dust cloud, confinement of the dust cloud, and an ignition source. If any one of these elements is missing, an explosion cannot occur, although a fire may still be possible.

The concentration of the dust in the air must lie between the lower explosive limit and the upper explosive limit. Ignition can be provided by an open flame, a spark, hot surfaces, friction or electrostatic discharge.

Effects

A dust explosion may cause damage to structures, equipment and personnel from overpressure and shrapnel effects. The flying debris may also cause secondary explosions by disturbing dust deposits elsewhere in the facility. Many dusts that do not burn in bulk form are capable of exploding when suspended in air; aluminium, iron, coal, grain, sugar, flour and many pharmaceutical powders are examples.

Prevention and mitigation

Control measures include good housekeeping to prevent accumulation of dust layers, ventilation and dust collection systems, inerting the atmosphere, explosion venting, suppression systems and careful control of ignition sources. Regulations such as NFPA 652 in the United States govern combustible dust hazards.
