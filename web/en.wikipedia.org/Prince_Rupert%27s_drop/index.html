Prince Rupert's drop

Prince Rupert's drops (also known as Dutch tears or Batavian tears) are toughened glass beads created by dripping molten glass into cold water, which causes it to solidify into a tadpole-shaped droplet with a long, thin tail. These droplets are characterized by their internally contradictory properties: the bulbous head can withstand a blow from a hammer, while the slightest disturbance of the thin tail causes the entire drop to explosively disintegrate into a fine powder.

History

The drops were brought to England in the 17th century by Prince Rupert of the Rhine, who presented them to King Charles II and the Royal Society as scientific curiosities. They were studied by early fellows such as Robert Hooke, who described them in his Micrographia (1665). Similar glass drops had been made by glassmakers in northern Europe and were known in several countries under local names.

Properties and explanation

When molten glass is dropped into cold water, the outer surface cools and solidifies almost instantly while the interior cools more slowly and tries to contract. This sets up very large compressive stresses in the outer layer and tensile stresses in the core. The compressive surface layer makes the head extremely resistant to damage — it can survive hammer blows and, in experiments, withstand enormous pressures. However, the tail is thin and its surface stresses are easily disturbed: snipping or nicking it lets cracks propagate through the tensile interior at speeds of up to roughly 1.5–1.9 kilometres per second, shattering the whole drop into powder.

Scientific study

Modern research using photoelasticity and high-speed photography has measured the stress distribution and the speed of the disintegration front. Prince Rupert's drops are a classic demonstration of residual stress and toughened glass, and their principles relate to those used in the manufacture of tempered glass.
