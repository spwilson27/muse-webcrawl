Ice calving

Ice calving, also known as iceberg calving or glacier calving, is the breaking of ice chunks from the edge of a glacier or ice shelf. It is a form of ice ablation or ice disruption, normally caused by the expansion of a crack in the ice — a crevasse — and is usually accompanied by a loud cracking sound and dramatic waves as the new iceberg tips, rolls, and settles. The collapse of entire ice shelves, such as Larsen B in 2002, involves calving on a massive scale.

Mechanism

As glacier ice flows toward a front terminating in water or over a void, tensile stresses open crevasses, often along pre-existing weaknesses filled or lubricated by meltwater (hydrofracture). When a fracture penetrates the full thickness of the ice, a block detaches. Buoyancy, tidal flexure, undercutting by warm ocean water, and wave action all influence the rate. Calving speed is often related to water depth at the front, which helps explain the rapid retreat of tidewater glaciers.

Significance

Calving is a major mechanism by which the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets lose mass, contributing to sea-level rise. Large events produce the biggest icebergs: iceberg A-68, which calved from Antarctica's Larsen C Ice Shelf in July 2017, had an area of about 5,800 km² — roughly the size of Delaware. Calving fronts of grounded glaciers also generate seismic signals ("glacial earthquakes") detectable worldwide, and floating ice from an ice cap's outlet glaciers typically calves into fjords.

Hazards

Calving generates dangerous local waves that threaten nearby boats and shoreline infrastructure, and the sudden collapse of ice shelves removes the buttressing that slows the glaciers behind them, accelerating their flow to the sea. Monitoring calving fronts by satellite is a key part of the assessments reviewed by the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report.
