Percent for Art

Percent for Art (sometimes written Percent-for-Art) is a programme, often embodied in law or policy, under which a fixed percentage — commonly one percent — of the construction cost of a public building or development project is set aside to fund and install public art. Such schemes operate in many cities and countries and are a principal funding mechanism for art in public places.

History

Philadelphia enacted the first Percent for Art ordinance in the United States in 1959, requiring one percent of municipal construction budgets to be spent on art; New York City followed with its own law in 1982. At the federal level, the U.S. General Services Administration's Art in Architecture programme, begun in 1963, reserves a percentage of federal construction costs for artworks. Comparable policies exist in many European countries, in parts of Canada, and in Australia, where developer contributions for public art are common in major cities.

Operation and debate

Funds are typically administered by a public art agency or panel, which selects artists through open competition or invitation and oversees commissioning, fabrication, installation and maintenance. Supporters argue the programmes enrich civic space, support artists and integrate art into everyday life at modest cost. Critics contend that mandated percentages can produce formulaic works, that the money might be better spent, or that the resulting art — sometimes derided as "plop art" dropped into plazas without regard to context — fails to engage its setting. Many programmes have responded with requirements for community consultation and site-specific design.
