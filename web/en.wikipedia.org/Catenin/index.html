Catenin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Catenins are a family of proteins found in complexes with cadherin cell-adhesion molecules in animal cells. The name derives from the Latin catena (chain), reflecting their role in linking cadherins to the actin cytoskeleton. The principal members are α-catenin, β-catenin, γ-catenin (plakoglobin) and δ-catenin (p120-catenin).

Adherens junctions

At adherens junctions, classical cadherins span the plasma membrane and bind identical cadherins on neighbouring cells. Inside the cell, β-catenin (or γ-catenin in some junctions) binds the cadherin's cytoplasmic tail, while p120-catenin binds a juxtamembrane region and regulates cadherin stability at the surface. α-Catenin joins the complex to actin filaments, though dynamic studies show it does so in a force-sensitive, catch-bond manner rather than as a static bridge. Loss of these links weakens tissue cohesion and is a hallmark of invasive cancer.

β-Catenin and Wnt signalling

β-Catenin has a celebrated second role as the central effector of the canonical Wnt signalling pathway. In the absence of Wnt, cytoplasmic β-catenin is captured by a "destruction complex" (Axin, APC, GSK3β, CK1), phosphorylated, and degraded by the proteasome. When Wnt binds Frizzled and LRP5/6 receptors, the complex is inhibited, β-catenin accumulates, enters the nucleus, and activates TCF/LEF transcription factors, switching on genes that control proliferation and cell fate. Mutations that stabilise β-catenin or inactivate APC are among the most common drivers of colorectal cancer.

Clinical significance

Germline and somatic mutations in catenin genes are associated with cancers, neurodevelopmental disorders (CTNNB1 syndrome, involving β-catenin), and skin and hair abnormalities (plakoglobin mutations in Naxos disease affect desmosomes and the heart). Catenins are active targets of drug-discovery efforts aimed at the Wnt pathway.
