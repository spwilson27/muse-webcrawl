Type Ia supernova

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Type Ia supernova is a type of supernova that occurs in binary systems in which one star is a white dwarf. Spectroscopically it is defined by the absence of hydrogen and the presence of a strong silicon absorption line near maximum light. The star is completely destroyed; no compact remnant is left behind.

Mechanism

In the classic single-degenerate model, a carbon–oxygen white dwarf accretes matter from a companion star until it approaches the Chandrasekhar limit of about 1.44 solar masses. Compression then ignites runaway carbon fusion in the core, and within seconds the fusion front incinerates the entire star, releasing about 1044 joules and synthesising roughly half a solar mass of radioactive nickel-56, whose decay powers the light curve. In the double-degenerate model, two white dwarfs merge to the same effect.

Standard candles

Because the exploding mass is nearly uniform, Type Ia supernovae have very consistent peak luminosities, and the empirical Phillips relation — brighter events decline more slowly — lets astronomers correct their brightness precisely. They are therefore premier "standardisable candles" for measuring cosmic distances. In 1998 two teams used distant Type Ia supernovae to discover that the expansion of the universe is accelerating, evidence for dark energy — work awarded the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Nearest events

SN 2014J in the galaxy M82, about 11.5 million light-years away, was the closest Type Ia supernova in decades. Type Ia events enrich galaxies with iron-group elements and are the main source of iron in the universe.
