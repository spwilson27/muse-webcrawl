Consonance

Consonance and dissonance
Field: Music theory, psychoacoustics
Consonant intervals: unison, octave, perfect fifth, thirds, sixths
Dissonant: seconds, sevenths, tritone

In music, consonance and dissonance are categorizations of simultaneous or successive sounds. Consonant intervals and chords are experienced as stable, pleasant, and at rest, while dissonant ones are perceived as tense or unstable and are traditionally expected to resolve to consonance.

Acoustic basis

Consonance is associated with simple frequency ratios: the octave (2:1), perfect fifth (3:2), and perfect fourth (4:3) were recognized in ancient Greek theory through the work associated with Pythagoras. Later accounts invoked the harmonic series, the coincidence of partials, and the absence of "roughness" — rapid beating between nearby partials within a critical band, as described by Helmholtz and quantified by Plomp and Levelt.

Historical change

What counts as consonant has changed over time. Thirds and sixths were treated as imperfect or dissonant in early medieval polyphony but became standard consonances by the Renaissance. In common practice harmony, dissonance was governed by strict rules of preparation and resolution; 20th-century composers increasingly emancipated dissonance, treating it as a resource in its own right.

Cultural factors

Experiments suggest that preference for consonance is partly learned and cultural, influenced by exposure to harmony and the timbres of familiar instruments, rather than being purely innate.
