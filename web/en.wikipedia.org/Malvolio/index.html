Malvolio

Malvolio is a fictional character in William Shakespeare's comedy Twelfth Night, or What You Will (written c. 1601–1602). He is the steward of the household of the Countess Olivia. His name is an Italian phrase roughly meaning "I want ill will" or "ill will" — a name that suits both the ill will others feel toward him and his own sour disposition. Although a supporting character in the subplot, Malvolio has become one of Shakespeare's most famous comic creations, and his part has often been played by leading actors.

Character

Malvolio is a puritanical, self-important, and humourless steward who disapproves of the revelry of Sir Toby Belch, Sir Andrew Aguecheek, and the clown Feste. He aspires above his station, fantasising about marrying Olivia and becoming "Count Malvolio". Sir Toby describes him scornfully, asking, "Dost thou think, because thou art virtuous, there shall be no more cakes and ale?" — a line that has become emblematic of the clash between festivity and puritan restraint in the play.

The gulling plot

To revenge themselves on Malvolio for his reprimands, Olivia's gentlewoman Maria and the revellers forge a letter in Olivia's handwriting, designed to make Malvolio believe that his mistress is in love with him and wishes him to smile constantly, wear yellow stockings cross-gartered, and behave rudely to the other servants. Malvolio falls for the trick completely; in the famous "box-tree" scene he finds the letter while Maria, Sir Toby, and Sir Andrew spy on him, and he reads his own ambitions into its ambiguous lines — concluding, "Some are born great, some achieve greatness, and some have greatness thrust upon 'em."

Acting on the letter, Malvolio appears before Olivia smiling inanely and wearing the ridiculous stockings. Olivia, bewildered, concludes he is mad, and he is confined in a dark room as a lunatic, where Feste torments him disguised as the curate "Sir Topas". When the plot is exposed at the play's end, Malvolio storms off with the vow: "I'll be revenged on the whole pack of you" — a note of discord that unsettles the comedy's festive resolution.

Interpretation and stage history

Critics have long debated how sympathetic Malvolio should appear: in some productions he is a pompous butt who deserves his humiliation; in others, his treatment reads as cruelty, and his exit line lands as a genuine grievance. Charles Lamb noted the dignity actors brought to the role, and the character is sometimes seen as a satire on Puritan social climbing and self-love. The dark-room scene touches on early modern attitudes toward madness. Notable Malvolios have included many of the leading Shakespearean actors of the nineteenth, twentieth, and twenty-first centuries.
