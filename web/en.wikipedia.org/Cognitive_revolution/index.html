Cognitive revolution

The cognitive revolution was an intellectual movement that began in the 1950s as an interdisciplinary study of the mind and its processes, encompassing psychology, linguistics, computer science, anthropology, neuroscience, and philosophy. It emerged as a reaction against behaviorism, which had dominated psychology by insisting that only observable behaviour — not internal mental states — could be studied scientifically.

Key developments

Several landmarks are conventionally cited: Noam Chomsky's 1959 critique of B. F. Skinner's Verbal Behavior, which argued that language could not be explained by conditioning alone; George Armitage Miller's 1956 paper on short-term memory limits ("The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two"); the 1956 Dartmouth workshop that launched artificial intelligence; Miller, Galanter, and Pribram's Plans and the Structure of Behavior (1960); and Ulric Neisser's textbook Cognitive Psychology (1967), which gave the new field its name.

Ideas

The movement treated the mind as an information-processing system, drawing an analogy with the digital computer: mental representations are stored, transformed, and retrieved according to rules. This made internal states such as beliefs, memories, and plans legitimate objects of scientific study and helped give rise to cognitive science as an interdisciplinary enterprise.

Legacy

By the 1970s cognitive psychology had displaced behaviorism as the dominant paradigm in experimental psychology. Later historians have questioned how sudden or complete the "revolution" was, noting continuities with earlier work, but the label remains standard for the mid-century reorientation of the study of mind.
