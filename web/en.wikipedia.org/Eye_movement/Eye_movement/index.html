Eye movement

Eye movement is the voluntary or involuntary movement of the eyes, which helps the brain to combine the two retinal images into a single three-dimensional image and to direct the high-resolution fovea onto objects of interest. In the case of a human eye reading or scanning a scene, the eyes make rapid, ballistic movements called saccades, separated by brief pauses called fixations during which visual information is acquired.

Anatomy

Each eye is rotated by six extraocular muscles: the medial, lateral, superior and inferior rectus muscles, and the superior and inferior oblique muscles. These are controlled by three cranial nerves — the oculomotor nerve (III), trochlear nerve (IV) and abducens nerve (VI) — coordinated by gaze centres in the brainstem, notably the paramedian pontine reticular formation for horizontal movements and the rostral interstitial nucleus of the medial longitudinal fasciculus for vertical movements.

Types of eye movement

Eye movements are conventionally divided into those that stabilise the image on the retina and those that redirect the line of sight. Fixational eye movements include microsaccades, drift and tremor. The vestibulo-ocular reflex and optokinetic response stabilise gaze during head movement. Saccades are rapid shifts of gaze reaching peak velocities of several hundred degrees per second. Smooth pursuit tracks moving targets, vergence movements align the two eyes on targets at different depths, and the combined version movements point both eyes in the same direction.

Reading and scene perception

During reading, the eyes make saccades of roughly seven to nine character spaces, with fixations averaging 200–250 milliseconds; about 10–15 per cent of saccades are regressions back to earlier text. Eye tracking of these patterns is used in psychology, usability research and marketing to study attention.

Clinical significance

Abnormal eye movements can indicate neurological disease. Nystagmus is an involuntary rhythmic oscillation of the eyes. Ophthalmoplegia denotes weakness or paralysis of the extraocular muscles, and disorders such as internuclear ophthalmoplegia, seen in multiple sclerosis, reveal lesions in the brainstem pathways coordinating gaze.
