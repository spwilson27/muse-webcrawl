The Dead Sea Scrolls are ancient Jewish and Hebrew religious manuscripts discovered between 1946/47 and 1956 in twelve caves near the ancient settlement of Khirbet Qumran, in the Judaean Desert on the northwest shore of the Dead Sea. Dating from roughly 250 BC to AD 68, the approximately 900 manuscripts include the oldest known surviving copies of books of the Hebrew Bible, predating previously known manuscripts by about a thousand years, alongside sectarian works of the community that lived at Qumran, widely identified with the Essenes. They are regarded as one of the greatest archaeological finds of the 20th century.

Discovery and publication

The first scrolls were found by Bedouin shepherds in what is now called Cave 1, and sold to dealers and scholars; seven major scrolls — including the Great Isaiah Scroll, the Community Rule, and the War Scroll — were acquired for the Hebrew University and the Syrian Orthodox metropolitan. Systematic excavation of eleven more caves followed, yielding tens of thousands of fragments. An international editorial team worked for decades; full scholarly publication was largely completed only in the 1990s–2000s, amid controversy over restricted access. The best-preserved scrolls are displayed in the Shrine of the Book at the Israel Museum, and high-resolution images have since been published online.

Contents

About 40% of the scrolls are copies of texts from the Hebrew Bible — every book except Esther is represented — confirming the antiquity and general fidelity of the Masoretic text while revealing earlier textual variety. Roughly 30% are "sectarian" documents of the Qumran community: the Community Rule, the Damascus Document, the War Scroll (an apocalyptic battle plan), biblical commentaries (pesharim), the Thanksgiving Hymns, and the Temple Scroll. The remainder include apocrypha and pseudepigrapha (such as Enoch and Jubilees), prayers, legal texts, and the unique Copper Scroll, a treasure list engraved on metal.

The Qumran community

The settlement at Qumran, with its ritual baths, communal rooms, and large cemetery, is generally associated with the Essenes described by Josephus, Philo, and Pliny the Elder — a separatist Jewish movement that withdrew to the desert in expectation of an apocalyptic war between the "Sons of Light" and the "Sons of Darkness". The site was destroyed by the Romans around AD 68 during the First Jewish–Roman War, when the scrolls were presumably hidden in the caves.

Significance

The scrolls revolutionized the study of the Bible, Second Temple Judaism, and the origins of Christianity, illuminating the diversity of Jewish belief in the period, the development of the biblical canon, and the background of the New Testament.
