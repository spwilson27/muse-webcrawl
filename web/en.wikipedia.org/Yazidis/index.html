Yazidis

The Yazidis (Êzidî) are a Kurdish-speaking ethnoreligious group native to northern Iraq, southeastern Turkey, and northwestern Syria, with large diaspora communities in Germany and elsewhere. Their religion, Yazidism (Şarfadîn), is a monotheistic faith with roots in ancient Mesopotamian and Iranian traditions, centred on worship of God and the archangel Melek Taus, the Peacock Angel.

Beliefs

Yazidis believe in one God who entrusted the world to seven Holy Beings (Heft Sirr), chief among them Melek Taus. Because outsiders have misunderstood Melek Taus — who refused to bow to Adam and was reconciled with God — Yazidis were falsely stigmatized as "devil worshippers," a calumny used to justify centuries of persecution. Society is hereditary and endogamous, divided into castes of murids (laypeople), sheikhs, and pirs. The holiest site is Lalish in the Shekhan district, containing the tomb of Sheikh Adi ibn Musafir (d. 1162), the central reformer of the faith, to which every Yazidi should make pilgrimage once in a lifetime.

History

Yazidis endured repeated massacres under Ottoman and local rulers, which the community counts as 74 "firmans" (genocides). In August 2014, the Islamic State attacked Sinjar, killing thousands of Yazidi men, enslaving thousands of women and children, and displacing the entire population — acts recognized by the UN and many states as genocide. Survivors such as Nobel laureate Nadia Murad brought the crime to global attention; thousands of captives remained missing years later.

Population

Estimates range from 700,000 to 1.5 million worldwide. Before 2014 the largest community was in Iraq — Sinjar and the Nineveh Plains/Shekhan — with communities in the Transcaucasus descended from Ottoman-era refugees, and a diaspora of over 200,000 in Germany.
