Western Wall

The Western Wall (Kotel) in Jerusalem, one of the holiest sites in Judaism.

The Western Wall (Hebrew: הַכֹּתֶל הַמַּעֲרָבִי, HaKotel HaMa'aravi, often shortened to the Kotel), also known as the Wailing Wall and in Islam as the Buraq Wall, is an ancient limestone wall in the Old City of Jerusalem. It is a relatively small segment of a far longer ancient retaining wall forming the western side of the Temple Mount platform, built by Herod the Great around 19 BCE to support his vast expansion of the Second Temple complex.

Religious significance

The wall is venerated in Judaism as the closest accessible place to the Holy of Holies of the destroyed Temple, and Jews have prayed there for centuries; written petitions are traditionally placed in the crevices between its stones. In Islamic tradition the wall is associated with al-Buraq, the winged steed that carried Muhammad on the Night Journey, and it forms part of the boundary of the al-Aqsa Mosque compound above.

History

After the Roman destruction of the Temple in 70 CE, the retaining wall survived. Jewish access to the site varied under Byzantine, Muslim, Crusader, Mamluk, and Ottoman rule. During the British Mandate (1920–1948) the wall was a focus of Jewish–Arab tension, erupting in the riots of 1929. From 1948 to 1967, when the Old City was under Jordanian control, Jews were barred from the wall entirely. Israel captured the Old City in the Six-Day War of June 1967, and the Moroccan Quarter in front of the wall was demolished to create the present prayer plaza.

The plaza today

The exposed prayer section is divided into men's and women's areas separated by a mechitza, in accordance with Orthodox practice; disputes over egalitarian prayer led to the designation of a separate mixed-gender section near Robinson's Arch. The wall's full extent continues north underground through the Western Wall Tunnel. Millions of visitors and worshippers come each year, and the site hosts national ceremonies and military inductions.
