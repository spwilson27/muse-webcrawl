Shannon Scheme

The Shannon Scheme was an ambitious plan, developed in the 1950s and early 1960s, to drain and reclaim large areas of the Shannon Estuary and associated wetlands in Ireland for agriculture and development. The River Shannon, the longest river in Ireland, flows through the centre of the country and enters the Atlantic through a wide estuary between counties Clare, Limerick, and Kerry.

Background

Following the success of earlier land reclamation and drainage works, including the Shannon hydroelectric scheme at Ardnacrusha in the 1920s, engineers proposed embanking and draining thousands of acres of tidal mudflats and callows along the estuary. The reclaimed land was intended for farming, and the works were expected to reduce flooding of low-lying farmland along the river.

Opposition and abandonment

The proposals drew criticism on economic and environmental grounds. The estuary's mudflats are important feeding grounds for migratory wading birds, and conservationists warned of the loss of internationally significant habitats. Changing agricultural economics and the high cost of the works led to the scheme being shelved, and the Shannon Estuary was later designated for protection under European nature conservation law as a Special Protection Area and Special Area of Conservation.
