Traffic flow

Traffic flow studies how vehicles interact on a roadway and how flow breaks down into congestion.

In mathematics and transportation engineering, traffic flow is the study of interactions between travellers (including pedestrians, cyclists, drivers, and their vehicles) and infrastructure (including highways, traffic signals, and signage), with the aim of understanding and developing an optimal transport network with efficient movement of traffic and minimal traffic congestion.

Attempts to produce a mathematical theory of traffic flow date back to the 1920s, when Frank Knight first produced an analysis of traffic equilibrium, later refined into Wardrop's principles in 1952. Despite significant advances, traffic remains notoriously difficult to predict because it involves the behaviour of individual human drivers.

Key variables

Traffic stream characteristics are described by three interrelated variables:

  • Flow (q) — the number of vehicles passing a point per unit time, usually vehicles per hour.
  • Density (k) — the number of vehicles occupying a unit length of roadway, usually vehicles per kilometre.
  • Speed (v) — the mean speed of the traffic stream.

These are connected by the fundamental relation of traffic flow: q = k × v. The relationships between them are depicted in the fundamental diagram of traffic flow, which shows that flow rises with density up to a critical density (capacity), beyond which flow declines as congestion sets in.

Flow regimes

Free flow

At low densities, vehicles travel at or near the free-flow speed and are largely unaffected by other vehicles. Flow increases almost linearly with density in this regime.

Congested flow

Above the critical density, traffic becomes unstable: small disturbances amplify into stop-and-go waves that propagate backwards through the stream. In this regime both speed and flow fall as density rises toward jam density, at which vehicles are stopped.

Models

Traffic flow models range from macroscopic continuum models—beginning with the Lighthill–Whitham–Richards model of 1955–56, which treats traffic as a compressible fluid obeyed by a conservation law—to microscopic car-following models describing the behaviour of individual drivers, such as the Intelligent Driver Model. Mesoscopic models occupy a middle ground.

Applications

Traffic flow theory underpins ramp metering, variable speed limits, congestion pricing, and the design of highway capacity. The Highway Capacity Manual codifies practical procedures for estimating the capacity and level of service of road facilities.
