Foraminifera

DomainEukaryota
PhylumForaminifera
Fossil rangeCambrian – present

Foraminifera (Latin for "hole bearers"; informally called "forams") are single-celled organisms, members of a phylum or class of amoeboid protists characterised by streaming granular ectoplasm for catching food and other uses; and commonly an external shell (called a "test") of diverse forms and materials. Tests are typically made of calcium carbonate (CaCO3) or agglutinated sediment particles. Most forams are marine and benthic, living on or in seafloor sediment, though planktonic forms float in the water column.

Diverse foraminiferal tests, mostly under 1 mm in size.

Ecology and life cycle

Foraminifera are found in all marine environments, from the intertidal zone to the deepest ocean trenches, and are abundant in the fossil record since the Cambrian. They feed on small organisms such as diatoms and bacteria, captured with a network of pseudopodia called reticulopodia that emerge through pores in the test. Many species harbour algal symbionts. Their life cycles characteristically alternate between haploid and diploid generations.

Importance

Because their tests fossilise readily and species evolved rapidly, foraminifera are key index fossils used in biostratigraphy, especially by the petroleum industry to date sedimentary rocks. The oxygen isotope ratios in their shells record past ocean temperatures and ice volumes, making forams central to paleoclimatology. The white cliffs of Dover and the limestone of the Egyptian pyramids are largely composed of foraminiferal remains, including the large benthic genus Nummulites.
