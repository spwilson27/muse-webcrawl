James Paul Mitchell (November 12, 1900 – October 19, 1964) was an American politician and businessman who served as the United States Secretary of Labor from 1953 to 1961 under President Dwight D. Eisenhower. Known as the "social conscience of the Republican Party", he was one of the most liberal members of the Eisenhower administration.

Early life and career

Mitchell was born in Elizabeth, New Jersey, and rose from a clerk's job with the Western Electric Company to become its director of industrial relations, one of the leading personnel-management posts in American industry. During the Second World War he served as director of industrial personnel for the War Department, overseeing labor relations for millions of workers.

Secretary of Labor

Eisenhower appointed Mitchell in October 1953 after the brief tenure of Martin Durkin. As secretary, Mitchell supported increases in the federal minimum wage (raised to $1.00 in 1955), expanded unemployment insurance coverage, strengthened mine and workplace safety enforcement, and promoted labor-management cooperation. He helped draft the Landrum–Griffin Act of 1959 regulating internal union affairs. Mitchell was frequently mentioned as a possible vice-presidential or gubernatorial candidate and was regarded as bringing organized labor's perspective into a Republican cabinet.

Later years

Leaving office in 1961, Mitchell ran unsuccessfully for governor of New Jersey that year, losing the Democratic primary-era contest in the general election to Richard J. Hughes. He returned to business and died in New York in 1964. The headquarters of the US Department of Labor, the Frances Perkins Building, succeeded buildings associated with his tenure; Mitchell's name is commemorated in labor-history circles as a model of bipartisan labor policy.
