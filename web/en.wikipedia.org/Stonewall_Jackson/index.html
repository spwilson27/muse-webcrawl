Thomas Jonathan "Stonewall" Jackson (21 January 1824 – 10 May 1863) was a Confederate general during the American Civil War and one of the most celebrated commanders in American military history. Serving under Robert E. Lee in the Army of Northern Virginia, he won fame for his Shenandoah Valley Campaign of 1862 and for the audacious flank march that decided the Battle of Chancellorsville — where he was mortally wounded by friendly fire.

Early life and career

Orphaned young and raised by relatives in western Virginia, Jackson graduated from the United States Military Academy at West Point in 1846 and won brevet promotions for gallantry in the Mexican–American War. He resigned from the army in 1852 to teach at the Virginia Military Institute in Lexington, where his eccentricities and stern Presbyterian piety made him a figure of mixed repute as "Tom Fool" Jackson to some cadets.

Civil War

Jackson earned his nickname at the First Battle of Bull Run (1861), where his brigade stood "like a stone wall" against the Union assault. In spring 1862 his small army marched 646 miles in 48 days in the Shenandoah Valley, defeating three larger Union commands and tying down forces needed elsewhere. He fought through the Seven Days, Second Bull Run, Antietam and Fredericksburg, earning promotion to lieutenant general and command of the Second Corps. At Chancellorsville on 2 May 1863 his corps crushed the Union right flank in one of the war's boldest manoeuvres; returning from a night reconnaissance he was shot by his own pickets, lost his left arm to amputation, and died of pneumonia eight days later. His reported last words were "Let us cross over the river, and rest under the shade of the trees."

Legacy

Lee regarded Jackson's death as the crippling blow of the war — "I have lost my right arm." Jackson remains a central figure of the Lost Cause tradition, which has made his reputation and monuments subjects of modern controversy; several statues were removed in 2020.
