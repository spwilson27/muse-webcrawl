Cutthroat trout

The cutthroat trout (Oncorhynchus clarkii) is a species of freshwater fish in the salmon family, native to cold-water tributaries of the Pacific Ocean, the Rocky Mountains, and the Great Basin of North America. It is named for the distinctive red or orange slash marks on the underside of its lower jaw.

Subspecies and distribution

About fourteen subspecies are recognised, including the coastal, westslope, Yellowstone, Lahontan, Bonneville, and Colorado River cutthroat trout. Historically the Lahontan cutthroat of Nevada's Pyramid Lake grew to exceptional size, with fish exceeding 18 kilograms reported. Cutthroat trout were the first New World trout encountered by Europeans, noted during the Coronado expedition and described scientifically from specimens collected by the Lewis and Clark Expedition, for whom the species is named.

Ecology

Cutthroat trout require clean, cold, well-oxygenated water with gravel spawning beds. Some populations are anadromous, migrating to the sea and returning to fresh water to spawn. They feed on aquatic insects, crustaceans, and smaller fish and are an important prey species for bears, eagles, and other wildlife in western watersheds.

Conservation

Many subspecies have declined through habitat loss, competition and hybridisation with introduced rainbow trout, and competition from non-native brook and lake trout. Two subspecies were formerly listed under the US Endangered Species Act; recovery efforts include removing non-native fish and restoring stream habitat. Cutthroat trout are the state fish of several western states, including Wyoming, Montana, and Utah.
