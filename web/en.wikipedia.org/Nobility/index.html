Nobility

Nobility is a social class found in many societies that have an aristocracy. It is normally appointed by and ranked immediately below royalty. Nobility has often been an estate of the realm with many exclusive functions and characteristics, and its members have enjoyed privileges and status beyond those of most other classes.

History

Noble classes arose in the ancient world — Roman patricians, Greek eupatridae — and became formalised in medieval Europe under feudalism, where land, military service and inherited rank intertwined. Titles such as duke, marquess, earl (count), viscount and baron form the typical peerage hierarchy in the British system, with continental equivalents. In many systems nobility was hereditary and could also be conferred by a monarch for service; in Poland–Lithuania the szlachta formed an unusually large noble estate, while in Imperial China rank flowed from the examination-based bureaucracy instead.

Privileges and decline

Nobles commonly held rights to bear coats of arms, exemptions from certain taxes, separate courts, and reserved offices. The French Revolution and subsequent democratisation abolished legal noble privilege in much of Europe, though titles survive in several monarchies and in social usage. In the United Kingdom, hereditary peers long sat in the House of Lords by right; reforms in 1999 removed most of them.
