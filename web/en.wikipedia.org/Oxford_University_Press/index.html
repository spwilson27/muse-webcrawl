Oxford University Press (OUP) is the university press of the University of Oxford. It is the largest university press in the world by the number of publications, publishing in more than 40 languages. The press is a department of the University of Oxford and is governed by a group of fifteen academics, the Delegates of the Press, appointed by the vice-chancellor.

History

Printing began in Oxford around 1478, within a decade of William Caxton's first press in England. The university obtained a decree confirming its right to print books in 1586, and in the seventeenth century Chancellor William Laud secured expanded privileges, including the right to print Bibles — Bible publishing long underwrote the press's scholarly work. In the nineteenth century OUP expanded internationally, opening a New York office in 1896, now its largest overseas operation. In 1978 the press moved its printing works' role toward publishing services as the Oxford printing house closed; printing is now outsourced.

Publishing

OUP is best known to general readers for its dictionaries, above all the Oxford English Dictionary, the principal historical dictionary of English, and for reference works such as the Oxford World's Classics series, the Very Short Introductions, and standard editions of classical and English literature. Its academic division publishes scholarly monographs and journals across the humanities, sciences, law, and medicine; its education division produces school textbooks and English-language teaching materials used worldwide. OUP is also a major publisher of sheet music, having represented composers such as Ralph Vaughan Williams.

Role within the university

As a department of a charitable university, OUP's surpluses support Oxford's research, scholarship, and education. The press operates offices in dozens of countries and distributes globally, while maintaining its Oxford headquarters at Walton Street in the Jericho district.
