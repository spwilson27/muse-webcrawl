Hostile work environment

Hostile work environment is a legal concept in employment discrimination law

In United States employment law, a hostile work environment exists when an employee experiences workplace harassment based on a protected characteristic — such as sex, race, religion, national origin, age or disability — that is sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of employment and create an abusive working environment. The doctrine arises under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and parallel statutes.

Legal standard

The U.S. Supreme Court recognized hostile-environment sexual harassment as a form of sex discrimination in Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson (1986). In Harris v. Forklift Systems (1993) the Court held that the conduct need not cause tangible psychological injury; courts weigh the frequency and severity of the conduct, whether it was physically threatening or humiliating, and whether it unreasonably interfered with work performance, considering the totality of the circumstances from both an objective and a subjective perspective.

Employer liability

Employers are liable for harassment by supervisors that results in a tangible employment action; where no such action occurs, an employer may raise the Faragher–Ellerth affirmative defence by showing that it exercised reasonable care to prevent and correct harassment and that the employee unreasonably failed to use its complaint procedures. Employers are also responsible for harassment by coworkers and third parties when they knew or should have known and failed to act.
