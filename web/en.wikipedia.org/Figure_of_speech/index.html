Figure of speech

A figure of speech or rhetorical figure is a word or phrase that intentionally deviates from straightforward language use or literal meaning in order to produce a rhetorical or intensified effect. Figures of speech are a central part of rhetoric, studied since antiquity, and are common in poetry, oratory and everyday language.

Categories

Traditionally, figures are divided into schemes, which alter the normal arrangement or pattern of words (such as alliteration, anaphora and parallelism), and tropes, which turn a word or phrase away from its literal meaning (such as metaphor, simile, irony and hyperbole).

Common examples

Well-known figures include metaphor ("time is a thief"), simile ("brave as a lion"), personification, metonymy ("the Crown" for the monarchy), synecdoche ("all hands on deck"), oxymoron ("deafening silence"), litotes and onomatopoeia. Classical rhetoricians catalogued hundreds of such devices.

History

The systematic study of figures began with Greek and Roman rhetoricians such as Aristotle, Cicero and Quintilian, whose Institutio Oratoria catalogued figures of speech and thought in detail. Renaissance rhetoricians expanded these lists, and figures remain a standard topic in the study of literature and composition.

Function

Figures of speech make abstract ideas vivid, add emphasis and emotional colour, aid memory, and can compress complex comparisons into a few words. Their effectiveness depends on the audience recognising the departure from literal usage.
