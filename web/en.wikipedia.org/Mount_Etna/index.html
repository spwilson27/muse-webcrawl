Mount Etna, or simply Etna (Italian: Etna; Sicilian: Muncibeddu), is an active stratovolcano on the east coast of Sicily, Italy, in the Metropolitan City of Catania, between the cities of Catania and Messina. It is one of the most active volcanoes in the world and is in an almost constant state of activity. Etna is also one of the tallest active volcanoes in Europe, standing roughly 3,350 metres high — a figure that changes as eruptions build and destroy its summit craters — and it covers an area of about 1,190 km² with a basal circumference of some 140 km.

Geology

Etna lies above the convergent margin where the African Plate subducts beneath the Eurasian Plate. Activity began about 500,000 years ago with submarine eruptions; successive volcanic centres built the modern edifice, crowned today by five principal summit craters (Voragine, Bocca Nuova, the Northeast Crater — usually the highest point — and the Southeast Crater complex). The broad Valle del Bove depression scars the eastern flank. Etna's frequent eruptions are typically effusive to mildly explosive, producing basaltic lava flows from summit and flank vents.

Eruption history

Etna's eruptions have been documented since antiquity; a major eruption in 396 BC reportedly diverted a Carthaginian army. The most destructive historic event, in 1669, sent lava through several villages and into Catania, reaching the sea and causing widespread devastation. The 1928 eruption destroyed the town of Mascali; in 1992 the Italian army and engineers diverted lava threatening Zafferana Etnea. Summit eruptions and ash emissions regularly close Catania's airport, and a new Southeast Crater cone that grew from 2011 onward made Etna measurably taller in 2021. In December 2018 a flank eruption accompanied by earthquakes damaged buildings on the lower slopes.

Mythology and culture

In Greek myth Etna housed the forge of Hephaestus and the Cyclopes, and the monster Typhon was imprisoned beneath it — his struggles causing its eruptions; Empedocles was said to have leapt into the crater. The fertile volcanic soils support vineyards (Etna DOC wines), orchards, and chestnut woods on the lower slopes, while the upper mountain is a protected regional park (Parco dell'Etna, established 1987). Etna was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2013 for its exceptional level of volcanic activity and its documentation for at least 2,700 years.
