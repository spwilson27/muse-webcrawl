Courtly love

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A lover and his lady in a medieval manuscript

Courtly love (Occitan: fin'amor; French: amour courtois) was a medieval European literary conception of love that emphasised nobility and chivalry. It originated in the lyric poetry of the troubadours of Aquitaine and Provence in the late 11th century and spread through the courts of Europe.

In its classic form, a knight devoted himself to a lady — usually married and of higher rank — serving her with humility, patience and feats of arms, while the love remained admiring, ennobling and often unconsummated. The term amour courtois was coined by Gaston Paris in 1883; Andreas Capellanus's treatise De amore (c. 1180s) codified its "rules".

Characteristics

  • The lover's total submission to the beloved, addressed as his feudal superior.
  • Love as suffering: sleeplessness, pallor and sighing as signs of sincerity.
  • Love as morally improving: the lover becomes braver, more courteous and more virtuous.
  • Secrecy and discretion, symbolised by the code name (senhal) for the lady.

Influence

Courtly conventions shaped the romances of Chrétien de Troyes, the Roman de la Rose, Dante's love for Beatrice, Petrarch's sonnets, and through them much of the Western literary tradition of romantic love. Modern scholars debate how far the literary ideal reflected actual social practice.
