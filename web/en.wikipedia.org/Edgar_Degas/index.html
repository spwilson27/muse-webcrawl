Edgar Degas (born Hilaire-Germain-Edgar De Gas, 19 July 1834 – 27 September 1917) was a French Impressionist artist famous for his pastel drawings and oil paintings of ballerinas. Degas also produced bronze sculptures, prints, and drawings. He is especially identified with the subject of dance; more than half of his works depict dancers. Although Degas is regarded as one of the founders of Impressionism, he rejected the term, preferring to be called a realist, and did not paint many landscapes.

Early life

Degas was born in Paris into a wealthy banking family of Neapolitan origin. After briefly studying law, he entered the École des Beaux-Arts in 1855 and travelled to Italy, where he copied Renaissance masters. Early paintings such as The Bellelli Family (1858–1867) were history paintings and portraits. In the 1860s he turned increasingly to contemporary subjects: racehorses, cafés, milliners, laundresses, and the Paris Opéra ballet.

Art

Degas exhibited with the Impressionists in seven of their eight exhibitions (1874–1886) and organised several of them, yet his draughtsmanship and studio-based practice set him apart from painters like Monet. He favoured unusual viewpoints, cropping, and artificial light, influenced by Japanese woodblock prints and photography. Works such as L'Absinthe (1875–76) and The Star (1878) exemplify his intimate scenes of modern Parisian life. As his eyesight failed in later life, he worked increasingly in pastel, wax sculpture, and monotype; his bronze Little Dancer Aged Fourteen, modelled in wax around 1881, provoked controversy when first exhibited.

Later life and reputation

Degas became progressively reclusive and nearly blind, wandering the streets of Paris; he died in 1917. An antisemitic stance during the Dreyfus affair damaged his relationships and reputation. On his death, his studio contained a vast body of work; major collections of his art are held by the Musée d'Orsay, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and the National Gallery.
