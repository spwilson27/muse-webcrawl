Aceh War

The Aceh War (1873–1904), also known as the Dutch War or the Infidel War, was an armed conflict between the Kingdom of the Netherlands and the Aceh Sultanate in northern Sumatra, in present-day Indonesia. It was one of the longest and most expensive colonial wars in Dutch history and ended in the annexation of Aceh into the Dutch East Indies, although Acehnese guerrilla resistance continued for years afterwards.

Background

The Sultanate of Aceh was a major regional power and a centre of the pepper trade. Under the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824 the British had guaranteed Aceh's independence, but the Sumatra Treaty of 1871 gave the Netherlands a free hand in Aceh in exchange for Dutch recognition of British interests elsewhere. Fearing Acehnese dealings with other powers, the Dutch issued an ultimatum and declared war in March 1873.

Course of the war

The first Dutch expedition was repulsed; a second expedition captured the sultan's palace (Kraton) in January 1874 and the sultan died of cholera, but Acehnese resistance reorganised under religious leaders (ulama) and warlords (uleebalang) as a guerrilla struggle framed as a holy war. The Dutch adopted a defensive "concentrated line" of forts for over a decade without success. In the 1890s, advised by the scholar Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje, who had studied Acehnese society, the Dutch under generals such as J. B. van Heutsz switched to aggressive mobile warfare, co-opting the uleebalang and ruthlessly suppressing the ulama-led resistance. Sultan Muhammad Daud Syah surrendered in 1903, and the Dutch declared Aceh pacified in 1904, though sporadic resistance continued until about 1914.

Human cost

The war is estimated to have caused the deaths of tens of thousands of people, including perhaps 60,000–100,000 Acehnese (from combat, disease and famine), about 10,000 Dutch and colonial troops, and thousands of coolies. Dutch punitive expeditions, such as those in the Gayo and Alas lands in 1904, involved massacres that later became notorious.

Legacy

The Aceh War left a lasting legacy of resistance in Acehnese identity that re-emerged in the Indonesian National Revolution and in the later Free Aceh Movement insurgency (1976–2005).
