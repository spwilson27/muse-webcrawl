Mérode Altarpiece


Labelled image: the Mérode Altarpiece, c. 1427–1432

The Mérode Altarpiece (or Annunciation Triptych) is an oil-on-oak panel triptych attributed to the workshop of the Early Netherlandish painter Robert Campin, sometimes identified as the Master of Flémalle, with major contributions likely from assistants, possibly including Rogier van der Weyden. Dated to about 1427–1432, it is one of the most celebrated works of early Northern Renaissance painting and has been in The Cloisters collection of the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York since the 1950s.

Description

The central panel depicts the Annunciation set, unusually, in a contemporary Flemish domestic interior: the Virgin, seated on the floor reading, does not yet notice the Archangel Gabriel, while a tiny figure of Christ carrying a cross descends on rays of light through the window — a depiction of the Incarnation. The left panel shows the donors kneeling in a garden doorway; the right panel shows Saint Joseph working in his carpentry shop, with two mousetraps prominently displayed — one on his workbench and one outside the window. The mousetraps are a visual metaphor drawn from Saint Augustine: the cross (or the Incarnation) as God's mousetrap to catch the Devil.

Iconography and attribution

The work is dense with disguised symbolism — the lilies, the extinguished candle, the water vessel, and the recently washed towel all carry theological meaning debated by art historians since Erwin Panofsky's pioneering analyses of Early Netherlandish painting. The altarpiece takes its name from the Belgian noble family of Mérode, in whose collection it was recorded in the nineteenth century. Its attribution and dating, and the relative contributions of Campin and his workshop, remain subjects of scholarly discussion.
