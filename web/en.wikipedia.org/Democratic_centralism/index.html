Democratic centralism is the organisational principle of most communist parties, in which policy is decided democratically but, once decided, is binding on all members. It combines free discussion before a decision with strict unity in carrying the decision out. Vladimir Lenin developed the concept for the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party in works such as What Is to Be Done? (1902) and One Step Forward, Two Steps Back (1904).

Principles

In its classic formulation, democratic centralism comprises: (1) election of all party organs from below upward; (2) periodic accountability of party bodies to their members and to higher bodies; (3) strict party discipline and subordination of the minority to the majority; (4) binding character of decisions of higher bodies on lower bodies. Lenin summed up the practice as "freedom of discussion, unity of action".

In the Soviet Union and other states

After 1917 democratic centralism became the constitutional organisational principle not only of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union but also of soviets, trade unions, and state administration, and it was adopted by ruling communist parties in China, Vietnam, Cuba, and elsewhere. The Tenth Congress of the Russian party in 1921 added the "ban on factions", which outlawed organized dissenting groups within the party.

Criticism

Critics — from Rosa Luxemburg (writing in 1904 and 1918) to Leon Trotsky and many later commentators — argued that in practice the "centralism" eclipsed the "democracy": elections became ratifications, the ban on factions suppressed debate, and power concentrated in the central apparatus and eventually in a single leader. Defenders maintained that disciplined unity was necessary for a revolutionary party operating under repression and civil war.

Modern use

The principle remains in the constitutions of many communist and some other parties and is written into the organisation of the Chinese Communist Party and the state structures of the People's Republic of China.
