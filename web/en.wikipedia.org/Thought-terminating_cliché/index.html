Thought-terminating cliché

Thought-terminating cliché
Coined byRobert Jay Lifton (1961)
FieldPsychology, rhetoric, propaganda studies
Related conceptsLoaded language, slogan, doublespeak

A thought-terminating cliché (also known as a semantic stop-sign, a loaded phrase, or, in Chinese, a "glorious cliché") is a commonly used phrase, sometimes passing as folk wisdom, used to quell cognitive dissonance or to end a discussion. Though the phrase in some cases might be a valid statement, its main effect is to impose a period on an argument, ending the debate with a cliché rather than a point.

Origin

The term was popularised by psychiatrist Robert Jay Lifton in his 1961 book Thought Reform and the Psychology of Totalism: A Study of "Brainwashing" in China. Lifton observed that the language of totalist environments is characterised by the "thought-terminating cliché": "The most far-reaching and complex of human problems are compressed into brief, highly selective, definitive-sounding phrases, easily memorised and easily expressed. They become the start and finish of any ideological analysis." Examples from the Chinese Communist context included phrases such as "the people's democratic dictatorship", used to foreclose further questioning.

Characteristics and examples

Thought-terminating clichés are typically short, definitive and emotionally charged. They may invoke an authority ("the science is settled"), dismiss an interlocutor ("let's agree to disagree", "it is what it is"), or reframe a burden as unquestionable ("boys will be boys", "everything happens for a reason"). In political rhetoric, slogans such as "support our troops" or "taxation is theft" can function in this way, as can corporate phrases like "we're a family here". The cliché need not be false; its thought-terminating quality lies in its use to stop analysis rather than to contribute evidence.

Criticism

The concept is related to George Orwell's notion of Newspeak in Nineteen Eighty-Four, in which a deliberately limited vocabulary constrains the range of expressible thought. Critics note that labelling a phrase a thought-terminating cliché can itself become a conversation-stopper, and that many clichés serve harmless social functions of politeness and closure rather than manipulation.
