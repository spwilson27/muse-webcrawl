Frequentist probability

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Relative frequencies of dice outcomes converging to the theoretical probability as trials increase]

Frequentist probability or frequentism is an interpretation of probability; it defines an event's probability as the limit of its relative frequency in many trials (the long-run probability). Probabilities can be found (in principle) by a repeatable objective process, and are thus ideally devoid of subjective opinion. The development of the frequentist account was motivated by the problems and paradoxes of the previously dominant viewpoint, the classical interpretation.

In the frequentist interpretation, probabilities are discussed only when dealing with well-defined random experiments (or random samples). The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment is called the sample space of the experiment. An event is defined as a particular subset of the sample space. For any given event, its probability is defined as the proportion of times the event would occur if the experiment were repeated indefinitely under identical conditions, grounded in the law of large numbers.

P(A) = limn→∞ nA / n

History

The frequentist view has roots in the work of Jacob Bernoulli and was developed as a formal philosophy in the early 20th century by Jerzy Neyman, Egon Pearson, Richard von Mises and R. A. Fisher, who built the framework of statistical hypothesis testing and confidence intervals that dominates applied statistics. Neyman and Pearson's theory of testing, and Fisher's significance tests and maximum likelihood estimation, are the characteristic tools of frequentist inference.

Frequentist inference

In frequentist inference, unknown parameters are treated as fixed constants, and probability statements attach to procedures rather than to parameters. A 95% confidence interval is one produced by a procedure that, in the long run, captures the true parameter in 95% of repetitions — it does not, strictly, mean there is a 95% probability the parameter lies in a particular computed interval. Similarly, a p-value is the probability, under a null hypothesis, of data at least as extreme as that observed.

Comparison with Bayesian probability

The Bayesian interpretation defines probability as a degree of belief, updated by Bayes' theorem from prior to posterior distributions. Frequentists object that priors introduce subjectivity; Bayesians object that frequentist methods can give counterintuitive results for single cases and cannot assign probabilities to single events or fixed parameters. The two schools coexisted uneasily through the 20th century, with Bayesian methods resurging as computing power grew.

Criticisms

Frequentism struggles with probabilities of unique events (it cannot readily say what the probability of a particular election outcome is), and its reliance on an infinite hypothetical sequence of trials — and on the reference class chosen for an event — raises philosophical difficulties known as the reference class problem.
