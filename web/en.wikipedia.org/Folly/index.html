Folly

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. This article is about the ornamental building. For lack of wisdom, see Foolishness.

In architecture, a folly is a building constructed primarily for decoration, though suggesting through its appearance some other purpose, or of such extravagant appearance that it transcends the normal range of garden ornaments. Follies were a hallmark of 18th- and 19th-century English landscape gardens and French jardins à l'anglaise: sham ruins, mock castles, artificial grottoes, hermitages, pagodas, pyramids, and Gothic temples placed to punctuate a view, start conversations, or commemorate events.

Characteristics

The name derives from the French folie ("folly," also a pleasure house), reflecting contemporary amusement at the expense. Classic examples include the ruins at Hagley Hall, the Chinese pagoda at Kew (1761), the ruined castle at Wimpole, and the enormous Broadway Tower in Worcestershire. Landowners sometimes hired ornamental hermits to inhabit garden hermitages. Some follies have practical uses — as eye-catchers terminating vistas, hunting lodges, tea houses, or water towers disguised as castles — but utility is secondary to effect.

Later follies

The fashion waned with Victorian seriousness but never disappeared: Fonthill Abbey's tower famously collapsed; in the 20th century the tradition continued in structures such as the McCaig's Tower obelisk in Oban (actually an unfinished museum), follies at West Wycombe, and American examples like Bishop Castle in Colorado. "Folly" has passed into general usage for any costly, useless structure, and organisations such as the Folly Fellowship document and preserve them.
