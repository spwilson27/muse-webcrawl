Contrapposto

Contrapposto is an Italian term that means "counterpoise". It is used in the visual arts to describe a human figure standing with most of its weight on one foot, so that its shoulders and arms twist off-axis from the hips and legs in the axis of the foot. This gives the figure a more dynamic, or alternatively relaxed, appearance. It can also be used to refer to the tension created by one leg bearing the body's weight (the engaged leg) and the other being free.

History

Contrapposto was first developed by the ancient Greeks and appears in the Kritios Boy of about 480 BC, where it displaced the rigid, frontal stance of Archaic kouros figures. Polykleitos's Doryphoros (c. 440 BC) is the most famous early example and became a model of harmonious proportion. The pose was revived in Renaissance art, notably by Donatello and later Michelangelo, whose David (1501–1504) is perhaps the best-known contrapposto figure of the modern era. It gives a sculpture a sense of life and movement, suggesting the figure has just shifted its weight or is about to move.

Description

In a contrapposto figure, the pelvis and shoulders tilt in opposite directions, and the spine takes on a gentle S-curve — a fuller curve related to the pose is sometimes called chiasmus in limbs. The relaxed leg is often bent at the knee. Renaissance theorists associated the pose with naturalism and grace, in contrast to the more static symmetry of medieval figures.
