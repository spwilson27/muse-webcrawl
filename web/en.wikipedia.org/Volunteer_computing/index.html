Volunteer computing is a type of distributed computing in which members of the public donate unused computing resources — CPU and GPU cycles on personal computers — to research-oriented projects. A project server divides a large computation into small work units, distributes them over the internet to volunteers' machines, and collects and validates the results.

History

Early projects included the Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search (GIMPS, 1996) and Distributed.net (1997). SETI@home, launched in 1999 at the University of California, Berkeley, popularised the model, attracting millions of participants to analyse radio telescope data for signals from extraterrestrial intelligence. In 2002 Berkeley released BOINC (Berkeley Open Infrastructure for Network Computing), a general-purpose middleware platform that hosts dozens of projects, including Rosetta@home, Einstein@Home, Climateprediction.net and World Community Grid. Folding@home, focused on protein folding and disease research, became in 2020 the first computing system to exceed an exaFLOP, driven by volunteers during the COVID-19 pandemic.

How it works

Volunteers install a client that runs computations when the computer is idle. Because donated hosts are untrusted and unreliable, servers typically send each work unit to several volunteers and compare results (redundant computing), and award "credit" to motivate participation. Projects are suited to embarrassingly parallel problems that split into many independent tasks.

Advantages and drawbacks

Volunteer computing gives researchers supercomputer-scale throughput at low cost and engages the public in science. Drawbacks include result-validation overhead, variable availability of hosts, and the energy cost borne by volunteers.
