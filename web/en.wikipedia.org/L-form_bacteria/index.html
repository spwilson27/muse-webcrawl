L-form bacteria

L-form bacteria, also known as L-phase bacteria or cell wall-deficient (CWD) bacteria, are strains of bacteria that lack cell walls. They were first isolated in 1935 by Emmy Klieneberger-Nobel, who named them "L-forms" after the Lister Institute in London where she was working.

Types

Two types of L-forms are distinguished: unstable L-forms (spheroplasts), which retain the ability to divide but may revert to the original morphology, and stable L-forms, which are unable to revert to the original bacteria. Some parasitic species of bacteria, such as mycoplasmas, also lack cell walls but are not considered L-forms because they are not derived from bacteria that normally have cell walls.

Research and controversy

L-forms can be induced in the laboratory by antibiotics that inhibit cell wall synthesis, such as penicillin, or by enzymes such as lysozyme. They have been proposed to play a role in recurrent and chronic infections because they can evade immune detection and some antibiotics, though their clinical significance remains debated.
