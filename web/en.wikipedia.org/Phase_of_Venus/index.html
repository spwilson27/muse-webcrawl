Phases of Venus

The phases of Venus are the variations of lighting seen on the planet's surface, similar to lunar phases. The first recorded observations are thought to have been made by Galileo Galilei in 1610. Unlike the Moon, which shows clear phases visible to the naked eye, Venus appears very small and shows no obvious phases without a telescope.

Observation

When Venus is on the far side of the Sun, it appears full and relatively small. As it moves around to the near side, it grows larger and shows gibbous, half, and crescent phases, reaching a thin crescent when almost between Earth and the Sun. The complete cycle of phases takes about 584 days, the synodic period of Venus.

Historical significance

Galileo's observation of a full set of phases was strong evidence against the geocentric Ptolemaic system, in which Venus, circling on an epicycle between Earth and the Sun, could never appear fully lit. The phases are naturally explained by the heliocentric model, in which Venus orbits the Sun inside Earth's orbit.
