Gene flow

In population genetics, gene flow (also known as gene migration or allele flow) is the transfer of genetic material from one population to another. If the rate of gene flow is high enough, then two populations will have equivalent allele frequencies and therefore can be considered a single effective population. It has been shown that it takes only "one migrant per generation" to prevent populations from diverging due to drift, although this rule of thumb depends on simplifying assumptions. Gene flow is an important mechanism for transferring genetic diversity among populations.

Mechanisms

Gene flow occurs whenever individuals (or their gametes) move between populations and reproduce. In plants it is often mediated by pollen and seed dispersal; in animals by migration of individuals; in microbes by horizontal gene transfer as well as dispersal. Barriers to gene flow — geographic features such as mountain ranges, oceans and deserts, or behavioural and mechanical isolation — promote divergence and can contribute to speciation.

Models

Sewall Wright's island model describes populations exchanging migrants at a uniform rate. When the product of effective population size and migration rate (Nm) exceeds about one, drift alone cannot cause substantial differentiation; measures such as FST summarise how allele frequencies differ among populations and are used to infer historical gene flow. Stepping-stone and isolation-by-distance models relax the assumption of uniform mixing.

Effects

Gene flow can introduce novel, potentially adaptive alleles into a population — a process called adaptive introgression — but it can also swamp local adaptation, a phenomenon known as migration load or outbreeding depression in some contexts. In conservation biology, managed gene flow ("genetic rescue") has been used to restore diversity to small inbred populations, as when Texas cougars were introduced to the Florida panther population in 1995.

Human examples

Ancient DNA studies have revealed extensive gene flow among prehistoric human groups, including admixture between modern humans and archaic populations such as Neanderthals and Denisovans. Contemporary human populations likewise exchange genes wherever migration occurs, and allele frequencies grade into one another along migration routes.
