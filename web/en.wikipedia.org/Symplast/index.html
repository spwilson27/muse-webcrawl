Symplast

Symplast
Field: Plant physiology
Connections: Plasmodesmata
Contrast: Apoplast

The symplast of a plant is the interconnected network of living protoplasts formed by the cytoplasm of adjacent cells joined through plasmodesmata, bounded by their plasma membranes. Water and solutes moving through the symplast travel from cell to cell without crossing a membrane after their initial uptake.

Function

The symplastic pathway is one of two main routes by which water and dissolved minerals absorbed by roots reach the xylem, the other being the apoplastic pathway through cell walls and intercellular spaces. In the root, the Casparian strip of the endodermis blocks the apoplast and forces everything entering the stele to pass through the selective membranes of the symplast, giving the plant control over nutrient uptake.

Significance

Through plasmodesmata the symplast also carries sugars, amino acids, signalling molecules, and even some viruses between cells, making the plant body a partially unified physiological continuum rather than a mere aggregate of isolated cells. The term was introduced by the German botanist Ernst Münch in 1930.
