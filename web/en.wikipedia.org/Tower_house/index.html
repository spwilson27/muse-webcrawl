Tower house

A tower house is a type of fortified dwelling built principally in the later Middle Ages and early modern period, in which the rooms are stacked vertically in a single tower of stone. Tower houses are especially characteristic of Scotland and Ireland, but examples occur across Europe and beyond, from the pele towers of the Anglo-Scottish border to the tower houses of Yemen and the Caucasus.

Design

A typical tower house rises three to five storeys, with thick rubble-stone walls, small windows, and a single easily defended entrance, sometimes reached by an external stair or ladder. The ground floor served as a store or byre, with vaulted ceilings; the principal living hall occupied the first floor, with private chambers above and battlements or a parapet walk at the top. Many were surrounded by a walled courtyard, the barmkin or bawn, containing outbuildings.

Distribution and history

In Ireland, thousands of tower houses were built between roughly 1400 and 1650, encouraged by a statute of 1429 offering a subsidy for defensive towers in the Pale. In Scotland, tower houses were the standard residence of lairds and clan chiefs, evolving into the turreted Scottish baronial style. As political conditions stabilized in the seventeenth century, owners added more comfortable wings, and the purely defensive tower gave way to the country house.
