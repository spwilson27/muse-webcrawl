Scapa Flow

Scapa Flow, Orkney Islands, Scotland.

Scapa Flow is a body of water in the Orkney Islands, Scotland, sheltered by the islands of Mainland, Graemsay, Burray, South Ronaldsay and Hoy. Its sheltered waters have played an important role in travel, trade and conflict throughout the centuries. Vikings anchored their longships in Scapa Flow more than a thousand years ago, and it is best known as the site of the United Kingdom's chief naval base during the First and Second World Wars.

History

The name Scapa Flow comes from the Old Norse Skalpaflói, meaning "bay of the long isthmus", referring to the thin strip of land at Kirkwall joining the East and West Mainlands.

First World War

During the First World War, Scapa Flow was the main base of the British Grand Fleet. Following the Armistice, the German High Seas Fleet was interned there in November 1918. On 21 June 1919, the German commanding officer, Rear Admiral Ludwig von Reuter, gave the order to scuttle the fleet to prevent the ships being seized by the Allies. Fifty-two of the 74 interned vessels sank; many of the wrecks were later salvaged, but those remaining are popular diving sites.

Second World War

On 14 October 1939, the German submarine U-47, under Kapitänleutnant Günther Prien, penetrated Scapa Flow and sank the battleship HMS Royal Oak with the loss of 835 lives. Following the attack, the First Lord of the Admiralty, Winston Churchill, ordered the construction of permanent barriers, the Churchill Barriers, which still link several of the islands and carry a road.

Post-war

The naval base closed in 1956. The Scapa Flow Visitor Centre is located at Lyness on Hoy. The remaining wrecks are protected as maritime war graves and are among the most famous wreck-diving destinations in the world.
