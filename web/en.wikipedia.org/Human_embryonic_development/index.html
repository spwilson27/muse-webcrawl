Human embryonic development

Human embryonic development, or human embryogenesis, is the development and formation of the human embryo. It is characterised by the processes of cell division and cellular differentiation of the embryo that occurs during the early stages of development. In biological terms, the development of the human body entails growth from a one-celled zygote to an adult human being.

The embryonic period covers the first eight weeks after fertilization; at the beginning of the ninth week the embryo is termed a fetus. Development is described by Carnegie stages, 23 standardized stages based on structural features rather than size or age alone.

Fertilization and the first week

Fertilization usually occurs in the ampulla of the fallopian tube, where a sperm penetrates the oocyte and the two pronuclei fuse to form a diploid zygote. The zygote undergoes rapid mitotic divisions called cleavage as it travels toward the uterus, becoming a solid ball of cells (the morula) and then a blastocyst — a hollow structure with an outer trophoblast layer and an inner cell mass, the embryoblast, which will form the embryo proper. Around day 6–7 the blastocyst begins implantation into the endometrium.

Second week: implantation and the bilaminar disc

Implantation completes during the second week. The trophoblast differentiates into cytotrophoblast and syncytiotrophoblast, which invades the uterine wall and establishes the earliest maternal–embryonic circulation. The inner cell mass forms a bilaminar disc of epiblast and hypoblast, with the amniotic cavity and yolk sac appearing on either side.

Third week: gastrulation

Gastrulation transforms the bilaminar disc into a trilaminar embryo with the three germ layers — ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm — from which all tissues derive. The primitive streak appears on the epiblast; cells migrating through it form the mesoderm and endoderm, and the notochord develops along the midline, inducing the overlying ectoderm to form the neural plate, the beginning of the central nervous system.

Weeks 4–8: organogenesis

During weeks four to eight all major organ systems are established. The neural tube closes (failure produces spina bifida or anencephaly); the heart tube begins beating around day 22 and loops into its definitive shape; somites segment the paraxial mesoderm; limb buds appear in week four; the face forms from merging prominences; and the embryo folds into a cylindrical body plan. By the end of week eight the embryo is about 30 mm long and recognizably human, though the head is disproportionately large. This is the period of greatest sensitivity to teratogens — agents such as alcohol, certain drugs and infections that can cause congenital malformations.

Germ layers and their derivatives

  • Ectoderm — nervous system, epidermis, hair, nails, enamel of teeth, sensory organs.
  • Mesoderm — muscle, bone, cartilage, blood and blood vessels, kidneys, gonads, dermis.
  • Endoderm — lining of the gut and respiratory tract, liver, pancreas, thyroid, bladder.
