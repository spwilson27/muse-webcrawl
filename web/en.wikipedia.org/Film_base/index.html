Film base

A film base is a transparent substrate which acts as a support medium for the photosensitive emulsion of photographic film. The base must be flexible, dimensionally stable, optically clear and strong enough to withstand the mechanical stresses of cameras and projectors while remaining chemically compatible with the emulsion layers coated upon it.

Materials

The earliest flexible film, introduced by George Eastman's company in 1889, used cellulose nitrate. Nitrate base gave excellent clarity and toughness but was dangerously flammable — chemically similar to guncotton — and decomposed over time, causing many archive fires and the loss of a large proportion of silent-era cinema. It was phased out by the early 1950s in favour of cellulose acetate "safety film", first diacetate and then triacetate. Acetate, however, is subject to "vinegar syndrome", an autocatalytic decay releasing acetic acid. From the 1990s, polyester (PET) base became common for many applications; it is far more chemically stable and tear-resistant, and is preferred for archival and high-use films, although its toughness can damage camera mechanisms in a jam and it historically was harder to splice.

Preservation

The differing decay mechanisms of nitrate, acetate and polyester bases shape film archive practice: nitrate requires isolated, fire-safe cold storage; acetate benefits from cold, dry conditions that slow vinegar syndrome; and polyester offers the longest expected life for master materials.
