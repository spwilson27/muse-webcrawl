Unconscious mind

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The unconscious mind (or the unconscious) consists of the processes in the mind that occur automatically and are not available to introspection and include thought processes, memories, interests, and motivations. Although these processes exist well under the surface of conscious awareness, they are theorised to influence behaviour. The term was coined by the 18th-century German Romantic philosopher Friedrich Schelling and later introduced into English by Samuel Taylor Coleridge.

The concept was central to the psychoanalysis of Sigmund Freud, who held that unacceptable urges, thoughts, and memories are repressed into the unconscious, from which they affect dreams, slips of the tongue ("Freudian slips"), and neurotic symptoms. Carl Jung developed the idea further, distinguishing a personal unconscious from a collective unconscious shared by all humans and populated by archetypes.

History

Ideas about mental processes outside awareness go back to antiquity and appear in Hindu, Buddhist, and Western philosophy. Thinkers such as Leibniz, Spinoza, Schopenhauer, and Eduard von Hartmann all wrote about unconscious mentation. In the later 19th century, psychologists including William James and Pierre Janet explored the idea experimentally and clinically, and Jean-Martin Charcot's work on hysteria and hypnosis influenced Freud's development of psychoanalytic theory.

Psychoanalytic view

In Freud's topographic model, the mind is divided into conscious, preconscious, and unconscious systems. In his later structural model, the id is entirely unconscious, and much of the ego and superego operates unconsciously as well, including defence mechanisms such as repression, denial, and projection. Psychoanalytic therapy aimed to make unconscious material conscious through free association and dream interpretation.

Cognitive psychology

Modern cognitive psychology accepts extensive nonconscious processing but describes it differently — often as the "cognitive unconscious": automatic processes such as implicit memory, priming, and procedural learning that proceed without awareness and without the emotionally charged, dynamic character Freud proposed. Much experimental work on subliminal perception, blindsight, and automaticity falls in this area. The scientific standing of the distinctively psychoanalytic unconscious remains contested.
