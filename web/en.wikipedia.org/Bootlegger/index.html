Bootlegger

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A bootlegger is a person who makes, transports, or sells alcoholic beverages illegally. The term became especially prominent in the United States during Prohibition (1920–1933), when the Eighteenth Amendment banned the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquors nationwide. The word is thought to derive from the practice of concealing a flask of liquor in the leg of one's boot.

Prohibition era

When Prohibition took effect in January 1920, an enormous black market in alcohol quickly emerged. Bootleggers supplied speakeasies — illegal bars — with liquor smuggled from Canada, Mexico, and the Caribbean ("rum-running"), or distilled domestically as moonshine and "bathtub gin." Organized crime syndicates, most famously that of Al Capone in Chicago, grew wealthy and powerful on the trade, and violent turf wars between gangs became a hallmark of the era.

Moonshine and rum-running

In rural areas, particularly Appalachia, illicit distillers produced moonshine in hidden stills and transported it at night in modified automobiles — a practice that later gave rise to stock car racing and NASCAR. Along the coasts, rum-runners operated fleets of fast boats that waited in international waters ("Rum Row") beyond the reach of the Coast Guard.

End of Prohibition

The Twenty-first Amendment repealed Prohibition in 1933, ending the great age of bootlegging in the United States, though illegal distilling persisted in dry counties and to evade taxes. The term "bootleg" has since been extended to any unauthorized production or distribution, including bootleg recordings of concerts.
