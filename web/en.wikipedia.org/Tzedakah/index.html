Tzedakah

A tzedakah box, traditionally kept in Jewish homes
Hebrewצְדָקָה
Literally"Righteousness", "justice"
TypeMitzvah (commandment)

Tzedakah (Hebrew: צְדָקָה, ṣədāqā) is the Hebrew word for acts of charity and philanthropy in Judaism. Unlike the English word "charity", which suggests voluntary benevolence, tzedakah is understood as a religious obligation — a mitzvah — rooted in the word for righteousness and justice. Jewish tradition holds that giving to the poor is not optional generosity but a duty owed in justice, and that even a person who receives tzedakah is obliged to give it.

Principles

The medieval philosopher Maimonides formulated a famous hierarchy of giving, the "Ladder of Tzedakah", ranking forms of charity from least to most meritorious. The lowest level is giving grudgingly; higher levels include giving anonymously and giving before being asked; the highest is helping someone become self-sufficient, for example through a job, a partnership, or an interest-free loan. Traditional practice includes giving a tithe (ma'aser) of one's income, leaving the corners of fields for the poor (pe'ah), and communal charity funds supervised by officials (gabbaim).

Tzedakah boxes (pushkes) are commonly kept in Jewish homes and synagogues, and coins are traditionally placed in them before Shabbat and holidays.
