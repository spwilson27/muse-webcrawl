Charles Perrault


Charles Perrault (1628–1703), French author of the Tales of Mother Goose.

Charles Perrault (12 January 1628 – 16 May 1703) was a French author and member of the Académie Française. He laid the foundations for a new literary genre, the fairy tale, with his works derived from earlier folk tales, published in his 1697 book Histoires ou contes du temps passé (Stories or Tales from Past Times), subtitled Les Contes de ma mère l'Oye (The Tales of Mother Goose).

Life and career

Born into a wealthy Parisian family, Perrault trained as a lawyer and became a senior civil servant under Jean-Baptiste Colbert, helping to found the Academy of Sciences and to revive the Academy of Inscriptions. He was elected to the Académie Française in 1671. In 1687 his poem Le Siècle de Louis le Grand provoked the Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns, in which Perrault championed the moderns against admirers of classical antiquity.

The fairy tales

The Contes contained eight prose tales, including "Sleeping Beauty", "Little Red Riding Hood", "Bluebeard", "Puss in Boots" and "Cinderella" — versions that fixed these stories in the Western canon. Published under his son Pierre's name, the tales were written in a polished, ironic style with a rhymed moral at the end, appealing to the salon culture of the court of Louis XIV as much as to children.

Legacy

Perrault's Cinderella supplied the pumpkin, fairy godmother and glass slippers familiar from later retellings. His tales influenced the Brothers Grimm and every subsequent collector and adaptor of fairy stories, and many have been turned into operas, ballets and films.
