Reconstruction Amendments

The Reconstruction Amendments, or Reconstruction Era Amendments, are the Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth amendments to the United States Constitution, adopted between 1865 and 1870 in the aftermath of the American Civil War. Together they abolished slavery, granted citizenship and equal protection of the laws, and prohibited the denial of voting rights on the basis of race — fundamentally transforming American federalism and civil rights.

Thirteenth Amendment

Ratified on 6 December 1865, the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery and involuntary servitude throughout the United States, except as punishment for a crime. It went beyond the Emancipation Proclamation of 1863, which had applied only to states in rebellion, and gave Congress power to enforce abolition by appropriate legislation.

Fourteenth Amendment

Ratified on 9 July 1868, the Fourteenth Amendment granted citizenship to all persons born or naturalised in the United States — including the formerly enslaved — and prohibited states from depriving any person of life, liberty or property without due process of law, or from denying any person equal protection of the laws. It also addressed representation, disqualified certain former Confederate officials from office, and repudiated Confederate debts. Its Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses have become among the most litigated provisions in American constitutional law, underpinning decisions on incorporation of the Bill of Rights, civil rights and substantive rights.

Fifteenth Amendment

Ratified on 3 February 1870, the Fifteenth Amendment prohibited the federal government and the states from denying a citizen the right to vote on account of "race, color, or previous condition of servitude". It enfranchised African American men in law, though literacy tests, poll taxes and violence later disenfranchised many in the South until the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Legacy

Historians describe the Reconstruction Amendments as a "Second Founding" that redefined the Constitution around principles of equality and national citizenship. Though their promises were curtailed during the Jim Crow era — through Supreme Court decisions such as the Slaughter-House Cases (1873) and Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) — the amendments later supplied the legal foundation for the civil rights movement and landmark rulings such as Brown v. Board of Education (1954).
