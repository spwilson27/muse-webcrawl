Non-official cover

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A non-official cover (NOC) is an intelligence officer who assumes a covert role with no apparent connection to the government they serve — for example, posing as a businessperson, journalist, aid worker or academic — rather than working from an embassy under diplomatic cover. The term is principally associated with the United States Central Intelligence Agency, though other services use equivalent arrangements. Because NOC officers cannot claim diplomatic immunity, they face far greater personal risk, including arrest, imprisonment or execution, if their true role is discovered.

Comparison with official cover

Officers under official cover work at an embassy or consulate with a plausible diplomatic position; if caught spying they are typically declared persona non grata and expelled. NOC officers, by contrast, must build and sustain an entirely independent "legend" — a fabricated or transplanted biography, employment and credentials able to withstand scrutiny. The CIA is reported to maintain a proprietary infrastructure of front companies and backstopped identities to support them. NOC assignments can allow access to targets unreachable from within an embassy and make the officer's espionage harder to detect, but require far more time and money to establish.

In law and popular culture

United States law, including the Intelligence Identities Protection Act of 1982, criminalizes the unauthorized disclosure of covert agents' identities; the 2003 outing of CIA officer Valerie Plame, who had served under forms of non-official cover, became a major political scandal. NOC officers feature prominently in espionage fiction, where the depth of their assumed lives and the danger of exposure are standard themes.
