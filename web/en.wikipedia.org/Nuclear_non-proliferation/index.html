Nuclear non-proliferation refers to the international efforts to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons and weapons-usable materials, while permitting peaceful uses of nuclear energy under safeguards. Its legal centrepiece is the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT), opened for signature in 1968 and in force since 1970.

The NPT bargain

The treaty rests on three pillars: non-proliferation (non-nuclear-weapon states agree not to acquire weapons), disarmament (the five recognised nuclear-weapon states — the US, Russia, UK, France and China — commit to pursue disarmament), and peaceful use (all parties may develop nuclear energy under IAEA safeguards). Review conferences are held every five years.

Supporting regime

Around the NPT sits a wider regime: IAEA safeguards inspections, nuclear-weapon-free zones, export-control groups such as the Nuclear Suppliers Group, the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty, and bilateral arms control. India, Israel and Pakistan never joined the NPT; North Korea withdrew in 2003 and has tested nuclear devices since 2006.

Challenges

Disputes over Iran's programme, North Korea's arsenal, stalled disarmament and the 2017 Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons — supported by many non-nuclear states but rejected by the nuclear-armed states — continue to strain the regime.
