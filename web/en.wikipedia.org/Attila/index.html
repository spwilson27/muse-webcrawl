Attila (c. 406 – 453), frequently called Attila the Hun, was the ruler of the Huns from 434 until his death in early 453. He was also the ruler of a tribal confederation consisting of Huns, Ostrogoths, Alans and Bulgars, among others, in Central and Eastern Europe.

During his reign, he was one of the most feared enemies of the Western and Eastern Roman Empires. He crossed the Danube twice and plundered the Balkans but was unable to take Constantinople. In 451 he invaded the Roman province of Gaul, where he was stopped at the Battle of the Catalaunian Plains. In 452 he invaded Italy, devastating the northern cities, but his army retreated without taking Rome.

Early life and accession

Attila was born into the leading family of the Huns around 406. His father Mundzuk was a nobleman of the royal clan; his uncles Octar and Rugila jointly ruled the Huns. In 434 the brothers Attila and Bleda inherited the empire from Rugila. In 445 Attila murdered Bleda — according to tradition on a hunting trip — and became sole ruler.

Campaigns in the Balkans

Exploiting Roman weakness, Attila repeatedly invaded the Eastern Roman provinces south of the Danube in the 440s, sacking cities such as Singidunum, Sirmium, Naissus and Serdica, and extracting ever-larger annual tributes of gold under successive treaties, including the Treaty of Margus and the Treaty of Anatolius. The historian Priscus, who visited Attila's court in 449 as part of an embassy, left the most important surviving eyewitness account of the Hun king and his wooden palace on the Hungarian plain.

Campaign in Gaul

In 451 Attila invaded Gaul with a vast army of Huns and subject peoples. After sacking Metz and other cities he besieged Orléans, but a coalition of Romans under Flavius Aetius and Visigoths under Theodoric I confronted him at the Catalaunian Plains near modern Châlons-en-Champagne. The battle, among the bloodiest of the ancient world, ended Attila's advance; Theodoric was killed, and Attila withdrew beyond the Rhine.

Invasion of Italy and death

In 452 Attila invaded Italy, destroying Aquileia so completely, tradition says, that its site was hard to find, and taking Milan and Padua. Famine and disease in Italy, together with an embassy from Pope Leo I (whose meeting with Attila became a famous subject of legend and art), preceded his withdrawal. He died in 453 at his camp after a night of heavy drinking following his marriage to a young bride named Ildico: according to Priscus, a nosebleed choked him in his sleep. His sons Ellac, Dengizich and Irnak divided the empire, which rapidly disintegrated after the Battle of Nedao in 454.

Legacy

In much of Western Europe Attila is remembered as the epitome of cruelty and rapacity, and in Latin tradition he was styled flagellum Dei, "the Scourge of God". In Hungarian and Turkish tradition he is instead a celebrated hero; Attila (Etele) remains a common given name in Hungary. He appears as Etzel in the Nibelungenlied and as Atli in Norse sagas.
