Moat


Image placeholder: a moated castle

A moat is a deep, broad ditch, either dry or filled with water, that is dug and surrounds a castle, fortification, building, or town, historically to provide it with a preliminary line of defence. In some places moats evolved into more extensive water defences, including natural or artificial lakes, dams and sluices. In older fortifications, such as hillforts, they are usually referred to simply as ditches, although they functioned in much the same way. In later periods, moats could also discourage vermin and control access for ceremonial or practical purposes.

Historical use

Medieval castles across Europe were frequently surrounded by moats, which forced attackers to cross exposed ground, slowed siege engines, and — crucially — prevented mining, since a water-filled ditch collapsed tunnels dug beneath the walls. Access was normally by a drawbridge that could be raised or destroyed. In Japan, moats are a prominent feature of castles such as Himeji and of the Imperial Palace in Tokyo. Famous examples in England include Bodiam Castle in Sussex, whose wide moat is as much a statement of status as a defence, and the Tower of London, whose moat was drained in the 19th century and is now grassed.

Modern uses

The moat principle persists in modern practice: security barriers around data centres and prisons, "moats" of cleared ground at military bases, and water features around zoos' animal enclosures all exploit the same idea. The term is also used metaphorically in business (Warren Buffett's "economic moat" for a durable competitive advantage) and in architecture, where dry moats admit light to basement storeys.
