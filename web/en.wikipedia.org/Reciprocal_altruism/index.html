Reciprocal altruism

In evolutionary biology, reciprocal altruism is a behaviour whereby an organism acts in a manner that temporarily reduces its fitness while increasing another organism's fitness, with the expectation that the other organism will act in a similar manner at a later time. The concept was initially developed by Robert Trivers to explain the evolution of cooperation as instances of mutually altruistic exchanges between unrelated organisms.

Reciprocal altruism: help given today is repaid in the future.

History

Trivers introduced the concept in his 1971 paper "The Evolution of Reciprocal Altruism". Together with W. D. Hamilton's theory of kin selection, reciprocal altruism became one of the two main explanations for how cooperative behaviour could evolve despite the apparent advantage of selfishness.

Examples in nature

A classic example is blood sharing in common vampire bats (Desmodus rotundus). Bats that have failed to find a blood meal may starve within a few nights; roost-mates that fed successfully often regurgitate blood to feed them. Studies by Gerald Wilkinson showed that bats are more likely to share with individuals that had shared with them in the past, and that unrelated roost-mates account for a large fraction of donations. Cleaner fish, which remove parasites from larger "client" fish, are another widely cited example: clients refrain from eating cleaners and sometimes queue for their services, while cleaners may "cheat" by biting client mucus; clients respond by leaving or chasing the cleaner.

Game theory

Reciprocal altruism is closely connected with the repeated prisoner's dilemma. Robert Axelrod's computer tournaments showed that the simple strategy tit for tat — cooperate first, then copy the partner's previous move — was remarkably successful. For reciprocity to work, interactions must be repeated, individuals must be able to recognize one another (or at least remember past behaviour), and cheaters must face consequences such as withdrawal of future help.

Conditions for evolution

Trivers identified three conditions favouring reciprocal altruism: repeated interactions between the same individuals, a benefit to the recipient that exceeds the cost to the donor, and the ability to detect and punish cheaters. These conditions are most often met in long-lived, social species with stable groups.

Criticism

Some biologists argue that many apparent cases can be explained by kin selection, by-product mutualism, or pseudo-reciprocity, in which helping is immediately beneficial to the helper. The relative importance of true reciprocity in non-human animals remains debated, though its role in human cooperation — often mediated by reputation and moral emotions such as gratitude, guilt, and outrage — is widely accepted.
