Temple of Artemis

The Temple of Artemis (Greek: Ἀρτεμίσιον, Artemísion; Latin: Templum Dianae) was a Greek temple dedicated to the goddess Artemis at Ephesus, on the Aegean coast of what is now Turkey. One of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, it was completely rebuilt twice and was by far the largest temple of the Greek world — about 115 by 55 metres (377 by 180 ft), roughly four times the footprint of the Parthenon — before its final destruction in late antiquity. Today only foundations and a single reconstructed column mark the site.

History

Cult at the site dates to the Bronze Age; the local Artemis absorbed features of the Anatolian mother goddess, depicted with rows of bulbous adornments. An early temple was destroyed by flood in the 7th century BCE. King Croesus of Lydia financed a grand marble rebuilding begun around 550 BCE by the Cretan architect Chersiphron and his son Metagenes, which took many decades. On the night of 21 July 356 BCE — by tradition the night Alexander the Great was born — a man named Herostratus burned the temple down purely to win fame, an act of arson so notorious that Ephesus forbade mentioning his name on pain of death (the origin of the term "Herostratic fame").

The citizens rebuilt the temple on an even grander scale, with work by Paeonius and Demetrius; Alexander offered to fund it in exchange for an inscription and was politely refused. The new Artemision, with 127 Ionic columns some 18 metres high, served also as an asylum and a kind of bank, and was admired by Antipater of Sidon, who wrote that having seen it, all other wonders lost their brilliance. Plundered by Nero, damaged by Gothic raiders in 262 CE, and progressively dismantled as Christianity rose, the temple was defunct by the 5th century; its stones were reused in buildings including Hagia Sophia.

Rediscovery

The site was located in 1869 by John Turtle Wood, sponsored by the British Museum, whose excavations recovered sculptured column drums and the famous coin hoard; Austrian excavations continued in the 20th century. Artefacts are displayed in the British Museum and the Ephesus Archaeological Museum in Selçuk.
