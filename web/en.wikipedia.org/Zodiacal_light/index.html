Zodiacal light

Zodiacal light is a faint, diffuse glow of light visible in the night sky, extending upward from the horizon along the ecliptic (the zodiac). It is caused by sunlight scattered by interplanetary dust particles that lie in a lens-shaped cloud in the plane of the Solar System, known as the zodiacal cloud.

Observation

The phenomenon is best seen after sunset in spring and before sunrise in autumn in mid-northern latitudes, when the ecliptic stands steepest to the horizon, and is easiest to observe from the tropics. It appears as a triangular wedge of light, sometimes called a "false dawn" — a term with a long history in Islamic tradition, where distinguishing it from true dawn matters for the timing of prayers and fasting. The related gegenschein is a faint bright spot in the zodiacal band directly opposite the Sun.

Origin of the dust

The dust is continually replenished, chiefly by comet activity and collisions among asteroids; grains slowly spiral into the Sun under the Poynting–Robertson effect. Spacecraft measurements and infrared observations from satellites such as IRAS and COBE have mapped the zodiacal cloud in detail, and interplanetary dust is a significant foreground in observations of the cosmic microwave background.
