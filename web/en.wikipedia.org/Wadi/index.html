A wadi (Arabic: وَادِي, romanized: wādī) is the Arabic term traditionally used to describe a river valley. In some instances, it may refer to a wet (ephemeral) riverbed that contains water only when heavy rain occurs.

Wadis are generally located in the flat or slightly rolling desert regions of the Arabian Peninsula, North Africa, and the Levant. Permanent channels do not exist because of the lack of continual water flow. Wadis have braided stream patterns because of the deficiency of water and the abundance of sediments. Water percolates down into the stream bed, causing an abrupt loss of stream velocity and facilitating sediment deposition.

Hydrology

Wadis can fill rapidly during sudden, intense rainstorms, producing dangerous flash floods. Because storms in deserts may be highly localized, a wadi can flood even when rain is not visible locally. Historically, caravan routes and settlements in arid regions were often located along wadis because groundwater near the surface supports vegetation, wells and occasional pools in otherwise waterless terrain.

Terminology and examples

The term appears in many place names across the Arab world and Iberia, where it entered Spanish river names such as Guadalquivir (from wādī al-kabīr, "the great river"). Famous wadis include Wadi Rum in Jordan, Wadi Mujib in Jordan, Wadi Shab in Oman, and the seasonal watercourses of the Sinai Peninsula. In the southwestern United States and northern Mexico, similar features are called arroyos.
