William Thomson, 1st Baron Kelvin

William Thomson, 1st Baron Kelvin
Born: 26 June 1824, Belfast, Ireland
Died: 17 December 1907, Largs, Scotland
Known for: Laws of thermodynamics; absolute temperature scale; transatlantic telegraph

William Thomson, 1st Baron Kelvin (26 June 1824 – 17 December 1907) was a British mathematician, mathematical physicist, and engineer born in Belfast. As professor of natural philosophy at the University of Glasgow for 53 years, he did important work in the mathematical analysis of electricity and in the formulation of the first and second laws of thermodynamics. The SI unit of temperature, the kelvin, is named after him.

Thermodynamics

In 1848 Thomson proposed an absolute temperature scale based on Carnot's theory, with absolute zero as its null point. With James Prescott Joule he studied the thermal effects of gas expansion (the Joule–Thomson effect). His 1851 paper "On the Dynamical Theory of Heat" gave one of the first statements of the second law of thermodynamics — that there is a universal tendency toward the dissipation of mechanical energy, the concept later formalised as entropy. His estimate of the age of the Earth (initially 20–400 million years, later narrowed) famously conflicted with geologists and evolutionists until radioactivity explained the Earth's internal heat.

Telegraphy and engineering

Kelvin played a central role in laying the first transatlantic telegraph cable, serving as electrician aboard the cable ships and inventing the sensitive mirror galvanometer and the siphon recorder that made commercial telegraphy across the Atlantic practical. He earned a fortune from patents and was knighted in 1866 for his work on the cable. In 1892 he was raised to the peerage as Baron Kelvin of Largs, the first British scientist so honoured. His contributions also include the Kelvin–Stokes theorem, the Kelvin circulation theorem, and the Kelvin–Helmholtz instability.

He is buried in Westminster Abbey, next to Isaac Newton.
