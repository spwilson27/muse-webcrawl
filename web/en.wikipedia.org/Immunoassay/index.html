Immunoassay

Immunoassay
[Image: A 96-well microplate used for an ELISA test]
PurposeMeasure the presence or concentration of an analyte in solution
BasisSpecific binding of antibody to antigen
Common typesELISA, radioimmunoassay, lateral flow

An immunoassay is a biochemical test that measures the presence or concentration of a molecule in a solution through the use of an antibody or an antigen. Because antibodies bind their targets with very high specificity, immunoassays can detect minute quantities of substances such as hormones, drugs, infectious agents, and tumour markers in complex mixtures like blood or urine.

Principles and types

In a typical immunoassay the target analyte is captured by an immobilised antibody, and a second, labelled antibody (or antigen) produces a measurable signal — from an enzyme, radioisotope, fluorescent dye, or magnetic particle — proportional to the amount of analyte. The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is the most widely used format; the radioimmunoassay, developed by Rosalyn Yalow and Solomon Berson in the late 1950s for measuring insulin, earned Yalow a Nobel Prize. Rapid lateral-flow immunoassays underlie home pregnancy tests and many point-of-care antigen tests. Assays are classified as competitive or non-competitive, and homogeneous (no separation step) or heterogeneous.

Applications

Immunoassays are fundamental in clinical diagnostics (thyroid and fertility hormones, cardiac troponin, infectious disease screening, blood-bank screening for HIV and hepatitis), drug testing, pregnancy testing, allergy testing, food safety, and biomedical research.
