Citrus is a genus of flowering trees and shrubs in the rue family, Rutaceae. Plants in the genus produce citrus fruits, including important crops such as oranges, lemons, grapefruits, pomelos, and limes. The genus originated in South, East, and Southeast Asia and spread throughout the world through trade and cultivation.

Taxonomy and origins

Citrus taxonomy is notoriously complex because many commercial fruits are hybrids. Genomic research has shown that most cultivated citrus derive from a small number of ancestral species — principally the citron (Citrus medica), pomelo (C. maxima), and mandarin (C. reticulata), together with papedas and the Australian finger lime. For example, the sweet orange is a pomelo × mandarin hybrid, and the lemon derives from citron crossed with a sour orange lineage. The center of origin of the genus lies in the foothills of the Himalayas and surrounding regions.

Description

Citrus plants are evergreen shrubs or small trees with glossy, ovate leaves, often thorny stems, and fragrant white or pinkish five-petaled flowers. The fruit is a hesperidium, a specialized berry with a leathery rind containing aromatic oil glands, a spongy white pith (albedo), and segments filled with juice vesicles.

Cultivation and uses

Citrus fruits are grown in tropical and subtropical climates worldwide; leading producers include China, Brazil, India, and Spain. Fruits are eaten fresh, juiced, and processed for flavorings, oils, and pectin. They are valued for their vitamin C content — citrus carried on ships historically prevented scurvy — and for culinary acidity. Major threats to production include citrus greening disease (huanglongbing), spread by the Asian citrus psyllid, which has devastated groves in Florida and elsewhere.
