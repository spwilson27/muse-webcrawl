Running shoe

A running shoe is a lightweight shoe designed specifically for the sport of running. Compared with general trainers, running shoes are built to cushion impact, support the motion of the foot through the gait cycle and provide traction, and are typically replaced every 500–800 kilometres as their foam loses resilience.

Design

The key element is the midsole, made of foamed polymers such as EVA (ethylene-vinyl acetate) or newer PEBA-based foams, which absorb impact forces of roughly two to three times body weight with each stride. The difference in height between heel and forefoot — the "drop" — traditionally measured 10–12 mm but now varies from zero-drop designs to high-stack racing shoes. Uppers are usually engineered mesh; outsoles use blown or carbon rubber in high-wear areas.

History

Spiked and plimsoll-style shoes served runners into the twentieth century. The modern cushioned shoe emerged with the 1970s running boom: companies such as Nike (founded as Blue Ribbon Sports by Bill Bowerman and Phil Knight), Adidas, New Balance and Brooks introduced EVA midsoles and motion-control designs. Categories proliferated — neutral, stability and motion-control shoes, minimalist and barefoot-style models in the 2000s, and maximalist cushioned shoes thereafter.

Carbon-plated racers

From 2016, racing shoes combining thick, highly resilient foam with a rigid carbon-fibre plate — pioneered by the Nike Vaporfly — produced measurable improvements in running economy, contributing to a wave of world records and prompting World Athletics to regulate sole thickness and plate configuration in 2020. The merits and fitting of such "super shoes" remain debated among scientists and runners alike.
