Hsp90

Hsp90
Full name: Heat shock protein 90
Class: Molecular chaperone
Mass: ~90 kDa
Function: Protein folding, client maturation
Human genes: HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, HSP90B1, TRAP1

Hsp90 (heat shock protein 90) is a molecular chaperone protein that assists other proteins to fold properly, stabilises proteins against heat stress, and aids in protein degradation. It is one of the most abundant proteins in cells — comprising roughly 1–2% of total cellular protein under normal conditions and increasing under stress — and is highly conserved from bacteria to humans.

Function

Hsp90 works as a dimer in an ATP-driven cycle, clamping and remodelling "client" proteins. Unlike general chaperones that fold newborn chains, Hsp90 specialises in the late-stage maturation and activation of specific clients, including steroid hormone receptors, many protein kinases (such as Src and Raf), transcription factors, and telomerase. Its activity depends on co-chaperones including Hsp70, Hop/Sti1, Cdc37 and p23, which deliver clients and regulate the cycle.

Clinical significance

Because cancer cells depend on Hsp90 to stabilise mutated and overexpressed oncoproteins, Hsp90 inhibitors — beginning with the natural product geldanamycin and its derivative 17-AAG (tanespimycin) — have been investigated extensively as anticancer drugs, though toxicity and resistance have limited clinical success. Hsp90 also buffers genetic variation, acting as a "capacitor" of phenotypic evolution: when its function is impaired by stress, hidden genetic variants can be revealed, a phenomenon first shown by Susan Lindquist in fruit flies and Arabidopsis.
