Texas annexation

The Texas annexation was the annexation of the Republic of Texas by the United States in 1845, admitting Texas as the 28th state on 29 December 1845. The move was a central issue in American politics of the 1840s and a direct cause of the Mexican–American War.

Background

Texas declared independence from Mexico in 1836 during the Texas Revolution, defeating General Santa Anna at the Battle of San Jacinto. The republic sought annexation by the United States from its earliest days, but Presidents Andrew Jackson and Martin Van Buren hesitated: Mexico still claimed Texas, and admitting a large new slave state threatened the sectional balance in Congress.

The Tyler treaty and its rejection

President John Tyler concluded an annexation treaty in April 1844, but the Senate rejected it, 35–16, amid accusations that it was a pro-slavery scheme. Annexation became the dominant issue in the election of 1844, won by the pro-annexation Democrat James K. Polk over Henry Clay.

Joint resolution and statehood

Because a treaty required a two-thirds Senate majority, Congress instead passed a joint resolution — requiring only simple majorities — offering annexation terms: Texas could enter as a state, keep its public lands, and potentially subdivide into up to five states. The resolution passed in February 1845 and was signed by Tyler on 1 March. Texas accepted; a state constitution was ratified, and Texas entered the Union on 29 December 1845.

Consequences

Mexico, which had warned that annexation would mean war, broke relations. Disputes over whether Texas's southern border was the Nueces River or the Rio Grande led to the Thornton Affair and the Mexican–American War (1846–1848), ending with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo. Texas's western land claims were settled in the Compromise of 1850, when Texas ceded territory to the federal government for $10 million.
