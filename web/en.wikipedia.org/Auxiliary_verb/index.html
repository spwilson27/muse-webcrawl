Auxiliary verb

In "She has been reading", has and been are auxiliary verbs supporting the main verb reading.

An auxiliary verb (abbreviated aux), also called a helping verb, is a verb that adds functional or grammatical meaning to a clause — expressing tense, aspect, modality, voice or emphasis — rather than supplying the main semantic content, which is provided by a principal verb. Auxiliaries are a feature of many languages, including English, the Romance languages and German.

In English

English auxiliaries fall into two groups. The primary auxiliaries — be, have and do — build compound tenses and constructions: be forms the progressive ("is running") and the passive ("was seen"); have forms the perfect ("has eaten"); do appears in questions, negation and emphasis ("Do you know?", "I do not know", "I do know"). The modal auxiliaries — can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would (and marginally ought, need, dare) — express possibility, permission, obligation and prediction.

English auxiliaries have distinctive grammatical behaviour, summarised by the acronym NICE: they take direct Negation ("is not"), Invert with the subject in questions ("Is she...?"), allow Code (ellipsis of the main verb: "She can swim and he can too"), and carry Emphasis ("She did come"). Unlike main verbs, modal auxiliaries are defective — they have no infinitive or participles — and are followed by the bare infinitive.

In other languages

Romance languages use forms of "have" and "be" to build perfect tenses, with French and Italian choosing the auxiliary partly by verb class; German likewise splits perfect formation between haben and sein. Many languages use auxiliaries where English uses word order or morphology, and serial-verb languages express similar meanings differently again. Historically, auxiliaries commonly arise through grammaticalisation of full verbs — will, for instance, once meant "want".
