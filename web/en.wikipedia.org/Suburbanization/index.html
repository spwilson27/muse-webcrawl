Suburbanization (also suburbanisation) is a population shift from central urban areas into suburbs, resulting in the formation and growth of suburban communities and, eventually, metropolitan regions in which suburbs contain most of the population. It is one stage of the urban development cycle, often followed by re-urbanization or counterurbanization.

History

Early suburbs grew along railways and streetcar lines in the 19th century, letting affluent households escape crowded industrial cities while commuting in. In the United States, suburbanization accelerated dramatically after the Second World War: returning veterans, the GI Bill's mortgage guarantees, FHA lending, mass-produced developments such as Levittown (from 1947), and the Interstate Highway System (from 1956) made low-density homeownership the national norm. Similar, if less extreme, suburban growth occurred in Europe, Australia, and later in rapidly urbanizing countries across Asia and Latin America.

Causes and effects

Drivers include cheaper land and housing at the urban fringe, desire for space and homeownership, highway and automobile access, and — particularly in the U.S. — racial dynamics such as white flight and discriminatory redlining that channeled investment outward while inner cities lost tax base. Consequences debated by planners include urban sprawl, car dependence and emissions, segregation by income and race, decline of central-city services, and loss of farmland; defenders point to improved housing quality, safety, and consumer preference. Since the 1990s some suburbs have themselves urbanized or diversified, while others face "suburban poverty" as growth moves further out to exurbs.
