Miracle of the Sun

The Miracle of the Sun (Portuguese: Milagre do Sol), also known as the Miracle of Fátima, is a series of events reported to have occurred on 13 October 1917 at Cova da Iria, near Fátima, Portugal. A large crowd—estimates range from 30,000 to 100,000 people—had gathered after three shepherd children, Lúcia Santos and her cousins Francisco and Jacinta Marto, predicted that the Lady they reported seeing in apparitions of Our Lady of Fátima would perform a great miracle at midday.

The event

After a period of rain, witnesses reported that the clouds parted and the Sun appeared as an opaque, spinning disc, casting multicoloured lights across the sky and landscape, then appearing to zigzag or plunge toward the earth before returning to its normal position. Many witnesses said their rain-soaked clothes had dried. The event was reported in contemporary Portuguese newspapers, including the secular Lisbon daily O Século, whose editor Avelino de Almeida published an eyewitness account.

Church response

After a canonical investigation, the Bishop of Leiria declared the Fátima apparitions "worthy of belief" in October 1930 and authorized the cult of Our Lady of Fátima. The Catholic Church has not issued a dogmatic definition of the solar phenomenon itself, but the event remains central to devotion at the Sanctuary of Fátima, one of the world's most visited Marian shrines.

Explanations and criticism

Skeptics have proposed natural and psychological explanations, including atmospheric optical phenomena such as a sun dog or parhelion, retinal afterimages from staring at the Sun, and mass suggestion among a primed and expectant crowd. Accounts varied considerably among witnesses—some present reported seeing nothing unusual—while no observatory recorded anomalous solar motion. Believers respond that the diversity of testimony, the presence of skeptical observers who also reported phenomena, and the reported drying of clothes support the miraculous interpretation.
