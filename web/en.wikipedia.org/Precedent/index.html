Precedent

A precedent is a principle or rule established in a prior legal case that is either binding on, or persuasive for, a court or other tribunal when deciding subsequent cases with similar issues or facts. The doctrine of precedent — commonly known by the Latin phrase stare decisis ("to stand by things decided") — is the foundation of common law systems.

Types of precedent

Binding (mandatory) precedent must be followed by a court: decisions of higher courts bind lower courts within the same jurisdiction. Persuasive precedent — decisions from other jurisdictions, lower courts, or dicta — may be considered but need not be followed. In the United States, federal courts of appeals bind the district courts within their circuit, and rulings of the Supreme Court bind all courts; in England and Wales, the Supreme Court and Court of Appeal bind lower courts, and their decisions structure the hierarchy of the common law.

Ratio decidendi and obiter dicta

Only the ratio decidendi — the legal reasoning essential to the decision — is binding. Other remarks in a judgment, the obiter dicta ("things said by the way"), are persuasive at most. Determining the ratio of a case, especially one decided by multiple judges giving separate opinions, is a central skill of common law practice.

Application and avoidance

Courts can depart from an apparent precedent by distinguishing the case — finding material differences in facts or issues. A higher court may overrule an earlier decision entirely, as the US Supreme Court did when Brown v. Board of Education (1954) rejected the "separate but equal" doctrine of Plessy v. Ferguson (1896). Judges weigh the stability and predictability that stare decisis provides against the need to correct erroneous decisions; overruling is considered especially sensitive in constitutional cases, yet statutes can be amended by legislatures when courts misread them.

In civil law systems

Civil law jurisdictions, such as those of continental Europe, formally base law on codes and do not treat prior decisions as binding, though in practice appellate case law (such as French jurisprudence constante) strongly guides lower courts toward consistency.
