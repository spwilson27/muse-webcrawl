Walter Lippmann

Walter Lippmann (September 23, 1889 – December 14, 1974) was an American writer, reporter, and political commentator. With a career spanning 60 years, he is famous for being among the first to introduce the concept of "Cold War," and for coining terms such as "stereotype" in the field of psychology, and the "manufacture of consent." Lippmann was a two-time recipient of the Pulitzer Prize for his syndicated newspaper column "Today and Tomorrow."

Early life and career

Lippmann was born in New York City to a wealthy German-Jewish family. He attended Harvard University, where he studied under George Santayana and William James, and graduated in 1910. In 1914 he helped found The New Republic magazine. During World War I he served as an adviser to President Woodrow Wilson, assisting in the drafting of the Fourteen Points.

Public Opinion

His best-known book, Public Opinion (1922), argued that modern democracy's complexity meant citizens could not fully understand public affairs, and instead operated on simplified "pictures in our heads" shaped by the media. The work remains a foundational text in media studies and political science.

Later career

Lippmann's nationally syndicated column ran from 1931 to 1967 and reached millions of readers. In his 1947 book The Cold War, he criticized the policy of containment he had helped popularize the name of. He won Pulitzer Prizes in 1958 and 1962, and received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1964.
