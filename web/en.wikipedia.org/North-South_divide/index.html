North–South divide

The North–South divide is a socio-economic and political division that has multiple meanings. In its broadest global sense, it refers to the division between the wealthy, industrialised countries of the "North" (largely in the Northern Hemisphere) and the poorer, developing countries of the "South". The concept was popularised by the Brandt Report of 1980, which drew the so-called Brandt line encircling the globe at roughly 30 degrees north latitude, with Australia and New Zealand counted among the developed North despite lying in the Southern Hemisphere.

Global divide

The global North–South divide is characterised by disparities in wealth, industrialisation, life expectancy, education, and political stability. Most nations classified as part of the "Global North" are members of the OECD or have high Human Development Index scores, while the "Global South" encompasses much of Africa, Latin America, and Asia. The terms "Global North" and "Global South" have largely replaced earlier labels such as "First World" and "Third World", or "developed" and "developing" countries.

Critics note that the division is a simplification: some Southern countries, such as China, India, and Brazil, have large and rapidly industrialising economies, while pockets of severe poverty exist within wealthy Northern states.

Within countries

The term is also applied within individual nations. In the United Kingdom, the North–South divide refers to economic and cultural differences between southern England (especially London and the South East) and the north of England, the Midlands, Wales, and Scotland, historically rooted in the Industrial Revolution and subsequent deindustrialisation. In Italy, a similar divide separates the industrial north from the poorer Mezzogiorno in the south. In the United States, cultural and political contrasts between the North and the South date to the era of slavery and the Civil War.

Causes

Explanations offered for the global divide include colonialism and the extraction of resources from colonised regions, unequal terms of trade, differing rates of industrialisation, geography and climate, and the structure of international financial institutions. Dependency theory, associated with scholars such as Raúl Prebisch and Andre Gunder Frank, argues that the prosperity of the core depends on the underdevelopment of the periphery.
