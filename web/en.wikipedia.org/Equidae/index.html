Equidae is the taxonomic family of horses and related animals, including the extant horses, asses, and zebras (all in the genus Equus), and many extinct genera known only from fossils. Equids are odd-toed ungulates (perissodactyls), bearing their weight chiefly on a single central toe enclosed in a hoof.

Evolution

The family evolved in North America during the Eocene, beginning with small, multi-toed forest browsers such as Hyracotherium ("Eohippus"), about the size of a small dog. Over some 50 million years equids adapted to expanding grasslands: body size increased, teeth became high-crowned (hypsodont) for grazing, legs lengthened, and the side toes were reduced until only the middle toe touched the ground. The genus Equus arose about 4–4.5 million years ago and spread to the Old World; horses became extinct in the Americas at the end of the Pleistocene and were reintroduced by Europeans.

Living species

Living equids comprise the domestic horse and Przewalski's horse, the African wild ass (ancestor of the donkey), the Asiatic onager and kulan, the kiang, and three zebra species (plains, mountain, and Grévy's). Several are threatened, and the quagga, a subspecies of plains zebra, was hunted to extinction in the 19th century.
