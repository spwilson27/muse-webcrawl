Salisbury Plain

Salisbury Plain is a chalk plateau in the south of England, covering about 300 square miles (780 km²) in Wiltshire and Hampshire. It is famous for its rich archaeology — above all Stonehenge — and for being the British Army's largest training area.

Geography

The plain consists of rolling chalk downland cut by dry valleys and the river valleys of the Avon, Bourne and Wylye. Never heavily ploughed or built upon in modern times, it preserves the largest expanse of unimproved chalk grassland in north-west Europe, supporting rare plants such as burnt-tip orchid and wildlife including the reintroduced great bustard.

History and archaeology

The area is dense with prehistoric monuments: Stonehenge, Avebury's neighbouring circle to the north, Woodhenge, Durrington Walls, the Stonehenge Cursus, and hundreds of Bronze Age round barrows. Iron Age hillforts and Romano-British settlements dot the downs. The city of Salisbury lies at the southern edge, near Old Sarum.

Military use

The War Office began buying land on the plain in 1897; Salisbury Plain Training Area now covers roughly half the plain — about 150 square miles — and hosts tank manoeuvres, live firing and exercises around camps such as Bulford, Larkhill and Tidworth. Villages were requisitioned for training, most famously Imber, evacuated in 1943 and still closed to the public except on occasional open days. Restricted access has inadvertently made the training area a wildlife refuge.
