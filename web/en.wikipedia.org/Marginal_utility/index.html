Marginal utility

In economics, marginal utility is a measure of satisfaction which quantifies the benefit or satisfaction a consumer gets from purchasing an additional unit of a commodity or service. It is a key concept at the heart of modern value theory and the theory of consumer choice.

Concept

Although the concept of utility is abstract, marginal utility can be understood through a simple example: the first glass of water on a hot day yields great satisfaction, the second less, and further glasses progressively less still. This observation is generalised as the law of diminishing marginal utility, formulated by Hermann Heinrich Gossen in 1854.

History

The marginalist revolution of the 1870s — associated with William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger and Léon Walras — made marginal utility the foundation of price theory, resolving the classical paradox of value: water, essential to life, is cheap, while diamonds are dear, because price reflects marginal rather than total utility.

Uses

Consumers are modelled as allocating a budget so that the marginal utility per unit of money spent is equalised across goods. The concept underpins demand curves, consumer surplus, progressive taxation arguments and aspects of welfare economics.

Criticisms

Critics question whether utility can be measured cardinally or compared between persons, leading to ordinal utility theory and revealed preference approaches that avoid direct measurement.
