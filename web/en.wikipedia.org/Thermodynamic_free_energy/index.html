Thermodynamic free energy

Thermodynamic free energy
Helmholtz free energyA = U − TS
Gibbs free energyG = H − TS
SI unitJoule (J)
SignificanceMaximum useful work obtainable from a system

In thermodynamics, the thermodynamic free energy is one of the state functions of a system, used to measure the work a system can perform. It is the energy of a system that is available to do work, as distinguished from the portion of the internal energy locked up as heat associated with entropy. Two free energies are in common use: the Helmholtz free energy A = U − TS (constant temperature and volume) and the Gibbs free energy G = H − TS = U + pV − TS (constant temperature and pressure).

Interpretation

The decrease in free energy in a process equals the maximum useful (non-expansion) work that can be extracted from the system under the corresponding conditions. Processes that decrease the Gibbs free energy (ΔG < 0) at constant temperature and pressure are thermodynamically spontaneous — a principle central to chemistry, where ΔG determines equilibrium constants, cell potentials in electrochemistry, and whether a reaction will proceed. At equilibrium the free energy is at a minimum: ΔG = 0.

History

The concept was developed in the 19th century by Hermann von Helmholtz, who introduced the term "free energy" (freie Energie), and by J. Willard Gibbs, whose 1876–78 work On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances laid the foundations of chemical thermodynamics. The free energy is a Legendre transform of the internal energy, trading dependence on entropy and volume for temperature, pressure, or other experimentally controlled variables.
