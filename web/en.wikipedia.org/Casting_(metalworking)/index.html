Casting (metalworking)

Process typeManufacturing
MaterialsMolten metal, mould material
Common methodsSand casting, die casting, investment casting
Known sincec. 3400 BC (Mesopotamia)

Casting is a manufacturing process in which a liquid material is usually poured into a mould, which contains a hollow cavity of the desired shape, and then allowed to solidify. The solidified part is also known as a casting, which is ejected or broken out of the mould to complete the process. Casting materials are usually metals or various time-setting materials that cure after mixing two or more components together; examples are epoxy, concrete, plaster and clay.

Casting is most often used for making complex shapes that would be otherwise difficult or uneconomical to make by other methods. Heavy equipment like machine tool beds, ships' propellers, etc. can be cast readily in the desired size, rather than fabricating by joining several small pieces. Casting is also used for producing works of art such as bronze statues.

History

Throughout history, metal casting has been used to make tools, weapons, and religious objects. The earliest known casting is a copper frog dated to 3200 BC, found in Mesopotamia. Lost-wax casting was used in the Indus Valley Civilisation around 3000 BC, and bronze casting flourished in China during the Shang dynasty, producing elaborate ritual vessels.

Processes

Modern casting processes are subdivided into two main categories: expendable and non-expendable mould casting. Sand casting uses sand as the mould material and accounts for the majority of metal casting production. Investment casting (lost-wax casting) produces parts with excellent surface finish and dimensional accuracy. Die casting forces molten metal under high pressure into reusable steel moulds and is widely used for aluminium and zinc components. Other processes include centrifugal casting, continuous casting, shell moulding, and plaster mould casting.

Defects

Common casting defects include gas porosity, shrinkage cavities, misruns, cold shuts, and hot tears. Foundry engineers use simulation software and careful gating and riser design to minimise these defects.
