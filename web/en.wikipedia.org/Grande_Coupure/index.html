Grande Coupure

The Grande Coupure ("Great Break") is a major faunal turnover event in Eurasia at the Eocene–Oligocene boundary, about 33.5 million years ago, in which endemic European mammal faunas were largely replaced by immigrant species from Asia. The term was coined by the Swiss palaeontologist Hans Georg Stehlin in 1909. It coincides with the Eocene–Oligocene extinction event and a major global cooling.

Date: ~33.5 million years ago (Eocene–Oligocene boundary)

Type: Faunal turnover / extinction event

Region: Europe and Asia

Cause: Global cooling, Antarctic glaciation, sea-level fall; Asian immigration

Event

During the late Eocene, Europe was an archipelago with a distinctive endemic fauna of archaic mammals, including palaeotheres (early horse relatives), anoplotheres, and many primates. At the boundary, global climate shifted abruptly — Antarctica glaciated, temperatures fell, and sea level dropped, drying the Turgai Strait between Europe and Asia. Asian mammals — including early rhinoceroses, ruminants, rodents, and carnivores — flooded into Europe, while many endemic groups (perhaps 60% of European mammal genera) disappeared.

Significance

The Grande Coupure marks the transition from the tropical "greenhouse" world of the Eocene to the cooler "icehouse" world of the Oligocene and afterward. It is treated as the European expression of a worldwide reorganisation of ecosystems, roughly contemporaneous with the Mongolian Remodelling of Asian faunas. Later research has debated how abrupt and synchronous the turnover was across regions, but it remains a standard marker in European mammal biostratigraphy, separating Palaeogene faunal zones.
