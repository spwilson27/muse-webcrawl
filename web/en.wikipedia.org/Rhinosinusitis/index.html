Rhinosinusitis

Rhinosinusitis is inflammation of the mucous membranes lining the nose and the paranasal sinuses. Because the nasal and sinus mucosa are continuous and the two conditions usually occur together, "rhinosinusitis" is preferred in modern practice to the older term sinusitis. It is classified by duration: acute (less than 4 weeks), subacute (4–12 weeks), chronic (more than 12 weeks) and recurrent acute.

Signs and symptoms

Diagnostic criteria require at least two symptoms, one of which should be nasal blockage/congestion or nasal discharge, with additional symptoms of facial pain or pressure and reduction or loss of smell. Acute rhinosinusitis most often follows a viral upper respiratory infection; only a small proportion of cases become secondarily bacterial. Chronic rhinosinusitis is divided into forms with and without nasal polyps, and is associated with allergy, asthma and, in some patients, aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis of acute rhinosinusitis is clinical; features suggesting bacterial infection include symptoms lasting more than 10 days without improvement, severe onset with high fever and purulent discharge, or "double worsening" after initial improvement. Nasal endoscopy and CT imaging of the sinuses are used mainly in chronic or complicated disease or before surgery.

Treatment

Most acute cases resolve without antibiotics; symptomatic treatment includes analgesics, saline irrigation and intranasal corticosteroids. Antibiotics are reserved for likely bacterial cases. Chronic rhinosinusitis is managed with long-term intranasal corticosteroids and saline irrigation, sometimes with systemic corticosteroids or antibiotics, and increasingly with biologic agents (such as dupilumab, omalizumab and mepolizumab) for severe disease with nasal polyps. Functional endoscopic sinus surgery is considered when medical therapy fails.

Complications

Rare complications include orbital cellulitis and abscess, meningitis, intracranial abscess, osteomyelitis of the frontal bone (Pott's puffy tumour) and cavernous sinus thrombosis — more frequent in children and in acute bacterial disease.
