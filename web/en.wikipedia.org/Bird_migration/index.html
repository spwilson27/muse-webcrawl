Bird migration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bird migration is the regular seasonal movement, often north and south along a flyway, between breeding and wintering grounds. Many species of bird migrate: roughly a fifth of the world's bird species are full migrants, and many more make shorter or irregular movements. Migration carries high costs in mortality and energy, but is favoured because it gives access to seasonally abundant food and safer breeding conditions.

Patterns and routes

Migrations range from short altitudinal shifts in mountains to the longest animal migrations on Earth — the Arctic tern travels between Arctic breeding grounds and the Antarctic each year, covering tens of thousands of kilometres. Major routes, or flyways, follow coasts, river valleys and mountain ridges; soaring birds such as storks and raptors concentrate at narrow land bridges where thermals are available, for example at Gibraltar and Panama. Many small songbirds migrate at night, while swallows and martins feed on the wing by day.

Navigation and physiology

Migrants navigate using a combination of cues: the sun and stars, landmarks, odour, and a magnetic sense — evidence suggests birds detect Earth's magnetic field partly through light-sensitive proteins in the eye and through magnetite-based receptors. Before departure, birds undergo hyperphagia and store fat, sometimes doubling their weight; some species shrink internal organs to save weight. Timing is controlled by an internal, genetically influenced programme triggered by changing day length.

Threats

Migratory birds face habitat loss at breeding, wintering and stopover sites, collisions with buildings and wind turbines, hunting along some flyways, and climate change, which can desynchronise migration timing from peaks in food availability. International agreements such as the African-Eurasian Migratory Waterbirds Agreement aim to protect species across their ranges.
