Causality

Causality (also referred to as causation, or cause and effect) is influence by which one event, process, state, or object (a cause) contributes to the production of another event, process, state, or object (an effect) where the cause is at least partly responsible for the effect, and the effect is at least partly dependent on the cause. In general, a process can have many causes, which are also said to be causal factors for it, and all lie in its past. An effect can in turn be a cause of, or causal factor for, many other effects, which all lie in its future. Some writers have held that causality is metaphysically prior to notions of time and space.

Causality is an abstraction that indicates how the world progresses. As such it is a basic concept; it is more apt to be an explanation of other concepts of progression than something to be explained by others more basic. The concept is like those of agency and efficacy. For this reason, a leap of intuition may be needed to grasp it. Accordingly, causality is implicit in the structure of ordinary language, as well as explicit in the language of scientific causal notation.

History

The concept of causality was central to ancient Greek philosophy. Aristotle distinguished four kinds of cause: the material cause, the formal cause, the efficient cause, and the final cause. In his Physics and Metaphysics, Aristotle argued that a full explanation of a thing requires knowledge of all four. Medieval scholastics developed these ideas further, and in the Islamic world thinkers such as Al-Ghazali questioned necessary connections between cause and effect.

The Scottish philosopher David Hume subjected causality to skeptical analysis in the 18th century. Hume argued that humans perceive only constant conjunctions of events, never a necessary connection, and that causal inference is a habit of mind rather than a deliverance of reason. Immanuel Kant responded by treating causality as an a priori category of the understanding, a condition of the possibility of experience itself.

Philosophy

Modern philosophical accounts of causation include counterfactual theories, associated with David Lewis, which analyze causation in terms of what would have happened had the cause not occurred; regularity theories descending from Hume; process theories, which understand causation as the transmission of conserved quantities along world lines; and interventionist accounts, developed by James Woodward, which relate causation to the results of hypothetical interventions. J. L. Mackie's INUS condition analyzes causes as insufficient but necessary parts of unnecessary but sufficient conditions.

Questions about causality intersect with debates about free will, determinism, and the direction of time. Russell famously suggested that the notion of cause plays no role in advanced physics, though most contemporary philosophers regard causal talk as indispensable at least at the level of the special sciences.

Science

In science, causal claims are supported by controlled experiments, statistical methods, and mechanistic models. The epidemiologist Austin Bradford Hill proposed considerations—such as strength of association, consistency, temporality, and biological gradient—for assessing whether an observed association is causal. Judea Pearl's work on causal graphs and the do-calculus formalized causal inference from observational data, distinguishing seeing from doing. Randomized controlled trials remain a gold standard because randomization breaks confounding.

Related concepts

Causality is closely related to determinism, the thesis that all events are necessitated by prior causes, and to correlation, which by itself does not imply causation. In physics, relativistic theories impose the constraint that causes precede their effects and that no influence travels faster than light.
