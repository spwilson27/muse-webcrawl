Anti-Catholicism

Anti-Catholicism is hostility towards Catholics or opposition to the Catholic Church, its clergy, and its adherents. At various points after the Protestant Reformation, many majority-Protestant states made anti-Catholicism, and the persecution of Catholics, a foundation of their national identity; later, anti-clerical movements in Catholic-majority states also targeted the Church's political power.

History

In Britain and Ireland, anti-Catholic penal laws excluded Catholics from public office and Parliament for centuries, and anti-Catholic riots such as the Gordon Riots of 1780 marked popular sentiment. In the British American colonies and the early United States, "popery" was widely feared; in the nineteenth century, nativist movements such as the Know Nothings opposed Catholic immigration from Ireland and Germany, and anti-Catholic rhetoric figured in the 1928 and 1960 presidential campaigns of Al Smith and John F. Kennedy.

Forms and contexts

Anti-Catholicism has taken theological, political, and conspiratorial forms: rejection of papal authority and Catholic doctrine; suspicion that Catholics owed allegiance to a foreign power; and, in countries such as Bismarck's Germany (the Kulturkampf) and revolutionary Mexico and France, state campaigns against the Church's institutional influence. Critics distinguish opposition to Church policies from bigotry against Catholics themselves, though the boundary has often been blurred in practice.
