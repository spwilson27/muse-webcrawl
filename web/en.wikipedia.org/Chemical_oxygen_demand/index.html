Chemical oxygen demand

Chemical oxygen demand (COD) is an indicative measure of the amount of oxygen that can be consumed by reactions in a measured solution. It is commonly expressed in mass of oxygen consumed per volume of solution, with SI units of milligrams per litre (mg/L). A COD test is commonly used to indirectly measure the amount of organic compounds in water and is widely applied in assessing water quality.

Overview

The COD test indicates the amount of oxygen required to oxidize all organic and inorganic compounds in a water sample, typically using a strong chemical oxidant such as potassium dichromate or potassium permanganate under acidic conditions and elevated temperature. The result reflects the total chemically oxidizable material, including substances not biodegradable, so COD is generally higher than the corresponding biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) value for the same sample.

Methods

Standard COD determination is usually performed by refluxing the sample with a known excess of dichromate in concentrated sulfuric acid, often with silver sulfate as a catalyst and mercuric sulfate to remove chloride interference. Modern laboratories frequently use sealed-tube colorimetric methods that measure the chromium(III) formed and are faster and safer. Permanganate-based variants (the permanganate index) are preferred for relatively clean waters.

Applications

COD is used to monitor wastewater treatment plants, assess industrial effluent loads, and set discharge limits in environmental regulation. Because the test takes only a few hours compared with five days for BOD, it is valuable for rapid process control and for detecting toxic discharges that inhibit biological treatment.

Limitations

COD does not distinguish between biodegradable and inert material, and chloride, nitrite, and ferrous iron can interfere with the measurement. Standard tests also generate hazardous waste containing hexavalent chromium, mercury, and silver, motivating interest in greener oxidation methods.
