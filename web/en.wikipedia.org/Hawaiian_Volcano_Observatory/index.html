The Hawaiian Volcano Observatory (HVO) is a volcano observatory of the United States Geological Survey (USGS) located in Hawai'i Volcanoes National Park on the island of Hawai'i, overlooking the summit caldera of Kīlauea, one of the world's most active volcanoes. It monitors the active volcanoes of Hawaii — principally Kīlauea, Mauna Loa, and Hualālai — for the protection of life and property.

History

HVO was founded in 1912 by Massachusetts Institute of Technology geologist Thomas A. Jaggar, inspired in part by the catastrophic 1902 eruption of Mount Pelée; his guiding motto was Ne plus haustae aut obrutae urbes — "No more burned or buried cities." Jaggar directed the observatory for decades, pioneering systematic volcano monitoring with seismographs, tiltmeters, and gas measurements. Administration passed through several agencies before becoming part of the USGS, and its monitoring network greatly expanded during and after the 35-year eruption of Pu'u 'Ō'ō (1983–2018).

Monitoring and eruptions

HVO operates networks of seismometers, GPS and tilt instruments, gas sensors, and webcams, issuing daily status reports and eruption updates. Major episodes it has documented include the 1959 Kīlauea Iki eruption, the 1984 Mauna Loa eruption, the 2018 lower East Rift Zone eruption of Kīlauea that destroyed hundreds of homes in Leilani Estates and collapsed the summit caldera, and the 2022 Mauna Loa eruption — the first from that volcano in 38 years. The observatory building on the caldera rim was damaged by the 2018 collapse events, and staff relocated while a new facility was planned nearby.
