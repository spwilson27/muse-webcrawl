Cellulose acetate film

Cellulose acetate film, or safety film, is a film base made from cellulose acetate used in photography and cinematography. It replaced highly flammable cellulose nitrate film in the first half of the 20th century, first for amateur formats and eventually, by the early 1950s, for professional 35 mm motion picture film.

Types

Early safety film used cellulose diacetate; from the 1940s it was superseded by cellulose triacetate, which remained the dominant film base until polyester (such as Kodak's ESTAR) bases gained ground for archival and release-print applications.

Deterioration

Acetate film is subject to "vinegar syndrome": hydrolysis of the acetate base releases acetic acid, giving off a vinegar odour and causing the film to shrink, become brittle, and warp. The process is autocatalytic and contagious to nearby reels, making cool, dry archival storage essential; affected films are often prioritised for digitisation.

History

Cellulose acetate was first prepared in 1865 by Paul Schützenberger. Kodak introduced acetate-based safety film for home movie formats in the 1920s, and the industry completed the transition from nitrate after a series of devastating archive and cinema fires.
