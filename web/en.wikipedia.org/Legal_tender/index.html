Legal tender

Legal tender
Medium of payment recognised by law to settle a debt or financial obligation.

Legal tender is a form of money that courts of law are required to recognise as satisfactory payment for any monetary debt. Each jurisdiction determines what constitutes legal tender, although essentially it is anything which when offered in payment of a debt extinguishes the debt. A creditor is not obligated to accept legal tender before a debt is due, but a debtor who tenders payment in legal tender at the time and place prescribed has, in most systems, discharged the obligation.

History

Legal tender laws evolved with coinage. In the ancient world, states stamped metal with official marks guaranteeing weight and purity. Paper money became legal tender much later; in Britain, Bank of England notes were made legal tender in 1833. The gold standard tied legal tender to convertibility, abandoned in most countries during the 20th century in favour of fiat money.

By jurisdiction

In the United States, coins and currency issued by the Treasury, including Federal Reserve notes, are legal tender for all debts, public charges, taxes and dues under 31 U.S.C. § 5103. There is no federal requirement that a private business accept cash. In the United Kingdom, Bank of England notes are legal tender in England and Wales; Scottish and Northern Irish banknotes are not, strictly, legal tender anywhere, though widely accepted. In the euro area, euro banknotes and coins are legal tender in all member states.

Legal tender and demonetisation

Governments occasionally withdraw legal tender status from certain denominations, a process called demonetisation, for example India's 2016 withdrawal of ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes. After demonetisation, the withdrawn notes cease to be legal tender after a specified date.
