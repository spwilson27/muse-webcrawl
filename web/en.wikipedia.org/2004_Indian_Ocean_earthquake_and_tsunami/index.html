The 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami (also known as the Boxing Day tsunami) occurred at 07:58:53 local time on Sunday, December 26, 2004. The undersea megathrust earthquake, with an epicenter off the west coast of Aceh in northern Sumatra, Indonesia, registered a magnitude of 9.1–9.3, making it one of the most powerful earthquakes ever recorded. It triggered a series of massive tsunami waves up to 30 metres (100 ft) high that devastated coastal communities around the Indian Ocean.

An estimated 227,898 people were killed in 14 countries, making it one of the deadliest natural disasters in recorded history. Indonesia was the hardest-hit country, followed by Sri Lanka, India, and Thailand. The disaster prompted an unprecedented worldwide humanitarian response and led to the creation of the Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System.

Earthquake

The earthquake struck where the Burma Plate subducts beneath the Sunda Plate along the Sunda megathrust. The rupture extended roughly 1,200–1,300 kilometres (about 750–800 miles) along the fault — the longest fault rupture ever observed — and lasted between eight and ten minutes, among the longest durations recorded. The seafloor rose several metres over a wide area, displacing an enormous volume of water. The event was powerful enough to vibrate the entire planet by as much as a centimetre and to trigger earthquakes as far away as Alaska. Initially reported at magnitude 9.0, later studies revised the estimate to 9.1–9.3, ranking it the third-largest earthquake since modern seismographic records began in 1900, after Chile (1960) and Alaska (1964).

Tsunami characteristics

The tsunami radiated across the Indian Ocean at speeds of up to 500–800 km/h in deep water, where its height was barely noticeable, then slowed and grew dramatically as it reached shallow coastal waters. Waves reached the Sumatran coast within about 15–20 minutes, Sri Lanka and eastern India within roughly two hours, and the coast of East Africa some seven hours later, where they still caused deaths as far away as South Africa. In Aceh, run-up heights locally reached around 30 metres; in many places the sea first receded far beyond its normal low-tide line, exposing the seabed — a natural warning sign that went unrecognized by most onlookers.

Impact by country

Indonesia: The province of Aceh, closest to the epicenter, suffered catastrophic destruction; the city of Banda Aceh and coastal towns such as Meulaboh were largely destroyed. Indonesia's confirmed death toll exceeded 130,000, with many more initially missing.

Sri Lanka: The tsunami struck the eastern, southern, and western coasts, killing more than 35,000 people and displacing over half a million. A passenger train near Telwatta was overwhelmed by the waves, killing roughly 1,700 people in the largest single rail disaster in history.

India: The Andaman and Nicobar Islands and the Tamil Nadu coast, including Nagapattinam district, were badly hit; around 12,000–16,000 deaths were recorded in India overall.

Thailand: The Andaman coast, including Phuket, Khao Lak, and the Phi Phi Islands, was struck; more than 5,000 people died, roughly half of them foreign tourists, among them many visitors from Northern Europe spending the Christmas holiday.

Deaths were also recorded in Malaysia, Myanmar, Bangladesh, the Maldives, Seychelles, Somalia, Kenya, Tanzania, Madagascar, and South Africa.

Damage and casualties

In total, an estimated 227,898 people were killed and some 1.7 million displaced across 14 countries. Entire coastal communities were erased; infrastructure including roads, railways, ports, water supplies, and fishing fleets was destroyed on a regional scale. Environmental damage included saltwater contamination of farmland and freshwater aquifers, and destruction of mangroves, coral reefs, and coastal vegetation.

Humanitarian response

The disaster triggered one of the largest relief operations in history. Governments, agencies, and private donors pledged more than US$14 billion in humanitarian aid. Militaries from dozens of countries, including a major U.S. carrier-group deployment (Operation Unified Assistance), assisted with logistics, search and rescue, and medical care. The scale of private charitable giving was unprecedented.

Tsunami warning system

At the time, the Indian Ocean had no tsunami warning system comparable to the one operating in the Pacific, and no effective mechanism existed to alert coastal populations — a factor widely blamed for the enormous death toll. In response, UNESCO's Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission coordinated the establishment of the Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning and Mitigation System, with deep-ocean detection buoys and regional warning centers, which became operational in the years following the disaster.

Long-term effects

In Aceh, the disaster helped catalyze the 2005 Helsinki peace accord ending the decades-long separatist conflict between the Indonesian government and the Free Aceh Movement. Recovery and reconstruction programs rebuilt housing and infrastructure over the following decade, and December 26 remains a day of commemoration in the affected countries.
