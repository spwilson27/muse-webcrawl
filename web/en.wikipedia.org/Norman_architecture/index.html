Norman architecture
Active periodc. 1066 – c. 1200 (in England)
Parent styleRomanesque
RegionsNormandy, England, Scotland, Ireland, southern Italy and Sicily
FeaturesRound arches, massive cylindrical piers, barrel and groin vaults, chevron ornament, keeps and castles
ExamplesDurham Cathedral, Tower of London (White Tower), Ely Cathedral, Monreale Cathedral (Sicily)

Norman architecture is the regional form of Romanesque architecture developed by the Normans in the lands under their dominion during the 11th and 12th centuries, most importantly in Normandy, in England after the Norman Conquest of 1066, and in Norman southern Italy and Sicily. It is distinguished by massive proportions, round arches, and relative austerity of ornament.

Characteristics

Norman churches are typically cruciform, with long naves carried on huge cylindrical or clustered piers, round-headed windows and doorways, and arcades decorated with geometric mouldings such as chevron (zigzag), billet and beakhead. Builders pioneered the rib vault — the earliest surviving high rib vaults are those of Durham Cathedral (begun 1093) — paving the way for Gothic. Secular Norman architecture is embodied in the great stone keep: square, multi-storey donjons such as the White Tower in London and the keeps of Norwich, Rochester and Hedingham.

Regional developments

In England, virtually every major church was rebuilt in the Norman style within a generation of the Conquest — Canterbury, Winchester, Ely, Peterborough and Durham among them — giving English Romanesque an exceptional grandeur and uniformity. In Sicily, Norman kings fused Romanesque structure with Byzantine mosaics and Islamic craftsmanship, as at Cefalù, Monreale and the Palatine Chapel in Palermo. From about 1180 the style gave way to Early English Gothic, with pointed arches replacing round ones.
