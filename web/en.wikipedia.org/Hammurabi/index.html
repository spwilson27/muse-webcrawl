Hammurabi (Akkadian: Ḫammurapi) was the sixth king of the First Babylonian Dynasty of the Amorite tribe, reigning from c. 1792 to c. 1750 BCE. He is known for the Code of Hammurabi, one of the earliest and most complete written legal codes in history, and for transforming Babylon from a minor city-state into the dominant power of Mesopotamia.

Reign and conquests

Hammurabi inherited a modest kingdom surrounded by stronger neighbours—Eshnunna, Elam, Larsa, and Mari. Through shifting alliances and well-timed campaigns, over his 42-year reign he defeated Larsa (taking the south), destroyed Mari, and subdued Eshnunna and Assyria, uniting nearly all of Mesopotamia under Babylonian rule. His administration is unusually well documented by his voluminous correspondence with officials, showing close royal supervision of irrigation, justice, and commerce. His empire did not long survive him; under his son Samsu-iluna the south seceded and the realm contracted.

The Code

Near the end of his reign Hammurabi promulgated his law collection: 282 casuistic laws ("if a man…") inscribed in Akkadian cuneiform on a black diorite stele over two metres tall, topped by a relief of the king before Shamash, god of justice. The laws cover contracts, wages, family, assault, and professional liability; penalties scale by social class and famously include talionic provisions—"an eye for an eye." Rediscovered at Susa in 1901–02, where an Elamite king had carried it off as war booty, the stele is now in the Louvre. Hammurabi's code influenced later Near Eastern law and made him an enduring symbol of the lawgiver; his image appears in the chamber of the U.S. House of Representatives and in the Supreme Court building.
