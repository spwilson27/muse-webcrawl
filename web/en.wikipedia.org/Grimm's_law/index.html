Grimm's law

Grimm's law, also known as the First Germanic Sound Shift, is a set of sound laws describing the Proto-Indo-European (PIE) stop consonants as they developed in Proto-Germanic in the first millennium BC. It was first systematically described by Jacob Grimm in the 1822 second edition of his Deutsche Grammatik, building on correspondences noted by Rasmus Rask. Its regularity made it a cornerstone of the comparative method in historical linguistics.

The shift

The law describes a chain shift of the PIE stops in three series:

Proto-Indo-EuropeanProto-GermanicExamples (Latin → English)
*p, *t, *k (voiceless stops)*f, *þ, *x (fricatives)piscis → fish; trēs → three; canis → hound
*b, *d, *g (voiced stops)*p, *t, *k (voiceless stops)decem → ten; genus → kin
*bʰ, *dʰ, *gʰ (voiced aspirates)*b, *d, *g (voiced stops/fricatives)frāter → brother

Voiceless stops remained stops after fricatives (e.g. Latin noctis, English night) and in some clusters. The well-known "exceptions" — where Germanic shows voiced fricatives instead of the voiceless ones Grimm predicted — were explained by Verner's law (1875), which showed that the outcome depended on the position of the PIE accent.

Significance

Grimm's law demonstrated that sound change could be regular and exceptionless in ways that allowed linguists to reconstruct unattested proto-languages and to establish the Germanic branch of Indo-European. It correctly predicts the systematic differences between English or German on one side and Latin, Greek or Sanskrit on the other — for example Latin pater beside English father, or Greek kardia beside English heart. A later change, the High German consonant shift, affected only the High German dialects.
