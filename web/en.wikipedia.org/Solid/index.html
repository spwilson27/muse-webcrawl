Solid

Solid is one of the four fundamental states of matter, along with liquid, gas, and plasma. The molecules in a solid are closely packed together and contain the least amount of kinetic energy among these states. A solid is characterized by structural rigidity and resistance to changes in shape or volume: unlike a liquid, a solid does not flow to take the shape of its container, nor does it expand to fill the available volume like a gas.

Microscopic structure

The atoms, ions, or molecules in a solid are held in place by strong intermolecular forces and vibrate about fixed average positions. In crystalline solids they form a regular repeating lattice (for example, metals, ice, and diamond); in amorphous solids such as glass and many polymers, the arrangement lacks long-range order. The type of bonding — ionic, covalent, metallic, or molecular — determines properties such as hardness, melting point, and conductivity.

Properties

Solids have definite shape and volume, low compressibility, and high density compared with gases. Their mechanical properties include elasticity (the ability to recover shape after deformation), plasticity, ductility, brittleness, and hardness. Thermal expansion, heat conduction, and melting at a characteristic temperature (for crystalline materials) are also defining features. The physics of solids is treated in solid-state physics and materials science.

Classes of solids

  • Metals — held by metallic bonding; good conductors, malleable and ductile.
  • Ionic solids — lattices of positive and negative ions; hard, brittle, high melting points.
  • Covalent network solids — atoms linked by covalent bonds throughout, e.g., diamond and quartz.
  • Molecular solids — discrete molecules held by weaker forces, e.g., ice and solid carbon dioxide.
  • Amorphous solids — glasses and some plastics without long-range order.

Phase changes

On heating, a solid melts into a liquid; the reverse is freezing. Some solids pass directly into gas by sublimation (for example, dry ice), and gases can deposit directly as solids. The study of defects in solids — vacancies, dislocations, and impurities — explains phenomena such as solid solution strengthening in alloys.

Related concepts

In chemistry, solids precipitated from solution are separated by filtration or centrifugation. In mechanics, the deformation and failure of solids are modeled by continuum mechanics and the theory of elasticity. Devices such as fuel cells may use solid electrolytes, as in the solid oxide fuel cell.
