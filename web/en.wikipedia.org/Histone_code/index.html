Histone code

The histone code is a hypothesis in molecular biology stating that gene transcription is regulated in part by chemical modifications to histone proteins — chiefly acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation and ubiquitination — which occur mainly on the unstructured N-terminal tails protruding from the nucleosome core. Combinations of these marks are proposed to be "read" by effector proteins that recruit activating or repressive machinery, forming a layer of epigenetic control beyond the DNA sequence itself.

The hypothesis

Proposed around 2000 by researchers including C. David Allis, Thomas Jenuwein and Danny Reinberg, the hypothesis grew from the observation that specific modifications correlate reproducibly with gene activity: acetylation of lysines on histones H3 and H4 generally marks active chromatin (euchromatin), while patterns of methylation can mark either active or silenced regions depending on the residue — trimethylation of H3 lysine 4 (H3K4me3) at active promoters versus H3K9me3 or H3K27me3 in heterochromatin and Polycomb-repressed genes.

Writers, erasers and readers

Marks are added by enzymes called writers (histone acetyltransferases, methyltransferases), removed by erasers (histone deacetylases, demethylases), and recognised by reader protein domains — bromodomains bind acetyl-lysine, while chromodomains, Tudor domains and PHD fingers bind methylated residues. This writer–eraser–reader vocabulary is now standard in epigenetics.

Criticism and refinement

Critics caution that "code" may overstate the case: many marks may be consequences rather than causes of transcription, and their effects are context-dependent rather than a fixed cipher. Genome-wide mapping projects such as ENCODE have nevertheless shown that combinations of marks segment the genome into reproducible chromatin states — promoters, enhancers, transcribed bodies, repressed regions — that predict gene behaviour across cell types, and disruptions of the machinery are implicated in cancers and developmental disorders.
