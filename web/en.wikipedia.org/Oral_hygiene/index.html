Oral hygiene

Oral hygiene is the practice of keeping one's oral cavity clean and free of disease and other problems (e.g. bad breath) through regular brushing of the teeth (dental hygiene) and cleaning between the teeth. It is important that oral hygiene be carried out on a regular basis to enable prevention of dental disease and bad breath. The most common types of dental disease are tooth decay (dental caries) and gum diseases, including gingivitis and periodontitis.

Brushing and flossing

General guidelines from dental associations suggest brushing twice a day: after breakfast and before going to bed, though ideally the mouth should be cleaned after every meal. Brushing with a fluoride toothpaste for about two minutes helps remove dental plaque, the bacterial biofilm that forms on teeth. Cleaning between the teeth is called interdental cleaning and is as important as tooth brushing; dental floss, interdental brushes, or water flossers are commonly used. Regular flossing removes plaque from areas the toothbrush cannot reach, particularly the surfaces where teeth meet.

Professional care

Regular examinations and professional cleaning by a dentist or dental hygienist, typically every six to twelve months, allow early detection of cavities, gum disease, and oral cancers. Professional cleaning removes calculus (tartar), which cannot be removed by brushing alone. Fluoride treatments and dental sealants may be applied, especially in children.

Other practices

Mouthwash can supplement brushing but does not replace mechanical cleaning. Diet also affects oral health: frequent consumption of sugars and acidic drinks increases the risk of decay and erosion. Thumb sucking, tongue piercing, smoking, and chewing tobacco can all harm oral health. Ancient practices such as chewing sticks (like the miswak) predate the modern toothbrush, which was mass-produced from the late eighteenth century.
