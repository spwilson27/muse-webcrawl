Weimar culture

Weimar culture was the flourishing of the arts and sciences in Germany during the Weimar Republic, from 1918 until the Nazi seizure of power in 1933. Centred on Berlin, it produced modernist movements of lasting global influence — the Bauhaus in design, Expressionist and New Objectivity art, groundbreaking cinema such as Metropolis and The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari, the theatre of Bertolt Brecht, and major advances in physics, philosophy and architecture — amid political instability, hyperinflation and, later, mass unemployment.

Context

Defeat in the First World War, the abolition of censorship under the Weimar constitution, and the collapse of old social hierarchies unleashed an experimental culture. The republic's instability — hyperinflation in 1923, relative stabilisation during the "Golden Twenties" (1924–29), and the crash after 1929 — gave the era its characteristic mixture of exuberance and dread, and Berlin became famous for cabaret, jazz and a liberal nightlife that attracted writers such as Christopher Isherwood, whose Berlin stories inspired Cabaret.

Art, architecture and film

The Bauhaus school, founded by Walter Gropius in Weimar in 1919 and later based in Dessau, united art, craft and technology and reshaped modern design. Berlin Dada and the artists of New Objectivity — George Grosz and Otto Dix — satirised war, corruption and bourgeois society. The UFA studios produced classics of Expressionist cinema: The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920), F. W. Murnau's Nosferatu (1922) and Fritz Lang's Metropolis (1927) and M (1931). In theatre, Brecht and Kurt Weill's The Threepenny Opera (1928) epitomised the era's political art.

Thought and science

German universities led the world in physics (Einstein, Heisenberg, Born), the Frankfurt School began its critical theory, and figures such as Martin Heidegger and Walter Benjamin reshaped philosophy and criticism. The Nazi rise to power ended the epoch abruptly in 1933: books were burned, "degenerate art" was purged, and much of this culture emigrated — to Hollywood, the New School in New York, and elsewhere.
