The Basílica i Temple Expiatori de la Sagrada Família ("Basilica and Expiatory Church of the Holy Family") is a large unfinished Roman Catholic church in the Eixample district of Barcelona, Catalonia, Spain. Designed by the Catalan architect Antoni Gaudí, it is his most famous work and one of the most visited monuments in Spain; parts of the building are a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

History

Construction began in 1882 under architect Francisco de Paula del Villar in a neo-Gothic design. After Villar resigned in 1883, the 31-year-old Gaudí took over and transformed the project into an expression of his mature style, fusing Gothic and Art Nouveau forms with geometry drawn from nature. Gaudí devoted the last years of his life entirely to the church and is buried in its crypt; at his death in 1926 less than a quarter was built. Work continued under successors, was interrupted by the Spanish Civil War — during which anarchists burned Gaudí's workshop and smashed his models — and resumed with the reconstruction of his designs. Pope Benedict XVI consecrated the church as a minor basilica in 2010.

Design

Gaudí planned eighteen spires representing the Twelve Apostles, the four Evangelists, the Virgin Mary, and — the tallest, at 172.5 metres — Jesus Christ. Three grand façades depict the Nativity, the Passion and the Glory. The interior uses branching columns, hyperboloid vaults and ruled surfaces to create a "stone forest" lit through stained glass. The church is planned to seat some 13,000 worshippers.

Completion

Modern construction, funded by donations and ticket sales and aided by computer-aided design, accelerated in the twenty-first century; the central towers rose in the 2010s and 2020s, and completion of the main structure has been targeted for the mid-2030s, with decorative work continuing thereafter — more than 150 years after the first stone was laid.
