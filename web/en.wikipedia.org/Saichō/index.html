Saichō

Saichō (最澄, 15 September 767 – 26 June 822) was a Japanese Buddhist monk credited with founding the Tendai school of Buddhism in Japan, based on the Chinese Tiantai tradition. He established the temple of Enryaku-ji on Mount Hiei near Kyoto, which became one of the most powerful religious institutions in Japanese history. He is posthumously honoured with the title Dengyō Daishi.

Life

Born in Ōmi Province, Saichō entered the priesthood at fourteen and was ordained at Tōdai-ji in Nara. Dissatisfied with the scholasticism and politics of the Nara schools, he withdrew in 785 to Mount Hiei, where he built a small hermitage — the nucleus of Enryaku-ji — and devoted himself to the Lotus Sutra. His writings attracted the attention of Emperor Kanmu, founder of the new capital at Kyoto.

In 804 Saichō sailed to Tang China as part of an official embassy, studying Tiantai doctrine on Mount Tiantai under Daosui and Xingman, as well as esoteric Buddhism and Zen. He returned in 805 with texts and initiations and, in 806, obtained imperial recognition of Tendai as an independent school with its own annual ordinands. His later years were consumed by a celebrated controversy with the Hossō scholar Tokuitsu and by his campaign to establish a Mahayana ordination platform (kaidan) at Enryaku-ji, independent of Nara; permission was granted only a week after his death in 822.

Legacy

Enryaku-ji trained many of the founders of Kamakura Buddhism — Hōnen, Shinran, Eisai, Dōgen, and Nichiren all studied on Mount Hiei — making Saichō a pivotal figure in Japanese religious history. He is also traditionally credited with introducing tea plants to Japan from China. In 866 the court granted him the posthumous name Dengyō Daishi ("Great Teacher, Transmitter of the Teaching").
