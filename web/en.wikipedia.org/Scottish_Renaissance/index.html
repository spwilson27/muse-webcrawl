Scottish Renaissance

The Scottish Renaissance was a mainly literary movement of the early to mid-20th century that sought to revive Scottish culture and literature after a perceived period of decline. Its leading figure was the poet Hugh MacDiarmid (Christopher Murray Grieve), whose work in a synthetic form of the Scots language — dubbed "Lallans" — aimed to restore Scots as a medium for serious literature.

Origins

The movement reacted against the sentimental "kailyard" school of Scottish writing and against the political and cultural unionism of the nineteenth century. MacDiarmid's lyric sequence A Drunk Man Looks at the Thistle (1926) became its most famous work, and his editorship of journals such as The Scottish Chapbook and The Northern Review gave the movement a platform from the early 1920s.

Key figures

Writers associated with the Renaissance include Edwin Muir, William Soutar, Sydney Goodsir Smith, Robert Garioch, and the Gaelic poet Sorley MacLean. Its ideas also influenced Scottish nationalism and the development of the modern Scottish National Party tradition.

Criticism

Edwin Muir famously broke with MacDiarmid, arguing in Scott and Scotland (1936) that Scots was no longer a complete language capable of expressing the full range of thought, and that Scottish literature's future lay in English. The debate between MacDiarmid and Muir remains central to discussions of Scottish literary identity.

Legacy

The Scottish Renaissance is credited with laying the cultural groundwork for later developments in Scottish writing and for the broader revival of confidence in Scottish culture that preceded political devolution.
