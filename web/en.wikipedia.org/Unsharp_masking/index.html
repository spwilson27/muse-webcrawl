Unsharp masking

Unsharp masking (USM) is an image sharpening technique, first implemented in the darkroom and now available in most digital image editing software. Despite its name, the technique uses a blurred, or "unsharp," copy of the image as a mask to increase the apparent sharpness by enhancing contrast along edges. It does not actually increase the information content or true resolution of an image; rather, it exploits the way the human visual system perceives acutance at boundaries.

Principle

The procedure subtracts a blurred version of the image from the original to isolate high-frequency detail (the "unsharp mask"), then adds a scaled portion of that detail back to the original. Formally, the sharpened image can be written as the original plus an amount k times the difference between the original and its Gaussian-blurred version. Subtracting the blurred copy leaves edges and fine texture; adding it back overshoots the brightness on either side of an edge, producing light and dark halos that read perceptually as increased sharpness.

Parameters

Digital implementations, such as the Unsharp Mask filter in Adobe Photoshop and GIMP, expose three controls:

  • Amount — the strength of the contrast increase along edges, typically expressed as a percentage.
  • Radius — the size of the blur used to create the mask, which determines the width of the halos and the scale of detail affected.
  • Threshold — the minimum brightness difference between neighbouring pixels before the effect is applied, used to avoid amplifying noise in smooth areas such as skies and skin.

Excessive amounts produce conspicuous halos and amplified noise; small-radius, low-amount sharpening is typical for correcting the softness introduced by sensors and anti-aliasing filters ("capture sharpening"), while larger amounts are used for output sharpening matched to print size.

History

The technique originated in German photomechanical reproduction in the 1930s and was widely used in mid-twentieth-century darkroom practice: a low-contrast positive mask was made from the negative on film, deliberately slightly out of register or blurred, and sandwiched with the negative during printing to boost edge contrast. A related method, using radioactive or dye-based masking, was used in radiology to enhance X-ray detail. Software implementations appeared with the first desktop image editors in the late 1980s and early 1990s, carrying over the darkroom name.

Related techniques

Unsharp masking is one of several sharpening approaches; alternatives include deconvolution sharpening, high-pass filtering, and clarity/local-contrast adjustments, which apply a similar method with a much larger radius to enhance mid-tone contrast rather than fine edges.
