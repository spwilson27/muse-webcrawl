Lightvessel

A lightvessel, or lightship, is a ship that acts as a lighthouse: anchored in waters too deep or otherwise unsuitable for lighthouse construction, it carries a powerful lamp on a tall mast to warn mariners of shoals, reefs, and harbour approaches, and its hull is usually painted a conspicuous red with the station name in large letters.

History

The first lightvessel was stationed at the Nore, at the mouth of the Thames, in 1732, and Robert Stevenson placed one on the Bell Rock in 1807 pending the lighthouse's completion. Britain, the United States, Germany, and other maritime nations built extensive lightship fleets through the 19th and early 20th centuries. Crews endured long, dangerous anchorages; several lightships were lost with all hands, including vessels sunk in storms or, in wartime, by attack — Britain's East Dudgeon lightvessel was bombed in 1940.

Decline

From the mid-20th century, large automatic navigation buoys, fixed platforms, and electronic navigation replaced lightships. The last US lightship station (Nantucket Shoals) was discontinued in 1985, and most British stations became unmanned automatic light floats. Surviving vessels, such as LV-112 Nantucket and the British LV-83 Swiftsure, are preserved as museum ships.
