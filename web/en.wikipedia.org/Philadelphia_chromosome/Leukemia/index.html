Leukemia

Leukemia (also spelt leukaemia) is a group of blood cancers that usually begin in the bone marrow and produce large numbers of abnormal blood cells. Leukaemias are classified by speed of progression (acute or chronic) and by the cell lineage affected (myeloid or lymphoid), giving four main types: acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL), chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), and chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL). Symptoms include fatigue, infections, easy bruising, and bone pain; diagnosis rests on blood counts, marrow examination, cytogenetics, and molecular testing.

The Philadelphia chromosome in leukaemia

The Philadelphia chromosome is the defining genetic lesion of CML and also occurs in a high-risk subset of ALL and occasionally in AML. It results from the reciprocal translocation t(9;22)(q34;q11), which fuses the BCR and ABL1 genes to create a constitutively active tyrosine kinase that drives uncontrolled proliferation of the malignant clone. Chronic myeloid leukaemia progresses through chronic, accelerated, and blast-crisis phases if untreated. The discovery of the Philadelphia chromosome in 1960 by Nowell and Hungerford — the first recurrent chromosome abnormality in cancer — transformed leukaemia classification, which is now based largely on genetic subtypes.

Treatment

Treatment varies by type: chemotherapy, immunotherapy, stem-cell transplantation, and targeted therapy. Philadelphia-positive leukaemias are treated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as imatinib, which converted CML from a fatal disease into a chronic, manageable condition for most patients.
