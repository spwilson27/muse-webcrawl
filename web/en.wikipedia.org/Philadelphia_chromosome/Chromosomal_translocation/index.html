Chromosomal translocation

TypeChromosome abnormality
Main formsReciprocal; Robertsonian
CausesNon-homologous recombination; errors in repair of DNA double-strand breaks
Notable examplesPhiladelphia chromosome t(9;22); t(8;14) in Burkitt lymphoma

A chromosomal translocation is a chromosome abnormality in which a segment of one chromosome breaks off and attaches to a different, non-homologous chromosome. Translocations are a major class of structural chromosomal rearrangement and can be harmless — when carried in balanced form — or can disrupt genes and cause infertility, miscarriage, developmental disorders, or cancer.

Types

In a reciprocal translocation, two non-homologous chromosomes exchange segments; the carrier has the normal amount of genetic material (balanced) and is usually phenotypically normal, though at risk of producing unbalanced gametes. In a Robertsonian translocation, the long arms of two acrocentric chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21, 22) fuse at the centromere with loss of the short arms; carriers have 45 chromosomes but are typically healthy. Robertsonian translocations involving chromosome 21 can produce offspring with translocation Down syndrome. Insertions move a segment into a new position within or between chromosomes.

Formation

Translocations arise when DNA double-strand breaks on different chromosomes are mis-repaired by non-homologous end joining, or through aberrant recombination between repeated sequences. During meiosis, balanced translocation carriers form quadrivalent structures that can segregate unevenly, yielding gametes with duplications and deletions.

Role in cancer

Recurrent translocations are hallmarks of many cancers. They act either by creating fusion genes with new activity — such as BCR::ABL1 from the Philadelphia chromosome t(9;22) in chronic myeloid leukemia — or by placing an oncogene under the control of a strongly active regulatory element, as in the t(8;14) of Burkitt lymphoma, which brings MYC next to immunoglobulin enhancers. Other examples include PML::RARA in acute promyelocytic leukemia and EWSR1::FLI1 in Ewing sarcoma; several are diagnostic markers and drug targets.

Detection

Translocations are detected by karyotyping, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), chromosomal microarray (for unbalanced products), PCR for fusion transcripts, and DNA sequencing.
