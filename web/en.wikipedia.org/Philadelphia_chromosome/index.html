Philadelphia chromosome

The reciprocal translocation t(9;22) creates the Philadelphia chromosome.

The Philadelphia chromosome or Philadelphia translocation (Ph) is a specific genetic abnormality in chromosome 22 of leukemia cancer cells (particularly chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) cells). This chromosome is defective and unusually short because of reciprocal translocation, t(9;22)(q34;q11), of genetic material between chromosome 9 and chromosome 22, and contains a fusion gene called BCR::ABL1. This gene is the ABL1 gene of chromosome 9 juxtaposed onto the breakpoint cluster region BCR gene of chromosome 22, coding for a hybrid protein: a tyrosine kinase signalling protein that is "always on", causing the cell to divide uncontrollably by interrupting the stability of the genome and impairing various signalling pathways governing the cell cycle.

The presence of this translocation is required for diagnosis of CML; in its absence, one has either atypical CML or another disease. The Philadelphia chromosome is also found in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL, most frequently the B-cell type) and occasionally in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) as well as mixed-phenotype acute leukemia (MPAL).

History

The Philadelphia chromosome was first described in 1959 by David Hungerford and Peter Nowell in Philadelphia — hence the name — and was the first consistent genetic abnormality associated with a specific human cancer. In 1973 Janet Rowley showed that it arose from a reciprocal translocation. Identification of the BCR::ABL1 fusion in the 1980s led directly to the development of the targeted drug imatinib (Gleevec), approved in 2001, which transformed CML from a fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition and launched the era of targeted cancer therapy.

Mechanism

The ABL1 protein normally functions as a tightly regulated tyrosine kinase. In the BCR::ABL1 fusion protein, the kinase is constitutively active, driving proliferation and survival of hematopoietic stem cells through pathways including Ras/MAPK, PI3K/AKT and STAT5, while reducing adhesion to the bone marrow stroma. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors block the ATP-binding site of the enzyme, suppressing the malignant clone.
