Phosphorylation

Phosphorylation is the biochemical addition of a phosphate group (PO43−) to an organic molecule. In proteins, enzymes called protein kinases transfer the γ-phosphate of ATP onto the hydroxyl groups of serine, threonine, or tyrosine residues, while phosphatases remove it; this reversible switch regulates enzyme activity, protein–protein interactions, and signal transduction throughout the cell. Phosphorylation is equally central to metabolism: glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation store and shuttle chemical energy in phosphate bonds, most importantly in ATP.

Phosphorylation and the Philadelphia chromosome

The Philadelphia chromosome exerts its cancer-causing effect precisely through phosphorylation. Its BCR::ABL1 fusion protein is a tyrosine kinase freed from its normal autoinhibition, so it phosphorylates tyrosine residues on dozens of substrates continuously and without the growth-factor signals that normally gate ABL1 activity. The resulting hyperphosphorylation switches on proliferative and survival pathways (including STAT5, PI3K/AKT, and RAS/MAPK) in the leukaemia cell. Consequently, the clinical monitoring of chronic myeloid leukaemia and the design of its drugs are framed in phosphorylation terms: tyrosine kinase inhibitors occupy the ATP-binding pocket of BCR::ABL1 and shut down its phosphate-transferring activity, and phospho-specific assays can measure pathway activity in patients' cells.

History

Protein phosphorylation was first observed with casein and vitellin in the early 20th century; its regulatory role was established by Edmond Fischer and Edwin Krebs, who won the 1992 Nobel Prize for describing reversible phosphorylation as a biological control mechanism.
