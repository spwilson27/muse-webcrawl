Protein kinase

A protein kinase is a kinase enzyme that modifies other proteins by chemically adding phosphate groups to them — phosphorylation. The human genome encodes about 518 protein kinases (the "kinome"), one of the largest gene families, and they regulate nearly every cellular process, including metabolism, cell-cycle progression, apoptosis, and signal transduction. Kinases are grouped by the residue they phosphorylate: serine/threonine kinases, tyrosine kinases (such as the insulin receptor and the Src family), and dual-specificity kinases.

ABL1 and the Philadelphia chromosome

Among tyrosine kinases, ABL1 is the centrepiece of the Philadelphia chromosome story. In normal cells the ABL1 kinase is tightly autoinhibited. The t(9;22) translocation fuses ABL1 to BCR, whose coiled-coil domain forces the fusion protein to oligomerize and keeps the kinase permanently switched on — a "constitutively active" kinase that drives chronic myeloid leukaemia. BCR::ABL1 thus became the paradigm of a druggable kinase: imatinib, a small molecule that locks the ABL1 kinase domain in its inactive conformation, was the first successful kinase inhibitor in medicine and the founding example of targeted therapy. Resistance mutations in the kinase domain (notably the T315I "gatekeeper") spurred second- and third-generation inhibitors such as dasatinib, nilotinib, and ponatinib.
