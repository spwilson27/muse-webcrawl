Signal transduction

Signal transduction is the process by which a cell converts an extracellular signal — a hormone, growth factor, neurotransmitter, or physical stimulus — into a functional response. A signalling molecule (ligand) binds a receptor, which activates intracellular relay proteins, often protein kinases in cascades, second messengers such as cyclic AMP and Ca2+, and ultimately effectors that alter gene expression, metabolism, or cell shape. Major pathways include MAPK/ERK, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, JAK/STAT, Wnt, Hedgehog, and Notch.

Hijacked signalling in Philadelphia-positive leukaemia

The Philadelphia chromosome causes cancer by corrupting signal transduction. Its BCR::ABL1 fusion kinase sits permanently "upstream-on": it phosphorylates adaptor proteins such as CRKL and GRB2 and thereby keeps the RAS/MAPK proliferation pathway, the PI3K/AKT survival pathway, and the STAT5 transcription pathway continuously active in the leukaemia stem cell, which no longer needs external growth factors. Signal-transduction research on BCR::ABL1 mapped much of this wiring and explained why blocking the single kinase with imatinib collapses the whole network: the malignant clone is "addicted" to that one aberrant signal. The example now anchors the broader field of signalling-targeted cancer therapy.
