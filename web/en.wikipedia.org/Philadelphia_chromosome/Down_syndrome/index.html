Down syndrome

Down syndrome (also Down's syndrome) is a genetic disorder caused by the presence of all or part of a third copy of chromosome 21 — trisomy 21. It is the most common chromosomal cause of intellectual disability, occurring in about 1 in 700–1,000 births, with incidence rising with maternal age. Characteristic features include a distinctive facial appearance, hypotonia, congenital heart defects (especially atrioventricular septal defect), increased risk of childhood leukaemia, and early-onset Alzheimer's disease. The condition is named after John Langdon Down, who described it in 1866; its chromosomal basis was established by Jérôme Lejeune in 1959.

Down syndrome, leukaemia, and the Philadelphia chromosome

Children with Down syndrome have a markedly elevated risk of acute leukaemias — both acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) and a distinctive acute megakaryoblastic leukaemia preceded by transient abnormal myelopoiesis driven by GATA1 mutations. This pairing makes Down syndrome a key comparator for the Philadelphia chromosome: both are chromosome abnormalities that predispose to leukaemia, but in fundamentally different ways. Trisomy 21 is a constitutional, numerical abnormality present in every cell; the Philadelphia chromosome is an acquired, structural translocation, t(9;22), arising in a single haematopoietic stem cell and defining chronic myeloid leukaemia and a subtype of ALL. Notably, Philadelphia-chromosome-positive ALL is itself treated differently from Down-syndrome ALL, for which standard chemotherapy toxicity demands particular care.

Screening and care

Prenatal screening (first-trimester combined testing, non-invasive prenatal testing) and diagnostic karyotyping identify trisomy 21 before or after birth. Care focuses on early intervention, cardiac and thyroid surveillance, and educational support.
