Imatinib

Trade namesGleevec, Glivec
TypeSmall-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitor
TargetsBCR::ABL1, KIT, PDGFRA/B
IndicationsChronic myeloid leukemia, gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GIST), others
Approved2001 (US FDA)
Legal statusPrescription only; WHO Essential Medicine

Imatinib, sold under the brand names Gleevec and Glivec, is an oral targeted therapy used to treat cancer. It is a tyrosine kinase inhibitor that blocks the BCR::ABL1 fusion protein responsible for chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), as well as the KIT and PDGF receptors. Approved in 2001, imatinib is widely regarded as the first successful targeted cancer drug and a proof of concept for precision medicine.

Development

The drug was developed at Novartis in the 1990s by a team including Nicholas Lydon and chemist Jürg Zimmermann, guided by the biology championed by oncologist Brian Druker, who led the first clinical trials in 1998. Results were dramatic: nearly all chronic-phase CML patients in the phase I trial achieved complete haematological responses. The FDA approved imatinib in May 2001 after one of the fastest reviews in its history, and Time magazine dubbed it a "magic bullet" against cancer.

Uses

Imatinib is first-line therapy for Philadelphia-chromosome-positive CML and acute lymphoblastic leukemia, and standard treatment for gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GIST), most of which are driven by mutant KIT. It is also used in dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, hypereosinophilic syndrome, and certain other rare conditions. It appears on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines.

Mechanism

Imatinib binds the ATP-binding site of its target kinases when they are in the inactive conformation, preventing phosphorylation of downstream substrates and shutting off the signals that drive cell proliferation and survival. In CML it selectively kills cells expressing BCR::ABL1 while sparing most normal cells.

Side effects and resistance

Common adverse effects include nausea, muscle cramps, oedema (especially periorbital), rash, diarrhoea, and cytopenias; most are mild and manageable. Resistance can develop, commonly through point mutations in the ABL1 kinase domain or amplification of BCR::ABL1, prompting switch to second- or third-generation inhibitors. Imatinib's original high price — rising above $120,000 per year in the US — became a prominent case in debates over drug pricing; generic versions became available from 2016.
