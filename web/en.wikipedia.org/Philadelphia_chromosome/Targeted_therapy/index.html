Targeted therapy

Targeted therapy is a type of cancer treatment that uses drugs designed to act on specific molecular targets — mutated proteins, fusion genes, or signalling pathways — that drive a cancer's growth, rather than the indiscriminate cell killing of conventional chemotherapy. The main categories are small-molecule inhibitors (many of them protein kinase inhibitors) and monoclonal antibodies.

Imatinib and the Philadelphia chromosome: the founding success

Targeted therapy began with the Philadelphia chromosome. Chronic myeloid leukaemia is driven by a single lesion, the BCR::ABL1 fusion kinase, whose abnormal signal transduction the tumour depends upon. Imatinib (Gleevec), developed in the 1990s by Nicholas Lydon, Brian Druker and colleagues and approved in 2001, blocks the ATP-binding site of BCR::ABL1. Responses were unprecedented: most chronic-phase patients achieved complete cytogenetic remission, and life expectancy approached that of the general population. Imatinib proved that a genetically defined cancer could be controlled by a pill aimed at its causal molecule, launching the era of precision oncology followed by trastuzumab for HER2-positive breast cancer, EGFR and ALK inhibitors in lung cancer, and many others — as well as second-generation ABL inhibitors for imatinib-resistant disease.
