Philadelphia chromosome


The t(9;22) translocation creates the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene.

The Philadelphia chromosome or Philadelphia translocation, designated Ph, is a reciprocal translocation mutation designated t(9;22)(q34;q11) involving chromosome 9 and chromosome 22, characteristic of chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML) and found in a subset of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia and acute myeloid leukaemia. It was the first consistent chromosomal abnormality discovered in any cancer.

Discovery and mechanism

The abnormality was described in 1960 by Peter Nowell of the University of Pennsylvania and David Hungerford of the Fox Chase Cancer Center, both in Philadelphia — hence its name. In 1973 Janet Rowley showed that the shortened chromosome 22 resulted from a reciprocal exchange with chromosome 9. The translocation fuses the BCR gene on chromosome 22 with the ABL1 proto-oncogene on chromosome 9, producing the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene. Its protein product is a constitutively active tyrosine kinase that drives uncontrolled proliferation of myeloid cells — a landmark demonstration that a specific genetic lesion causes a specific cancer.

Clinical significance

The Philadelphia chromosome is present in over 90% of CML cases and in roughly a quarter of adult acute lymphoblastic leukaemias, where it historically conferred a poor prognosis. Detection by cytogenetics, FISH, or PCR for BCR::ABL1 transcripts is used for diagnosis and for monitoring residual disease during treatment.

Treatment

Understanding the fusion's kinase activity led directly to imatinib (Gleevec), the first targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor, approved in 2001, which transformed CML from a fatal disease into a chronic, manageable condition for most patients. Later-generation inhibitors such as dasatinib, nilotinib, bosutinib, ponatinib, and the allosteric inhibitor asciminib address resistance mutations including T315I.
