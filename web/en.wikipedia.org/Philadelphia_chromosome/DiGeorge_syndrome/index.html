DiGeorge syndrome

DiGeorge syndrome, also known as 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, is a disorder caused by a small deletion on the long arm of chromosome 22 at band q11.2. It is one of the most common chromosomal microdeletion syndromes, occurring in roughly 1 in 4,000 births. Features classically include conotruncal heart defects (such as tetralogy of Fallot), thymic underdevelopment causing immune deficiency, hypoparathyroidism with low calcium, cleft palate or velopharyngeal insufficiency, characteristic facial features, and learning difficulties. Angelo DiGeorge described the association of thymic aplasia and congenital heart disease in the 1960s.

Chromosome 22 and the Philadelphia chromosome

The 22q11.2 region lies close to the breakpoint involved in the Philadelphia chromosome, t(9;22)(q34;q11), which fuses BCR at 22q11 with ABL1 from chromosome 9 to drive chronic myeloid leukaemia. The two conditions illustrate opposite faces of chromosome 22 pathology: DiGeorge syndrome is a constitutional (germline) chromosome abnormality present in every cell from birth, whereas the Philadelphia translocation is an acquired, somatic change arising in a single blood stem cell. The BCR gene region is also a known hotspot of genomic instability flanked by low-copy repeats that mediate deletions and translocations alike.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is confirmed by chromosomal microarray or FISH testing for the 22q11.2 deletion. Management is multidisciplinary: cardiac surgery, calcium supplementation, immune monitoring, speech therapy, and psychiatric surveillance, since the deletion also raises the risk of schizophrenia.
