Human genome

The human genome is the complete set of nucleic acid sequences of humans, stored as DNA in 23 chromosome pairs (22 autosomes plus the X and Y sex chromosomes) in cell nuclei, plus a small genome in mitochondria. It comprises roughly 3.1 billion base pairs and about 19,000–20,000 protein-coding genes. The Human Genome Project produced the first near-complete sequence in 2003, and the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium published a truly complete, gapless assembly in 2022.

Rearrangements: the Philadelphia chromosome in genomic terms

Genomic medicine reads the genome not only base by base but also in large-scale structure. The Philadelphia chromosome was discovered in 1960 — decades before sequencing — as a visibly shortened chromosome 22 in the leukaemia cells of patients with chronic myeloid leukaemia. Genome analysis showed it to be a balanced reciprocal translocation, t(9;22)(q34;q11), joining the ABL1 tyrosine kinase gene to BCR, creating the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene. Modern whole-genome sequencing of cancers now detects such structural variants systematically, and the BCR::ABL1 fusion remains the archetype of a genomically defined disease: a diagnosis made by the rearrangement itself and treated with a drug, imatinib, designed against its protein product.

Variation

Any two human genomes differ at millions of positions; large projects such as 1000 Genomes and gnomAD catalogue this variation, underpinning the interpretation of both inherited disorders such as DiGeorge syndrome (a 22q11.2 microdeletion) and acquired cancer rearrangements.
