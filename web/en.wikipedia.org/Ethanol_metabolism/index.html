Ethanol metabolism

Main oxidative pathway of ethanol metabolism

Ethanol metabolism is the process by which the human body breaks down ethanol (the alcohol in alcoholic beverages), principally in the liver. Ethanol is oxidised in two steps: first to acetaldehyde, then to acetate, which is further converted to carbon dioxide and water in peripheral tissues.

Pathway

The main enzyme of the first step is alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH), a cytosolic enzyme that converts ethanol to acetaldehyde while reducing NAD+ to NADH. Acetaldehyde, a toxic and carcinogenic intermediate, is then rapidly oxidised to acetate by aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH), mainly the mitochondrial enzyme ALDH2. A secondary route, the microsomal ethanol-oxidising system centred on CYP2E1, becomes significant at higher concentrations and in chronic drinkers; catalase plays a minor role.

Rate and effects

Because ADH is saturated at low ethanol concentrations, elimination proceeds at a roughly constant rate (zero-order kinetics), typically lowering blood alcohol concentration by about 0.015 per hour. The accumulation of NADH shifts metabolic equilibria, contributing to lactic acidosis, hypoglycaemia, and fatty liver in heavy drinkers; acetaldehyde accumulation causes flushing, nausea, and tachycardia. Genetic variants in ADH and ALDH — notably the ALDH2*2 allele common in East Asian populations — alter these responses and the risk of alcohol dependence. The drug disulfiram inhibits ALDH and is used to deter drinking by making acetaldehyde accumulate after alcohol intake.
