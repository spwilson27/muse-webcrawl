Old Norse religion, also known as Norse paganism, is the most widely attested form of Germanic paganism, the set of religious traditions and practices followed by the Norse peoples of Scandinavia and their settlements during the Viking Age and earlier. It was a polytheistic folk religion without scripture, creed, or central authority: practice centred on ritual sacrifice (blót), feasting, seasonal festivals, and the veneration of gods (Æsir and Vanir), ancestors, and land spirits (landvættir), at sites ranging from household shrines to great temples such as the one Adam of Bremen described at Uppsala.

Cosmology

Norse cosmology holds that the world was created from the body of the primordial being Ymir by Odin and his brothers. The universe is structured around the immense ash tree Yggdrasil, whose roots and branches connect realms including Asgard (home of the Æsir), Midgard (the human world), Jotunheim (giants), and Hel (the dead). Fate (wyrd, ørlög) woven by the Norns at the Well of Urd binds gods and mortals alike; the cosmos is destined to end in the cataclysm of Ragnarök — a great battle in which many gods die — after which a renewed world rises from the sea.

Practice

Public cult was led by chieftains (goðar), who combined political and priestly roles, especially in Iceland. Rituals included sacrificial feasts of horses and other animals, toasts to gods and ancestors, oath-taking on rings and boars, divination, and seiðr (a form of sorcery associated with Freyja and Odin). Burial practices ranged from cremation to inhumation in ships, mounds, and chamber graves with rich goods. Thor, god of thunder, appears to have been the most widely beloved deity among farmers and seafarers; Odin was more associated with kings, warriors and poets; Freyr with fertility and good harvests.

Christianisation and sources

Scandinavia converted gradually between the 8th and 12th centuries: Denmark under Harald Bluetooth (c. 965), Iceland by decision of the Althing (999/1000), Norway under the two Olafs, and Sweden last (12th century). Because the mythology was written down mainly by Christian Icelanders of the 13th century — above all Snorri Sturluson — interpreting the sources requires care. Archaeology (the Oseberg and Gokstad ship burials, picture stones, hoards of Thor's-hammer pendants) and hundreds of theophoric place names supplement the texts. Since the 19th century the religion has inspired art and national romanticism, and since the 1970s it has been revived as modern Heathenry/Ásatrú, recognised as a religion in Iceland (1973) and elsewhere.
