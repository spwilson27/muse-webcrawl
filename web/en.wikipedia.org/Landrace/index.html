Landrace

Traditional maize landraces, valued for their genetic diversity and local adaptation.

A landrace is a domesticated, locally adapted, often traditional variety of a species of animal or plant that has developed over time, through adaptation to its natural and cultural environment of agriculture and pastoralism, and due to isolation from other populations of the species. Landraces are generally distinguished from cultivars and from standard breeds, although the term "landrace breed" is sometimes used to distinguish between animals within a species.

Specimens of a landrace tend to be genetically similar, though more diverse than members of a standardised or formal breed. Some formal breeds originated from landraces through decades of focused breeding work; in other cases, formally standardising a landrace may result in the landrace becoming a formal breed.

Characteristics

Landraces are characterised by their dynamic adaptation to local conditions — soils, climate, pests, diseases and farming practice — and by continuing evolution under both natural selection and the informal selection of farmers who save seed or breeding stock from year to year. Plant landraces are important reservoirs of genetic diversity for crop breeding, including resistance genes lost in modern uniform cultivars. Examples include the many maize landraces of Mexico, Andean potato landraces, Ethiopian barley and wheat landraces, and rice landraces across Asia.

Animal landraces

In livestock, landraces include cattle, pig, sheep, goat, horse and poultry populations adapted to particular regions — for example the Icelandic horse, the Shetland sheep, or hardy hill breeds of cattle. Native pig breeds whose names include "Landrace" (such as the Danish Landrace or British Landrace) began as landraces but have since been standardised into formal breeds.

Conservation

Because modern commercial agriculture favours a small number of high-yielding uniform varieties, many landraces are endangered. Conservation efforts include community seed banks, on-farm conservation by farmers, and ex situ storage in gene banks such as the Svalbard Global Seed Vault. International frameworks such as the FAO's International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources recognise farmers' rights in maintaining landrace diversity.
