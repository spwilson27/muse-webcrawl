Modernist literature

1922, the annus mirabilis of modernism, saw the publication of Ulysses and The Waste Land

Modernist literature is a body of writing originating in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, mainly in Europe and North America, characterised by a self-conscious break with traditional ways of writing and by radical experimentation with form. Modernist writers, responding to industrialisation, urbanisation, the First World War, and new ideas from Freud, Einstein, and anthropology, sought — in Ezra Pound's slogan — to "make it new".

Characteristics

Hallmarks include stream of consciousness and interior monologue; fragmented, non-linear narrative; multiple or unreliable narrators; dense allusion to myth and earlier literature; free verse in poetry; and a preoccupation with subjective experience over external description. Virginia Woolf argued that "on or about December 1910 human character changed", and resolved to render consciousness itself — "an ordinary mind on an ordinary day" — rather than conventional plot.

Major works and authors

Landmark works include James Joyce's Ulysses (1922), T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land (1922), Woolf's Mrs Dalloway (1925) and To the Lighthouse (1927), William Faulkner's The Sound and the Fury (1929), Marcel Proust's In Search of Lost Time (1913–27), Franz Kafka's The Trial (1925), and Robert Musil's The Man Without Qualities. Poets included Pound, W. B. Yeats, Wallace Stevens, and H.D.; dramatists and later novelists such as Samuel Beckett extended the movement into "late modernism".

Legacy

Modernism reshaped the novel and poetry permanently; its techniques were absorbed into mainstream writing, while postmodernism in the later 20th century both continued and parodied its experiments.
