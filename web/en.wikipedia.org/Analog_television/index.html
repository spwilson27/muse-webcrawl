Analog television

[Image placeholder]

Analog television is the original television technology that uses analog signals to transmit video and audio. The first practical systems, including mechanical scanning devices demonstrated by John Logie Baird in the 1920s, gave way to fully electronic systems developed in the 1930s by Philo Farnsworth, Vladimir Zworykin and others. Regular broadcasts began before the Second World War in Britain, Germany and the United States, and after 1945 analog television spread worldwide.

Technical principles

An analog television signal encodes brightness (luminance) and colour (chrominance) as a continuously varying waveform, scanned line by line. Principal broadcast systems were NTSC (525 lines, 59.94 fields per second, used in North America, Japan and elsewhere), PAL (625 lines, 50 fields, used in most of Europe, Asia and Oceania) and SECAM (used in France, Russia and parts of Africa and the Middle East), each paired with standards designating channel bandwidth and modulation. Sound was typically carried on a frequency-modulated subcarrier.

Digital transition

From the 1990s onward, digital television standards — DVB in Europe, ATSC in North America, ISDB in Japan and South America, DTMB in China — replaced analog broadcasts, offering more channels, better picture quality and interactive services in the same spectrum. Countries switched off analog transmitters one by one: the Netherlands and Luxembourg were among the first in 2006, the United States ended full-power analog broadcasts in 2009, most of Europe followed by 2012, and Japan in 2011–12. Some countries in Africa, Asia and Latin America completed the transition later. Analog signals persist in some cable systems, closed-circuit installations and amateur television.
