Asepsis

Asepsis is the state of being free from disease-causing microorganisms such as pathogenic bacteria, viruses and fungi. The term also refers to the practices used to achieve and maintain that state, collectively called aseptic technique, which are fundamental to surgery and modern clinical care.

Concept

Asepsis differs from antisepsis: aseptic technique aims to prevent microorganisms from reaching a vulnerable site at all, whereas antisepsis reduces the microbial load on living tissue with chemical agents. It also differs from sterility, an absolute condition achieved by sterilisation of instruments and materials.

Practices

Aseptic technique includes hand hygiene, sterile gloves and gowns, sterilised instruments, disinfected skin at the operative site, sterile drapes, and maintenance of a sterile field. In the operating theatre these are reinforced by filtered air flow, limited traffic and strict protocols for handling sterile supplies. Similar "no-touch" aseptic methods are used for wound dressings, intravenous lines and urinary catheters in everyday care.

History

The principles of asepsis developed in the late nineteenth century out of the germ theory of disease and Joseph Lister's antiseptic surgery, with surgeons such as Ernst von Bergmann promoting steam sterilisation and aseptic ritual in the operating room.
