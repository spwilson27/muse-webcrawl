Indigenous Australians

Indigenous Australians are people with familial heritage from, or membership in, the ethnic groups that lived in Australia before British colonisation. They consist of two distinct groups: the Aboriginal peoples of the Australian mainland and surrounding islands, and the Torres Strait Islander peoples of the Torres Strait Islands. The term Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples is also used. Genetic and archaeological evidence indicates that Indigenous Australians descend from people who arrived in Australia at least 50,000–65,000 years ago, making theirs among the oldest continuous cultures in the world.

History

Prior to colonisation, Indigenous Australians spoke at least 250 distinct languages and organised into hundreds of clan groups, each with its own territory, law (the Dreaming, or Tjukurrpa) and ceremonial life. The arrival of the First Fleet in 1788 began a long period of dispossession, frontier violence, introduced disease and forced removal of children (the Stolen Generations, roughly 1905–1969). The 1967 referendum enabled the federal government to legislate for Aboriginal people and count them in the census. The High Court's 1992 Mabo decision rejected the doctrine of terra nullius and led to the Native Title Act 1993.

Culture

Indigenous Australian art — including rock art, bark painting and the Western Desert dot-painting movement — is internationally renowned. The didgeridoo (yidaki) developed in Arnhem Land. Dreaming stories encode law, geography and kinship, and concepts such as the songline link people to country across the continent.

Demographics

In the 2021 census, 812,728 people identified as Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander, about 3.2% of the Australian population. Indigenous Australians experience persistent gaps in life expectancy, health, education and employment relative to non-Indigenous Australians, the subject of the national "Closing the Gap" framework.
