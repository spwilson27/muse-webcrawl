Operation Moses

Operation Moses (Hebrew: Mivtza Moshe) was the covert evacuation of Ethiopian Jews (Beta Israel) from Sudan to Israel during the Ethiopian Civil War and Sudanese famine of 1984–1985. Between 21 November 1984 and 5 January 1985, some 7,000–8,000 Ethiopian Jews who had fled on foot to refugee camps in Sudan were flown to Israel in a secret airlift organised by the Mossad with Sudanese cooperation.

Background

Thousands of Beta Israel had trekked from their villages in Gondar and Tigray to Sudan, enduring robbery, disease and death on the journey, hoping to reach Israel. In the camps they concealed their Jewish identity. After years of small-scale clandestine evacuations, Israel negotiated the large airlift with Sudanese president Jaafar Nimeiri, mediated by the United States, on condition of strict secrecy.

The airlift

Jews were brought by bus to Khartoum and flown out on near-daily flights, routed through European cities, by the Belgian charter airline Trans European Airways. The operation ended abruptly in January 1985 after details leaked and Israeli prime minister Shimon Peres confirmed it at a press conference; Arab pressure forced Sudan to halt the flights, stranding several thousand Jews in Sudan. A follow-up US airlift, Operation Joshua, evacuated about 500 more in March 1985.

Legacy

Many families were separated in the hurried departures. The remaining Beta Israel were eventually brought to Israel, most dramatically in Operation Solomon in 1991, which airlifted over 14,000 people in 36 hours. Operation Moses marked the beginning of mass Ethiopian aliyah and the integration of the Beta Israel community into Israeli society.
