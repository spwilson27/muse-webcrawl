The Book of Genesis (from Greek génesis, "origin"; Hebrew: Bereshit, "In the beginning") is the first book of the Hebrew Bible and the Christian Old Testament. It recounts the creation of the world, the earliest history of humanity, and the origins of the Israelites through the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

Contents

The book falls into two broad parts. The primeval history (chapters 1–11) contains two accounts of creation, the story of Adam and Eve in the Garden of Eden and the Fall, Cain and Abel, the great Flood survived by Noah, and the Tower of Babel. The patriarchal history (chapters 12–50) follows God's covenant with Abraham, the near-sacrifice of Isaac, Jacob's family and his twelve sons, and the story of Joseph, sold into slavery in Egypt, who rises to become Pharaoh's minister — setting the scene for the Exodus.

Composition

Tradition attributes Genesis to Moses, but modern scholars generally hold that it was composed from several sources (commonly labelled J, E, and P in the documentary hypothesis) and reached its final form in the Persian period, around the 5th century BC, drawing on older Israelite traditions and a wider ancient Near Eastern background — including Mesopotamian flood stories such as the Epic of Gilgamesh.

Themes

Central themes include creation and order from chaos, human disobedience and its consequences, covenant, blessing, election, and God's faithfulness to promises despite human failure. Genesis has profoundly shaped Jewish, Christian, and Islamic thought, art, and literature.
