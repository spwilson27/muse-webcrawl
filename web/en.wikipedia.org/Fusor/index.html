Fusor

A fusor is a device that uses an electric field to heat ions to conditions suitable for nuclear fusion. Developed principally by Philo Farnsworth — the inventor of electronic television — from the 1960s, the fusor is a form of inertial electrostatic confinement (IEC) fusion. It is the most common device used by amateur fusion experimenters because it can be built relatively simply and produces measurable fusion reactions, though not net energy.

Mechanism

A fusor consists of a vacuum chamber containing two concentric wire cages. A large voltage difference (typically tens of kilovolts) is applied between the cages, with the inner cage at negative potential. Ionised gas — usually deuterium — is attracted toward the centre: positive ions accelerate inward, pass through the inner grid, and collide at the dense core, where some undergo fusion, producing neutrons and helium-3. The glowing ball of plasma visible at the centre is sometimes called a "star in a jar".

History

Farnsworth and his team at ITT developed the fusor in the 1960s; Robert Hirsch later joined and improved the design, and the Hirsch–Meeks fusor remains the standard configuration. A small community of hobbyists ("fusioneers") builds fusors, largely for education and neutron production. Lists maintained by the community record amateurs who have achieved genuine fusion.

Limitations

Fusors lose energy through grid collisions, bremsstrahlung, and conduction, and their fusion rates are far too low for power generation; typical amateur devices produce on the order of thousands to millions of neutrons per second. Commercially, fusors and related IEC devices are used as compact neutron sources for applications such as materials analysis and explosives detection.
