Coronene

Coronene is a polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) composed of seven fused benzene rings arranged in a roughly circular, disc-like molecule. Its chemical formula is C24H12. Also historically called superbenzene, it is a yellow solid that fluoresces blue-white under ultraviolet light and is notable as a model compound for the structure of graphite and soot.

Occurrence and synthesis

Coronene occurs naturally in the mineral karpatite (carpathite), found as yellow crystals in mercury deposits, and in trace amounts in coal tar and petroleum fractions. It was first synthesised in 1932 by Kurt Meyer. Like other large PAHs, it forms during incomplete combustion and has been detected in interstellar dust spectra as a candidate carrier of some unidentified infrared emission bands.

Properties

Coronene is planar, highly symmetric (D6h), and almost insoluble in most solvents. It has been widely used in surface science as a model for graphene flakes: coronene molecules adsorbed on metal surfaces form ordered monolayers studied by scanning tunnelling microscopy. Because it is a PAH formed in combustion, its presence in environmental samples is monitored alongside other potentially carcinogenic PAHs, though coronene itself is comparatively less toxic than smaller PAHs such as benzo(a)pyrene.
