Official language

An official language is a language given a special legal status in a particular country, state, or other jurisdiction. Typically the term is used for the language or languages used in government documents, legislation, courts, and public administration, and often in publicly funded education.

Characteristics

Official status may be defined by a country's constitution, by statute, or by practice (de facto). Some countries have a single official language (e.g., France with French), others have two or more (e.g., Canada with English and French; India with Hindi and English at the Union level plus scheduled languages), and some have none officially designated, relying on a de facto national language (e.g., the United States at the federal level for most of its history, and Australia with de facto English).

Official versus national languages

An official language is distinct from a national language, which symbolises national identity and may or may not be used in administration. Some states recognise minority or regional languages officially within part of their territory, such as Welsh in Wales or Catalan in Catalonia.

International organisations

International bodies also adopt official languages: the United Nations has six, the European Union has 24, and the African Union and ASEAN maintain their own sets, balancing representativeness with the cost of translation and interpretation.

Issues

Choice of official languages can be politically contentious, affecting access to government services, education, and employment, and sometimes fuelling separatist or identity movements. Policies range from monolingual assimilation to extensive multilingual provision.
