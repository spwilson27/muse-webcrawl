Homology (mathematics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, homology is a general way of associating a sequence of algebraic objects, such as abelian groups or modules, with other mathematical objects such as topological spaces. Homology groups were originally used in algebraic topology to express the failure of certain kinds of chains to be boundaries; intuitively, they count "holes" of different dimensions in a space. Similar constructions are available in a wide range of other contexts, such as abstract algebra, groups, Lie algebras, Galois theory, and algebraic geometry.

For a topological space, the singular homology groups Hn(X) are built from a chain complex of formal sums of continuous maps from standard simplices into X, with a boundary operator satisfying ∂² = 0; the n-th homology group is the quotient of cycles (chains with zero boundary) by boundaries. Its rank — roughly, the number of n-dimensional holes — is the n-th Betti number: a sphere has one 2-dimensional hole, a torus has two independent 1-dimensional cycles and one 2-dimensional hole, and so on.

History and generalisations

The notion grew out of Henri Poincaré's foundational work on Analysis Situs (1895), where he defined homology in terms of submanifolds and Betti numbers, and from Emmy Noether's insight in the 1920s that the underlying structures are groups, not merely numbers. Simplicial, singular, cellular, and de Rham approaches were shown to compute the same invariants for reasonable spaces, and the Eilenberg–Steenrod axioms (1945) characterised homology theories axiomatically. Variants include cohomology, which carries a ring structure and contravariant functoriality, extraordinary theories such as K-theory and cobordism, and Floer and Khovanov homologies in symplectic geometry and knot theory. In homological algebra, derived functors such as Tor and Ext, and the homology of chain complexes in general categories, extend the same circle of ideas far beyond topology.

Applications

Homology provides some of the most powerful invariants distinguishing topological spaces, underpinning results such as the Brouwer fixed-point theorem and the hairy ball theorem. Persistent homology, which tracks homology classes across a filtration of a data set, is a central tool of topological data analysis, applied to sensor networks, materials science, and neuroscience.
