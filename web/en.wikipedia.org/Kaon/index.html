Kaon

In particle physics, a kaon, also called a K meson or K, is any of a group of four mesons distinguished by a quantum number called strangeness. Kaons contain one strange quark (or antiquark) plus one up or down quark or antiquark: K+ (u + anti-s), K− (s + anti-u), and the neutral K0 and anti-K0. Their mass is about 494–498 MeV/c², roughly half that of the proton.

Discovery and properties

Kaons were discovered in cosmic rays in 1947 by George Rochester and Clifford Butler at Manchester, among the "V particles" that decayed surprisingly slowly — behaviour explained by the new strangeness quantum number proposed by Murray Gell-Mann and Kazuhiko Nishijima. Charged kaons live about 1.24 × 10−8 seconds; neutral kaons form the short-lived KS (8.95 × 10−11 s) and long-lived KL (5.1 × 10−8 s) mixtures.

CP violation

In 1964, James Cronin and Val Fitch discovered that the long-lived neutral kaon occasionally decays into two pions, violating the combined charge-parity (CP) symmetry — a landmark result that won the 1980 Nobel Prize and remains central to explaining the matter–antimatter asymmetry of the universe. Kaon experiments such as NA62 at CERN continue to probe rare decays for physics beyond the Standard Model.
