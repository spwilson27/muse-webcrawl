Monotheism

Monotheism
DefinitionBelief in the existence of one god
Major traditionsJudaism, Christianity, Islam, Sikhism, Baháʼí Faith
Contrasts withPolytheism, atheism, henotheism

The Abrahamic faiths are the best-known monotheistic traditions.

Monotheism is the belief that there is only one deity, an all-encompassing God, distinguishable from a plurality of gods. The word derives from the Greek monos ("single") and theos ("god"). A distinction may be drawn between exclusive monotheism and forms in which one god is worshipped without denying that others may worship different gods with equal validity.

Historical development

An early experiment in monotheism occurred in ancient Egypt under Pharaoh Akhenaten (14th century BCE), who promoted the sun-disc Aten above other gods. Zoroastrianism centred devotion on Ahura Mazda. Ancient Israelite religion developed from monolatry — worship of one god among many acknowledged — into the strict monotheism proclaimed in texts such as Second Isaiah.

Abrahamic religions

Judaism, Christianity, and Islam all affirm one creator God, understood to descend from the faith of Abraham. Christianity's doctrine of the Trinity is generally held by its adherents to be consistent with monotheism, a point debated by Jewish and Muslim theologians. Sikhism and the Baháʼí Faith are also strictly monotheistic.
