Pontiac fever

Pontiac fever is a mild, flu-like illness caused by inhalation of aerosolized water contaminated with Legionella bacteria, most often Legionella pneumophila. It is the non-pneumonic form of legionellosis, in contrast to the more severe Legionnaires' disease. Unlike Legionnaires' disease, Pontiac fever usually resolves on its own without treatment and is not associated with pneumonia.

History

The condition was named after Pontiac, Michigan, where it was first recognized in 1968, when 144 employees of the county health department building fell ill with fever, headache, and muscle aches. The source was traced to a defective air-conditioning system that had aerosolized contaminated water. The episode was only explained retrospectively, after the 1976 Philadelphia outbreak of Legionnaires' disease led to the discovery of Legionella; stored serum from the Pontiac patients showed antibodies to the newly identified bacterium.

Symptoms and course

Symptoms typically develop within 24 to 72 hours of exposure and include fever, chills, headache, myalgia, fatigue, and sometimes cough or dizziness. The attack rate in exposed groups is characteristically very high — often above 90 percent — but the illness lasts only two to five days and resolves spontaneously. Diagnosis is rarely confirmed clinically; outbreaks are usually identified epidemiologically and by serology or urinary antigen testing of the exposed group.

Sources

Implicated sources include cooling towers, air-conditioning systems, whirlpool spas, humidifiers, and showers. Because cases are usually mild and not individually reported, Pontiac fever is believed to be substantially under-recognized.
