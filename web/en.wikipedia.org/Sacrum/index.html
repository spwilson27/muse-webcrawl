Sacrum

Sacrum
Part ofPelvis, vertebral column
ArticulationsL5 vertebra above, coccyx below, ilium on both sides
Identifiers (Latin)os sacrum
ComponentsFive fused sacral vertebrae (S1–S5)

The sacrum is a large, triangular bone at the base of the spine, formed by the fusion of the five sacral vertebrae (S1–S5), typically completed between the ages of 18 and 30. It sits between the two hip bones of the pelvis, wedged between the ilia, and forms the posterior wall of the pelvic cavity. The name comes from Latin os sacrum, "sacred bone", possibly because it was offered in sacrifices or believed to be where the soul resided.

Structure

The sacrum has a wide base above, articulating with the fifth lumbar vertebra, and an apex below that meets the coccyx. Its pelvic (anterior) surface is concave and bears four pairs of foramina through which the anterior divisions of the sacral nerves pass. The dorsal surface is convex and roughened for muscle and ligament attachment, with a median sacral crest formed by fused spinous processes. The sacral canal runs through the bone, containing the cauda equina and ending at the sacral hiatus. On each side, the ear-shaped auricular surface articulates with the ilium at the sacroiliac joint.

Function

The sacrum transmits the weight of the upper body from the vertebral column to the pelvis and lower limbs. Its keystone-like wedging between the ilia, reinforced by strong sacroiliac ligaments, gives the pelvis stability while allowing slight movement. It also protects the sacral nerves and provides attachment for muscles including the gluteus maximus, piriformis and erector spinae.

Clinical significance

Sacral fractures occur with high-energy trauma or, in osteoporotic patients, as insufficiency fractures. Dysfunction of the sacroiliac joint is a recognised source of lower back pain. The sacral hiatus is the entry point for caudal epidural anaesthesia. Variations include lumbarisation of S1 or sacralisation of L5, in which the lowest lumbar vertebra fuses with the sacrum.
