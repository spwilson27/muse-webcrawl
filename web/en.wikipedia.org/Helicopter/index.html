Helicopter

A helicopter is a type of rotorcraft in which lift and thrust are supplied by one or more horizontally spinning rotors. This allows the helicopter to take off and land vertically, hover, and fly forward, backward, and laterally — capabilities that fixed-wing aircraft do not have, and that make helicopters invaluable in congested or isolated areas without runways.

Design

In the most common configuration, a single main rotor provides lift and thrust while a smaller tail rotor on the tail boom counteracts the torque that would otherwise spin the fuselage. Other configurations include tandem rotors (as on the CH-47 Chinook), coaxial rotors, intermeshing rotors, and the NOTAR system. The pilot controls the rotor disc's tilt and blade pitch through the cyclic and collective controls. In a hover, a helicopter can also perform an emergency autorotation landing if engine power is lost.

History and uses

Early experimenters included Paul Cornu (1907) and the designers of the German Focke-Wulf Fw 61 (1936), but the first practical, mass-produced helicopter was Igor Sikorsky's VS-300 and R-4 of the early 1940s, which set the single-main-rotor pattern. Helicopters are used for military transport and attack, search and rescue, air ambulances, firefighting, aerial construction, news gathering, and executive transport. Their main limitations compared with aeroplanes are lower speed, shorter range, and higher operating cost.
