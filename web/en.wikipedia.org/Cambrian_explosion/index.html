Cambrian explosion

The Cambrian explosion (also known as Cambrian radiation or Cambrian diversification) is an interval of time approximately 538.8 million years ago in the Cambrian period of the early Paleozoic, during which practically all major animal phyla started appearing in the fossil record. It lasted for about 13–25 million years and produced a remarkable diversification of multicellular organisms, including the first representatives of most modern animal body plans.

Fossil evidence

The event is documented in famous fossil deposits (Lagerstätten) such as the Burgess Shale in Canada and the Chengjiang biota in China, which preserve soft-bodied organisms including trilobites, anomalocaridids such as Anomalocaris, the five-eyed Opabinia, and early chordate-like forms such as Pikaia and Haikouichthys. Small shelly fossils and trace fossils mark the base of the Cambrian.

Possible causes

Proposed explanations include rising atmospheric and oceanic oxygen levels, the end of the Snowball Earth glaciations, the evolution of predation driving an evolutionary arms race, the development of complex developmental genetic toolkits (such as Hox genes), the appearance of eyes, and ecological feedbacks as animals began burrowing, grazing and engineering marine ecosystems. The "explosion" may partly reflect improved preservation, since hard skeletons evolved in many lineages around the same time.

Interpretation

Charles Darwin regarded the abrupt appearance of animal fossils in Cambrian rocks as a problem for his theory of evolution by natural selection, calling it a "serious" difficulty. Modern research suggests the diversification, while geologically rapid, unfolded over tens of millions of years and had roots in the preceding Ediacaran period, with molecular clock studies indicating earlier divergences of animal lineages.
