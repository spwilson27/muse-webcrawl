Melamine

Melamine is an organic compound with the formula C3H6N6. This white solid is a trimer of cyanamide, with a 1,3,5-triazine skeleton. Like cyanamide, it contains 67% nitrogen by mass, and its derivatives have fire-retardant properties due to the release of nitrogen gas when burned or charred.

Uses

Melamine is combined with formaldehyde and other agents to produce melamine resins — durable thermosetting plastics used in high-pressure laminates such as Formica, melamine dinnerware, laminate flooring, and dry-erase boards. Melamine foam is used as insulation and soundproofing material and in cleaning products such as the Magic Eraser. It is also a component of pigments and of some fertilizers.

Production

Melamine was first synthesized by the German chemist Justus von Liebig in the 1830s. In modern production, urea is converted in the presence of catalysts at high temperature — urea first decomposes to cyanic acid and ammonia, and the cyanic acid polymerizes to melamine with release of carbon dioxide.

Toxicity and food scandals

Melamine itself has low acute toxicity, but combined with cyanuric acid it can form insoluble crystals in the kidneys. Its fraudulent addition to diluted milk and pet food to falsely raise apparent protein content caused the 2007 pet food recalls and the 2008 Chinese milk scandal, in which hundreds of thousands of infants were affected.
