Phoenician alphabet

TypeAbjad
Periodc. 1050 BC – 150 BC

LanguagesPhoenician, Punic

The Phoenician alphabet is an alphabet (more specifically an abjad, writing consonants only) known in modern times from the Canaanite and Aramaic inscriptions of the ancient Phoenicians, a maritime trading culture of the eastern Mediterranean. Developed by about 1050 BC from Proto-Canaanite script, it consisted of 22 letters, all consonants, written from right to left.

The alphabet's letter names followed the acrophonic principle: each sign depicted an object whose name began with the sound it represented — ʾālep "ox", bēt "house", gīml "camel/throwstick", dālet "door/fish", and so on. These names and letter order are largely preserved in the Hebrew, Greek and Latin alphabets.

Spread and descendants

Phoenician merchants spread the alphabet across the Mediterranean and Near East. Adapted by the Greeks (probably in the 8th century BC), who repurposed unused consonant signs to write vowels, it became the Greek alphabet, ancestor of the Latin, Cyrillic and Coptic scripts. In the east, the Aramaic alphabet derived from Phoenician gave rise to the Hebrew, Syriac and Arabic scripts, and arguably, through debated routes, to scripts of South and Central Asia. Nearly all modern alphabets descend from Phoenician.

Decline

By the Hellenistic period, Phoenician was increasingly written in Aramaic and Greek scripts; its western Punic form survived in North Africa, in a cursive Neo-Punic variant, until roughly the 2nd–3rd centuries AD.
