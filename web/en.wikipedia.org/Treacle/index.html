Treacle

Treacle is any uncrystallised syrup made during the refining of sugar. The most common forms of treacle are golden syrup, a pale variety, and black treacle, a darker, more bitter variety similar to molasses. Treacle is a traditional ingredient in British cooking and confectionery.

Types

Golden syrup is a light amber syrup with a mild, buttery flavour, widely used in baking and as a topping. Black treacle is a dark, viscous syrup with a slightly burnt, bitter taste, used in rich fruit cakes, gingerbread, and treacle toffee. In modern usage the word "treacle" in Britain most often refers to black treacle, while golden syrup is sold under its own name.

Culinary uses

Treacle features in desserts such as treacle tart, treacle sponge pudding, and parkin. Treacle toffee, also known as bonfire toffee, is traditionally eaten around Guy Fawkes Night on 5 November. Historically, treacle was also used in medicines; the word derives from the Latin theriaca, a compound antidote for poisons and venomous bites.

Etymology

The word entered English via Old French triacle from Latin theriaca, itself from Greek thēriakē, an antidote. The sweet syrup took the name because it was used as a vehicle for medicinal compounds.
