HANS device

Full nameHead and Neck Support device
InventorsDr Robert Hubbard and Jim Downing (1980s)
PurposeReduce head/neck loads and basilar skull fracture risk in crashes
Mandated inFormula One (2003), NASCAR (2001), WRC and most major series

The HANS device (Head and Neck Support device) is a safety item worn by racing drivers to reduce the likelihood of head and neck injuries, particularly basilar skull fractures, in a crash. It consists of a high collar — usually of carbon fibre — worn over the driver's shoulders under the seat belts, connected by flexible tethers to the driver's helmet.

Function

In a frontal impact the driver's torso is restrained by the harness while the unsupported head continues forward, loading the base of the skull. The HANS device's tethers limit the head's forward motion relative to the chest while allowing limited rotation, transferring crash loads from the neck to the torso and the device itself. Sled testing showed dramatic reductions in head and neck injury criteria.

History

The device was developed in the 1980s by biomechanical engineer Robert Hubbard of Michigan State University together with his brother-in-law, sports-car driver Jim Downing. Adoption was slow through the 1990s, but a series of fatal basilar skull fractures in American racing — most prominently the death of Dale Earnhardt at the 2001 Daytona 500 — led NASCAR to mandate HANS or equivalent devices in late 2001. The FIA made it compulsory in Formula One from 2003, and it is now standard across virtually all professional motorsport categories.

Variants

Devices are certified to FIA 8858 and SFI 38.1 standards and come in different angles and sizes matched to seating position. Hybrid devices combine a HANS-style yoke with additional lateral restraint.
