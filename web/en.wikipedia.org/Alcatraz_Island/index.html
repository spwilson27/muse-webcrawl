Alcatraz Island is a small island 1.25 miles (2.01 km) offshore from San Francisco, California, in San Francisco Bay. Nicknamed "The Rock", it is famous as the site of a maximum-security federal penitentiary that operated from 1934 to 1963 and held some of America's most notorious criminals, including Al Capone and Robert Stroud, the "Birdman of Alcatraz".

History

The island was named La Isla de los Alcatraces ("Island of the Pelicans") by Spanish explorer Juan Manuel de Ayala in 1775. The United States built a fortress and lighthouse there in the 1850s — the lighthouse was the first on the West Coast — and the island served as a military prison from the Civil War era. The U.S. Army transferred it to the Federal Bureau of Prisons in 1933.

Federal penitentiary

Alcatraz Federal Penitentiary opened in 1934 for prisoners who caused trouble at other federal prisons. Surrounded by cold, swift currents, it was considered escape-proof: of 36 men involved in 14 escape attempts, none is confirmed to have reached shore alive, though the fate of Frank Morris and John and Clarence Anglin, who escaped in June 1962, remains a famous mystery. High operating costs led Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy to close the prison in 1963.

Occupation and park

From November 1969 to June 1971, Native American activists of the "Indians of All Tribes" occupied Alcatraz, claiming it under the Treaty of Fort Laramie; the occupation drew national attention to Native American civil rights. The island became part of the Golden Gate National Recreation Area in 1972 and is now one of San Francisco's most popular tourist attractions, drawing about 1.5 million visitors a year.
