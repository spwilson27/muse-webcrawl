Glenn Theodore Seaborg (April 19, 1912 – February 25, 1999) was an American chemist whose work on the synthesis and chemistry of the transuranium elements earned him a share of the 1951 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. His team at the University of California, Berkeley, discovered or co-discovered ten elements, including plutonium, americium, curium, berkelium, and californium.

Work

During the Second World War Seaborg led the group at Chicago's Metallurgical Laboratory that devised the chemistry for industrial-scale production of plutonium for the Manhattan Project. His actinide concept — placing the actinides as a second f-block series in the periodic table — restructured the table and guided the search for new elements. He served as chairman of the US Atomic Energy Commission from 1961 to 1971.

Honours

Element 106, seaborgium, was named in his honour in 1997 while he was still alive — a rare distinction. Seaborg is often noted as the only person who could write his address in chemical element symbols: Sg, Lr, Bk, Cf, Am (seaborgium, lawrencium, berkelium, californium, americium).
