Mycenaean Greece

Period: c. 1600–1100 BC
Region: Mainland Greece, Aegean, Crete
Language: Mycenaean Greek (Linear B)
Major centres: Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos, Thebes, Athens

Mycenaean Greece (or Mycenaean civilisation) was the last phase of the Bronze Age in ancient Greece, spanning roughly 1600 to 1100 BC. Named after the citadel of Mycenae in the Argolid, it was the first advanced, distinctly Greek civilisation on the mainland, characterised by palace states, monumental architecture, a warrior aristocracy, and the earliest written Greek.

History

The civilisation emerged as Greek-speaking elites grew wealthy through trade and raiding; the shaft graves of Mycenae (c. 1600–1500 BC) display gold masks, weapons and imported luxuries. Palatial kingdoms arose at Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos, Thebes, Orchomenos and Athens, administering agriculture, crafts and trade through scribal bureaucracies. Around 1450 BC the Mycenaeans took over Minoan Crete, occupying Knossos. Their goods and settlements spread around the Aegean and to Cyprus, the Levant, Egypt, Sicily and Italy.

Society and writing

Each palace was ruled by a wanax (king), supported by officials and dependent labour. The palaces recorded inventories on clay tablets in the Linear B syllabic script — an early form of Greek, deciphered by Michael Ventris in 1952. Religion centred on deities ancestral to the later Greek pantheon, including Zeus, Poseidon, Hera and an early Dionysus.

Collapse and legacy

Around 1200 BC the palaces were destroyed by fire amid the wider Late Bronze Age collapse; causes debated include invasion or internal revolt, drought, and systems collapse. Writing, monumental building and centralised administration vanished, and Greece entered its Dark Ages. Memory of the era survived in epic tradition: the world of Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, and figures such as Agamemnon of "golden Mycenae," preserve distorted echoes of the Mycenaean age, as Heinrich Schliemann's excavations in the 1870s dramatically revealed.
