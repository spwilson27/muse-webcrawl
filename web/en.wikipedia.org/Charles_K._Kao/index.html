Charles K. Kao

Sir Charles Kuen Kao (4 November 1933 – 23 September 2018) was a Chinese-British-American physicist and electrical engineer whose work made fibre-optic communications practical. He shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics "for groundbreaking achievements concerning the transmission of light in fibers for optical communication", and is widely regarded as the father of fibre optics.

Early life and research

Kao was born in Shanghai and educated in Hong Kong before studying electrical engineering in London. Working at Standard Telecommunication Laboratories in Harlow, England, he and George Hockham published a landmark 1966 paper arguing that the high losses in existing glass fibres were caused by impurities, not by any fundamental limit, and that sufficiently pure glass could carry light signals over long distances. At the time losses were around 1,000 decibels per kilometre; Kao showed that below 20 dB/km, optical communication would become feasible.

Impact

Within a few years manufacturers produced ultra-pure fibres meeting Kao's target, and fibre-optic cables became the backbone of global telecommunications and the internet. He later directed engineering at ITT, became vice-chancellor of the Chinese University of Hong Kong, and founded an electronics company.

Honours

Besides the Nobel Prize, Kao received the Faraday Medal, the Marconi Prize and a knighthood in 2010. He died in Hong Kong in 2018 after living with Alzheimer's disease.
