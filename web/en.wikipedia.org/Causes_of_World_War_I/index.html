Causes of World War I

The causes of World War I include long-term structural factors — competing alliance systems, imperial rivalry, militarism and nationalism — and the short-term crisis following the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary by the Bosnian Serb Gavrilo Princip in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914. Historians continue to debate the relative responsibility of the great powers.

Long-term factors

By 1914 Europe was divided into two armed blocs: the Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy, and the Triple Entente of France, Russia and Britain. An arms race — especially the Anglo-German naval race — colonial competition in Africa and Asia, and Franco-German hostility over Alsace-Lorraine heightened tension. In the Balkans, Serbian nationalism and the decline of the Ottoman Empire destabilised the region, and alliance commitments and mobilisation timetables created pressure for pre-emptive war once a crisis began.

The July Crisis

Austria-Hungary, assured of German support (the "blank cheque"), sent Serbia a deliberately harsh ultimatum and declared war on 28 July 1914. Russia mobilised in Serbia's defence; Germany declared war on Russia (1 August) and France (3 August) and invaded Belgium under the Schlieffen Plan, bringing Britain into the war on 4 August. Within weeks the conflict had generalised across Europe and its empires.

Historiography

Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles assigned "war guilt" to Germany, a verdict contested ever since. Fritz Fischer's work in the 1960s argued Germany sought continental hegemony; more recent accounts, such as Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers, emphasise shared miscalculation among all the powers.
