The Rwandan Civil War was a conflict between the Rwandan Armed Forces, representing the Hutu-led government of President Juvénal Habyarimana, and the Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF), composed largely of Tutsi refugees and their descendants who had fled earlier waves of ethnic violence. The war began with the RPF's invasion from Uganda on 1 October 1990 and, interrupted by the Arusha Accords of 1993, resumed amid the Rwandan genocide of 1994, ending with the RPF's military victory.

Background

Ethnic tensions between the Hutu majority and Tutsi minority, hardened under German and Belgian colonial rule, erupted in the "social revolution" of 1959 and massacres that drove hundreds of thousands of Tutsi into exile. In Uganda, exiles formed the RPF under Fred Rwigyema and later Paul Kagame, seeking the right of return.

Course of the war

The initial RPF offensive stalled after Rwigyema was killed on the second day, and French, Belgian, and Zairian troops helped repel the invasion. Kagame rebuilt the RPF as a guerrilla force in the Virunga Mountains and launched renewed offensives in 1992–93. International pressure produced the Arusha Accords (August 1993), a power-sharing agreement accompanied by the UN peacekeeping mission UNAMIR under Roméo Dallaire. Hardliners in the government camp, however, prepared for "final" violence against Tutsi and moderate Hutu.

Genocide and RPF victory

On 6 April 1994 Habyarimana's plane was shot down over Kigali, triggering the genocide, in which Hutu extremists and the Interahamwe militia murdered roughly 800,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu in about one hundred days. The RPF resumed its offensive, captured Kigali on 4 July, and declared victory on 18 July 1994. A new government was formed under the Arusha framework with Pasteur Bizimungu as president and Kagame as vice-president and defence minister. Some two million Hutu fled to Zaire, setting the stage for the Congo wars.
