Sparkler


A burning sparkler

A sparkler is a type of hand-held firework that burns slowly while emitting bright, intense sparks. Typically made from a thin metal wire coated with a pyrotechnic composition, sparklers are popular at celebrations such as Independence Day in the United States, Guy Fawkes Night in Britain, Diwali in India, and New Year's Eve, and are frequently used at weddings and birthdays.

Composition

The coating of a classic metal-wire sparkler consists of a metallic fuel (such as iron, steel, aluminium, or titanium powder), an oxidizer (usually potassium perchlorate, potassium nitrate, or barium nitrate), and a binder such as dextrin. The metal particles oxidize as they are ejected, producing the characteristic branching sparks. Different metals colour the sparks: iron and steel give golden sparks, titanium produces bright white sparks. Burning temperatures can reach 1,000–1,600 °C (1,800–3,000 °F), hot enough to melt some metals.

Safety

Despite their innocent reputation, sparklers cause a significant share of fireworks injuries, especially to children under five, because the burnt wire remains extremely hot. Safety guidance recommends holding sparklers at arm's length, supervising children, never relighting dud sparklers, and plunging spent wires into water or sand. Some jurisdictions restrict or ban them; alternatives include glow sticks and LED sparklers. "Morning glory" sparklers, mounted on bamboo or wooden sticks with paper wrapping, change colour and crackle as they burn through successive layers.

History

Sparklers descend from early Chinese hand-held fireworks and from the "Cherosiphon" star-spraying devices described in Byzantine times. Modern wire sparklers were developed in Germany in the early 20th century, where they are known as Wunderkerzen ("wonder candles").
