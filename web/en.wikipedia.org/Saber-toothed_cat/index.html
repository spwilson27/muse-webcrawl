Saber-toothed cat

A saber-toothed cat (also written sabre-toothed cat) is any member of various extinct groups of predatory mammals characterised by long, curved, blade-like canine teeth that protruded from the mouth even when closed. The saber-toothed morphology evolved independently at least five times among mammals — a classic example of convergent evolution — in true felids of the subfamily Machairodontinae and in unrelated groups including the nimravids, barbourofelids, sparassodonts and the marsupial-relative Thylacosmilus.

Smilodon, the iconic saber-toothed cat of Pleistocene North and South America.

Anatomy and hunting

The best-known genus, Smilodon, lived in the Americas during the Pleistocene and became extinct about 10,000 years ago. Saber-toothed cats typically had stocky, muscular forelimbs, reduced lower canines and a wide jaw gape, suggesting they used their powerful arms to pin large prey before delivering a precision bite with the upper canines to the throat's soft tissues, rather than the suffocating bite of modern big cats. Their canines were strong laterally compressed blades but relatively fragile against sideways forces and contact with bone.

Evolution and extinction

Machairodont cats such as Homotherium and Megantereon ranged across Africa, Eurasia and the Americas for millions of years. The last saber-toothed cats disappeared at the end of the Pleistocene, during the Quaternary extinction event that removed much of the world's megafauna, likely through a combination of climate change, prey loss and human pressure.
