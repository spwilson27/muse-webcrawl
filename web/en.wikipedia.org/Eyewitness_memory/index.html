Eyewitness memory

Eyewitness memory is a person's episodic recollection of an event they personally experienced, most often studied in the context of crimes a witness observed. Although courts and juries tend to treat confident eyewitness testimony as compelling evidence, decades of psychological research show that eyewitness memory is fallible, reconstructive, and vulnerable to suggestion.

Research findings

Elizabeth Loftus and colleagues demonstrated the misinformation effect: misleading information presented after an event can alter a witness's memory of it, as in experiments where the wording of a question ("smashed" versus "hit") changed speed estimates and even implanted memories of details that were never present. The weapon focus effect describes how the presence of a weapon draws attention away from a perpetrator's face, reducing identification accuracy. The cross-race effect shows that witnesses are generally better at recognising faces of their own racial group than those of other groups.

Stages of memory

Researchers divide the factors affecting eyewitness accuracy into estimator variables — features of the event itself, such as lighting, distance, duration, stress and the witness's own state — and system variables, which the justice system can control, including lineup construction, instructions given to witnesses, and interview technique. The cognitive interview was developed to improve the completeness and accuracy of witness recall.

Legal impact

Mistaken eyewitness identification is the leading contributor to wrongful convictions later overturned by DNA evidence, featuring in the majority of such cases documented by the Innocence Project. Reforms adopted in several jurisdictions include double-blind lineup administration, sequential rather than simultaneous lineups, recording witness confidence at first identification, and expert testimony on memory science.
