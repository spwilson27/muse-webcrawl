Kritik

Kritiks are a distinctive feature of competitive policy debate.

In policy debate, a kritik (also known as a K, critique, or *k) is a type of argument that challenges the assumptions of the opposing team's case. Kritiks often take the form of a philosophical or theoretical objection to the other team's arguments, claiming that the mindset, language, or epistemology of the other team is inherently flawed and therefore their arguments should be rejected on that basis. Kritiks are most commonly used by the negative team, although some affirmative teams use them too.

Structure

A kritik typically has three parts, following the structure of a disadvantage or counterplan:

  • Link — the way in which the opposing team (usually the affirmative) perpetuates or represents the problematic mindset, assumption or discourse the kritik attacks. For example, an affirmative might be said to "link" to a security kritik by framing a policy issue in terms of threats and state security.
  • Impact — the consequences of that mindset or discourse if it is not repudiated, such as the claim that securitised thinking leads to war, or that anthropocentric assumptions drive environmental destruction.
  • Alternative — the action or orientation the kritik advocates instead, often framed as rejecting the affirmative's assumptions or adopting a different framework or method. The alternative is said to "solve" the impact while competing with the affirmative plan.

Common kritiks

Frequently argued kritiks draw on bodies of philosophy, social theory and literature. Common examples include critiques of capitalism (the "Cap K"), of biopower and disciplinary power drawing on Michel Foucault, of psychoanalysis (Freud and Lacan), of security discourse, of anthropocentrism and environmental philosophy, of ableism, of settler colonialism, and of identity categories in feminist, critical race and queer theory. Set-colonialism, Afro-pessimism and Nietzsche kritiks are also widely run at the high-school and collegiate levels.

Debate about kritiks

Kritiks are controversial within the debate community. Supporters argue that they allow debaters to interrogate the deeper premises of policy arguments and engage with education about philosophy and injustice. Critics argue that kritiks make debate less accessible and predictable, replace policy analysis with obscure theory, and can be deployed generically against almost any affirmative. Related practices include kritikal affirmatives, which run kritik-style arguments on the affirmative side, and framework debates over how judges should evaluate kritik rounds.
