Rum Rebellion

The Rum Rebellion of 1808 was a coup d'état in the British penal colony of New South Wales, in which Governor William Bligh was deposed by the New South Wales Corps under Major George Johnston, acting with the landowner and former officer John Macarthur. It is the only successful armed takeover of a government in Australian history.

Background

Bligh, famous for the mutiny on HMS Bounty, arrived in 1806 determined to break the Corps officers' lucrative monopoly over the trade in spirits — rum served as a de facto currency in the colony — and to curb their land speculation and dominance of the courts. His confrontational style alienated the military and leading settlers. When Bligh ordered Macarthur's arrest over a shipping dispute and moved to try him before a court whose members Macarthur challenged, tensions came to a head.

The coup

On 26 January 1808 — the twentieth anniversary of the colony's founding — Johnston led some 400 soldiers to Government House and arrested Bligh, who was famously caricatured as hiding under a bed. Johnston assumed the title of lieutenant-governor, suspended officials, and granted land and pardons to supporters. Joseph Foveaux, and later William Paterson, administered the colony until the arrival of a new governor.

Aftermath

Governor Lachlan Macquarie arrived at the end of 1809 with his own regiment and restored order; the New South Wales Corps was recalled to Britain and renumbered. Johnston was cashiered by court martial in 1811, while Macarthur, though barred from the colony for several years, eventually returned to become a leading figure in the Australian wool industry. Historians debate whether rum truly motivated the revolt: the de facto currency and the trade monopoly mattered, but so did land, legal authority and Bligh's personality — the name "Rum Rebellion" was popularised only decades later.
