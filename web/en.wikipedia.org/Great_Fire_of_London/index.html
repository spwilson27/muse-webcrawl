The Great Fire of London was a major conflagration that swept through central parts of London from Sunday, 2 September to Thursday, 6 September 1666. The fire gutted the medieval City of London inside the old Roman city wall, but did not reach the aristocratic district of Westminster. It threatened, but did not reach, Charles II's Palace of Whitehall. It consumed 13,200 houses, 87 parish churches, St Paul's Cathedral, and most of the buildings of the City authorities. It is estimated to have destroyed the homes of 70,000 of the City's 80,000 inhabitants.

The death toll is generally thought to have been relatively small, although some historians have suggested the deaths of poorer Londoners might not have been recorded. The reasons for the fire, and its ignition point at a bakery in Pudding Lane, remain debated, and conspiracy theories attributing it to foreign agents or Catholics circulated at the time; a French watchmaker, Robert Hubert, was executed after confessing to starting it, despite strong evidence he had not even arrived in London until after it began.

Origins and spread

The fire began shortly after midnight on Sunday, 2 September, in the bakery of Thomas Farriner in Pudding Lane. Strong easterly winds fanned the flames, and the closely packed timber-framed houses with jettied upper floors, the summer's drought, and the City's inadequate firefighting organisation — firebreaks by demolition were ordered late and hesitantly by Lord Mayor Sir Thomas Bloodworth — allowed the blaze to spread rapidly westward along the Thames-side warehouses full of flammable goods. King Charles II and his brother James, Duke of York, took direct charge of firefighting efforts, ordering demolitions and using gunpowder to create firebreaks, which finally halted the fire's advance by Wednesday.

Aftermath and rebuilding

The fire ended the Great Plague of London epidemic era (plague had peaked in 1665), though the popular belief that the fire itself eradicated the plague is disputed. Rebuilding began quickly under the Rebuilding of London Act 1666 and the Fire of London Disputes Act 1667, which established the Fire Court to resolve property disputes. Grand redesigns proposed by Christopher Wren, John Evelyn and others were rejected in favour of rebuilding largely on the old street plan, but with widened streets, brick construction and new building regulations that transformed the City's fabric. Wren rebuilt St Paul's Cathedral (1675–1710) and more than 50 City churches. The Monument, a 61-metre Doric column designed by Wren and Robert Hooke, was erected near Pudding Lane to commemorate the fire.

Eyewitness accounts

The diarists Samuel Pepys and John Evelyn left famous first-hand accounts of the fire, describing the flight of refugees, the collapse of St Paul's, and Pepys burying his wine and Parmesan cheese in his garden to protect them from the flames.
