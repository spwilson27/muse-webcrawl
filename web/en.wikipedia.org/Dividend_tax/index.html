Dividend tax

Dividend tax

Type: Income tax on dividends

Paid by: Shareholders receiving dividends

Key issue: Double taxation of corporate profits

Relief systems: Imputation, reduced rates, exemptions

A dividend tax is a tax imposed by a jurisdiction on dividends paid by a corporation to its shareholders. Because dividends are paid out of profits that have usually already been taxed at the corporate level, taxing them again in shareholders' hands is often described as double taxation — a controversy that has shaped very different national approaches to relieving it.

Approaches by country

  • United States — "qualified dividends" are taxed at the lower capital gains rates (0, 15, or 20 percent for most taxpayers), while non-qualified dividends are taxed as ordinary income.
  • United Kingdom — dividends above an annual allowance are taxed at special rates by income band.
  • Australia and New Zealand — an imputation system gives shareholders franking credits for tax already paid by the company, largely eliminating double taxation of domestic profits.
  • Classical systems (used in several countries) — corporate profits and dividends are taxed separately with no relief, while some jurisdictions, such as Hong Kong, impose no dividend tax at all.

Withholding tax

Many countries levy a withholding tax on dividends paid to foreign investors, commonly at statutory rates of 15–30 percent, reduced by bilateral tax treaties. Withholding also applies within the European Union under relief provided by the Parent-Subsidiary Directive for corporate shareholders.

Economic debate

Economists debate whether dividend taxes discourage investment, distort companies toward retaining earnings or taking on debt, and affect share prices. Some studies find that dividend tax cuts mainly raise payouts rather than investment; others argue that relief from double taxation lowers the cost of capital. The topic intersects with the taxation of capital gains, since investors can receive returns in either form.
