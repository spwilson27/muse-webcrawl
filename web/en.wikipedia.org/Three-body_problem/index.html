Three-body problem

In physics, specifically classical mechanics, the three-body problem is the problem of taking the initial positions and velocities of three point masses and solving for their subsequent motion according to Newton's laws of motion and Newton's law of universal gravitation. Unlike the two-body problem, which has a general closed-form solution in terms of conic sections, the three-body problem has no general closed-form solution: for most initial conditions the system is chaotic, meaning small differences in initial conditions grow exponentially and long-term prediction becomes practically impossible.

History

The problem dates to Isaac Newton's Principia (1687), where he treated the perturbation of the Moon's motion by the Sun. Mathematicians including Euler and Lagrange found special solutions, such as the configuration in which the three bodies remain at the vertices of an equilateral triangle (Lagrange points). In 1887, work by Heinrich Bruns and Henri Poincaré showed that no additional independent algebraic integrals of motion exist, and Poincaré's analysis laid the foundations of chaos theory. In 1912 Karl Fritiof Sundman found a convergent series solution valid for general initial conditions (excluding zero angular momentum), though it converges far too slowly for practical use.

Special solutions

Particular periodic solutions are well known. In the restricted three-body problem, one mass is negligible and the system reduces to a test particle moving in the field of two orbiting masses, giving rise to the five Lagrange points. In 1993 Cristopher Moore discovered a figure-eight solution in which three equal masses chase each other along the same figure-eight orbit; in 2000 Alain Chenciner and Richard Montgomery gave a rigorous proof of its existence. Hundreds of families of periodic orbits have since been found numerically, including solutions for unequal masses.

Approaches and applications

In practice, three-body systems are studied with numerical integration (for example, specialised n-body codes), perturbation theory, or statistical methods. The problem matters in celestial mechanics — for example, the stability of the Earth–Moon–Sun system, the dynamics of triple stars, and gravitational-wave astronomy involving three black holes. General-relativistic versions of the problem are studied for the dynamics of compact-object triples. The problem has also achieved broad cultural fame through Liu Cixin's science-fiction novel The Three-Body Problem (2008) and its adaptations.
