Exclave

An exclave is a portion of a state's territory that is geographically separated from the main part of the state by the territory of one or more other states. The same territory viewed from the perspective of the surrounding state is an enclave if it is fully surrounded by a single other state's territory. The terms are relational: Kaliningrad Oblast is an exclave of Russia; Baarle-Nassau and Baarle-Hertog form a famously complex mosaic of Belgian and Dutch enclaves and exclaves within each other.

Examples

  • Kaliningrad Oblast, Russia — separated from mainland Russia by Lithuania and Poland, bordering the Baltic Sea.
  • Nakhchivan, Azerbaijan — separated by Armenia.
  • Ceuta and Melilla, Spain — on the North African coast, surrounded by Morocco and the Mediterranean.
  • Alaska and the Northwest Angle, United States — Alaska is separated from the contiguous US by Canada; the Northwest Angle is separated from the rest of Minnesota by the Lake of the Woods.
  • Baekdu/Paektu and others — subnational exclaves are common, arising from historical boundary quirks.

Causes and issues

Exclaves often result from wars, treaties, dynastic inheritance, or shifting river courses. They can create practical difficulties in administration, transport, customs, and utilities, and residents may depend on transit agreements with the intervening state. Some exclaves, such as Germany's Büsingen am Hochrhein (surrounded by Switzerland), adopt the surrounding country's currency and customs arrangements informally or by treaty.
