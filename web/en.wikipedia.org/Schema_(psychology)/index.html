Schema (psychology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In psychology and the cognitive sciences, a schema (pl. schemata or schemas) is a pattern of thought or behaviour that organizes categories of information and the relationships among them—a mental framework that helps individuals interpret information, fill in gaps, and predict what will happen. Schemas can represent objects, situations, events, sequences of actions (often called scripts), social roles, and the self.

History

The concept has roots in philosophy (Kant's schemata) and was developed psychologically by Henry Head and popularized by Frederic Bartlett in his 1932 book Remembering, which showed how people reconstruct memories in line with culturally shaped schemas. Jean Piaget made schemas (his term: schèmes) the building blocks of cognitive development, which grow through the twin processes of assimilation (fitting new information into existing schemas) and accommodation (modifying schemas to fit new information). Later cognitive psychology, notably the work of David Rumelhart and others in the 1970s–80s, formalized schema theory in information-processing and connectionist models.

Effects on cognition

Schemas make processing efficient: they direct attention, structure encoding, and support inferences about unstated information. They also bias memory and judgment—people better recall schema-consistent information, and may distort memories toward what the schema predicts (Bartlett's participants reshaped the folk tale "The War of the Ghosts" toward their own cultural expectations). Specific kinds of schemas include self-schemas, person schemas, role schemas, event schemas (scripts), and gender and cultural schemas. In clinical psychology, Aaron Beck proposed that maladaptive core schemas underlie depression and personality disorders, a notion developed further in schema therapy.

Criticism

Critics have noted that "schema" can be used vaguely—as a label rather than an explanation—and that some schema theories are difficult to falsify. Nevertheless, the concept remains influential in cognitive psychology, education, and artificial intelligence, where frame-based representations owe much to schema theory.
