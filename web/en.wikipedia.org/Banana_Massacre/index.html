Banana Massacre

The Banana Massacre (Spanish: Matanza de las bananeras) was a massacre of workers of the United Fruit Company that occurred between December 5 and 6, 1928, in Ciénaga, near Santa Marta, Colombia. A paramilitary unit of the Colombian Army killed an unknown number of striking workers after the government declared them subversives acting for the Soviet Union.

Background

Workers for the United Fruit Company in the Magdalena banana zone demanded written contracts, eight-hour days, six-day weeks, and the elimination of company scrip paid in coupons redeemable only at company stores. When negotiations failed, a strike began in November 1928 involving thousands of workers.

The massacre

The government of President Miguel Abadía Méndez sent troops under General Carlos Cortés Vargas, who declared martial law and ordered the strikers to disperse. On the night of 5–6 December, soldiers fired on a crowd gathered in the plaza of Ciénaga. Estimates of the dead vary widely, from dozens to as many as 2,000; the absence of precise records remains controversial in Colombia.

Aftermath

Congressman Jorge Eliécer Gaitán investigated and denounced the massacre, raising his national profile. The events featured prominently in Gabriel García Márquez's novel One Hundred Years of Solitude, which depicts a fictionalized death toll of three thousand. The massacre became a landmark in Colombian labor history and in criticism of United Fruit's influence in Latin America.
