The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, officially titled the Treaty of Peace, Friendship, Limits and Settlement between the United States of America and the Mexican Republic, was signed on 2 February 1848 at Guadalupe Hidalgo, then a village just north of Mexico City. It ended the Mexican–American War (1846–1848) and transferred an enormous territory — the Mexican Cession — to the United States.

Terms

Mexico recognised the Rio Grande as the boundary of Texas and ceded to the United States its northern territories, including Alta California and Santa Fe de Nuevo México — land comprising all or parts of the present-day states of California, Nevada, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado and Wyoming, some 1.36 million square kilometres (525,000 square miles). In return the United States paid Mexico $15 million and assumed up to $3.25 million in claims of American citizens against Mexico. The treaty set the boundary from the Rio Grande westward to the Pacific just south of San Diego, adjusted by the Gadsden Purchase of 1853.

Mexican residents and land grants

Articles VIII and IX offered Mexican nationals in the ceded territories the choice of relocating to Mexico or remaining and becoming United States citizens, and promised that their property would be "inviolably respected". The United States Senate struck out Article X, which would have explicitly guaranteed Mexican land grants; the Protocol of Querétaro, signed when ratifications were exchanged, stated that this deletion did not impair the grants' legitimacy. In practice, many Californio and Hispano landholders lost land through lengthy confirmation proceedings under the California Land Act of 1851, legal costs, taxation and squatting. The treaty's guarantees later became a touchstone for Chicano land-rights movements, and disputes over treaty land grants continued into the twentieth century.

Negotiation and legacy

The treaty was negotiated by Nicholas Trist, chief clerk of the US State Department, who concluded it despite having been recalled by President James K. Polk — an act of insubordination that nevertheless produced terms Polk accepted. The Senate ratified the treaty on 10 March 1848 by 38 votes to 14. By ending the war on these terms, the treaty fixed the modern US–Mexico border (apart from the Gadsden strip) and made the United States a continental power, while in Mexico it remains a symbol of national trauma: Mexico lost roughly half its territory. The treaty's provisions on citizenship and property are still cited in legal and historical discussions of the rights of Mexican Americans in the Southwest.
