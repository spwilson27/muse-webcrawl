Sea lines of communication

The world's principal sea lines of communication carry the bulk of global trade.

Sea lines of communication (SLOC) is a term describing the primary maritime routes between ports, used for trade, logistics and naval operations. Two major categories exist: strategic SLOCs and operational SLOCs, which differ in the extent of their significance and vulnerability.

Strategic importance

Because the overwhelming majority of world trade by volume travels by sea, the security of SLOCs is a central concern of naval strategy. Nations dependent on imported energy and raw materials — such as Japan, South Korea and China — regard the protection of their sea lanes as vital. The concept is closely related to sea power theory as developed by Alfred Thayer Mahan, who argued that command of maritime communications was the key to national greatness.

Chokepoints

SLOCs frequently pass through narrow chokepoints where shipping is concentrated and vulnerable, including the Strait of Malacca, the Strait of Hormuz, the Suez Canal, the Panama Canal, the Bab-el-Mandeb and the Strait of Gibraltar.

Threats and protection

Threats to SLOCs include hostile navies, submarines, naval mining, piracy and anti-ship missiles. During the Cold War, NATO planning focused heavily on keeping the North Atlantic SLOCs open against Soviet submarine attack so that reinforcements could reach Europe. Modern anti-access/area-denial capabilities have renewed interest in convoying, mine countermeasures and the dispersal of shipping.
