A hexagram (Greek: ἑξάγραμμα) is a six-pointed geometric star figure formed by two overlapping equilateral triangles, one pointing up and one pointing down. It is a compound polygon rather than a single continuous star polygon, and it is one of the most widely used religious and cultural symbols in the world.

Geometry

The hexagram can be constructed by superimposing two congruent equilateral triangles sharing the same centre. Their intersection forms a regular hexagon whose side length is one third that of the triangles' sides; the figure has six points and full dihedral symmetry of order 12. In Schläfli notation it is written {6/2} or as the compound 2{3}. There are also irregular hexagrams, and the unicursal hexagram — drawable in one stroke — is used in Thelema.

Religious and cultural uses

In Judaism the hexagram is the Star of David (Magen David, "Shield of David"), a symbol attested on medieval Jewish seals and flags that became the pre-eminent emblem of Jewish identity in the 19th century and appears on the flag of Israel. In Islam the figure is the Seal of Solomon (Khatam Sulayman), associated with the prophet-king Solomon and used on coins, flags and architecture. In Hinduism the Shatkona represents the union of Shiva (upward triangle, fire) and Shakti (downward triangle, water) and is the yantra of several deities. The symbol also appears in Jainism, Buddhism, and in the Raëlian movement, and it is used as a heraldic charge and in national and municipal flags.

Occult and other uses

In alchemy the two triangles signified fire and water and their union the philosopher's stone; in ceremonial magic the hexagram figures in planetary invocations, and the unicursal hexagram is a symbol of Thelema. Hexagrams also occur as the figures of the I Ching — though those are composed of six stacked lines (solid or broken) rather than a star shape — and as a common decorative motif in many cultures.
