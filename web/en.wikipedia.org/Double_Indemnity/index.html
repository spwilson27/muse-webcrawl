Double Indemnity is a 1944 American psychological thriller film noir directed by Billy Wilder and co-written by Wilder and Raymond Chandler, based on James M. Cain's 1936 novella of the same name. It stars Fred MacMurray as insurance salesman Walter Neff, Barbara Stanwyck as the seductive housewife Phyllis Dietrichson, and Edward G. Robinson as claims manager Barton Keyes. The title refers to a clause in a life insurance policy that doubles the payout for accidental death in specified circumstances, here death on a train.

Plot

Wounded and bleeding, Neff dictates a confession into a Dictaphone for Keyes, his friend and boss. In flashback, Neff recalls how Phyllis Dietrichson lured him into murdering her husband: the husband is signed up for a policy with a double indemnity clause, then is killed and his body placed on the tracks behind a moving train so the death looks like an accident. Neff, impersonating the husband, stages the fall from the observation car.

The scheme frays under Keyes's instinctive suspicion of the claim and the lovers' growing mutual distrust. Phyllis shoots Neff, who shoots her dead; staggering to his office, he finishes his confession as Keyes arrives. In a famous final exchange, Neff says he murdered for money and a woman — "I didn't get the money and I didn't get the woman."

Reception and legacy

The film was nominated for seven Academy Awards, including Best Picture, Best Director, and Best Actress for Stanwyck's archetypal femme fatale. Its hard-boiled dialogue, John F. Seitz's low-key cinematography, and Miklós Rózsa's score made it a defining work of film noir. In 1992 it was selected for the United States National Film Registry, and it regularly appears on lists of the greatest films ever made.
