Epoxy

A syringe of "two-part" epoxy adhesive

Epoxy is the family of basic components or cured end products of epoxy resins. Epoxy resins, also known as polyepoxides, are a class of reactive prepolymers and polymers which contain epoxide groups. The epoxide functional group is also collectively called epoxy. Epoxy resins may be reacted (cross-linked) either with themselves through catalytic homopolymerisation, or with a wide range of co-reactants often referred to as hardeners or curatives.

Chemistry

Most common epoxy resin monomers are produced from the reaction of epichlorohydrin (ECH) with bisphenol A, yielding diglycidyl ether of bisphenol A (DGEBA). When mixed with a hardener such as a polyamine, the epoxide groups react to form a cross-linked thermosetting polymer with strong mechanical properties, excellent adhesion, and good chemical and heat resistance.

Applications

Epoxy-based materials are widely used as adhesives, including structural adhesives sold as "epoxy glue"; as protective and decorative coatings for floors, metals, and marine use; in electrical insulation and printed circuit boards; and as the matrix resin in fibre-reinforced composites such as carbon-fibre and fibreglass materials used in aircraft, boats, and wind turbine blades. Epoxy is also used for encapsulating electronic components, for casting, and in art and jewellery.

History

Epoxy resins were first developed in the 1930s. Pierre Castan in Switzerland and Sylvan Greenlee in the United States independently developed bisphenol-A-based resins, and Castan's work was licensed and commercialised by Ciba in the 1940s. Devoe & Raynolds commercialised Greenlee's resins in the United States.

Health risks

Uncured epoxy resins and hardeners can be skin sensitisers, and epichlorohydrin and bisphenol A are of concern as endocrine-related compounds. Fully cured epoxy is comparatively inert, though dust from machining cured material should be controlled.
