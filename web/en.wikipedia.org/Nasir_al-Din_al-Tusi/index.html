Nasir al-Din al-Tusi

Born: 1201, Tus, Persia
Died: 1274, Baghdad
Fields: astronomy, mathematics, philosophy, theology

Khawaja Muhammad ibn Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Tusi (1201–1274), known as Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, was a Persian polymath — astronomer, mathematician, philosopher, theologian and physician — regarded as one of the greatest scholars of the Islamic Golden Age. He wrote more than 150 works in Arabic and Persian.

Life

Born in Tus, al-Tusi studied in Nishapur and later joined the Ismaili court at Alamut. When the Mongols under Hulagu conquered Alamut (1256) and Baghdad (1258), al-Tusi entered Hulagu's service and persuaded him to fund the great observatory at Maragheh in northwestern Iran, which he directed from 1259 and where he compiled the Zij-i Ilkhani star tables.

Contributions

In mathematics he is credited with founding trigonometry as a discipline independent of astronomy, in his Treatise on the Quadrilateral, which expounds plane and spherical trigonometry including the sine law. In astronomy he devised the "Tusi couple" — a device generating linear motion from two circles — to reform Ptolemaic models; it later appears in the work of Copernicus. His critique of Ptolemy's Almagest influenced generations of astronomers. He also wrote influential works on ethics (Akhlaq-i Nasiri), logic, and Shi'a theology, and his recension of Euclid's Elements included a famous discussion of the parallel postulate.

Legacy

A lunar crater bears his name, and the Tusi couple is recognised as a key link between Islamic and European astronomy.
