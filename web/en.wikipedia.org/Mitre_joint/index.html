Mitre joint

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mitre joint (often spelled miter in American English) is a joint made by cutting two pieces of material — usually wood — at an angle (most often 45 degrees each) and fitting them together to form a corner, typically of 90 degrees. It is used where an end grain would be unsightly, as in picture frames, door and window trim, mouldings, and cabinet work, since the joint hides end grain and presents a clean, continuous profile.

Characteristics

Because the cut surfaces are largely end grain, a plain glued mitre is relatively weak and tends to open with seasonal wood movement; it is therefore commonly reinforced with splines, keys, dowels, biscuits, brads, or screws. Mitres are cut by hand with a mitre box and backsaw or with a mitre saw or table saw. In metalwork and piping, mitred joints join sections at an angle by cutting and welding.

Advantages and disadvantages

The joint's chief advantages are appearance — grain and moulding profiles flow around the corner — and simplicity. Its disadvantages are weakness, sensitivity to inaccurate cutting (small angular errors are magnified at the corner), and difficulty of clamping, since the angled faces tend to slip under pressure. Box mitres and lock mitres are strengthened variants cut on a router table.
