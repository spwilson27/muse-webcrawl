The Pacific cod (Gadus macrocephalus) is a commercially important species of ray-finned fish in the cod family, Gadidae. It is found in the northern Pacific Ocean, from the Yellow Sea and the Sea of Japan, through the Bering Sea and Aleutian Islands, and south along the North American coast to California.

Description

Pacific cod can grow to about 1 metre in length and weigh up to 15 kilograms, though most adults are smaller. The body is elongated, brown to grey on the back with mottled patterning, and pale below. Like other true cods, it has three dorsal fins, two anal fins, and a distinctive barbel on the chin.

Ecology

The species lives on or near the bottom over the continental shelf and upper slope, generally at depths of 100 to 400 metres. It feeds on other fish, crabs, shrimp, and invertebrates. Spawning occurs in winter and early spring, when females release large numbers of adhesive eggs that settle on the seabed — unlike the floating eggs of Atlantic cod.

Fishery

Pacific cod supports one of the largest groundfish fisheries in the world, prosecuted principally by the United States, Russia, Japan, and Korea. In Alaska it is caught by trawl, longline, and pot vessels and is marketed fresh, frozen, and as salted cod. Stocks in the Gulf of Alaska declined sharply after the 2014–2016 Pacific marine heatwave, leading to temporary fishery closures.
