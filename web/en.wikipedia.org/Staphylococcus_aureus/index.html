Staphylococcus aureus

Clusters of S. aureus bacteria.

Staphylococcus aureus is a Gram-positive, round-shaped bacterium that is a member of the Bacillota and a usual member of the microbiota of the body, frequently found in the upper respiratory tract and on the skin. It is often positive for catalase and can grow in media containing up to 10% salt. Its colonies appear golden-yellow, hence the species name aureus ("golden").

Pathogenicity

Although S. aureus usually acts as a harmless commensal—about 20–30% of people are persistent nasal carriers—it can become an opportunistic pathogen and is a leading cause of skin and soft-tissue infections, abscesses, respiratory infections, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, bacteremia and food poisoning. It produces a range of virulence factors, including toxins responsible for toxic shock syndrome and staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome.

Antibiotic resistance

Penicillin resistance appeared within a few years of penicillin's introduction, mediated by a β-lactamase. Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), first identified in 1961, is resistant to nearly all β-lactam antibiotics and became a major cause of hospital-acquired infection, with community-associated strains spreading from the 1990s onward. Vancomycin remains a mainstay treatment, though strains with reduced susceptibility have emerged.

Identification

In the laboratory, S. aureus is distinguished from other staphylococci by the coagulase test and by fermentation of mannitol on mannitol salt agar, which turns the medium yellow.
