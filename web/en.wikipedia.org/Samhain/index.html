Samhain

Samhain (pronounced "sow-in") is a Gaelic festival marking the end of the harvest season and the beginning of winter, traditionally celebrated from sunset on 31 October to sunset on 1 November — roughly halfway between the autumn equinox and the winter solstice. It is one of the four Gaelic seasonal festivals, along with Imbolc, Beltane and Lughnasa, and is widely regarded as a principal ancestor of Halloween.

History

Samhain is attested in early Irish literature as one of the year's great assemblies, when kings held feasts and the herds were brought down from summer pastures, some animals being slaughtered for winter stores. In Irish mythology it is a liminal time when the sídhe — the mounds of the Otherworld — stand open and the dead and supernatural beings can cross into the world of the living; many tales set climactic events at Samhain. Bonfires were lit on hilltops, and in later folk custom people in Scotland and Ireland went guising in costume, carried turnip lanterns, and practised divination games concerning future spouses.

Connection to Halloween and All Saints

The Christian feasts of All Saints' Day (1 November) and All Souls' Day (2 November) came to coincide with Samhain, producing the triduum of Allhallowtide. Irish and Scottish emigrants carried the customs to North America, where they developed into modern Halloween with pumpkin jack-o'-lanterns and trick-or-treating.

Modern observance

Samhain is a major sabbath in Wicca and modern Celtic neopaganism, observed as a time to honour ancestors and the dead, often with rituals, "dumb suppers" and bonfires. The Samhuinn Fire Festival has been staged in Edinburgh since the 1980s–90s by the Beltane Fire Society.
