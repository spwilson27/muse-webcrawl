A geomagnetic storm, also known as a magnetic storm, is a temporary disturbance of the Earth's magnetosphere caused by a solar wind shock wave that interacts with the Earth's magnetic field. Storms are driven mainly by coronal mass ejections (CMEs) whose embedded magnetic field points southward, allowing magnetic reconnection with Earth's field and the injection of energetic particles into the magnetosphere.

Geomagnetic storms drive auroras to unusually low latitudes and induce damaging currents in power grids.

Effects

Storm-time currents in the ionosphere and magnetosphere produce auroras visible far from the poles and induce geomagnetically induced currents (GICs) in long conductors on the ground. Consequences can include:

  • Power grids: GICs saturate transformers; in March 1989 a storm collapsed the Hydro-Québec grid, leaving six million people without power for nine hours.
  • Spacecraft: surface charging, single-event upsets and increased atmospheric drag — in February 2022 a modest storm caused the loss of up to 40 newly launched Starlink satellites.
  • Communications and navigation: HF radio blackouts, GPS/GNSS errors from ionospheric scintillation.
  • Pipelines: accelerated corrosion from induced currents.

Notable storms

The Carrington Event of 1–2 September 1859, the most intense storm in recorded history, produced auroras seen in the tropics and set telegraph wires sparking. Other major events include the May 1921 "New York Railroad Storm", the March 1989 storm, the "Halloween storms" of 2003, and the severe May 2024 storm (the "Gannon" storm), which brought auroras to mid-latitudes worldwide. A Carrington-class CME narrowly missed Earth in July 2012.

Forecasting

Agencies such as NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center monitor the Sun and the solar wind at the L1 point (spacecraft DSCOVR and ACE) to give warnings of tens of minutes to a few days. Storm intensity is classified on the G-scale, G1 (minor) to G5 (extreme).
