An intangible asset is an asset that lacks physical substance. Examples are patents, copyrights, franchises, goodwill, trademarks, and trade names, as well as software. This is in contrast to physical assets (machinery, buildings, inventory) and financial assets (government securities). An intangible asset is usually very difficult to evaluate. They suffer from typical market failures of non-rivalry and non-excludability.

Definition

An intangible asset is a non-monetary asset without physical substance that is identifiable, controlled by an entity, and from which future economic benefits are expected to flow. Under International Accounting Standard 38 (IAS 38), an intangible asset may be recognised on the balance sheet only if it is identifiable, the entity controls the resource, and it is probable that future economic benefits will flow to the entity and can be measured reliably.

Types

Intangible assets are commonly grouped into two broad categories: intellectual property and goodwill. Intellectual property includes patents, trademarks, copyrights, trade secrets and licensing agreements, which have legally enforceable rights. Goodwill arises in business combinations when the purchase price exceeds the fair value of identifiable net assets acquired; it reflects elements such as reputation, customer relationships and workforce expertise that cannot be separately identified.

Valuation

Valuing intangible assets is inherently uncertain. Common approaches include the cost approach (what it would cost to recreate the asset), the market approach (prices paid for comparable assets), and the income approach (the present value of future earnings attributable to the asset). Internally generated intangible assets, such as brands built in-house, are generally not recognised under accounting standards because their cost cannot be distinguished from the cost of developing the business as a whole.

Amortisation and impairment

Intangible assets with finite useful lives are amortised over those lives, while those with indefinite lives — including goodwill under US GAAP — are not amortised but are tested annually for impairment. Research costs are expensed as incurred, whereas development costs may be capitalised if strict criteria are met.
