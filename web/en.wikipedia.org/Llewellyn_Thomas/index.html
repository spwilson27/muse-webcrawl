Llewellyn Hilleth Thomas (21 October 1903 – 20 April 1992) was a Welsh theoretical physicist and applied mathematician best known for three contributions that all bear his name: the relativistic effect of Thomas precession, the Thomas–Fermi statistical model of the atom, and the Thomas algorithm for solving tridiagonal systems of equations, widely used in numerical computation.

Life and career

Thomas was born in Swansea, Wales, and educated at Cambridge University, where he worked in the orbit of the Cavendish Laboratory during the golden age of quantum mechanics. In 1927 he moved to the United States, joining Ohio State University, where he remained for many years before moving to the Thomas J. Watson Scientific Computing Laboratory at Columbia University, associated with IBM, becoming a pioneer of scientific computing. He later settled at North Carolina State University in Raleigh, where he spent the rest of his career.

Thomas precession

In 1926 Thomas showed that an electron moving in a curved orbit undergoes a relativistic precession of its spin axis—a purely kinematic consequence of successive non-collinear Lorentz transformations. Applying this "Thomas half" correction resolved a factor-of-two discrepancy in the theory of atomic fine structure and the spin–orbit interaction, at a time when electron spin itself was new. The effect, rediscovered and generalized in the Bargmann–Michel–Telegdi equation, remains a standard part of relativistic dynamics and also applies to gyroscopes in orbit.

Thomas–Fermi model

In 1927, independently of Enrico Fermi, Thomas published a statistical theory of the atom in which electrons are treated as a degenerate Fermi gas distributed in the self-consistent electrostatic potential. The Thomas–Fermi model yields approximate electron densities and energies for heavy atoms and ions and became foundational to density functional thinking: it is the historical precursor of modern density functional theory, expressing kinetic energy as an explicit functional of the electron density.

Computing work

Thomas's simple elimination scheme for tridiagonal linear systems—published in the 1940s in the context of engineering calculations—is still taught as the Thomas algorithm. At the IBM Watson Laboratory he worked on early electronic computers and numerical methods for hydrodynamics and other applied problems, helping to establish scientific computing as a discipline.
