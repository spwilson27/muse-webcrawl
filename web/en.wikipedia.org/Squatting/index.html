Squatting

Squats are often formerly empty buildings occupied without the owner's permission.

Squatting is the action of occupying an abandoned or unoccupied area of land or a building, usually residential, that the squatter does not own, rent, or otherwise have lawful permission to use. The United Nations estimated in 2003 that there were one billion slum residents and squatters worldwide. Squatting occurs worldwide and tends to occur when poor and homeless people find empty buildings or land to occupy for housing; it has also been used as a political protest strategy.

Overview

Most squatting is residential in nature, undertaken out of necessity for shelter. Throughout the world there are huge informal settlements, often built on land occupied without permission, including the favelas of Brazil, the villas miseria of Argentina, and shanty towns across Africa and Asia. In developed countries there are also squats organized as social centres, artist spaces, or autonomous political projects — for example the squatting movements of Berlin, Copenhagen (Freetown Christiania), London, and Amsterdam, where squatting was lawful in some circumstances until a 2010 ban.

Legal status

The legality of squatting varies widely by jurisdiction. In some legal systems, long-term occupation can give rise to rights through adverse possession (sometimes called "squatter's rights"), under which a person who occupies land openly and continuously for a statutory period may gain legal title. In England and Wales, residential squatting was criminalised in 2012, while non-residential squatting remains a civil matter. In the United States, squatting laws vary by state, and adverse possession typically requires between 5 and 30 years of occupation depending on the state.

History

Squatting has a long history, from the Diggers of 17th-century England, who occupied common land to farm collectively, to the organised squatting of ex-servicemen after both world wars. Post-war Britain saw large-scale squatting campaigns that led to the licensing of empty properties to housing cooperatives. In the 1960s and 1970s squatting movements merged with countercultural and anarchist politics in many European cities, and in the 21st century anti-eviction movements such as Spain's okupa movement and the Platform for People Affected by Mortgages have used occupation to resist repossession during housing crises.
