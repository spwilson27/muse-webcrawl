Chinese jade


A jade bi disc

Chinese jade refers to the carved jade objects produced within the scope of Chinese culture, from the Neolithic period to the present day. In Chinese tradition, "jade" (yù 玉) primarily means nephrite, although jadeite — imported from Burma from the 18th century — and various other hardstones are also included. More valued than gold or silver, jade was associated with purity, moral virtue, beauty and immortality, and occupied a central ritual and artistic role for nearly seven thousand years.

History

Neolithic cultures such as Hongshan and Liangzhu produced sophisticated jades, including the bi (flat disc with a central hole) and cong (tube), whose ritual functions remain debated. During the Shang and Zhou dynasties, carved jade was used for ritual vessels, weapons, ornaments and burial objects, and texts attributed to Confucius metaphorised the virtues of the gentleman through the qualities of jade. The Han dynasty produced famous burial suits sewn with jade plaques, including those of Prince Liu Sheng. Later periods saw a growing emphasis on decorative carvings, scholar's objects, jewellery and large-scale sculptural pieces, especially under Qianlong in the Qing dynasty, when jadeite and huge carvings such as the "Yu the Great Taming the Waters" boulder demonstrated imperial wealth.

Working and symbolism

Because jade is too hard to carve with steel blades in the ordinary sense, it has traditionally been worked by abrasion — sawing, drilling and grinding with quartz, garnet or corundum sands. Motifs draw on a rich symbolic vocabulary: dragons, phoenixes, peaches (longevity), bats (good fortune), and the forms of archaic bronzes. Jade remains culturally and commercially significant today, worked in centres such as Suzhou and Yangzhou and prized across Greater China and the diaspora.
