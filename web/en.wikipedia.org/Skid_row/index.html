Skid row

A skid row, also called a skid road, is an impoverished area of a city, typically inhabited or frequented by individuals marginalized by poverty and homelessness, often associated with drug addiction, alcoholism and transient populations. The term originated in the Pacific Northwest of the United States, where skid roads were used in the logging industry to haul logs over greased wooden tracks. Over time, the districts adjacent to those roads — where loggers, sailors and itinerant workers lived — took on the name.

Origin of the term

The phrase derives from the skid roads of Seattle and other logging towns in the late nineteenth century. In Seattle, Henry Yesler's mill stood near what became Yesler Way, along which logs were skidded downhill to the waterfront. The surrounding neighborhood, home to saloons, cheap hotels and brothels catering to loggers, became known as "Skid Road" or, in the corrupted form, "Skid Row." The term spread across North America to describe comparable districts in cities such as Vancouver, Los Angeles, New York and San Francisco.

Characteristics

Skid rows historically developed near rail yards, ports and industrial districts, where an abundance of single-room occupancy (SRO) hotels, missions, soup kitchens, cheap bars and pawn shops served a largely male, transient workforce. Following World War II, deindustrialization, the decline of seasonal labor and cuts to social housing increased the concentration of chronic homelessness in these areas. Urban renewal programs of the mid-twentieth century demolished many skid row districts or displaced their residents, while others persisted into the era of widespread homelessness in the 1980s.

Notable skid rows

One of the most prominent examples is the Skid Row neighborhood of downtown Los Angeles, an officially designated area of roughly fifty city blocks that has long had one of the largest stable populations of homeless people in the United States. Other notable examples include Vancouver's Downtown Eastside, San Francisco's Tenderloin, and New York's Bowery, the latter famous in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries for its flophouses, though it has since undergone extensive gentrification.

Policy responses

Responses to skid row conditions have ranged from containment policies, which tolerates the concentration of poverty in one district, to dispersal through supportive housing and "Housing First" programs. Critics argue that containment concentrates services but also concentrates crime and public-health hazards, while advocates note that established skid rows provide dense networks of shelters, clinics and meal programs. Gentrification pressure in central cities has repeatedly raised questions about displacement of the populations these districts serve.
