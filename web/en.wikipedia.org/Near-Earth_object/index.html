Near-Earth object

A near-Earth object (NEO) is any small Solar System body whose orbit brings it into proximity with Earth. By convention, an object is a NEO if its closest approach to the Sun (perihelion) is less than 1.3 astronomical units (AU). Near-Earth objects include near-Earth asteroids (NEAs) and a small number of near-Earth comets, together with meteoroids large enough to be tracked in space.

Classification

Near-Earth asteroids are divided by their orbital characteristics into groups:

  • Atira asteroids orbit entirely within Earth's orbit.
  • Aten asteroids have semi-major axes smaller than 1 AU but cross Earth's orbit.
  • Apollo asteroids have semi-major axes larger than 1 AU and cross Earth's orbit; they form the largest group.
  • Amor asteroids approach but do not cross Earth's orbit, with perihelia between 1.017 and 1.3 AU.

Potentially hazardous objects

NEOs whose orbits come within 0.05 AU of Earth's orbit and which are larger than about 140 metres are designated potentially hazardous objects (PHOs), because an impact could cause significant regional damage. Tens of thousands of NEOs have been catalogued, including more than 90 per cent of the estimated population larger than one kilometre. Events such as the 2013 Chelyabinsk meteor, which injured around 1,500 people, have sharpened attention on smaller, undetected objects.

Surveys and planetary defence

Telescope surveys such as Pan-STARRS, the Catalina Sky Survey and ATLAS search for NEOs, and missions like NASA's NEOWISE have characterised many of them. In 2022 the DART spacecraft deliberately impacted the asteroid moonlet Dimorphos, demonstrating that a kinetic impactor can alter an asteroid's orbit — the first full-scale test of planetary defence.
