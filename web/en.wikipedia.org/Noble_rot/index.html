Noble rot

OrganismBotrytis cinerea (grey mould fungus)
French termPourriture noble
Famous winesSauternes, Tokaji aszú, German Trockenbeerenauslese
ConditionsMisty mornings followed by warm, dry afternoons

Noble rot (French: pourriture noble; German: Edelfäule; Italian: muffa nobile; Hungarian: aszú) is the beneficial form of a grey fungus, Botrytis cinerea, affecting wine grapes. Infestation by Botrytis requires moist conditions, and if the weather stays wet, the damaging form known as grey rot can destroy crops. Grapes typically become infected when they are ripe.

Mechanism

Under the right alternation of humid mornings and dry afternoons, the fungus punctures the grape skins and feeds on the moisture inside, causing the berries to shrivel like raisins. Water evaporates while sugars, acids, and flavour compounds concentrate, and the fungus contributes distinctive honey, apricot, and marmalade flavours to the resulting wine.

Famous wines

The most celebrated noble-rot wines include Sauternes and Barsac from Bordeaux (made from Sémillon, Sauvignon blanc, and Muscadelle), Tokaji aszú from Hungary (Furmint grapes), and the German Riesling categories Beerenauslese and Trockenbeerenauslese. Because the shrivelled berries yield very little juice and must often be picked one by one over multiple passes through the vineyard, these sweet wines are among the most labour-intensive and expensive to produce.

Other regions

Producers in Austria, Italy, California, Australia, and New Zealand also make botrytised wines where vintage conditions allow, though consistent noble rot depends heavily on local microclimates near rivers and lakes that generate morning mists.
