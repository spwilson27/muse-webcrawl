HIV/AIDS

HIV/AIDS is a spectrum of conditions caused by infection with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). The term encompasses the entire course of infection, from initial infection through the development of acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), the most advanced stage, defined by a CD4 cell count below 200 cells per microlitre or the presence of certain opportunistic infections and cancers.

Stages of infection

Acute infection occurs two to four weeks after exposure and may cause a flu-like illness with fever, rash and sore throat while viral load is very high. A period of clinical latency follows, lasting years, during which the virus continues to replicate at low levels and CD4 cells gradually decline. As immune function collapses, symptoms such as weight loss, chronic diarrhoea and fevers appear, and opportunistic conditions — including Pneumocystis pneumonia, Kaposi's sarcoma, tuberculosis and certain lymphomas — define AIDS.

Transmission and prevention

HIV spreads through unprotected sexual contact, blood exposure (including shared injecting equipment and, historically, transfusions), and vertically from mother to child. Prevention combines condoms, needle-exchange programmes, screening of blood products, voluntary medical male circumcision, PrEP and PEP, and antiretroviral treatment that renders viral load undetectable and untransmittable.

Treatment

Modern antiretroviral therapy — typically a combination of two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors with an integrase inhibitor or other agent — is recommended for all people diagnosed with HIV, regardless of CD4 count, following trials such as START (2015). With sustained treatment, life expectancy approaches that of the general population.

History and society

AIDS was first recognised clinically in 1981 in the United States; HIV was identified as its cause by researchers at the Pasteur Institute and in the US in 1983–84. The epidemic has killed around 40 million people, transformed global public health, and driven major funding initiatives such as PEPFAR (2003) and the Global Fund. Stigma and discrimination remain significant barriers to testing and care.
