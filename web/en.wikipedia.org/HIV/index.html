FamilyRetroviridae
GenusLentivirus

The human immunodeficiency viruses (HIV) are two species of Lentivirus (a subgroup of retrovirus) that infect humans. Over time, they cause acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), a condition in which progressive failure of the immune system allows life-threatening opportunistic infections and cancers to thrive.

HIV infects vital cells of the human immune system, principally CD4+ T helper cells, macrophages and dendritic cells. It is transmitted through contact with infected blood, semen, vaginal fluids, rectal fluids or breast milk — most commonly by unprotected sex, contaminated needles, or from mother to child during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding. It is not spread by casual contact, saliva or insect bites.

Classification and origins

HIV-1, responsible for the global pandemic, originated from a simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) of chimpanzees in Central Africa; HIV-2 derives from a sooty mangabey SIV in West Africa and is generally less transmissible and less virulent. HIV-1 group M is subdivided into subtypes (clades) with distinct geographic distributions.

Course and treatment

Following infection there is typically an acute flu-like illness, then a long clinically latent period. Without treatment, average survival after infection is about 9–11 years, as the CD4 count falls and AIDS develops. There is no cure or vaccine, but antiretroviral therapy (ART) can suppress viral load to undetectable levels, giving near-normal life expectancy; sustained undetectable viral load also means the virus cannot be transmitted sexually ("U=U"). Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) prevent infection in people at risk.

Global burden

Since the beginning of the epidemic, more than 80 million people have been infected and around 40 million have died of AIDS-related illnesses. Roughly 39 million people were living with HIV in the early 2020s, with sub-Saharan Africa bearing the greatest burden.
