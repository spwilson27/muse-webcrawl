Archdiocese

[Image placeholder]

An archdiocese (also known as an archbishopric or archsee) is a diocese of especially high rank, headed by an archbishop, in certain Christian churches, notably the Roman Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox Church, Oriental Orthodox churches, and the Anglican Communion. The name derives from the Greek ἀρχι- ("chief") and the word diocese.

Catholic practice

In the Catholic Church, an archdiocese is usually a particularly large, historic, or otherwise important diocese. A metropolitan archdiocese heads an ecclesiastical province composed of itself and several suffragan dioceses; its archbishop, the metropolitan, has limited supervisory authority over the suffragans and traditionally receives the pallium from the pope. Some archdioceses are not metropolitan — they may be directly subject to the Holy See or hold the rank for historical or honorary reasons. The diocese of Rome, whose bishop is the pope, is the preeminent archdiocese of the Latin Church. Worldwide, the Catholic Church has several hundred archdioceses, including major sees such as New York, Paris, Milan, Mexico City, Manila and São Paulo.

Other traditions

In Eastern Orthodoxy, archdioceses or archbishoprics vary in status between autocephalous churches; some archbishops rank immediately below patriarchs. In the Anglican Communion, the two English provinces are headed by the archbishops of Canterbury and York, and archdioceses (often called provinces or extra-provincial dioceses) exist in several national churches. Some Lutheran and Methodist bodies also use the title for senior regional jurisdictions.
