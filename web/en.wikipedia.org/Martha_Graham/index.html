Martha Graham

Martha Graham
Born: May 11, 1894, Allegheny, Pennsylvania, U.S.
Died: April 1, 1991, New York City, U.S.
Occupation: Dancer, choreographer
Movement: Modern dance

Martha Graham (May 11, 1894 – April 1, 1991) was an American modern dancer and choreographer. Her unique style of movement — based on the interplay of contraction and release — revolutionized dance and is widely regarded as one of the most significant developments in twentieth-century performing art. Over a career spanning more than seven decades, she choreographed over 180 works and founded a company and school that trained generations of dancers.

Early life

Graham was born in Allegheny, Pennsylvania (now Pittsburgh), and moved with her family to Santa Barbara, California. After seeing Ruth St. Denis perform in 1911, she became determined to study dance and in 1916 enrolled at the Denishawn school in Los Angeles, founded by St. Denis and Ted Shawn. She remained there until 1923, when she left to pursue choreography in New York.

The Graham technique

Graham's technique is built on the principle of "contraction and release" — the tightening and opening of the torso, rooted in the breath, which Graham believed expressed the fundamental drama of the human spirit. Her early solos, such as Lamentation (1930) and Frontier (1935), stripped away the decorative conventions of ballet to reveal stark emotional landscapes. She frequently collaborated with the sculptor Isamu Noguchi and composers including Aaron Copland, whose score for her ballet Appalachian Spring (1944) won the Pulitzer Prize.

Later works and legacy

Graham continued performing into her seventies. Her works exploring myth and the female psyche — Errand into the Maze, Cave of the Heart, Night Journey and Phaedra — remain cornerstones of the modern dance repertoire. She received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1976 and was called the "Dancer of the Century" by Time magazine. The Martha Graham Dance Company, founded in 1926, remains the oldest American dance company.
