Sleet

Sleet is a form of precipitation consisting of small ice pellets, formed when snowflakes falling through a layer of warm air partially melt and then refreeze in a deeper layer of sub-freezing air near the ground. In the United States and Canada, "sleet" refers to these ice pellets, whereas in much of the rest of the English-speaking world the term denotes a mixture of rain and snow.

Sleet

Type: Winter precipitation (ice pellets)
METAR code: PL
Forms: Melted snow refrozen before reaching ground

Formation

Sleet develops in a characteristic temperature profile: a warm layer aloft (the "warm nose") melts snow into rain or slush, and a sufficiently deep cold layer below refreezes the drops into translucent ice pellets that bounce on impact. This distinguishes sleet from freezing rain, where the cold layer is too shallow and drops freeze on contact with surfaces.

Effects

Sleet accumulations are generally less hazardous than freezing-rain ice, though heavy sleet can accumulate like snow, coat roads and produce noisy, stinging precipitation during winter storms.
