Cause in fact

Cause in fact, also called actual cause or factual cause, is a concept in tort law: the requirement that the defendant's conduct be the actual source of the plaintiff's injury. Together with proximate cause, it forms the causation element a plaintiff must prove to establish liability for negligence.

The "but-for" test

The standard test asks: but for the defendant's breach of duty, would the plaintiff's injury have occurred? If the injury would not have happened absent the defendant's conduct, cause in fact is established. If the injury would have occurred anyway, the test fails.

Substantial factor test

Where two or more causes combine — for example, two fires merging to destroy a house, each sufficient on its own — the but-for test can fail for both defendants. Courts then apply the substantial factor test: a defendant's conduct is a cause in fact if it was a substantial factor in producing the harm.

Related doctrines

Special problems include alternative liability (as in Summers v. Tice), market-share liability, and loss-of-chance claims in medical malpractice. Cause in fact is a question of fact for the jury, distinct from the policy-laden inquiry of proximate cause.
