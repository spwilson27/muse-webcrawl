Duality (order theory)

In order theory, duality is the principle that every definition, theorem or concept about partially ordered sets has a mirror-image counterpart obtained by reversing all order relations. Formally, for every poset (P, ≤) there is a dual (or opposite) order (P, ≥), and a statement is true in all posets if and only if its dual statement is.

Examples

Dual pairs of concepts include: greatest element and least element; upper bound and lower bound; supremum (join) and infimum (meet); maximal and minimal elements; increasing and decreasing maps; and filters and ideals. De Morgan's laws in lattices are an expression of duality, with union and intersection, top and bottom, interchanged.

Duality principle

The duality principle says that to prove a result about all posets it suffices to prove one of each pair of dual statements; the other follows automatically by applying the first to the dual order. This halves the work in lattice theory and in the theory of Boolean algebras, where the dual of a theorem is obtained by swapping ∧ and ∨, 0 and 1, and reversing implications.

Self-dual notions

Some notions are unchanged under duality — they are self-dual — such as the concept of a lattice itself, or an order automorphism that is an involution between a poset and its dual, called a dual automorphism.
