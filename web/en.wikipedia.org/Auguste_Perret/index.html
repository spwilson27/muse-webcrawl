Auguste Perret, pioneer of reinforced concrete architecture.

Auguste Perret (12 February 1874 – 25 February 1954) was a French architect and a pioneer of the architectural use of reinforced concrete. His major contributions include the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées (1913) in Paris, the Church of Notre-Dame du Raincy (1922–23), the Mobilier National (1937) and the French Economic, Social and Environmental Council building (Palais d'Iéna, 1939). After World War II he designed the reconstruction of the centre of the port city of Le Havre, a vast concrete cityscape inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2005.

Life and career

Perret was born in Belgium, where his father, a stonemason, had taken refuge after the Paris Commune. He studied architecture at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris but left without a diploma to join the family construction firm. With his brothers Gustave and Claude he built one of the first apartment houses in reinforced concrete, at 25 bis rue Franklin in Paris (1903), whose exposed concrete frame, thin infill panels and large windows prefigured modern architecture. His garage on rue de Ponthieu (1905) and the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées (1911–13) demonstrated that concrete could carry a refined classical expression rather than crude industrial forms.

Perret's atelier trained or employed several leading figures of modernism, most famously Le Corbusier, who worked for him in 1908–09. Perret believed in "honest" construction: the structure should remain visible, and ornament should arise from the logic of the frame — a doctrine he called classical structural rationalism.

Le Havre

After the destruction of Le Havre's centre in 1944, Perret, then in his seventies, directed its rebuilding (1945–64) using a modular concrete grid of prefabricated elements. The reconstructed city, with its monumental axes, the church of Saint-Joseph with its towering lantern, and uniform concrete façades enlivened by varied aggregates, is considered a masterpiece of post-war urbanism.
