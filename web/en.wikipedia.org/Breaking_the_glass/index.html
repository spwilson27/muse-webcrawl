Breaking the glass

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Breaking the glass under the chuppah

Breaking the glass is a traditional ritual performed near the end of a Jewish wedding ceremony. Under the wedding canopy (chuppah), the groom — in some egalitarian ceremonies, both partners — crushes a glass underfoot, usually wrapped in cloth or a napkin for safety. The breaking of the glass is followed by cheers of "Mazel Tov!" from the guests and marks the joyful climax of the ceremony.

Origins and interpretations

The custom has roots in the Talmud, which relates that at a wedding feast a rabbi broke a valuable glass to temper excessive merriment. The most widespread interpretation is that the shattered glass recalls the destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem, a reminder of sorrow even at the height of personal joy. Other explanations offered over the centuries include symbolising the fragility of human relationships, the irrevocability of the marriage bond, warding off evil spirits with the noise, and recalling the impermanence of life. Some couples keep the shards, sometimes set into a mezuzah or artwork, as a keepsake.

Practice

The ritual typically occurs after the exchange of rings, the reading of the ketubah and the Seven Blessings, just before the couple is declared married. A thin glass is used so that it breaks easily and completely. In some communities a light bulb was historically substituted where glass was scarce. The moment is often accompanied by the recitation of a verse from Psalm 137: "If I forget you, O Jerusalem, let my right hand forget its skill."
