Clinical psychology

Clinical psychology is an integration of social science, theory and clinical knowledge for the purpose of understanding, preventing and relieving psychologically based distress or dysfunction and promoting subjective well-being and personal development. Central to its practice are psychological assessment, clinical formulation, and psychotherapy, although clinical psychologists also engage in research, teaching, consultation and forensic testimony.

History

The field is often dated to 1896, when Lightner Witmer founded the first psychological clinic at the University of Pennsylvania. Clinical psychology expanded after the Second World War, when the U.S. Veterans Administration funded training to treat returning soldiers; the Boulder Conference of 1949 endorsed the scientist–practitioner model, requiring competence in both research and practice. Later models, such as the Vail model (1973), emphasised professional practice and led to the Doctor of Psychology (PsyD) degree.

Therapeutic approaches

Clinical psychologists draw on diverse orientations: psychodynamic therapy, humanistic approaches, and cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT), which has the largest evidence base for many disorders. Assessment uses clinical interviews, psychological testing (intelligence, personality and symptom measures) and behavioural observation. Clinicians work in hospitals, private practice, community mental health centres and schools, treating conditions from depression and anxiety to psychosis and personality disorders.

Training and regulation

In most jurisdictions clinical psychologists are licensed health professionals, typically requiring a doctoral degree, supervised clinical experience and a licensing examination.
