Transform fault

A transform fault or transform boundary, also known as a conservative plate boundary, is a fault along a plate boundary where the motion is predominantly horizontal. It ends abruptly where it connects to another plate boundary, either a transform, a spreading ridge, or a subduction zone. Most such faults occur in oceanic crust, offsetting mid-ocean ridges.

Concept

The transform fault concept was proposed by Canadian geophysicist J. Tuzo Wilson in 1965 and became a cornerstone of plate tectonics: it predicted that offset ridge segments are connected by faults along which the sense of motion is opposite to what the offset naively suggests, terminating at the ridge crests.

Examples

Well-known continental transform faults include the San Andreas Fault in California, the Alpine Fault in New Zealand, the Dead Sea Transform, and the North Anatolian Fault in Turkey — all sites of significant earthquakes. Oceanic fracture zones are the inactive extensions of transform faults away from ridge axes.
