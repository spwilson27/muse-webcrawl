Avvakum Petrovich (Russian: Авваку́м Петро́вич; 1620–1682) was a Russian archpriest who led the opposition to Patriarch Nikon's reforms of the Russian Orthodox Church. His followers, the Old Believers, rejected the changes to ritual and texts, causing the schism (Raskol) of 1666. Avvakum's autobiography, written in Siberian exile, is regarded as a masterpiece of early Russian literature — the first substantial work in Russian written in a vivid vernacular style.

Life

Born in Grigorovo near Nizhny Novgorod, Avvakum became a village priest noted for his zeal and strictness. He joined the circle of reformers known as the Zealots of Piety, but broke with Nikon when the patriarch's reforms (1653 onward) altered the sign of the cross (from two fingers to three), the spelling of Jesus's name, and liturgical texts to conform to Greek practice. Avvakum defended the old Russian usages as sacred and unchangeable.

Exile and death

Avvakum was exiled to Siberia, enduring a brutal journey to Tobolsk, Yakutsk and Dauria in which much of his family suffered alongside him. Recalled and then condemned by a church council in 1666–67, he was defrocked, cursed and imprisoned in an earthen pit at Pustozersk above the Arctic Circle. There he wrote his Life (1672–75) and many letters and polemics. After fourteen years, following further defiance of the tsar, he was burned at the stake in a log hut on 14 April 1682.

Legacy

Old Believers revere Avvakum as a saint and martyr. His Life of the Archpriest Avvakum, Written by Himself pioneered autobiography in Russian literature and is still admired for its mixture of Church Slavonic and colloquial Russian, its humour, and its unflinching account of suffering.
