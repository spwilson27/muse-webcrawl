RNA vaccine

RNA vaccine
Type: Vaccine platform
Active component: Messenger RNA (mRNA)
Delivery: Lipid nanoparticles
First approved: 2020 (COVID-19)

An RNA vaccine is a type of vaccine that uses a copy of messenger RNA (mRNA) to produce an immune response. The mRNA encodes a protein from a pathogen — typically a viral surface protein — and is delivered into the body's cells inside protective lipid nanoparticles. The cells translate the mRNA and display the protein, training the immune system to recognise the real pathogen, after which the mRNA rapidly degrades.

Development

The technology rested on decades of research, including the work of Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman, whose nucleoside modifications (such as pseudouridine) tamed the inflammatory response to synthetic mRNA and earned them the 2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The first RNA vaccines to be authorised were the COVID-19 vaccines of Pfizer–BioNTech and Moderna in late 2020, developed and approved at unprecedented speed during the pandemic.

Advantages and challenges

RNA vaccines can be designed quickly from a pathogen's genome sequence and manufactured without growing the pathogen itself. Drawbacks include the fragility of mRNA, which initially required ultra-cold storage, and reactogenicity such as fever and sore arms. Research continues into RNA vaccines for influenza, RSV, HIV and personalised cancer vaccines.
