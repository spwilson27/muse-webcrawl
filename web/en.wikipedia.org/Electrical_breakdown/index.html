Electrical breakdown

Electrical breakdown is the rapid reduction in the electrical resistance of an electrical insulator when the voltage applied across it exceeds the breakdown voltage. The insulator becomes electrically conductive, often suddenly and dramatically, producing a spark or arc. Any insulator can break down given a sufficiently strong electric field.

Breakdown of air produces the sparks and arcs seen in high-voltage equipment—and lightning itself.

Mechanism

In gases, a few free electrons are always present. A strong field accelerates them until they collide with atoms and ionise them, freeing more electrons in an avalanche process described by Townsend theory; the gas becomes a plasma and conducts. In liquids and solids, breakdown involves a combination of ionisation, heating, and electromechanical stress, and in solids it is usually destructive and irreversible.

Breakdown strength

Each material has a characteristic dielectric strength. Dry air breaks down at roughly 3 million volts per metre, which is why high-voltage equipment uses large clearances, or insulators such as oil, SF₆ gas, porcelain, and polymers. Lightning is a natural large-scale breakdown of air between clouds and the ground.

Applications and hazards

Breakdown is exploited in spark plugs, gas-discharge lamps, spark gaps, and some protective devices, and is the working principle behind avalanche breakdown in Zener diodes. Uncontrolled breakdown, by contrast, causes insulation failure, fires, and equipment damage in power systems, and partial discharges within insulation slowly degrade it over time.
