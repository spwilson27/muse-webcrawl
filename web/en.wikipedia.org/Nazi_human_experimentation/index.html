Nazi human experimentation

Nazi human experimentation was a series of medical experiments on large numbers of prisoners, including children, by Nazi Germany in its concentration camps in the early to mid 1940s, during World War II and the Holocaust. Prisoners — chiefly Jews from across Europe, but also Romani, Sinti, ethnic Poles, Soviet prisoners of war, and disabled Germans — were coerced into participating; the experiments typically resulted in death, trauma, disfigurement, or permanent disability, and were conducted without the consent of the subjects. They are regarded as a profound example of medical atrocity and torture.

Experiments

The experiments, performed mainly at Auschwitz, Dachau, Ravensbrück, Sachsenhausen, Buchenwald, and other camps, included: high-altitude and freezing experiments to aid the Luftwaffe; seawater-drinking experiments; malaria, typhus, and other disease experiments; poison and incendiary-bomb injury studies; sterilization experiments; bone, muscle, and nerve regeneration and transplantation experiments; and the twin studies of Josef Mengele at Auschwitz. Doctors including Sigmund Rascher, Karl Brandt, and Horst Schumann directed or conducted many of them.

Aftermath

After the war, these crimes were prosecuted in the Doctors' Trial (United States v. Karl Brandt, et al.) at Nuremberg in 1946–47; seven defendants were executed. The trial produced the Nuremberg Code (1947), a foundational statement of research ethics requiring voluntary informed consent. The scientific validity of the results is widely regarded as poor, and the ethics of citing data derived from the experiments remains debated.
