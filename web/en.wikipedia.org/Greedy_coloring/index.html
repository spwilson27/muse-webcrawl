Greedy coloring

Problem: Graph coloring

Method: Greedy algorithm

Guarantee: Uses at most Δ + 1 colors (Δ = maximum degree)

In the study of graph coloring problems in mathematics and computer science, a greedy coloring or sequential coloring is a coloring of a graph's vertices produced by a greedy algorithm that considers the vertices of the graph in sequence and assigns each vertex its first available color — the smallest positive integer not used by its already-colored neighbours.

Properties

Greedy colorings can be very poor compared with an optimal coloring: the number of colors used depends heavily on the vertex ordering, and there exist orderings of a bipartite graph (which has chromatic number 2) on which the greedy algorithm uses arbitrarily many colors, as shown by the crown graph examples. However, if the vertices are ordered by decreasing degree — the basis of the Welsh–Powell algorithm — results are typically better, and an ordering by degeneracy guarantees at most d + 1 colors, where d is the degeneracy of the graph. For any graph, some ordering exists for which the greedy algorithm is optimal: such an ordering is obtained by coloring the graph optimally and listing the vertices color class by color class.

A greedy coloring automatically produces a proper coloring in linear time and gives the standard bound that the chromatic number is at most Δ + 1, where Δ is the maximum degree. The Grundy number of a graph is the maximum number of colors that a greedy coloring can use over all possible vertex orderings.

Applications

Greedy coloring and its variants are used in register allocation in compilers, scheduling problems, frequency assignment and other applications where a fast approximation to optimal coloring is acceptable. Related heuristics, such as DSATUR, choose the next vertex dynamically rather than fixing the order in advance.
