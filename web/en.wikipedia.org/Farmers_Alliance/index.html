Farmers' Alliance

The Farmers' Alliance was an organized agrarian economic movement among American farmers that developed and flourished in the 1870s and 1880s. It comprised a number of state and regional organizations, principally the Northern (or Northwestern) Alliance and the Southern Alliance, along with the separate Colored Farmers' Alliance for African American farmers. The movement sought to improve economic conditions for farmers through cooperatives and political action, and it was a forerunner of the Populist Party.

Origins and growth

Following the example of the Grange, farmers in Texas organized an Alliance in the 1870s that spread across the South and Plains states. The Southern Alliance, formally the National Farmers' Alliance and Industrial Union, claimed several million members at its peak around 1890 under leaders such as Charles W. Macune. The Northern Alliance organized farmers in the Midwest and Plains. Black farmers, excluded from the Southern Alliance, formed the Colored Farmers' Alliance in 1886, which also grew to claim over a million members.

Program

The Alliances founded cooperative stores, mills and exchanges to escape middlemen and creditors, and campaigned against railroads, banks and monopolies. Their political demands, set out in platforms such as the Cleburne Demands (1886) and the Ocala Demands (1890), included regulation of railroads, an end to national banks, a graduated income tax, free and unlimited coinage of silver, and the subtreasury plan, under which farmers could store crops in government warehouses and borrow against them.

Decline and legacy

Cooperative ventures largely failed for want of capital and credit, pushing the movement toward politics. In 1892 Alliance members and other reform groups formed the People's Party (Populists), which won significant support in the elections of 1892 and 1894 before fusing with the Democrats in 1896 and declining. Many Alliance demands were later enacted during the Progressive Era and New Deal.
