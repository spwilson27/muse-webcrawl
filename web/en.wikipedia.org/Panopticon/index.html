Panopticon

The panopticon is a design of institutional building with an inbuilt system of control, originated by the English philosopher and social theorist Jeremy Bentham in the 18th century. The concept is to allow all prisoners of an institution to be observed by a single custodian or guard, without the inmates knowing whether or not they are being watched.

Design

Bentham's design, set out most fully in his 1791 Panopticon; or, The Inspection-House, arranges an annular building of cells around a central inspection tower. Each cell is backlit and open toward the tower, so the occupants are permanently visible in silhouette, while blinds and partitions in the tower conceal the inspector. Because prisoners cannot tell when they are observed, they must behave as if watched at all times — producing, in Bentham's phrase, "a new mode of obtaining power of mind over mind".

History

The idea was inspired in part by the military school in Paris and by the inspection arrangements Samuel Bentham used at workshops in Russia. Bentham spent years and much of his fortune promoting a panopticon penitentiary in London, but it was never built in England in his lifetime. A number of prisons approximating the design were later constructed, including the Presidio Modelo in Cuba, Stateville Correctional Center in Illinois, and the Koepelgevangenis in the Netherlands; the concept was also applied to factories, schools, hospitals, and workhouses.

Philosophical legacy

The panopticon became a central metaphor in modern social theory through Michel Foucault's Discipline and Punish (1975), which used it to characterise "panopticism" — the disciplinary power of surveillance in modern institutions. The term is now widely used in debates about CCTV, workplace monitoring, and digital mass surveillance.
