Polycythaemia vera (PV), also called primary polycythaemia, is a chronic myeloproliferative neoplasm in which the bone marrow produces too many red blood cells. White blood cell and platelet counts are often also raised. The resulting increase in blood viscosity raises the risk of thrombosis — blood clots in arteries and veins — which is the principal cause of illness and death in the condition.

Cause

More than 95% of patients carry an acquired mutation in the JAK2 gene, most commonly the V617F substitution, in a blood-forming stem cell. The mutation makes blood cell progenitors hypersensitive to growth factors such as erythropoietin, driving uncontrolled production. Serum erythropoietin levels are typically low.

Signs and symptoms

Many patients are asymptomatic and are detected on a routine blood count. Symptoms, when present, include headaches, dizziness, visual disturbance, fatigue, reddening of the face (plethora), and a distinctive itching after contact with warm water (aquagenic pruritus). An enlarged spleen is common. Complications include stroke, myocardial infarction, deep vein thrombosis, Budd–Chiari syndrome and bleeding. Over years, PV may progress to myelofibrosis or, rarely, acute leukaemia.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on World Health Organization criteria combining raised haemoglobin or haematocrit, bone marrow findings of panmyelosis, the presence of a JAK2 mutation, and a subnormal erythropoietin level. Secondary causes of polycythaemia — such as chronic hypoxia or erythropoietin-secreting tumours — must be excluded.

Treatment

Treatment aims to reduce thrombotic risk. Venesection (phlebotomy) is used to keep the haematocrit below about 45%, and low-dose aspirin is commonly given. Patients at higher risk may receive cytoreductive therapy such as hydroxycarbamide or interferon, and JAK inhibitors such as ruxolitinib are used in selected cases.
