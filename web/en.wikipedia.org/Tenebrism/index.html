Tenebrism

Tenebrism, from the Italian tenebroso ("dark, gloomy, mysterious"), also occasionally called dramatic illumination, is a style of painting characterized by striking contrasts of light and dark, where darkness becomes a dominating feature of the image. The artist sharply increases the contrast between areas of strong illumination and deep shadow, so that figures emerge from near-black backgrounds.

Characteristics

Tenebrism differs from the more general technique of chiaroscuro in the degree of contrast and the subordination of detail: in tenebrist works, most of the canvas is swallowed in darkness and only selected passages — a face, a hand, a shoulder — are picked out by a shaft of light, often from an unseen source at upper left. The effect isolates the subject, heightens psychological drama, and lends even everyday scenes a sense of staged theatricality.

Origins and Caravaggio

The style is inseparably associated with the Italian painter Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio (1571–1610), whose mature works such as The Calling of Saint Matthew (1599–1600) and Judith Beheading Holofernes (c. 1598–1599) established the vocabulary of tenebrism. Caravaggio's followers, the Caravaggisti, carried the manner across Europe.

Spread

In Spain, tenebrism shaped the early work of Jusepe de Ribera and Francisco de Zurbarán. In France, Georges de La Tour adapted it to candlelit scenes. In the Netherlands, the Utrecht Caravaggisti — including Gerrit van Honthorst, nicknamed "Gherardo delle Notti" ("Gerard of the Nights") — popularized nocturnal subjects. Tenebrist lighting also influenced later painting, and the term is sometimes applied to dramatic low-key lighting in photography and cinema.
