The Westminster Shorter Catechism is a catechism in the Reformed tradition, composed in question-and-answer form by the Westminster Assembly in the 1640s. Together with the Westminster Larger Catechism and the Westminster Confession of Faith, it forms the Westminster Standards, the subordinate doctrinal standards of most Presbyterian churches. Despite its name, the Shorter Catechism was intended not merely for children but for all believers "of weaker capacity".

History

The Westminster Assembly was directed by Parliament in 1643 to draw up a catechism as part of its reform of the Church of England. Finding it easier to produce two, the Assembly completed the Shorter Catechism in the autumn of 1647 and presented it to Parliament in November of that year; the General Assembly of the Church of Scotland adopted it in July 1648. Memorisation of the catechism by children and adults became a hallmark of Scottish and Presbyterian religious education for the following three centuries.

Contents

The catechism contains 107 questions and answers. Its opening is among the most famous in Christian literature:

Q. 1. What is the chief end of man?
A. Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him for ever.

The body of the work follows the structure of the Confession of Faith: the doctrine of God and the decrees (Q. 1–11); creation, providence and the fall (Q. 12–19); redemption in Christ (Q. 20–38); the application of redemption and the means of grace (Q. 39–107), including expositions of the Ten Commandments (Q. 39–81), the sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper (Q. 91–97), and the Lord's Prayer (Q. 98–107).

Influence

The Shorter Catechism was carried wherever Scottish Presbyterianism spread — to Ireland, North America, Australia and mission fields worldwide. Adaptations were made by Baptists (Keach's Catechism) and Congregationalists. Generations of children were examined on it, and its terse, balanced prose has often been praised as a model of English theological writing.
