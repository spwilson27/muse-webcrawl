Confirmation

In Christian denominations that practice infant baptism, confirmation is seen as the sealing of the covenant created in baptism. Those being confirmed are known as confirmands. In some denominations, confirmation also bestows full membership in a local congregation upon the recipient. In others, such as the Roman Catholic Church, confirmation "renders the bond with the Church more perfect", because, while a baptized person is already a member, "reception of the sacrament of Confirmation is necessary for the completion of baptismal grace".

The sacrament is administered by anointing with chrism (holy oil) and the laying on of hands, traditionally by a bishop, together with the words of the rite.

Denominational practice

In the Latin Church of the Catholic Church, confirmation is one of the seven sacraments and is usually conferred in adolescence, with the age varying by diocese. In Eastern Catholic and Orthodox churches, chrismation is ordinarily conferred immediately after baptism, including for infants. Lutheran, Anglican, and many other Protestant churches retain confirmation as a rite of affirmation of baptismal promises, though not always as a sacrament.

Confirmation name

In some traditions, the confirmand chooses a confirmation name, usually that of a saint, as an additional patron and model for the Christian life.

Effects

The Roman Catholic Church teaches that confirmation imprints an indelible spiritual mark (character) on the soul, deepens incorporation into Christ, and strengthens the recipient to witness to the Christian faith. The traditional gifts associated with the sacrament are the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit: wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety, and fear of the Lord.
