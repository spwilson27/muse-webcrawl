Mimicry

In evolutionary biology, mimicry is an evolved resemblance between an organism and another object, often an organism of another species. Mimicry may evolve between different species, or between individuals of the same species. Often, mimicry functions to protect a species from predators, making it a form of anti-predator adaptation. The resemblance may be in appearance, behaviour, sound or scent, and usually involves a signal receiver (such as a predator) whose response benefits the mimic.

[Image: A harmless viceroy butterfly mimicking the toxic monarch butterfly]

Types

  • Batesian mimicry — a harmless species imitates the warning signal of a harmful species (e.g. hoverflies resembling wasps). Named after Henry Walter Bates.
  • Müllerian mimicry — two or more genuinely unpalatable species share a common warning pattern, spreading the cost of educating predators. Named after Fritz Müller.
  • Aggressive mimicry — a predator or parasite imitates a harmless or attractive signal to lure prey, as in anglerfish lures or cuckoo eggs.
  • Automimicry — members of a species mimic another part of their own body, as in eyespots on butterfly wings or fish tails.
  • Masquerade and crypsis — resemblance to inedible objects such as twigs, leaves or bird droppings.

Examples

Classic cases include monarch and viceroy butterflies, coral snakes and their harmless kingsnake imitators, orchids that mimic female insects to achieve pollination, and octopuses that imitate sea snakes and flatfish. Mimicry in plants (Vavilovian mimicry) occurs when weeds evolve to resemble crops under artificial selection.
