Yogurt

Yogurt (from Turkish: yoğurt) is a food produced by bacterial fermentation of milk. The bacteria used — "yogurt cultures", principally Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus — ferment lactose into lactic acid, which gives yogurt its tangy flavour and thick, gel-like texture.

History

Fermented milks arose spontaneously wherever milk was stored warm. Yogurt-like foods were consumed in the Balkans, Anatolia and Central Asia for millennia; the word itself is Turkish. The Bulgarian scientist Stamen Grigorov identified the yogurt bacillus in 1905, and Nobel laureate Élie Metchnikoff popularised yogurt in Western Europe, theorising that lactic-acid bacteria contributed to the longevity of Bulgarian peasants. Isaac Carasso commercialised yogurt in Barcelona in 1919 — founding Danone — and it spread worldwide in the 20th century.

Production and varieties

Milk is heated, cooled to about 43 °C, inoculated with starter culture, and incubated until it sets, then chilled. Varieties include set and stirred yogurt, Greek yogurt (strained to remove whey, hence thicker and higher in protein), skyr (Icelandic), kefir-like drinks, frozen yogurt, and plant-based yogurts made from soy, almond or coconut. Yogurt is a staple of many cuisines — in Indian raita and lassi, Turkish ayran and cacık, Greek tzatziki, and Middle Eastern labneh.

Nutrition and health

Yogurt provides protein, calcium, B vitamins and live cultures; fermentation partially breaks down lactose, so many lactose-intolerant people tolerate it. Health claims for probiotic effects are regulated in many jurisdictions.
