Digital dividend

The digital dividend is the radio spectrum released in the process of digital television transition. When television broadcasters switch from analog to digital transmission, a large portion of the electromagnetic spectrum previously used for broadcasting becomes available for other services because digital television uses spectrum more efficiently. A sub-band of the UHF spectrum, typically around 700–800 MHz, is commonly cleared and reallocated.

Uses of the released spectrum

The low UHF frequencies released are highly prized because signals propagate far and penetrate buildings well. Regulators have generally assigned the dividend to mobile broadband services (IMT/4G LTE and 5G), which generate substantial auction revenue and extend rural coverage, though parts have also been reserved for public-safety communications, wireless microphones and additional broadcasting.

Policy process

Allocation decisions are coordinated internationally through the International Telecommunication Union's World Radiocommunication Conferences and regional bodies. In Europe, the 800 MHz band (790–862 MHz) was harmonised for mobile services following the digital switchover completed in most member states by 2012, with the 700 MHz band cleared later as a "second digital dividend". In the United States, the 700 MHz auction of 2008 followed the 2009 digital transition. Governments must balance auction income against coverage obligations and the needs of existing spectrum users, such as programme-making and special events (PMSE) users of wireless microphones.

Debates

Broadcasters have argued for retaining spectrum for terrestrial television and future broadcasting technologies, while the mobile industry has pressed for larger allocations to meet data demand. Consumer groups have raised concerns about interference with remaining television reception and the cost of replacing aerials and filters.
