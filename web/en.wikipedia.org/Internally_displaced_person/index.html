An internally displaced person (IDP) is someone who has been forced to flee their home but who remains within their country's borders. The concept is described in the United Nations Guiding Principles on Internal Displacement, which define IDPs as persons or groups "forced or obliged to flee or to leave their homes or places of habitual residence, in particular as a result of or in order to avoid the effects of armed conflict, situations of generalized violence, violations of human rights or natural or human-made disasters, and who have not crossed an internationally recognized State border."

IDPs remain under the legal protection of their own government, unlike refugees who cross borders.

Legal status

Unlike refugees, IDPs are not covered by a dedicated binding international convention; they remain citizens or habitual residents of their own state, which bears primary responsibility for their protection. The Guiding Principles, presented in 1998 by Francis Deng, the Representative of the UN Secretary-General on IDPs, restate how existing human-rights and humanitarian law applies to them. At the regional level, the African Union's Kampala Convention (adopted 2009, in force 2012) is the first legally binding treaty on internal displacement.

Causes and scale

Conflict and violence are the largest causes, with countries such as Sudan, Syria, Colombia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Ukraine hosting some of the largest internally displaced populations in recent years. Sudden-onset disasters — floods, storms, earthquakes — displace tens of millions more each year, although most disaster displacement is shorter in duration. The Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre publishes the leading global figures in its annual Global Report on Internal Displacement.

Challenges

IDPs frequently suffer higher mortality and malnutrition rates than surrounding populations, lose documents and livelihoods, and face difficulties accessing housing, education, and healthcare. Because they remain under the authority of a government that may itself be a party to the conflict, securing protection and humanitarian access can be difficult. Durable solutions comprise voluntary return, local integration, or settlement elsewhere in the country.
