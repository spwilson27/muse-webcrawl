History of Ireland

The history of Ireland spans from the first human settlement after the last ice age to the modern era of two jurisdictions on the island: the Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland, part of the United Kingdom. Its major themes include the flourishing of early medieval monastic culture, Viking and Norman invasions, centuries of English and British rule, the catastrophe of the Great Famine, the struggle for independence, and partition.

Prehistory and early Ireland

Humans first settled Ireland around 12,500 years ago; Neolithic farmers built the great passage tombs of Newgrange and Knowth (c. 3200 BCE), older than the Egyptian pyramids. Celtic languages and culture arrived during the Iron Age. Ireland was never part of the Roman Empire, though it traded with Roman Britain. Christianity came in the 5th century — traditionally with St Patrick — and Irish monasteries became renowned centres of learning whose missionaries, such as Columba and Columbanus, helped re-Christianise post-Roman Europe. Viking raiders from the 790s founded coastal towns including Dublin, Waterford and Limerick.

Norman invasion and English rule

In 1169–71 Anglo-Norman forces, invited by a deposed Irish king and followed by Henry II of England, began the conquest that created the Lordship of Ireland. Over succeeding centuries English control contracted to the Pale around Dublin while Gaelic lordships revived; the Tudor reconquest of the 16th century, the Flight of the Earls (1607) and the Plantation of Ulster brought large-scale settlement by English and Scottish Protestants, and the Cromwellian conquest and Williamite war of the 17th century settled a confessional landowning order enforced by Penal Laws against Catholics.

Union, famine and nationalism

The rebellion of the United Irishmen (1798) was followed by the Act of Union 1800, which abolished the Irish Parliament and joined Ireland to Great Britain. Catholic emancipation came in 1829. The Great Famine (1845–1852), caused by potato blight, killed about one million people and drove another million to emigrate; Ireland's population fell from 8.2 million in 1841 to about 4.4 million by 1911 and has never regained its pre-famine level. Nationalist movements — constitutional under Parnell, revolutionary in the Fenians — pressed the land and Home Rule questions through the later 19th century.

Independence and partition

The Easter Rising of 1916, the War of Independence (1919–21) and the Anglo-Irish Treaty produced the Irish Free State in 1922, while six north-eastern counties remained in the United Kingdom as Northern Ireland. The Free State became a republic in 1949. In Northern Ireland, discrimination against the Catholic minority fuelled the civil-rights movement and then the Troubles (c. 1968–1998), a conflict that killed more than 3,500 people and ended with the Good Friday Agreement of 1998. The Republic, in the European Union since 1973, transformed from a poor emigrant society to the high-growth "Celtic Tiger" economy of the 1990s–2000s, surviving a severe crash and bailout after 2008. Brexit renewed debate about the island's constitutional future, while the Good Friday institutions continue to frame politics in the North.
