Evidential problem of evil

The evidential problem of evil is the claim that the existence, nature, and distribution of evil and suffering constitute strong evidence against the existence of an omnipotent, omniscient, and wholly good God. Unlike the logical problem of evil, which alleges a strict contradiction between God and evil, the evidential version argues more modestly that evil makes God's existence improbable.

Formulations

The philosopher William L. Rowe gave the best-known formulation, based on apparently pointless suffering — his famous example is a fawn burned in a forest fire, dying slowly over days with no observer and no apparent greater good served. Rowe argues: (1) there exist instances of intense suffering which an omnipotent, omniscient being could have prevented without losing some greater good or permitting some equally bad evil; (2) such a being would prevent any such suffering it could; therefore (3) such a being does not exist. Paul Draper developed a related argument, the "hypothesis of indifference," arguing that the pattern of pain and pleasure is much more likely on naturalism than on theism.

Responses

Responses include sceptical theism, which holds that human beings are in no position to judge that a given evil is truly pointless, since God's reasons may lie beyond our cognitive reach; free-will and soul-making theodicies, which propose that much evil is a necessary by-product of goods such as significant freedom or moral growth; and criticisms that probabilistic judgments about what God would do are unjustified. The problem is closely related to discussions of evidentiality in the broader epistemology of religious belief.
