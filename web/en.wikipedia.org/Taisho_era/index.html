Taishō era

The Taishō era (大正時代, Taishō jidai) is a period of Japanese history corresponding to the reign of Emperor Taishō (Yoshihito), from 30 July 1912 to 25 December 1926. It followed the Meiji era and preceded the Shōwa era. The era name, meaning "great righteousness," was drawn from classical Chinese texts.

Overview

Emperor Taishō suffered from chronic ill health, including neurological problems, which limited his ability to perform the active political role his father, Emperor Meiji, had played. Power consequently shifted toward the elected Diet, political parties, and the genrō (elder statesmen), giving rise to the period known as "Taishō democracy."

Taishō democracy

The era saw the growth of party politics, mass movements, and liberal ideas. The Rice Riots of 1918, sparked by soaring rice prices, led to the appointment of Hara Takashi, the first commoner and party politician to serve as prime minister. Universal male suffrage was enacted in 1925, greatly expanding the electorate, though the same year also saw passage of the Peace Preservation Law restricting dissent.

World War I and after

Japan entered the First World War on the Allied side under the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, seizing German possessions in the Pacific and in Shandong, China. The war brought an economic boom and increased international standing, including a permanent seat on the Council of the League of Nations. The era also witnessed the devastating Great Kantō earthquake of 1923, which destroyed much of Tokyo and Yokohama.

Culture

Taishō culture blended Western influences with Japanese tradition: modern literature, café culture, cinema, and new fashions flourished in the cities. Because of the emperor's illness, his son Hirohito served as regent (sesshō) from 1921 until Taishō's death in 1926.
