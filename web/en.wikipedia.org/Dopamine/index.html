Dopamine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dopamine (DA, a contraction of 3,4-dihydroxyphenethylamine) is a neuromodulatory molecule that plays several important roles in the cells of organisms. It is an organic chemical of the catecholamine and phenethylamine families, synthesised from the amino acid tyrosine. In the brain, dopamine functions as a neurotransmitter involved in reward, motivation, motor control, and the release of various hormones.

Functions in the brain

Dopamine is produced in several areas of the brain, including the substantia nigra and the ventral tegmental area. Four major dopaminergic pathways — mesolimbic, mesocortical, nigrostriatal and tuberoinfundibular — mediate functions ranging from reward prediction and reinforcement learning to voluntary movement and the inhibition of prolactin release. Popularly described as a "pleasure chemical", dopamine is more precisely linked to motivation and the anticipation of reward.

Clinical significance

Degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra causes Parkinson's disease, treated with the dopamine precursor L-DOPA. Dysregulation of dopamine signalling is implicated in schizophrenia (treated with dopamine-receptor-blocking antipsychotics), ADHD, restless legs syndrome and addiction; most addictive drugs increase dopamine activity in the reward system. Outside the brain, dopamine acts on the kidneys, blood vessels and immune system, and pharmaceutical dopamine is used to treat low blood pressure and slow heart rate.

History

Dopamine was first synthesised in 1910 by George Barger and James Ewens, and its role as a neurotransmitter in its own right was established by Arvid Carlsson in the 1950s, work for which Carlsson shared the Nobel Prize in 2000.
