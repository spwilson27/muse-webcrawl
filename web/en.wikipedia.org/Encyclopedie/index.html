The Encyclopédie, ou dictionnaire raisonné des sciences, des arts et des métiers (English: Encyclopedia, or a Systematic Dictionary of the Sciences, Arts, and Crafts), better known simply as the Encyclopédie, was a general encyclopedia published in France between 1751 and 1772, initially edited by Denis Diderot and, until 1759, co-edited by Jean le Rond d'Alembert.

The Encyclopédie is most famous for representing the thought of the Enlightenment. According to Dennis Diderot in the article "Encyclopédie", the work's aim was "to change the way people think". He and the other contributors, known as the philosophes, advocated the advancement of science and secular thought and supported religious tolerance.

Structure and publication

The work ultimately comprised 28 volumes: 17 volumes of text containing some 71,818 articles and 11 volumes of plates containing 2,885 illustrations. It began as a project to translate Ephraim Chambers's Cyclopaedia into French but grew into an original and far more ambitious undertaking. Publication was repeatedly threatened by censorship: the work was suppressed in 1752 and again in 1759, when its privilege was revoked, and Diderot completed the later text volumes clandestinely. Supplements and an index followed in the 1770s.

Contributors and contents

More than 140 contributors, the société de gens de lettres, wrote for the work, including Voltaire, Rousseau, Montesquieu, Quesnay and Turgot. Its articles ranged over philosophy, theology, the natural sciences, technology and the mechanical arts, whose trades were documented in unprecedented detail in the plates. Cross-references (renvois) were used to subvert orthodox readings and quietly advance heterodox arguments.

Legacy

The Encyclopédie is widely regarded as one of the most influential works of the Enlightenment, a publishing triumph that reached thousands of subscribers across Europe and helped disseminate the ideas that transformed European intellectual life in the decades before the French Revolution.
