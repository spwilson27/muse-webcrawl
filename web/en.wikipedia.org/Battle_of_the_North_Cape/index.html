Battle of the North Cape

Date26 December 1943
LocationOff North Cape, Norway, Barents Sea
ResultBritish victory; Scharnhorst sunk
BelligerentsUnited Kingdom vs. Nazi Germany
CommandersAdmiral Sir Bruce Fraser; Vice-Admiral Robert Burnett vs. Konteradmiral Erich Bey (killed)

The Battle of the North Cape was a naval battle fought on 26 December 1943 off Norway's North Cape during the Second World War. The German battleship Scharnhorst, attempting to attack the Arctic convoy JW 55B bound for the Soviet Union, was intercepted and sunk by a Royal Navy force centred on the battleship HMS Duke of York. It was the last battle fought between big-gun capital ships of Britain and Germany.

Radar-directed fire in the polar night decided the battle.

Background

Arctic convoys carried vital supplies to the Soviet ports of Murmansk and Archangelsk through some of the most dangerous waters of the war. In December 1943 German naval forces in Norway, under pressure to act, sent Scharnhorst and five destroyers under Konteradmiral Erich Bey to sea against convoy JW 55B. British intelligence, including Ultra decrypts, forewarned Admiral Fraser, who positioned the convoy's close escort cruisers under Burnett between Scharnhorst and the convoy, with his own battleship force approaching from the west.

The battle

In heavy seas and near-total Arctic darkness, Scharnhorst twice encountered Burnett's cruisers (Belfast, Norfolk and Sheffield) on the morning of 26 December and was driven off, losing her main radar to a hit from Norfolk. Turning south for home, she ran into Fraser's force. Duke of York's radar picked her up at long range, and at about 16:50 the British battleship opened fire, quickly straddling and hitting her. A long chase followed; a hit on a boiler room slowed Scharnhorst, and British destroyers closed in with torpedoes. Battered by gunfire and torpedo hits, she capsized and sank at about 19:45. Of her crew of 1,968, only 36 survived. Bey went down with his ship.

Aftermath

The sinking of Scharnhorst removed the last effective German surface threat to the Arctic convoys; her sister threat Tirpitz lay disabled in a Norwegian fjord. Arctic convoys thereafter ran with substantially reduced risk from surface attack.
