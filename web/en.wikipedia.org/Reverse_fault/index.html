Reverse fault

A reverse fault is a type of dip-slip fault in which the hanging wall moves upward relative to the footwall. Reverse faults occur in areas undergoing compression — that is, where the Earth's crust is being shortened horizontally. The fault plane of a reverse fault typically dips at an angle greater than 45 degrees; a low-angle reverse fault with a dip of less than 45 degrees (and especially one with a very shallow dip and large displacement) is called a thrust fault.

Because the hanging wall moves up, older rocks may be emplaced over younger ones, and strata may be repeated in a borehole drilled through the fault. Reverse and thrust faults are characteristic structures of mountain belts (orogens) such as the Himalayas, the Alps, the Andes, and the Appalachians, where tectonic compression folds and stacks the crust.

Terminology and geometry

In any inclined fault, the block of rock above the fault plane is the hanging wall and the block below is the footwall. A reverse fault shortens the crust: the hanging wall rides up and over the footwall, so a horizontal line drawn across the fault becomes shorter after displacement. The opposite motion, in which the hanging wall moves down under extension, defines a normal fault. Reverse faults can be recognized in the field by repeated stratigraphic sections and by the juxtaposition of older over younger rocks.

Thrust faults

Thrust faults are low-angle reverse faults, commonly dipping less than 30 degrees. Large thrusts can transport sheets of rock (nappes) tens or hundreds of kilometres from their origin. Systems of branching thrusts — an imbricate fan of slices, or a duplex bounded by floor and roof thrusts — thicken the crust in fold-and-thrust belts that form at convergent plate margins and in front of growing mountain ranges. The geometry of thrust systems is analyzed by balanced cross-sections, which attempt to restore strata to their undeformed state.

Formation and settings

Reverse faults form when the maximum principal stress is horizontal and the minimum principal stress vertical, as at convergent boundaries where plates collide or where one plate subducts beneath another. The great subduction-zone megathrusts — such as those offshore Chile, Japan, Sumatra, and Cascadia — are giant low-angle reverse faults. Reverse faults are also reactivated normal faults in some basins that undergo tectonic inversion.

Earthquakes

Slip on reverse faults generates earthquakes, often shallower and more widely felt for a given magnitude than normal-fault events, and subduction megathrusts produce the largest earthquakes ever recorded, such as the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile and the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake in Japan. Surface rupture on reverse faults commonly produces a fault scarp, though many reverse faults are "blind" and do not reach the surface; the 1994 Northridge earthquake in California occurred on a blind thrust.
