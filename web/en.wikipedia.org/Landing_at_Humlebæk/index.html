Landing at Humlebæk

The Landing at Humlebæk (Danish: Landgangen ved Humlebæk) took place on 3–4 August 1700 (Old Style dates vary by calendar), when a Swedish army under King Charles XII landed on the Danish island of Zealand at Humlebæk, north of Copenhagen. It was the opening move of the Great Northern War and forced Denmark–Norway out of the conflict within weeks.

Background

In 1700 Denmark–Norway, Saxony-Poland, and Russia formed a coalition against the young Charles XII of Sweden. Denmark attacked Sweden's ally Holstein-Gottorp, and Charles responded not in Holstein but with a bold amphibious stroke at the Danish capital itself, convoyed and covered by a combined Swedish, English, and Dutch fleet that had forced the Danish navy to stay in port.

The landing

Some 2,500 Swedish troops (sources vary) went ashore in boats near Humlebæk, south of Helsingør, wading ashore under covering fire from the fleet. The defending Danish force of local militia and a few hundred cavalry offered brief resistance and withdrew; Swedish casualties were very light, around a handful killed and wounded. Charles XII famously waded ashore with his men and, on hearing musket balls strike the water, remarked that this would henceforth be his music. Reinforcements brought the Swedish force on Zealand to roughly 10,000 men, threatening Copenhagen.

Outcome

Faced with the Swedish army before his capital and bombardment threats, Frederick IV of Denmark agreed to the Peace of Travendal (18 August 1700), recognising Holstein-Gottorp's sovereignty and leaving the anti-Swedish coalition. Charles then turned east against Russia, winning the Battle of Narva that November.
