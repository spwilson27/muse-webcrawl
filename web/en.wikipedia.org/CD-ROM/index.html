CD-ROM

A CD-ROM (compact disc read-only memory) is a type of read-only memory consisting of a pre-pressed optical compact disc that contains data computers can read but cannot write or erase. Developed by Philips and Sony and standardized in the 1985 "Yellow Book", CD-ROMs became the dominant physical distribution medium for software, games and multimedia during the 1990s.

Format

A standard 120 mm CD-ROM holds about 650–700 megabytes of data. Data is stored as a spiral track of pits and lands read by a low-power infrared laser; the ISO 9660 file system (later supplemented by Joliet, Rock Ridge and UDF) organized files for cross-platform reading. Discs are organized into sectors of 2,048 bytes of user data in Mode 1, with layered error detection and correction (CIRC and EDC/ECC) inherited from and extending the audio CD format.

Drive speeds were originally rated against audio playback: 1× equals 150 KB/s, and drives were marketed by multiples (2×, 4×, up to 52× and beyond). Variants included CD-ROM XA, Photo CD, CD-i and Video CD.

History and decline

The first CD-ROM drives appeared in 1985; through the late 1980s and 1990s titles such as encyclopedias (notably Microsoft Encarta), reference works and games like Myst drove adoption. Recordable CD-R and rewritable CD-RW media followed. From the 2000s, DVD, broadband downloads and USB storage progressively displaced CD-ROM, and optical drives disappeared from most new computers in the 2010s.
