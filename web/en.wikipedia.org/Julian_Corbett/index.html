Sir Julian Stafford Corbett (1854 – 1922) was a British naval historian and geostrategist who, together with the American Alfred Thayer Mahan, is regarded as a foundational theorist of naval strategy. His most famous work, Some Principles of Maritime Strategy (1911), applied the theories of Clausewitz to war at sea.

Life and career

Educated at Marlborough and Trinity College, Cambridge, Corbett was called to the bar but turned to writing, first travel books and novels, then naval history. He lectured at the Royal Naval War College, Greenwich, and became an influential adviser within the Admiralty's circle, helping draft war plans before 1914 and writing the official history of British naval operations in the First World War — a work whose frankness caused friction with the Admiralty. He was knighted in 1917.

Strategic thought

Corbett argued that command of the sea is normally in dispute and is a means to an end — control of maritime communications — rather than an end in itself. He emphasized limited war, joint operations with armies, and the political object of war, differing from Mahan's focus on decisive battle between battle fleets. His concepts of sea control and sea denial remain central to modern maritime doctrine.

Major works

Besides Some Principles of Maritime Strategy, Corbett wrote Drake and the Tudor Navy (1898), The Successors of Drake (1900), England in the Mediterranean (1904), The Campaign of Trafalgar (1910) and the first volumes of Naval Operations in the official History of the Great War.
