Fringe theatre

Experimental, low-budget theatre outside the mainstream

Fringe theatre is theatre that is experimental in style or subject matter, typically produced on low budgets in small or unconventional spaces, outside the established commercial and subsidised mainstream. The term derives from the Edinburgh Festival Fringe, founded in 1947 when eight theatre companies turned up uninvited to the first Edinburgh International Festival and performed on its "fringe".

Characteristics

Fringe productions are usually staged in black-box studios, pub rooms, found spaces or adapted venues, with minimal sets and short runs. Without the financial pressures of large houses, fringe companies can take risks with new writing, devised work, physical theatre and politically or formally challenging material. Many successful plays, comedians and companies began on the fringe before transferring to larger stages.

Fringe festivals

The Edinburgh Festival Fringe grew into the world's largest arts festival, and the model spread globally: the Adelaide Fringe, Edmonton International Fringe Festival (the oldest in North America), and hundreds of city fringes operate on an open-access basis, where any performer who secures a venue may take part. In London, "fringe" also denotes the network of small professional theatres such as the Finborough, the Bush (in its early years) and pub theatres above bars.
