Electromagnetic radiation

In physics, electromagnetic radiation (EMR) consists of waves of the electromagnetic field, which propagate through space carrying momentum and radiant energy. It includes radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays, which together form the electromagnetic spectrum. All electromagnetic radiation travels in vacuum at the speed of light, c ≈ 299,792 km/s.

Properties

An electromagnetic wave consists of oscillating electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation. It is generated by accelerating electric charges. The frequency f and wavelength λ are related by c = fλ. In quantum mechanics, EMR is also described as a stream of massless particles called photons, each carrying energy E = hf — the wave–particle duality central to modern physics. James Clerk Maxwell's equations (1865) first predicted electromagnetic waves; Heinrich Hertz experimentally generated and detected radio waves in 1887.

The spectrum

From longest to shortest wavelength: radio waves (used in broadcasting and communications), microwaves (radar, microwave ovens, satellite links), infrared (heat radiation), visible light (the narrow band the human eye detects), ultraviolet (causing sunburn), X-rays (medical imaging) and gamma rays (emitted by radioactive nuclei and cosmic sources). Radiation with photon energies above about 10 eV is ionising and can damage living tissue; lower-frequency radiation is non-ionising.

Interaction with matter

EMR can be emitted, absorbed, reflected, refracted and scattered by matter; spectroscopy exploits these interactions to identify substances and to probe everything from molecules to distant galaxies.
