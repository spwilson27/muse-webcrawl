Pearl

A pearl is a hard, glistening object produced within the soft tissue (specifically the mantle) of a living shelled mollusk, or another animal such as fossil conulariids. Just like the shell of a mollusk, a pearl is composed of calcium carbonate in minute crystalline form, deposited in concentric layers. The finest pearls display a lustrous iridescence known as nacre, or mother-of-pearl.

Pearl
Composition: Calcium carbonate (aragonite) and conchiolin
Produced by: Pearl oysters and other mollusks
Used as: Gemstone

Formation

A pearl forms when an irritant—often a parasite or a deliberately inserted nucleus in culture—becomes trapped inside the mollusk, which coats it with successive layers of nacre. Natural pearls are rare; nearly all pearls sold today are cultured.

Cultured pearls

Culture techniques, pioneered in Japan by Kokichi Mikimoto and others in the early 20th century, involve implanting a bead and mantle tissue into an oyster. Akoya, South Sea, Tahitian and freshwater pearls dominate the modern market, which is centred in China and Japan.

Value and symbolism

Pearls are judged by lustre, surface quality, shape, size and colour. Long associated with purity and wealth, they are the birthstone of June and have been prized since antiquity.
