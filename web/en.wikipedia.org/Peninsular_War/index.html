The Peninsular War (1807/1808–1814) was the military conflict fought in the Iberian Peninsula by Spain, Portugal and the United Kingdom against the invading and occupying forces of the French Empire during the Napoleonic Wars. In Spain it is known as the Spanish War of Independence (Guerra de la Independencia Española). It is famous for giving English the word "guerrilla" — the "little war" waged by Spanish irregulars against French communications — and for draining French strength so persistently that Napoleon is said to have called it his "Spanish ulcer".

Course of the war

French troops entered Spain in 1807 to invade Portugal and stayed; in 1808 Napoleon deposed the Spanish Bourbons and installed his brother Joseph Bonaparte as king, provoking the Dos de Mayo uprising in Madrid and nationwide revolt. The French surrender at Bailén (July 1808) — the first major defeat of a Napoleonic army — shocked Europe. Britain landed an expeditionary force in Portugal under Arthur Wellesley, the future Duke of Wellington. Behind the Lines of Torres Vedras, Wellington repelled Masséna's invasion of Portugal (1810–11), then advanced into Spain, winning at Salamanca (1812) and decisively at Vitoria (21 June 1813), which drove Joseph from Spain. In 1814 Allied armies crossed the Pyrenees and fought the last battles on French soil around Toulouse as Napoleon abdicated.

Significance

The war tied down several hundred thousand French troops, cost France perhaps 300,000 casualties, and demonstrated the power of popular insurgency combined with a conventional army supplied by sea power. In Spain it produced the liberal Constitution of Cádiz (1812) but also devastation, political polarisation and the weakening that hastened the independence of Spain's American colonies.
