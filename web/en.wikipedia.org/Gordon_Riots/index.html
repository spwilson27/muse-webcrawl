Gordon Riots


Newgate Prison ablaze during the riots, June 1780.

The Gordon Riots of 1780 were the most destructive urban riots in London during the eighteenth century. Lasting from 2 to 9 June 1780, they began as an anti-Catholic protest against the Papists Act 1778, which had begun to roll back penal laws against Roman Catholics, and escalated into a week of mob violence against prisons, chapels, distilleries and symbols of authority.

Background

The Papists Act 1778 relieved Catholics who took an oath of allegiance from some of the harsher penalties of earlier legislation. The Protestant Association, led by Lord George Gordon, campaigned for its repeal amid wartime fears of Catholic France and Spain in the American Revolutionary War. On 2 June 1780 Gordon led a march of some 40,000–60,000 people to Westminster to present a petition to Parliament.

The riots

When Parliament delayed debate, the crowd turned violent. Catholic chapels of foreign embassies were burned, Catholic homes and businesses attacked, and the violence spread to Newgate, the Fleet and other prisons, whose inmates were freed. The Bank of England was attacked on the night of 7 June, and the gin distillery of Thomas Langdale in Holborn was set alight, killing many who drank the burning spirits or were caught in the flames. Order was restored only when the military was deployed; soldiers killed an estimated 285 rioters, and more were later hanged after trial.

Aftermath

Some 25 rioters were executed; Lord George Gordon was tried for high treason but acquitted on the ground that he had not intended the violence. The riots delayed Catholic emancipation for a generation and left a lasting mark on British culture, most famously in Charles Dickens's novel Barnaby Rudge (1841).
