Terra nullius

Australia was claimed under the terra nullius doctrine

Terra nullius (Latin: "land belonging to no one") is a principle in international law meaning territory that has never been subject to the sovereignty of any state, or over which a sovereign has relinquished sovereignty. It was historically used to justify the acquisition of territory by occupation.

Application

European colonial powers invoked the concept to claim lands inhabited by indigenous peoples whose forms of land tenure were not recognized as sovereignty. The doctrine was most famously applied to Australia: despite Aboriginal occupation for tens of thousands of years, the continent was treated as terra nullius when Britain claimed it in 1788, on the basis that Aboriginal peoples were not considered to have a recognizable system of land ownership or government.

Overturning in Australia

In the landmark Mabo v Queensland (No 2) decision of 1992, the High Court of Australia rejected the terra nullius doctrine, holding that the Meriam people held native title to their lands and that native title had survived British acquisition of sovereignty. The ruling led to the Native Title Act 1993 and transformed Australian land law, though it did not call British sovereignty itself into question. Similar ideas were examined in the International Court of Justice's 1975 Western Sahara advisory opinion, which found that territory inhabited by organized peoples was not terra nullius.
