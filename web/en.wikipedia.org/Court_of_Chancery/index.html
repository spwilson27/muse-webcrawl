Court of Chancery

The Court of Chancery was a court of equity in England and Wales that operated from the fifteenth century until its jurisdiction was merged into the High Court of Justice by the Judicature Acts of 1873–75. Presided over by the Lord Chancellor, it developed the body of law known as equity to mitigate the rigidity of the common law.

Origins and jurisdiction

The court grew out of petitions addressed to the king and his chancellor — the "keeper of the king's conscience" — asking for justice where the common-law courts could give no remedy. Chancery developed distinctive remedies such as injunctions, specific performance, trusts, and rescission, and its procedures relied on written pleadings, discovery, and decrees rather than juries. Its classic fields included trusts, mortgages, fraud, and the administration of estates.

Delays and reform

By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the court was notorious for expense and delay, famously satirised in Charles Dickens's novel Bleak House, whose fictional suit of Jarndyce v Jarndyce consumes the lives of its parties. Reforms added vice-chancellors and streamlined procedure before the Judicature Acts fused the administration of law and equity in a single High Court, whose Chancery Division inherited the old court's work.
