Boron is a chemical element with the symbol B and atomic number 5. In its crystalline form it is a brittle, dark, lustrous metalloid; in its amorphous form it is a brown powder. It is the lightest element of group 13 of the periodic table and is electron-deficient, forming compounds with unusual three-center two-electron bonds.

Boron is not found free in nature; it occurs in more than a hundred minerals, principally borates such as borax, kernite, and ulexite, which are mined in Turkey (the world's largest producer), the United States, and South America. Elemental boron was first isolated in 1808 by Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac and Louis Jacques Thénard, and independently by Humphry Davy.

Properties and compounds

Crystalline boron is extremely hard — second only to some forms of carbon among the elements — chemically unreactive at room temperature, and a poor electrical conductor that becomes more conductive when heated. Its most important compounds include boric acid, borax (sodium borate), boron carbide (one of the hardest known materials, used in armor and abrasives), boron nitride (in its cubic form also superhard), and the borohydrides.

Applications

The largest uses of boron compounds are in borosilicate glass (such as Pyrex), detergents, ceramics, and agricultural micronutrients — boron is an essential trace element for plants. Isotope boron-10 is an efficient neutron absorber used in nuclear reactor control and in boron neutron capture therapy for cancer. Elemental boron fibers and boron-based semiconductors (as a p-type dopant) have niche technological uses.
