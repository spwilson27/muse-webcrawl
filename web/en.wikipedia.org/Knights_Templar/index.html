The Poor Knights of Christ and of the Temple of Solomon, commonly known as the Knights Templar, was a French military order of the Catholic Church founded around 1119 by the knight Hugues de Payns and a small band of companions to protect Christian pilgrims travelling to the Holy Land after the First Crusade. Headquartered in the Al-Aqsa Mosque on Jerusalem's Temple Mount — believed to stand on the site of Solomon's Temple — the order combined monastic vows of poverty, chastity, and obedience with the life of mounted knights, an innovation endorsed by the Church at the Council of Troyes in 1129.

Rise

Promoted by Bernard of Clairvaux's tract In Praise of the New Knighthood and the papal bull Omne datum optimum (1139), the Templars grew into an international institution: some 9,000 estates and commanderies across Europe, a fleet, the great fortress network of the Crusader states, and a pioneering financial system — deposits, transfers, and credit — that made them bankers to kings and pilgrims. Their white mantles with a red cross became one of the most recognisable insignia of the Crusades; they fought in most major campaigns, including Hattin (1187), La Forbie (1244), and the defence of Acre (1291).

Suppression

With the Holy Land lost, the order's purpose was questioned and its wealth envied. On Friday, 13 October 1307, agents of King Philip IV of France — deeply in the Templars' debt — arrested the French Templars on charges of heresy, idolatry, and blasphemy. Under torture many confessed; Pope Clement V, under French pressure, dissolved the order by the bull Vox in excelso in 1312, transferring most of its property to the Knights Hospitaller. Grand Master Jacques de Molay, who had retracted his confession, was burned at the stake in Paris in 1314, allegedly cursing the king and pope — both died within the year. Modern historians generally regard the charges as fabricated and the trial as a politically driven suppression.

Legacy

The Templars' dramatic fall generated enduring legends — the Holy Grail, hidden treasure, Friday the 13th superstition — and their name and cross have been revived by numerous later orders, fraternal bodies, and works of fiction.
