Cartridge (firearms)

Components of a modern cartridge: (1) bullet, (2) case, (3) propellant, (4) rim, (5) primer

A cartridge, also known as a round, is a type of pre-assembled firearm ammunition packaging a projectile (bullet, shot, or slug), a propellant substance (usually smokeless powder or black powder) and an ignition device (primer) within a metallic, paper, or plastic case that is precisely made to fit within the barrel chamber of a breechloading gun, for the practical purpose of convenient transportation, handling, and loading during shooting.

Components

  • Case: usually brass, steel, or aluminium, holding all components together.
  • Primer: a small charge of impact-sensitive explosive compound that ignites the propellant when struck by the firing pin. Cartridges are classified as centerfire or rimfire according to the primer's location.
  • Propellant: a chemical charge, today typically smokeless powder, whose rapid combustion produces the gas pressure that drives the projectile.
  • Projectile: the bullet, shot, or slug fired from the barrel.

History

Paper cartridges containing a measured powder charge and ball were in use by the later sixteenth century for muzzleloading arms. Self-contained metallic cartridges were developed in the nineteenth century: the pinfire cartridge was patented in the 1830s, followed by rimfire designs (such as the .22 Short of 1857) and the modern centerfire cartridge, which became dominant by the end of the century. The metallic cartridge enabled reliable breechloading rifles, revolvers, and later semi-automatic and automatic firearms.

Types

Cartridges are produced in an enormous variety of calibres and loadings for handguns, rifles, and shotguns (where they are usually called shells). Related types include blank cartridges (no projectile), dummy rounds for training, and shotshells containing multiple pellets.
