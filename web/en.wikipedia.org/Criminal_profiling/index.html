Criminal profiling

Criminal profiling, also known as offender profiling, is a behavioural and investigative tool intended to help investigators identify likely suspects and analyse patterns that may predict future offences. A profile is a set of inferences about the demographic, physical, and behavioural characteristics of an unknown offender, deduced from crime scene evidence, victimology, and the manner in which the offence was committed. Profiling is most commonly associated with serial murder and sexual offences.

A profiler reviewing case materials

History

Early attempts at profiling include Dr Thomas Bond's 1888 assessment of the "Jack the Ripper" murders and psychiatrist James Brussel's profile of New York's "Mad Bomber" in the 1950s. Systematic profiling began with the FBI's Behavioral Science Unit at Quantico in the 1970s, where agents such as John Douglas and Robert Ressler interviewed imprisoned serial killers and developed typologies — notably the organised/disorganised offender classification — that became the basis of the FBI approach.

Approaches

The main approaches include the FBI's crime scene analysis method; David Canter's investigative psychology, which applies statistical analysis of offender behaviour; geographic profiling, which analyses the locations of a crime series to infer the offender's likely base; and clinical approaches relying on the judgement of forensic psychologists and psychiatrists. In practice a profile may suggest an offender's age range, occupation, living situation, habits, and likely post-offence behaviour, and is used to prioritise suspects and to guide interview and media strategies.

Criticism

Profiling has been criticised for weak empirical support: several classic typologies rest on small, unrepresentative samples, and profiles are often written in general terms that could fit many people. Studies of professional profilers' accuracy have produced mixed results, and profilers were faulted for misleading investigators in some high-profile cases. Critics nevertheless acknowledge that structured behavioural advice can assist investigations when its limits are understood.

In popular culture

Profilers feature prominently in fiction, including Thomas Harris's Hannibal Lecter novels, and television series such as Criminal Minds and Mindhunter, which have greatly popularised — and often exaggerated — the practice.
