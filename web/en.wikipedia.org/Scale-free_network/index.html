Scale-free network

A scale-free network is a network whose degree distribution follows a power law, at least asymptotically. That is, the fraction P(k) of nodes in the network having k connections decays as a power of k, so a few "hub" nodes hold very many connections while most nodes hold few. Such networks lack a characteristic scale for node degree.

Defining propertyPower-law degree distribution

History

Power-law distributions in networks were noticed in citation patterns by Derek de Solla Price in the 1960s. The concept was popularised in 1999 when Albert-László Barabási and Réka Albert showed that the web's link structure follows a power law and proposed the Barabási–Albert model, in which new nodes attach preferentially to already well-connected nodes — "the rich get richer" — generating scale-free structure as the network grows.

Properties

Scale-free networks are notably robust to random node failures, because most nodes have low degree, but are vulnerable to targeted attacks on hubs. The existence of hubs also accelerates the spread of information — and of epidemics, which on such networks may lack an epidemic threshold. Whether many real networks are truly scale-free, rather than merely heavy-tailed, has been debated in statistical studies since the 2010s.

Examples

Networks reported to be approximately scale-free include the hyperlink structure of the World Wide Web, scientific citation networks, airline routes, metabolic and protein–protein interaction networks, and some social networks.
