Tuskegee University is a private, historically Black land-grant university in Tuskegee, Alabama, United States. It was founded on 4 July 1881 as the Tuskegee Normal School for Colored Teachers, with Booker T. Washington as its first principal, a post he held until his death in 1915. The campus was designated a National Historic Site in 1974.

History

Under Washington, the institute grew from classes held in a borrowed church into a major educational institution emphasising practical and vocational training alongside academics; students themselves constructed many of the early campus buildings. The agricultural scientist George Washington Carver joined the faculty in 1896 and conducted his celebrated research on peanuts, sweet potatoes and crop rotation there. During the Second World War, Tuskegee trained the first African American military aviators, the famed Tuskegee Airmen.

Academics

The university comprises several colleges and schools, including engineering, agriculture, veterinary medicine — one of the few veterinary programmes at a historically Black institution — business, nursing and arts and sciences. It is classified among research universities with a notable heritage of producing African American scientists, engineers and professionals.
