Lotharingia

Lotharingia (Latin: Lotharii regnum) was a medieval kingdom and later a region in western Europe that succeeded the Carolingian kingdom of Middle Francia. It was named after its first king, Lothair II, and stretched from the North Sea in the north to the Jura mountains and the Saône in the south, encompassing much of the modern Low Countries, western Germany, eastern France, and Switzerland.

Formation

Lotharingia arose from the Treaty of Prüm (855), which divided Middle Francia among the three sons of Emperor Lothair I. The northern share, running from Frisia down to northern Italy's doorstep along the Rhine and Meuse corridors, fell to Lothair II. Its name literally means "the kingdom of Lothair".

Contested inheritance

When Lothair II died in 869 without a legitimate heir, his uncles Charles the Bald of West Francia and Louis the German of East Francia partitioned Lotharingia by the Treaty of Meerssen (870). Thereafter the territory was repeatedly disputed between the western and eastern Frankish kingdoms. In 880 the Treaty of Ribemont awarded the whole of Lotharingia to the eastern kingdom, and in 911 the Lotharingian nobility transferred allegiance to the West Frankish king Charles the Simple, before Henry the Fowler of East Francia regained it in 925.

Later divisions

In 959 Lotharingia was divided into the duchies of Lower Lorraine and Upper Lorraine. The names Lorraine (French Lorraine, from Lotharingia) survive in the modern French region, while "Lotharingia" as a political concept has periodically been revived in discussions of a middle European buffer between France and Germany.
