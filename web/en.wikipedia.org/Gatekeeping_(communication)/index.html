Gatekeeping (communication)

Gatekeeping is the process through which information is filtered for dissemination — publication, broadcasting, or posting — within communication systems. Those who control the "gates" decide which messages reach an audience and in what form.

Origins

The concept derives from Kurt Lewin's work on channels in social life (1947). David Manning White applied it to news selection in his 1950 study of a telegraph editor, "Mr. Gates", shaping the classic model of the individual editor as gatekeeper. Later research extended the analysis to organisational routines, professional norms, and institutional forces.

Theory and levels

Shoemaker and Reese's hierarchy of influences model organises gatekeeping forces into five levels: the individual communicator, media routines, the organisation, social institutions, and the wider social system. Agenda-setting research links gatekeeping choices to the public's sense of which issues matter.

Digital era

Social media and search engines have shifted gatekeeping from human editors to algorithms and to users themselves, giving rise to concepts such as "gatewatching", in which audiences curate and circulate news rather than merely receive it.
