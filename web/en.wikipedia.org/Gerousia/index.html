Gerousia

The gerousia (Greek: γερουσια, "council of elders") was the Spartan council of elders in ancient Greece. It was one of the central institutions of the Spartan constitution, traditionally attributed to the lawgiver Lycurgus.

Composition

The gerousia consisted of 30 members: 28 men over the age of 60, known as gerontes, together with the two Spartan kings, who sat in the council regardless of age. Members were elected for life by the assembly of citizens (the apella) by acclamation — the candidates passed before the assembly, and those greeted with the loudest shouting were declared elected. Aristotle criticised this method of election as childish.

Only men of recognised merit and standing, typically from aristocratic families, were chosen. Because members had to be over 60, they were beyond the age of military service and were expected to bring experience and stability to the state.

Functions

The gerousia prepared the business to be put before the apella, deliberating proposals in advance and framing the motions on which citizens voted. It also acted as the highest criminal court in Sparta, trying the most serious cases, including those involving the kings themselves, and it could impose sentences of death, exile, or loss of civic rights.

Together with the kings and the five annually elected ephors, the gerousia formed the governing core of the Spartan state. Its lifetime membership and senior character made it a conservative force in Spartan politics.

Similar institutions

Councils of elders existed in many other Greek cities, including Crete and Carthage, and the word gerousia was later used for councils of elders in Jewish communities of the Roman world and for the governing bodies of some Greek cities under Roman rule.
