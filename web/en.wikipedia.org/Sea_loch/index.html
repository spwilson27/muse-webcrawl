Sea loch

Loch Torridon, a typical Scottish sea loch.

A sea loch is a loch (a Scottish Gaelic word for a lake or fjord-like inlet) that is open to the sea. In Scotland, sea lochs are long, narrow inlets of the sea, many of which were carved by glacial action during the last ice age and are in effect fjords. In Ireland, the equivalent term is "sea lough".

Formation

Most Scottish sea lochs occupy glacially over-deepened troughs. As glaciers advanced toward the coast they scoured out deep U-shaped valleys; when the ice retreated and sea levels rose, the lower parts of these valleys were flooded, producing inlets that may be much deeper than the sea immediately offshore. A shallow sill often marks the seaward end of the loch.

Examples

Well-known sea lochs include Loch Linnhe, Loch Fyne, Loch Torridon, Loch Etive, Loch Long and Loch Gairloch. The longest, Loch Fyne, extends some 65 kilometres (40 miles) inland. In Ireland, examples include Belfast Lough, Carlingford Lough and Lough Foyle.

Ecology and use

Sea lochs provide sheltered waters used for fish farming, particularly Atlantic salmon and mussels, as well as anchorages, ferry routes and naval facilities; the deep water of lochs on the Clyde estuary has long hosted submarine bases. Their stratified waters, with fresh water flowing out over denser salt water at depth, support distinctive ecosystems.
