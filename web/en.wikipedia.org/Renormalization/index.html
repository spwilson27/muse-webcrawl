Renormalization

Renormalization is a collection of techniques in quantum field theory, statistical field theory, and the theory of self-similar geometric structures, used to treat infinities arising in calculated quantities by altering values of these quantities to compensate for effects of their self-interactions. The procedure systematically absorbs divergences into a finite number of measurable parameters — masses and coupling constants — leaving finite, testable predictions.

History

The problem of infinities surfaced in the 1930s in calculations of the electron's self-energy. In the late 1940s, Hans Bethe's non-relativistic calculation of the Lamb shift suggested that the infinities could be absorbed into mass and charge parameters. Julian Schwinger, Richard Feynman, Sin-Itiro Tomonaga and Freeman Dyson then developed renormalized quantum electrodynamics (QED), whose predictions, such as the anomalous magnetic moment of the electron, agree with experiment to extraordinary precision.

Renormalizable and non-renormalizable theories

A theory is called renormalizable if only a finite number of counterterms are needed to absorb all divergences at every order of perturbation theory. QED, quantum chromodynamics and the electroweak theory of the Standard Model are renormalizable, a property that guided model building for decades. Kenneth Wilson's renormalization group reframed the subject: non-renormalizable interactions are understood as effective interactions suppressed by powers of a high-energy scale, so that any quantum field theory can be treated as an effective field theory valid below some cutoff.

Regularization

To manipulate divergent integrals, physicists first "regularize" them, for example with a momentum cutoff, Pauli–Villars regulators, lattice discretisation, or dimensional regularization (introduced by 't Hooft and Veltman), which continues spacetime dimension away from four. A renormalization scheme (such as minimal subtraction) then defines precisely how the divergences are removed.
