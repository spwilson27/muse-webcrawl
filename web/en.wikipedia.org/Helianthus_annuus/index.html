Helianthus annuus, the common sunflower, is a species of large annual forb of the daisy family Asteraceae, grown as a crop for its edible oily seeds and oil. It is also grown as a garden ornamental for its spectacular flower heads. Native to North America, it was domesticated by indigenous peoples several thousand years ago and was brought to Europe in the sixteenth century, becoming a major oilseed crop especially in Russia and Ukraine.

Description

Sunflower plants typically grow 1.5–3.5 metres tall, with broad, rough, hairy leaves and a thick stem. What appears to be a single flower is actually a head (capitulum) of many small florets: sterile ray florets around the rim give the impression of yellow petals, while the disc florets in the centre mature into seeds arranged in striking spiral patterns related to the Fibonacci sequence. Young flower heads exhibit heliotropism, tracking the sun across the sky — a behaviour driven by circadian rhythms in the growing stem.

Cultivation and uses

The sunflower is one of the world's most important oil crops; its seeds yield a light cooking oil, and the seeds are also eaten whole as a snack or used in bird feed. Sunflowers are used for phytoremediation, drawing contaminants such as lead and even radionuclides from soil and water — famously after the Chernobyl disaster — and the plant is the national flower of Ukraine and the state flower of Kansas.
