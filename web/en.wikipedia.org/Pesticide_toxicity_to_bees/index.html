Pesticide toxicity to bees

Pesticide toxicity to bees concerns the harmful effects of agricultural chemicals on honey bees and wild bees, which are essential pollinators of many crops and wild plants. Bees can be exposed when pesticides are sprayed on flowering crops, through systemic insecticides taken up into nectar and pollen, through contaminated water and dust, and through residues in hive materials. Acute exposure can kill foragers outright, while sublethal doses can impair navigation, learning, immunity and reproduction.

Neonicotinoids

The systemic neonicotinoid insecticides — imidacloprid, clothianidin, thiamethoxam and others — have been the focus of scientific and regulatory debate since the 1990s and have been implicated as one factor in pollinator declines alongside parasites such as the Varroa mite, habitat loss and disease. Following risk assessments by the European Food Safety Authority, the European Union restricted and then in 2018 banned outdoor uses of the three main neonicotinoids, while regulators in other countries adopted mitigation measures such as label restrictions on application during bloom.

Measuring toxicity

Toxicity is commonly expressed as an LD50 (the dose lethal to half of test bees) by contact or oral exposure, and products are classified from practically non-toxic to highly toxic. Labels carry bee-hazard warnings and instructions such as not spraying flowering crops or when bees are actively foraging.

Mitigation

Beekeepers and growers reduce losses through communication about spray timing, buffer zones, avoiding dust drift from treated seed, and integrated pest management that reduces unnecessary applications.
