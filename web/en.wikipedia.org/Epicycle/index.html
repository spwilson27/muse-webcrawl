Epicycle

In the geocentric model of the Solar System developed by ancient Greek astronomers and refined by Ptolemy, an epicycle (literally "upon the circle") was a small circle whose center moves around the circumference of a larger circle called the deferent. A planet was assumed to move around the epicycle while the epicycle's center moved along the deferent, which was centered near the Earth.

Basic elements of Ptolemaic astronomy: a planet on an epicycle whose center moves on a deferent

Purpose

Epicycles were introduced to explain the apparent retrograde motion of the planets — their periodic reversal of direction against the background stars — while preserving the ancient commitment to uniform circular motion. Adding epicycles, and in some cases epicycles upon epicycles, allowed the model to fit observations with reasonable accuracy for over a thousand years.

History

The scheme was developed by Apollonius of Perga and Hipparchus and reached its mature form in Ptolemy's Almagest (2nd century CE), which also employed the eccentric and the equant. Copernicus retained epicycles in his heliocentric model to maintain circular orbits; they were finally made unnecessary by Kepler's discovery that planetary orbits are ellipses.
