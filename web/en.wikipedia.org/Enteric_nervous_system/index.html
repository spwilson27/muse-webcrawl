Enteric nervous system

The enteric nervous system is the intrinsic nervous network of the gastrointestinal tract. Often called the “second brain,” it contains hundreds of millions of neurons and can coordinate many digestive reflexes independently while still communicating with the central nervous system.

Organization

Its major plexuses are the myenteric plexus, between muscle layers and chiefly concerned with motility, and the submucosal plexus, which regulates secretion and local blood flow. Enteric neurons use transmitters including acetylcholine, nitric oxide, serotonin, and vasoactive intestinal peptide.

Functions

The system controls peristalsis, segmentation, sphincter activity, mucosal secretion, and responses to distension or chemical stimuli. Through the vagus nerve and sympathetic pathways it participates in the gut–brain axis affecting appetite, mood, and immune activity.

Disorders

Abnormal development or injury can cause Hirschsprung disease, achalasia, gastroparesis, and functional bowel symptoms. Research also explores enteric changes in Parkinson disease and inflammatory disorders. See also autonomic nervous system and peristalsis.
