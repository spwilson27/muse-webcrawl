Battle of Waterloo

The Battle of Waterloo was fought on Sunday 18 June 1815, near Waterloo (at that time in the United Kingdom of the Netherlands, now in Belgium), marking the end of the Napoleonic Wars. A French army under Napoleon Bonaparte was defeated by two armies of the Seventh Coalition: an Anglo-allied army under the Duke of Wellington and a Prussian army under Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher. The battle ended Napoleon's rule as emperor of the French, concluded his Hundred Days return from exile, and led to his second abdication and exile to Saint Helena.

Background

Napoleon returned from Elba in March 1815 and marched into Belgium to defeat the Coalition armies separately before they could unite. On 16 June he beat the Prussians at Ligny while Ney fought Wellington to a draw at Quatre Bras. The Prussians, however, retreated north toward Wavre — remaining within supporting distance of Wellington — rather than east away from him, a decision of decisive consequence. Wellington fell back to a prepared ridge position at Mont-Saint-Jean, south of Waterloo, and stood to fight on the promise of Prussian support.

The battle

Heavy rain on 17 June left the ground sodden, delaying Napoleon's opening attack until about 11:30 a.m. so the mud would dry for his artillery and cavalry. The fighting opened with a diversion against the château of Hougoumont on Wellington's right, which consumed French infantry all day without falling. D'Erlon's infantry corps then advanced on the Allied left-centre and was shattered by the charge of the British heavy cavalry — the Scots Greys among them — though the British brigades over-pursued and were cut up in turn.

In the afternoon Marshal Ney, misreading Allied movements, launched massed cavalry charges — perhaps 9,000 horsemen — against unbroken infantry squares on the ridge; without infantry or horse artillery support they failed. The farm of La Haye Sainte in the centre fell late in the day, bringing French artillery dangerously close, but from about 4:30 p.m. Prussian corps began arriving on Napoleon's right flank at Plancenoit, forcing him to divert the Young Guard to hold it.

At about 7:30 p.m. Napoleon committed his last reserve, battalions of the Imperial Guard, up the ridge against Wellington's centre. They were repulsed by volley fire and a flank attack, and the cry "La Garde recule!" ("The Guard retreats!") spread panic through the French army. Wellington waved his hat to signal a general advance, the Prussians stormed Plancenoit, and the French army dissolved into a rout, pursued through the night by Prussian cavalry.

Aftermath

Napoleon abdicated on 22 June and surrendered to the British on 15 July; he was exiled to Saint Helena, where he died in 1821. The battle — roughly 41,000 French and 24,000 Coalition casualties in a single day — ended twenty-three years of near-continuous warfare in Europe. "Waterloo" has entered the language as a byword for decisive final defeat, and the battlefield, with its Lion's Mound, remains one of Europe's most visited historic sites.

In popular culture

The battle has been depicted in literature from Byron and Hugo — whose account in Les Misérables is famous — to the 1970 film Waterloo, and commemorated in ABBA's 1974 Eurovision-winning song of the same name.
