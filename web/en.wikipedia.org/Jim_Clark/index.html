Jim Clark

James "Jim" Clark Jr. (4 March 1936 – 7 April 1968) was a British racing driver from Scotland, widely regarded as one of the greatest drivers in the history of motorsport. Driving for Colin Chapman's Team Lotus, he won the Formula One World Championship in 1963 and 1965, the Indianapolis 500 in 1965, and set records for Grand Prix wins (25) and pole positions (33) that stood for decades. Equally masterful in saloon cars, sports cars, and Formula Two, Clark was killed in a Formula Two accident at the Hockenheimring in Germany in 1968, aged 32.

Early life and rise

Clark was born in Kilmany, Fife, and raised on the family's sheep farm at Chirnside in the Scottish Borders — he farmed throughout his career and described himself as a farmer who raced. He began competing in local rallies and hillclimbs, and with the Border Reivers team raced Jaguars, Porsches, and a Lotus Elite. His speed in a Lotus single-seater at Brands Hatch on Boxing Day 1958 impressed Colin Chapman, who signed him; Clark made his Formula One debut at the 1960 Dutch Grand Prix. At the 1961 Italian Grand Prix his Lotus collided with Wolfgang von Trips's Ferrari in an accident that killed von Trips and fourteen spectators — a trauma Clark carried quietly.

Championship years

In the revolutionary monocoque Lotus 25, Clark won seven of ten races in 1963 to take his first World Championship with maximum points under the scoring system then in force. He narrowly lost the 1962 and 1964 titles through mechanical failures in deciding races. In 1965 he achieved a season without parallel: he won six Grands Prix to secure his second championship, won the Indianapolis 500 (leading 190 of 200 laps in the rear-engined Lotus 38, the first mid-engined car to win at Indianapolis), and also won the Tasman Series and the French Formula Two championship in the same year — despite skipping the Monaco Grand Prix to race at Indianapolis. His smooth, economical style extracted speed with minimal drama, and his partnership with Chapman is considered one of the defining driver-engineer relationships in the sport.

Final seasons and death

The move to 3-litre engines in 1966 left Lotus underpowered, yet Clark won the United States Grand Prix with the BRM H16 engine and took the new Lotus 49 with the Ford-Cosworth DFV to victory on its debut at Zandvoort in 1967. He won the opening race of 1968 in South Africa — his 25th Grand Prix win, breaking Juan Manuel Fangio's record — and secured a third Tasman title. On 7 April 1968, racing a Formula Two Lotus 48 at a rain-soaked Hockenheim in the Deutschland Trophäe, his car veered off the track at high speed and struck trees; Clark died of a broken neck and skull fracture. No definitive cause was established; a deflating rear tyre was considered the most likely explanation. He was buried at Chirnside, and a museum dedicated to him stands at Duns in the Scottish Borders.

Legacy

Clark's peers — including Jackie Stewart and Juan Manuel Fangio admirers across generations — ranked him among the very finest drivers ever; in a 2009 poll of Formula One drivers conducted by Autosport he was voted among the top few of all time. His records of 25 wins and 33 poles stood until Alain Prost and Ayrton Senna surpassed them in the late 1980s, and his record of eight Grand Slam performances (pole, win, fastest lap, leading every lap) remains among the sport's benchmarks. The Jim Clark Trophy and numerous memorials, including at Hockenheim and Duns, commemorate him.
