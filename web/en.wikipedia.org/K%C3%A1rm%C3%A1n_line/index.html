Kármán line

The Kármán line is a boundary 100 kilometres (62 miles) above mean sea level that is conventionally used to delimit outer space. Named after Hungarian-American engineer and physicist Theodore von Kármán (1881–1963), it is accepted by the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale (FAI) as the boundary between Earth's atmosphere and space.

Origin of the definition

Von Kármán calculated the altitude at which the atmosphere becomes so thin that an aircraft would have to travel faster than orbital velocity to generate enough aerodynamic lift to support itself — roughly 84 kilometres. The FAI rounded the figure to 100 km, and the line became the standard for aeronautics-versus-astronautics records; flight above it qualifies a person as an astronaut under FAI rules.

Debate

The United States has historically used a 50-mile (80.5 km) boundary for awarding astronaut wings. Research based on satellite orbits has suggested a value near 80 km, and in 2018–2020 the FAI considered revising its definition, though the 100 km line remains the international standard. Legally, no treaty fixes an altitude boundary for outer space.
