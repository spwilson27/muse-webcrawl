Kavad I

Kavad I (Middle Persian: Kawād; 473 – 13 September 531) was the Sasanian King of Kings of Iran from 488 to 531, with an interruption of two years (496–499). A son of Peroz I, he was crowned by the nobles in place of his deposed and blinded uncle Balash.

At the outset of his reign, Kavad shared power with his powerful uncle Sukhra, who effectively ruled the empire. Kavad eventually had Sukhra executed and attempted to reform the empire, supporting the religious movement of the priest Mazdak, whose teachings threatened the entrenched nobility and Zoroastrian clergy. These policies led to his deposition and imprisonment in 496.

Second reign

Kavad escaped prison — legend says with the help of his wife, who smuggled him out wrapped in a carpet — and fled to the Hephthalites, whose king gave him an army. In 499 he returned and reclaimed the throne from his brother Zamasp. To repay his Hephthalite allies he was forced to pay tribute, which strained the empire's finances and led to a war with the Byzantine Empire in 502–506, during which the Sasanians captured the fortress city of Amida.

Later years and legacy

In his later reign Kavad distanced himself from the Mazdakites and allowed, or encouraged, a massacre of Mazdak and his followers around 528, clearing the way for the accession of his chosen son, Khosrow I, who completed many of the administrative reforms Kavad had begun. Kavad died in 531 during a campaign against the Byzantines. He is remembered as a bold but controversial reformer whose reign laid the groundwork for the flourishing Sasanian state of his successor.
