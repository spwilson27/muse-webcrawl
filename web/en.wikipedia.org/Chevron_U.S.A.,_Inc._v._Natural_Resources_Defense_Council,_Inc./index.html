Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984), was a landmark decision of the US Supreme Court that established the framework known as Chevron deference: courts were required to defer to a federal agency's reasonable interpretation of an ambiguous statute that the agency administers.

Background

The case concerned the Clean Air Act and the Environmental Protection Agency's 1981 adoption of the "bubble concept", which treated an entire industrial plant as a single stationary source so that firms could modify one part of a plant without triggering stringent permitting if total plant emissions did not increase. The Natural Resources Defense Council challenged the rule as contrary to the Act's pollution-control purposes; Chevron and other companies intervened on the EPA's side.

The two-step test

Writing for a unanimous Court, Justice Stevens set out a two-step analysis: first, whether Congress has directly spoken to the precise question at issue; if the statute is silent or ambiguous, second, whether the agency's answer is based on a permissible construction of the statute. Under this framework the Court upheld the bubble concept as a reasonable policy choice.

Legacy and overruling

Chevron became one of the most cited administrative-law decisions in American history, cited in tens of thousands of cases, and a central — and increasingly contested — feature of the modern administrative state. On 28 June 2024 the Supreme Court overruled it in Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, holding that courts must exercise independent judgment in interpreting statutes under the Administrative Procedure Act.
