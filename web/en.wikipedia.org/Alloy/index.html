Alloy

Alloy
CompositionMetal + other element(s)
TypesSubstitutional, interstitial
ExamplesSteel, bronze, brass, solder

An alloy is a mixture of chemical elements of which at least one is a metal. Unlike chemical compounds with fixed stoichiometry, alloys retain the characteristic properties of metals — lustre, electrical and thermal conductivity, malleability — while their properties are engineered to differ from, and usually improve on, those of their component elements. Most alloys are prepared by melting the components and cooling the mixture to form a solid solution or a mixture of phases.

Structure

In substitutional alloys, atoms of the solute replace atoms of the host metal in the crystal lattice, which works best when the atoms are of similar size (as in brass, where zinc substitutes for copper). In interstitial alloys, much smaller atoms — typically carbon, nitrogen or boron — occupy the gaps between host atoms, as carbon does in steel; even small amounts strongly impede dislocation movement and dramatically increase strength. Real engineering alloys may contain several phases, precipitates and intermetallic compounds, whose distribution is controlled by heat treatment.

Properties and design

Alloying is used to tailor strength, hardness, corrosion resistance, melting point, castability and special functional properties. Stainless steel resists rust because chromium forms a passive oxide film; aluminium alloys gain strength from precipitation hardening in aircraft structures; nickel superalloys retain strength at temperatures where ordinary metals creep, enabling jet-engine turbine blades; shape-memory alloys such as nitinol recover their shape on heating; and amalgams, solders and bearing metals exploit low melting points.

History

Alloying is ancient: the Bronze Age takes its name from bronze (copper with tin, and later other additions), worked from around 3300 BC, and brass (copper–zinc) followed in antiquity. Iron–carbon alloys — cast iron, wrought iron and steel — underpinned the Iron Age and the Industrial Revolution, and the twentieth century added systematic alloy theory, phase diagrams and thousands of standardised grades.

Common alloys

  • Steel — iron + carbon (stainless grades add chromium and nickel)
  • Bronze — copper + tin
  • Brass — copper + zinc
  • Duralumin — aluminium + copper, magnesium, manganese
  • Solder — traditionally tin + lead; modern lead-free grades use tin with silver and copper
  • Amalgam — mercury with silver, tin and other metals
  • Pewter — tin with antimony, copper and (historically) lead
