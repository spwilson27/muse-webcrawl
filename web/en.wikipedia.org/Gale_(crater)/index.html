Gale is a crater, and probable dry lake, at 5.4°S 137.8°E on Mars near the northwestern part of the Aeolis quadrangle. It is 154 km (96 mi) in diameter and estimated to be about 3.5 to 3.8 billion years old. The crater was named after Walter Frederick Gale, an amateur astronomer from Sydney, Australia, who observed Mars in the late 19th century. Mount Sharp is a mountain in the middle of Gale and rises 5.5 km (18,000 ft) high relative to the northern crater floor and 4.5 km (15,000 ft) high relative to the southern crater floor.

Overview

Gale is located on the lowland boundary of Mars in the vicinity of the dichotomy boundary separating the northern lowlands from the southern highlands. Its interior contains a massive layered mound, informally called Mount Sharp, that rises higher than the crater's southern rim. The mound is composed of sedimentary strata whose mineralogy records a long history of water on early Mars.

Exploration

On August 6, 2012, NASA's Curiosity rover, part of the Mars Science Laboratory mission, landed in Gale crater at a site named Bradbury Landing. Since then the rover has traversed the crater floor, confirmed that Gale once hosted an ancient freshwater lake, detected organic molecules in mudstone, and climbed the lower slopes of Mount Sharp, studying the transition from clay-bearing to sulfate-bearing strata.

Geology

The crater's layered sediments suggest that water pooled in Gale for long periods, forming a lake that may have persisted for millions of years. Orbital spectroscopy detected clay minerals and sulfates in the crater's strata, evidence of changing environmental conditions as Mars dried out.
