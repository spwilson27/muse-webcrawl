Martyr
Etymology: Greek mártys, "witness"
Christianity: recognized since the 2nd century
Islam: shahid
Judaism: kiddush ha-Shem

A martyr (from the Greek mártys, "witness") is someone who suffers persecution and death for advocating, renouncing, or refusing to renounce a religious belief or other cause as demanded by an external party. In the martyrdom narrative of the remembering community, this refusal to recant is typically celebrated as heroic. The term has been applied in Christianity, Islam, Judaism, and other traditions, and by extension to secular causes.

Meaning and etymology

The Greek word originally meant a witness who testified in court; in the New Testament it came to describe those who bore witness to Christ, and by the 2nd century it specifically denoted those killed for their testimony. Martyrs are distinguished from confessors, who suffer persecution but are not killed. The Roman persecutions produced numerous martyrs, beginning under Nero in 64 AD, and acts of the martyrs became a major genre of early Christian literature.

In major religions

In Christianity, Stephen is regarded as the first martyr (protomartyr), stoned to death as recorded in Acts; the Roman Catholic Church canonizes martyrs through beatification and celebrates their feast days, and Eastern Orthodoxy venerates martyrs such as St. George. In Islam, a martyr (shahid, "witness") is one who dies in the path of God; the Qur'an promises paradise to such believers, and Husayn ibn Ali, killed at Karbala in 680, is the central martyr of Shia Islam. In Judaism, martyrdom is sanctification of God's name (kiddush ha-Shem), exemplified by the Maccabean martyrs and by Rabbi Akiva under Rome. Sikhism honors martyrs (shaheeds) such as Guru Arjan and Guru Tegh Bahadur; the Baháʼí Faith commemorates the Báb and thousands of persecuted followers.

Secular and political usage

The term is widely extended to people who die for political causes—national liberation, labor rights, or resistance to tyranny—such as Jan Hus's followers, hunger strikers, and executed revolutionaries. "Martyr" narratives play a powerful role in collective memory and mobilization, and the label is often contested: one community's martyr may be another's criminal. Modern usage also includes the psychological concept of a "martyr complex," in which a person seeks suffering or persecution to gain attention or moral standing.
