Theatre of Pompey

The Theatre of Pompey (Latin: Theatrum Pompeii) was a structure in ancient Rome built by the general Pompey the Great and completed around 55 BC. It was the first permanent stone theatre in Rome, and its semicircular design served as a model for Roman theatres throughout the empire.

Design

The theatre could seat an estimated 10,000–20,000 spectators. Behind the stage building stood a large portico with gardens, the Porticus Pompei, containing the Curia of Pompey, a meeting hall for the Senate. To circumvent Roman prohibitions on permanent theatres, Pompey placed a temple to Venus Victrix atop the seating, presenting the cavea as steps to the shrine.

Historical significance

The complex is famous as the site of the assassination of Julius Caesar on the Ides of March, 44 BC, when the Senate was meeting in the Curia of Pompey because the Senate house was being rebuilt. Little of the theatre survives above ground, though its curved footprint is still traced by streets and buildings near the Campo de' Fiori. See also Pompey and Julius Caesar.
