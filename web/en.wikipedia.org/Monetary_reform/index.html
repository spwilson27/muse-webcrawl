Monetary reform

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Monetary reform is any proposal or change to a country's system of creating and controlling money, or to the institutions that regulate it. Reforms vary enormously in scope: they may be limited technical adjustments — such as changing a currency's denominations or its exchange-rate regime — or far-reaching restructurings of who is permitted to create money and on what terms.

Types of reform

Currency reform replaces one monetary unit with another, often after hyperinflation or a political transition; examples include the introduction of the Rentenmark in Germany in 1923 and Brazil's Plano Real in 1994. Regime changes alter how monetary policy is conducted — for instance, adopting inflation targeting, a currency board, dollarization, or membership of a monetary union such as the euro area.

More fundamental proposals challenge the fractional-reserve basis of commercial-bank money creation. The Chicago Plan, drafted by University of Chicago economists in 1933 during the Great Depression, proposed separating the monetary and credit functions of banking by requiring 100% reserves against demand deposits. In the 2010s, "sovereign money" campaigns in Switzerland (the 2018 Vollgeld initiative, rejected by voters) and studies by the Bank of England and the IMF revived interest in such ideas. Other strands of reform advocacy include complementary and local currencies, free banking, and the introduction of central bank digital currencies (CBDCs).

Motivations

Proponents of monetary reform typically cite financial instability, seigniorage accruing to private banks, public debt burdens, inequality, and the procyclical behaviour of credit creation as reasons to change the system. Critics of radical reform warn of transition risks, credit shortages, and the difficulty of controlling substitute forms of money-like credit once bank money creation is restricted.
