Gravimetry

Gravimetry is the measurement of the strength and spatial variation of a gravitational field. In geophysics and geodesy it usually refers to measuring the Earth's gravity field with instruments called gravimeters, in order to study the planet's shape, interior structure and distribution of mass.

Methods and units

Absolute gravimeters measure the acceleration of a falling test mass, while relative gravimeters — often based on a suspended spring — measure differences in gravity between locations. Gravity is expressed in gals or milligals (1 Gal = 1 cm/s²), or in SI units of m/s²; the Earth's surface gravity is about 9.8 m/s². Corrections must be applied for latitude, elevation, tides and the gravitational effect of surrounding terrain before anomalies can be interpreted.

Applications

Gravity anomalies reveal subsurface density contrasts, so gravimetry is used in mineral and hydrocarbon exploration, volcanology, hydrology and the study of tectonics and isostatic compensation. Satellite missions such as GRACE and GOCE have mapped the Earth's gravity field from orbit, allowing scientists to track changes in ice sheets, groundwater and ocean mass.

Historical note

Systematic gravity measurement began with pendulum observations; Richer's 1672 observation that a pendulum clock ran slower near the equator was early evidence that the Earth is an oblate spheroid. Gravimetry is also the name of a classical method of quantitative chemical analysis, in which an analyte is measured by weighing a precipitate — a distinct usage in analytical chemistry.
