Copyleft is a legal technique that uses copyright law to ensure that a work — most often software — and all modified and extended versions of it remain free. A copyleft licence grants everyone the rights to use, study, modify, and redistribute the work, on the condition that derivative works are distributed under the same licence terms, preserving those freedoms downstream. The name is a play on "copyright", and its informal symbol is a reversed copyright sign.

The copyleft symbol — a mirror image of the copyright symbol. Unlike the copyright sign, it has no legal standing of its own.

History

The concept arose in the 1970s–80s hacker culture; the word appeared in the Tiny BASIC community ("Copyleft — all wrongs reserved") around 1976. Richard Stallman formalised the idea for the GNU Project after encountering proprietary restrictions on software he wished to improve, implementing it first in the GNU Emacs General Public License (1985) and then in the GNU General Public License (GPL), first released in 1989. The Free Software Foundation describes copyleft as turning copyright "on its head": instead of restricting copying, it guarantees freedom to copy and modify.

Types of copyleft

Strong copyleft (e.g., the GPL and AGPL) requires that works incorporating the licensed code be distributed under the same licence. Weak copyleft licences, such as the GNU Lesser General Public License (LGPL) and Mozilla Public License, apply the requirement only to modifications of the licensed component itself, allowing linking into proprietary programs. The GNU Affero GPL adds a "network use" clause closing the loophole for software run as a service. In non-software contexts, the share-alike condition of Creative Commons licences (CC BY-SA) is an analogous mechanism, used for example by Wikipedia.

Debate

Supporters argue copyleft prevents free riders from enclosing commons-based work into proprietary products. Critics, including advocates of permissive licences such as BSD and MIT, call strong copyleft "viral" and argue it can deter commercial adoption. The choice between copyleft and permissive licensing remains a central strategic question in open-source projects; the Linux kernel, licensed under GPLv2, is the most prominent copyleft success.
