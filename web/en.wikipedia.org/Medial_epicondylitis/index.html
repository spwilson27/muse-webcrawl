Medial epicondylitis

Medial epicondylitis, commonly known as golfer's elbow, is a tendinopathy of the common flexor tendon that originates at the medial epicondyle of the humerus, on the inner side of the elbow. It is characterised by pain at the medial elbow that is aggravated by resisted wrist flexion and forearm pronation. Despite its nickname, most cases arise from occupational or recreational activities involving repetitive gripping and wrist loading rather than from golf.

Signs and symptoms

Pain is localised just distal to the medial epicondyle and may radiate down the forearm. Sufferers report aching when shaking hands, turning a doorknob or lifting with the palm facing down. Stiffness of the elbow and weakness of grip strength are common. The condition is considerably less frequent than its lateral counterpart, lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow).

Cause

Medial epicondylitis results from repetitive eccentric loading of the flexor–pronator muscle group, leading to microtrauma and degenerative change (tendinosis) at the tendon origin rather than true inflammation. Risk factors include racquet sports, throwing sports, weightlifting, and manual trades such as carpentry and plumbing.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical: tenderness over the medial epicondyle and pain reproduced by resisted wrist flexion or pronation. Imaging is usually unnecessary, though ultrasound or MRI may be used to exclude other causes of medial elbow pain such as ulnar neuropathy or medial collateral ligament injury.

Treatment

First-line management is conservative: activity modification, ice, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, counterforce bracing and a structured physiotherapy programme emphasising progressive eccentric strengthening. Corticosteroid injections may give short-term relief. Surgery — debridement and reattachment of the flexor origin — is reserved for cases that fail 6–12 months of conservative care.
