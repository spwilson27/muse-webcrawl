Prosthetics

In medicine, a prosthesis (plural: prostheses), or a prosthetic implant, is an artificial device that replaces a missing body part, which may be lost through physical trauma, disease, or a condition present at birth (congenital disorder). Prostheses are intended to restore the normal functions of the missing body part. Amputee rehabilitation is primarily coordinated by a physiatrist as part of an inter-disciplinary team consisting of physiatrists, prosthetists, nurses, physical therapists, and occupational therapists.

Types

Prostheses include limb prostheses — artificial arms and legs fitted after amputation — as well as artificial joints, heart valves, dental implants and crowns, artificial eyes, and cochlear implants. Limb prostheses range from simple cosmetic covers to body-powered devices operated by cables and harnesses, externally powered myoelectric hands controlled by muscle signals, and carbon-fiber running blades used by Paralympic athletes.

History

The oldest known prosthesis is a wooden toe found on an Egyptian mummy dating to around 1000 BC. Iron prosthetic hands, such as that of the German knight Götz von Berlichingen in the 16th century, allowed some function. The world wars drove large advances in prosthetics as governments funded care for injured veterans. After World War II, the myoelectric hand and the modern modular prosthetic leg were developed.

Modern developments

Current research includes osseointegration (anchoring a prosthesis directly to bone), targeted muscle reinnervation to improve control, sensory feedback, and brain–computer interfaces. Osseointegrated implants and 3D-printed, low-cost prostheses have expanded access, particularly for children.

Prosthetist

A prosthetist is a clinician trained to design, fabricate, and fit prostheses, working with the patient's residual limb to create a custom socket — the interface between body and device, widely considered the most critical part of fit and comfort.
