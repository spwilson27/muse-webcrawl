Endospore

Produced by: Certain Gram-positive bacteria, e.g. Bacillus, Clostridium

Purpose: Survival in dormant, highly resistant form

An endospore is a dormant, tough, and non-reproductive structure produced by some bacteria in the phylum Bacillota. The primary function of most endospores is to ensure the survival of a bacterium through periods of environmental stress; they are not a means of reproduction, since a single cell produces a single spore.

Structure and resistance

The endospore consists of the bacterium's DNA, ribosomes, and large amounts of dipicolinic acid, surrounded by a cortex, a resistant protein spore coat, and sometimes an outer exosporium. Dehydration of the core and the protection of DNA by small acid-soluble spore proteins make endospores extraordinarily resistant to heat, radiation, desiccation, and chemicals; they can remain viable for many years, and claims of revival after far longer periods are debated.

Formation and germination

Sporulation is triggered by nutrient limitation and involves asymmetric cell division, engulfment of the forespore, and maturation within the mother cell, which eventually lyses to release the spore. When conditions improve, the spore germinates, rapidly taking up water and resuming vegetative growth.

Medical significance

Important pathogens form endospores, including Bacillus anthracis (anthrax), Clostridium botulinum (botulism), Clostridioides difficile, and Clostridium tetani. Their resistance means that reliable sterilization requires autoclaving or equivalent methods.
