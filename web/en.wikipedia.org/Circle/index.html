Circle

A circle is a shape consisting of all points in a plane that are at a given distance from a given point, the centre. The distance between any point of the circle and the centre is called the radius. The circle has been known since before the beginning of recorded history. Natural circles are common, such as the full moon or a slice of round fruit.

Terminology

  • Centre: the point equidistant from the points on the circle.
  • Radius: a segment joining the centre to a point on the circle; also its length, usually denoted r.
  • Diameter: a segment through the centre with endpoints on the circle; its length is twice the radius, usually denoted d = 2r.
  • Circumference: the perimeter of the circle, C = 2πr.
  • Chord: a segment whose endpoints lie on the circle.
  • Arc: a connected part of the circle's curve.
  • Sector and segment: regions bounded by radii and arcs, or by a chord and an arc, respectively.
  • Tangent: a line touching the circle at exactly one point; secant: a line intersecting it at two points.

Properties

The ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter is π (pi), an irrational number approximately equal to 3.141592654. The area enclosed by a circle of radius r is A = πr2. In Cartesian coordinates, the circle with centre (a, b) and radius r satisfies (x − a)2 + (y − b)2 = r2.

The circle is the curve enclosing the largest area for a given perimeter (the isoperimetric inequality). It has the highest symmetry of any plane curve: it is invariant under all rotations about its centre and all reflections across diameters.

History

Compass-drawn circles appear in ancient art and engineering worldwide. Greek geometers studied the circle extensively; Apollonius of Perga wrote a treatise on conic sections in which the circle features prominently, and the problem of "squaring the circle" — constructing a square of equal area using only compass and straightedge — occupied mathematicians for millennia until it was proven impossible in 1882, following the proof that π is transcendental.
