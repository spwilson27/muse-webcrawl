Glucagon

Glucagon is a peptide hormone produced by the alpha cells of the pancreas. It raises the concentration of glucose and fatty acids in the bloodstream and is considered the main catabolic hormone of the body. Glucagon works in opposition to insulin: when blood glucose falls, glucagon is secreted and stimulates the liver to break down glycogen (glycogenolysis) and to synthesise new glucose by gluconeogenesis, restoring blood sugar levels.

Structure and production

Glucagon is a 29-amino-acid peptide cleaved from the precursor proglucagon, which is encoded by the GCG gene and also gives rise to the incretin hormones GLP-1 and GLP-2 in the gut. Its secretion is stimulated by hypoglycaemia, amino acids, and adrenaline, and inhibited by high glucose, insulin, and somatostatin.

Function

Binding to the glucagon receptor (a G protein-coupled receptor) on hepatocytes, glucagon activates adenylyl cyclase and cAMP-dependent protein kinase A, switching the liver from glucose storage to glucose release: glycogen breakdown and gluconeogenesis are switched on, glycolysis and glycogen synthesis are switched off, and fatty-acid oxidation and ketogenesis are promoted.

Medical use

Pharmaceutical glucagon is used to treat severe hypoglycaemia when a person with diabetes cannot safely take oral glucose, and as an antidote in beta-blocker overdose. Glucagon-secreting tumours (glucagonomas) are a rare cause of a characteristic rash and diabetes. Drugs that modulate the glucagon/GLP-1 system are central to modern treatment of type 2 diabetes and obesity.
