John Simon (10 October 1816 – 23 July 1904) was an English surgeon and pathologist who became one of the founders of public health medicine in Britain. As the first Medical Officer of Health for London and later chief medical officer to the Privy Council, he directed the sanitary reforms that transformed urban public health in the Victorian era.

Early life and career

Simon was born in London, the son of a stockbroker. He studied medicine at King's College London and St Thomas's Hospital, qualifying as a surgeon. He worked as a demonstrator of anatomy and later as a lecturer in pathology at St Thomas's, publishing research on the thymus gland and on inflammation that earned him the Astley Cooper Prize.

Public health work

In 1848 Simon was appointed the first Medical Officer of Health for the City of London. His annual reports documented the connection between filth, overcrowding and epidemic disease, and were models of epidemiological investigation. From 1855 he served as medical officer to the General Board of Health, and from 1858 to 1876 he was chief medical officer to the Privy Council, overseeing vaccination policy and investigations into outbreaks of cholera, typhoid and smallpox across the country.

Legacy

Simon's insistence that public health was a responsibility of the state helped shape the Public Health Act 1875. He was made a Knight Commander of the Order of the Bath and a Fellow of the Royal Society. His English Sanitary Institutions (1890) remains a classic history of the sanitary movement.
