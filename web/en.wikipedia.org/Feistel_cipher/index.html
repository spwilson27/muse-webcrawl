Feistel cipher

A Feistel network: each round combines one half of the block with a round function of the other half.

In cryptography, a Feistel cipher (also known as a Feistel network) is a symmetric block cipher structure used in the construction of many block ciphers. It is named after the German-born physicist and cryptographer Horst Feistel, who pioneered the design at IBM in the early 1970s; the structure was first used commercially in the Lucifer cipher and most famously in the Data Encryption Standard (DES).

Structure

A Feistel network splits the plaintext block into two halves, L and R. In each round, the right half is passed through a round function F together with a round subkey, and the result is XORed with the left half; the two halves are then swapped. After a fixed number of rounds (16 in DES), the halves are combined to form the ciphertext. The elegant property of the design is that decryption is identical in structure to encryption, using the subkeys in reverse order — and the round function F itself need not be invertible.

Examples and theory

Feistel ciphers include DES, Blowfish, Twofish, Camellia, GOST and the SIMON and SPECK families in generalised form. The Luby–Rackoff theorem (1988) showed that a Feistel network with enough rounds and cryptographically secure pseudorandom round functions yields a strong pseudorandom permutation, giving the structure a firm theoretical foundation.
