Limited animation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Limited animation is a process of making animated cartoons that does not follow the "full animation" techniques of traditional hand-drawn animation. Instead it reuses frames, moves only parts of characters, and relies on staging, dialogue, and sound to carry scenes, greatly reducing the number of drawings and therefore the cost of production. The term arose largely in television animation, where budgets were a fraction of those for theatrical shorts.

Techniques

  • Hold and cycle: characters are held static while only mouths move for dialogue; walk cycles and other repeated actions are looped.
  • Layered cels: separate cels for a moving body part (an arm, a head) are placed over a static body drawing.
  • Camera and cel movement: pans, zooms, and shaking the cel or camera impart motion to otherwise still images.
  • Stock footage and backgrounds: backgrounds and action shots are reused across episodes; "bank systems" of standard poses are maintained for principal characters.
  • Dialogue-driven scenes: scripts emphasize verbal humour, narration, and sound effects over physical action.

History

United Productions of America (UPA), founded in the 1940s by former Disney artists, pioneered a stylized, graphically flat approach to theatrical shorts (such as Gerald McBoing-Boing, 1950) that deliberately moved away from Disney realism and, incidentally, cost less. When animation moved to television in the 1950s, studios such as Hanna-Barbera industrialized these economies: series like The Huckleberry Hound Show (1958) and The Flintstones (1960) were produced for a small fraction of the cost per minute of theatrical animation, using limited techniques that became the visual signature of early TV cartoons. Cambria Productions' "Syncro-Vox" (as in Clutch Cargo, 1959) superimposed live-action lips on still drawings. Japanese television anime, beginning with Osamu Tezuka's Astro Boy (1963), adopted similar economies (often animating on threes or with extensive held frames) and developed its own aesthetic conventions that persist today.

Reception and legacy

Limited animation was long criticized by champions of full animation as cheap and stilted, but critics and historians have also praised the graphic wit of UPA's work and the writing-driven successes of Hanna-Barbera, Jay Ward (whose Rocky and Bullwinkle paired crude animation with sophisticated scripts), and later series such as South Park, which consciously adopted a cut-out limited style digitally. Digital tools (Flash/Toon Boom, puppet rigs) made limited techniques the default for much modern 2D television and web animation.
