A docudrama (or documentary drama) is a genre of television, film, radio, and theatre programming that presents dramatised re-enactments of actual events, usually with actors performing scripted dialogue based on documented facts. The term blends "documentary" and "drama". Docudramas strive for factual fidelity in setting, chronology, and the broad outline of events, while necessarily inventing dialogue and interior detail that the historical record does not preserve.

Characteristics

Docudramas typically focus on well-documented subjects: trials, disasters, wars, crimes, and political crises, as well as biographies of real people. They often use narration, archival footage, on-screen captions identifying real persons and dates, and techniques borrowed from documentary film to claim authenticity. The genre is distinct from the purely historical drama, which takes greater liberties, and from the biopic, which centres on a life story.

Controversy

Because docudramas blend fact and invention, they are frequently criticised for distorting the record, defaming living persons, or presenting speculation as fact. Broadcasters have faced lawsuits and regulatory complaints over titles such as Death of a Princess (1980) and dramatisations of recent crimes. Advocates respond that dramatisation makes history accessible and emotionally legible in ways straight documentary cannot.

Notable examples

  • Cathy Come Home (1966), Ken Loach's television play about homelessness
  • United 93 (2006), about the hijacked flight of 11 September 2001
  • The Social Network (2010), a dramatisation of the founding of Facebook
  • Chernobyl (2019), the HBO/Sky miniseries about the 1986 disaster
