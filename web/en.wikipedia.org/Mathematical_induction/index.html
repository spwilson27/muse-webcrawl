Mathematical induction

Mathematical induction is a method for proving that a statement P(n) is true for every natural number n, that is, that the infinitely many cases P(0), P(1), P(2), ... all hold. It proceeds in two steps: a base case, proving the statement for the first number, and an inductive step, proving that if the statement holds for one number it holds for the next.

The method

Informally, induction can be compared to a row of falling dominoes: if the first domino falls, and each domino knocks over the next, then all dominoes fall. Formally, to prove ∀n P(n):

1. Base case: P(0) holds.
2. Inductive step: for every k, if P(k) holds then P(k+1) holds.
Conclusion: P(n) holds for all n.

A standard example proves that 1 + 2 + ... + n = n(n+1)/2 for all n.

Variants and foundations

Strong induction assumes the statement for all values up to k to prove it for k+1. Induction follows from the well-ordering principle of the natural numbers, and is one of the Peano axioms. Early uses appear in Euclid and Bhaskara; the method was formalised by Pascal and later axiomatised by Peano and Dedekind in the 19th century.
