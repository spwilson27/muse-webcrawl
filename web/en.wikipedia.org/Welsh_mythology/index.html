Welsh mythology

Welsh mythology consists of the folk traditions and mythological tales of Wales, developed over centuries by the Welsh people and preserved in medieval Welsh manuscripts. Its best-known source is the Mabinogion, a collection of prose tales including the Four Branches of the Mabinogi, which feature the gods and heroes of Welsh legend such as Rhiannon, Pryderi, Arawn, Gwydion and Lleu Llaw Gyffes. Other traditions include the Welsh Triads, the Book of Taliesin, and an extensive body of Arthurian material, since King Arthur has Welsh origins.

Sources

Welsh myth was transmitted orally by professional poets (bards) before being written down in manuscripts of the 14th and 15th centuries, notably the White Book of Rhydderch and the Red Book of Hergest, which preserve tales composed centuries earlier. Lady Charlotte Guest's translation (1838–49) introduced the Mabinogion to a wide readership. The Welsh Triads (Trioedd Ynys Prydein) catalogue characters and episodes in groups of three and preserve many references to lost stories.

The Four Branches

The Four Branches of the Mabinogi are interrelated tales. The First Branch tells of Pwyll, prince of Dyfed, who exchanges places for a year with Arawn, king of Annwn (the Otherworld), and wins Rhiannon as his wife; their son Pryderi links all four branches. The Second Branch, Branwen Daughter of Llŷr, recounts a disastrous war between Britain and Ireland over the mistreatment of the giant-king Brân's sister. The Third Branch follows Pryderi and his mother Rhiannon through enchantment and hardship. The Fourth Branch centres on the magician Gwydion, his nephew Lleu Llaw Gyffes, and Blodeuwedd, the woman made of flowers who betrays Lleu.

Arthurian and other traditions

Arthur appears in early Welsh poems and in the tale Culhwch and Olwen, one of the earliest Arthurian narratives, long before Geoffrey of Monmouth popularised him. Welsh folklore also includes lake maidens, the Tylwyth Teg (the fair folk), the spectral Cŵn Annwn (hounds of Annwn), and saints' lives blending Christian and older motifs. Many Welsh gods correspond to deities known from Gaulish and Irish Celtic religion, such as Lleu (cf. Irish Lugh) and the goddess Dôn.
