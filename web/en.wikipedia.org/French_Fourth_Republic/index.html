The French Fourth Republic (French: Quatrième République française) was the republican government of France from 1946 to 1958, established by the constitution of 27 October 1946 after the Second World War. It closely resembled the Third Republic in structure — a powerful parliament and a weak executive — and was plagued by ministerial instability, with more than twenty governments in twelve years.

Achievements

Despite its political fragility, the Fourth Republic presided over remarkable reconstruction and modernization: the Monnet Plan and nationalizations laid the framework of the post-war welfare state and the Trente Glorieuses growth era; France became a founding member of NATO (1949), the European Coal and Steel Community (1951), and the European Economic Community under the Treaty of Rome (1957).

Colonial wars and fall

The regime was steadily weakened by decolonization conflicts: the First Indochina War ended in defeat at Dien Bien Phu in 1954 (followed by Pierre Mendès France's Geneva settlement), and the Algerian War began the same year. In May 1958, amid fears of a military coup by settlers and soldiers in Algiers, the Republic turned to Charles de Gaulle, who returned to power on condition of drafting a new constitution. The Fifth Republic took effect on 4 October 1958.
