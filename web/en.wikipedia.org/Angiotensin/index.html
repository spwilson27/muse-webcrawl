Angiotensin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Angiotensin is a peptide hormone that causes vasoconstriction and an increase in blood pressure. It is part of the renin–angiotensin system, a hormone cascade that regulates blood pressure and fluid balance in the body.

Forms

The system begins with angiotensinogen, a protein produced by the liver and released into the bloodstream. The enzyme renin, secreted by the kidneys in response to low blood pressure, cleaves angiotensinogen to form angiotensin I, a decapeptide with only mild biological activity. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), found primarily in the lungs, then removes two amino acids to produce angiotensin II, an octapeptide that is the principal active form.

Effects

Angiotensin II is a potent vasoconstrictor: it narrows blood vessels, raising blood pressure. It also stimulates the release of aldosterone from the adrenal cortex, which causes the kidneys to retain sodium and water, and it promotes the secretion of antidiuretic hormone (vasopressin). Together these actions increase blood volume and pressure. Further cleavage produces angiotensin III and angiotensin IV, which have lesser or more specialised effects, including in the brain.

Clinical significance

Because of its central role in hypertension, the renin–angiotensin system is a major drug target. ACE inhibitors block the formation of angiotensin II, while angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) prevent it from binding to its AT1 receptors. Both classes are widely used to treat high blood pressure, heart failure and kidney disease.
