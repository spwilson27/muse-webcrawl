Transient receptor potential channel

TRP channels are six-transmembrane-domain cation channels assembled as tetramers.

Transient receptor potential channels (TRP channels) are a group of ion channels found mainly on the plasma membrane of numerous animal cell types. They mediate a wide variety of sensations, including temperature, pain, taste, pressure, and vision. The family takes its name from a Drosophila mutant whose photoreceptors produced only a transient, rather than sustained, response to bright light.

Most TRP channels are non-selective cation channels permeable to calcium, sodium, and magnesium. They are composed of six transmembrane segments (S1–S6) with a pore-forming loop between S5 and S6, and assemble as tetramers.

Families

Mammalian TRP channels are divided into subfamilies by sequence homology:

  • TRPC (canonical) — activated downstream of phospholipase C signalling.
  • TRPV (vanilloid) — includes TRPV1, the capsaicin and heat receptor central to pain research.
  • TRPM (melastatin) — includes TRPM8, the cold/menthol receptor.
  • TRPA (ankyrin) — TRPA1 detects irritants such as mustard oil and tear gas compounds.
  • TRPP (polycystin) and TRPML (mucolipin) — associated with polycystic kidney disease and lysosomal function.

Sensory functions

Different TRP channels form a molecular thermometer spanning the temperature range: TRPV1 and TRPV2 open at noxious heat, TRPV3/TRPV4 at warmth, TRPM8 at cool temperatures, and TRPA1 (in some species) at noxious cold. TRPV1's sensitivity to capsaicin—the compound that makes chilli peppers hot—explains why spicy food feels "hot." David Julius's work using capsaicin to identify TRPV1 shared the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Clinical significance

Mutations in TRP channel genes cause channelopathies including familial episodic pain syndrome, mucolipidosis type IV, and polycystic kidney disease. TRP channels are major drug targets for analgesics, though TRPV1 antagonists have been limited by side effects on body temperature.
