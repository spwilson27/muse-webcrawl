Volcano

A volcano is a rupture in the crust of a planetary-mass object, such as Earth, that allows hot lava, volcanic ash, and gases to escape from a magma chamber below the surface. On Earth, volcanoes are most often found where tectonic plates diverge or converge, and most are submarine, though volcanic landforms occur on every continent. Erupting volcanoes can pose many hazards, not only near the eruption but also, through ash clouds and climate effects, across continents.

Plate tectonics and hotspots

Most of Earth's volcanoes lie along plate boundaries. At mid-ocean ridges and rift zones, plates pull apart and magma rises to form new crust. At subduction zones, one plate descends beneath another; water released from the sinking slab lowers the melting point of the mantle above it, generating magma that feeds chains of volcanoes such as the Andes and the island arcs of Japan and Indonesia. Volcanoes can also form far from plate boundaries above mantle plumes, or hotspots, as in Hawaii and Yellowstone.

Types of volcanoes

Shield volcanoes

Broad, gently sloping cones built by fluid basaltic lava flows that travel far before solidifying. Mauna Loa and Kīlauea in Hawaii are classic examples; Olympus Mons on Mars is the largest known shield volcano in the solar system.

Stratovolcanoes

Steep, conical volcanoes built of alternating layers of lava and pyroclastic material. They produce some of the most explosive eruptions; examples include Mount Fuji, Mount St. Helens, and Vesuvius.

Cinder cones

Small, steep cones of loose volcanic fragments ejected from a single vent, such as Parícutin in Mexico, which grew in a cornfield beginning in 1943.

Lava domes and calderas

Viscous lava may pile up over a vent as a dome. When a magma chamber empties and the ground collapses, a large depression called a caldera forms, as at Yellowstone and Crater Lake.

Eruptions

Eruptions range from quiet lava effusion to catastrophic explosions. Explosivity depends largely on magma viscosity and gas content; it is measured by the Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI). Eruption products include lava flows, pyroclastic flows (fast-moving clouds of hot gas and rock), lahars (volcanic mudflows), ashfall, and volcanic gases. Sustained large eruptions can inject sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere and cause a volcanic winter.

Hazards and benefits

Volcanic hazards have killed roughly a quarter of a million people since 1700. The deadliest events include Tambora (1815), Krakatoa (1883), Mount Pelée (1902), and Nevado del Ruiz (1985). Yet volcanoes also build new land, enrich soils for agriculture, concentrate mineral deposits, and provide geothermal energy exploited in Iceland, New Zealand, and elsewhere.

Monitoring

Volcanologists monitor volcanoes using seismometers (which detect magma movement), ground-deformation instruments (GPS and tiltmeters), gas sensors, and satellites. Monitoring underpins forecasts and evacuations, notably before the 1991 eruption of Pinatubo, which saved thousands of lives.

Extraterrestrial volcanoes

Volcanism is not unique to Earth. Mars hosts giant shield volcanoes; Venus shows extensive volcanic plains; Jupiter's moon Io is the most volcanically active body known, resurfaced continually by silicate eruptions; and icy moons such as Enceladus and Triton exhibit cryovolcanism, erupting water and other volatiles.
