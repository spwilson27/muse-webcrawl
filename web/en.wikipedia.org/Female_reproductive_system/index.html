The female reproductive system is the system of sex organs in females concerned with reproduction. It consists of internal organs — the ovaries, fallopian tubes, uterus, cervix, and vagina — and external structures collectively called the vulva. The system produces the female gametes (ova, or egg cells), secretes sex hormones such as estrogen and progesterone, provides the site for fertilisation, and supports the development of a fetus during pregnancy.

Internal organs

The ovaries are paired almond-shaped glands that produce ova and hormones. At birth they contain on the order of one to two million immature follicles, of which only a few hundred will mature and be released during a lifetime. In each menstrual cycle, typically one follicle matures and releases an egg during ovulation. The fallopian tubes (uterine tubes) carry the egg toward the uterus and are the usual site of fertilisation. The uterus is a hollow muscular organ whose inner lining, the endometrium, thickens each cycle in preparation for implantation; if no implantation occurs, it is shed during menstruation. The cervix is the narrow lower end of the uterus opening into the vagina, the muscular canal connecting the uterus to the outside of the body.

External organs

The vulva includes the mons pubis, the labia majora and labia minora, the clitoris, the vestibular glands, and the openings of the urethra and vagina. The breasts, though not part of the reproductive tract itself, are often grouped with the system because of their role in lactation after childbirth.

Menstrual cycle

Regulated by hormones of the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis, the menstrual cycle averages about 28 days and comprises the follicular phase, ovulation, and the luteal phase. Puberty begins with menarche, usually between ages 10 and 16, and fertility ends at menopause, typically around age 50, when ovarian function ceases.

Development

The system develops from the Müllerian (paramesonephric) ducts, which form the fallopian tubes, uterus, cervix, and upper vagina, while the ovaries arise from the gonadal ridges. In the absence of anti-Müllerian hormone and testicular androgens, these structures persist and differentiate along the female pattern.

Clinical significance

Common conditions affecting the system include polycystic ovary syndrome, endometriosis, uterine fibroids, pelvic inflammatory disease, and cancers of the ovary, cervix, and endometrium. Regular screening, including cervical cytology (Pap smear) and HPV testing, is widely recommended for early detection of cervical disease.
