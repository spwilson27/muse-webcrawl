Modern Druidry

Modern Druidry, also called Druidism or Neo-Druidry, is a modern spiritual and religious movement that draws inspiration from the Druids, the priestly class of the ancient Celtic peoples. Practitioners generally revere nature, celebrate the seasonal cycle of the year, and honour ancestors and the deities of Celtic mythology. Modern Druidry is not a direct survival of ancient practice — almost nothing is known of historical Druidic ritual, which was transmitted orally — but a modern construction developed over the last three centuries.

History

Interest in the Druids revived during the Romantic era, when figures such as William Stukeley romanticised them as patriarchal sages. Fraternal and cultural Druid orders appeared in the 18th century, including the Ancient Order of Druids (1781). In Wales, Edward Williams (Iolo Morganwg) constructed ceremonies for the Gorsedd of Bards in the 1790s. The 20th century brought explicitly religious Druidry: the Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids (OBOD), founded in 1964 by Ross Nichols, became the largest Druid organisation in the world, while the British Druid Order and, in North America, Ár nDraíocht Féin (ADF, founded 1983 by Isaac Bonewits) developed their own traditions.

Beliefs and practices

Modern Druids commonly celebrate eight seasonal festivals (the Wheel of the Year), hold ceremonies outdoors or at stone circles such as Stonehenge, and structure training in the grades of Bard, Ovate, and Druid. Beliefs vary widely: practitioners may be polytheist, animist, pantheist, or regard Druidry as a philosophy rather than a religion. Reverence for the natural world and environmental activism are recurrent themes.

Recognition

In 2010 the Charity Commission for England and Wales recognised the Druid Network as a charity promoting a religion, a milestone widely reported as the first official recognition of Druidry as a religion in Britain. Census figures count tens of thousands of adherents in the English-speaking world.
