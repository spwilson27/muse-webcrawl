Ice axe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An ice axe is a multi-purpose hiking and climbing tool used by mountaineers in both the ascent and descent of routes that involve snow, ice, or frozen conditions. It is used for balance, as a walking aid, for cutting steps, for probing snow, and—critically—for self-arrest in the event of a slide or fall.

Components

An ice axe consists of a head, typically of steel, from which extend a pointed pick and a flat adze; a shaft of wood, aluminium, or carbon fibre; and a spike at the base. A leash or lanyard may attach the axe to the climber's wrist or harness. General mountaineering axes typically have shafts 60–90 cm long, while technical ice tools are shorter and have more steeply curved picks and shafts.

History

The ice axe evolved in the 19th century from the alpenstock and the guide's axe of early Alpine climbing. Early axes had long wooden shafts; through the 20th century designs shortened and lightened. The axe used on the first ascent of Everest in 1953 was of a classic wooden-shafted pattern.

Uses

Principal uses include self-belay and self-arrest on snow slopes, step-cutting, use as a deadman or buried anchor in belays, crevasse rescue, and probing snow bridges. On steep ice, climbers use two short technical tools, one in each hand, in a technique called dry- or ice-tooling when combined with crampons.

Standards

Axes and tools are rated under UIAA and European (CE) standards, broadly divided into basic (type 1/B) axes for general mountaineering and technical (type 2/T) tools rated for higher loads.
