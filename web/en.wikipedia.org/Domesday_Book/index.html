The Domesday Book (also Doomsday Book) is a manuscript record of the "Great Survey" of much of England and parts of Wales completed in 1086 at the order of King William the Conqueror. It is the most complete surviving record of pre-industrial landholding in any European country and an unmatched source for the society and economy of medieval England: for each manor it names the holder in 1086 and in the time of Edward the Confessor (1066), and records land, ploughs, villagers, mills, livestock, and taxable value — evidence of the near-total transfer of English land to Norman hands within twenty years of the Battle of Hastings.

The survey

William ordered the inquest at his Christmas court at Gloucester in 1085, against a background of threatened Danish invasion and the need to fix the geld (land tax) and feudal obligations. Commissioners travelled in circuits through the shires, questioning sheriffs, priests, reeves, and village juries under oath. The returns were then abbreviated — apparently at Winchester — into the fair copy known as Great Domesday. The process was astonishingly fast: the whole survey was completed within about a year.

The two volumes

Great Domesday covers most counties south of the Tees in a single abbreviated volume of 413 folios. Little Domesday, covering Norfolk, Suffolk, and Essex, survives in its fuller, unabbreviated form — richer in detail, even counting farm animals — because the abbreviating process halted before it reached those returns. The far north (Northumberland, Durham, Cumberland and northern Lancashire) and some towns, including London and Winchester, were never surveyed. The strange name "Domesday" — the Book of Judgement — is first attested in the 12th century: as Richard fitz Nigel wrote, its verdicts, like those of the Last Judgement (Doomsday), admitted no appeal.

Content and significance

The survey names over 13,000 places — the earliest written record for most English villages — and some 268,984 individuals, almost all of them male heads of households, among a population perhaps approaching two million. It reveals a countryside already scarred by the Norman wars: entries for Yorkshire manors read "waste", legacy of the Harrying of the North. Legally, Domesday became the bedrock authority on land title, cited in English courts for centuries; remarkably, it remained in official use into the modern era and has never been repealed. Published in facsimile by the Ordnance Survey in the 1860s and in a standard county-by-county translation (the Phillimore edition) in the late 20th century, the whole text is now freely searchable online via the Open Domesday project.
