The Pallava dynasty was a prominent South Indian dynasty that ruled a large portion of the Deccan and northern Tamil country, known in their inscriptions as Tondaimandalam, from about 275 CE to 897 CE. From their capital at Kanchipuram, the Pallavas were patrons of learning, the arts, and temple building, and their conflicts with the Chalukyas of Vatapi shaped the politics of early medieval South India.

History

The early Pallavas issued charters in Prakrit and then Sanskrit from the 3rd to 6th centuries. The dynasty rose to major power under Simhavishnu (c. 575–600), who is credited with defeating the Kalabhras and expanding southward. His son Mahendravarman I (c. 600–630), a Jain-turned-Shaivite king, poet, and innovator of rock-cut architecture, began a long rivalry with the Western Chalukya king Pulakeshin II. Mahendravarman's son Narasimhavarman I (630–668) avenged earlier defeats, invaded the Chalukya capital Vatapi in 642, and took the title Vatapikonda; he founded the port of Mamallapuram (Mahabalipuram).

Later kings, including Rajasimha (Narasimhavarman II), builder of the Shore Temple and the Kailasanathar temple at Kanchipuram, presided over a florescence of structural temple architecture. Pallava power waned in the 9th century; the last king, Aparajita, was defeated around 897 by the Chola king Aditya I, with his Ganga allies.

Art, architecture and culture

Pallava art marks the transition from rock-cut cave temples and monolithic rathas — as at Mahabalipuram, with its great open-air relief the Descent of the Ganges — to free-standing structural stone temples that founded the Dravidian style later developed by the Cholas. The Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang visited Kanchipuram in the 7th century and described a flourishing Buddhist and Jain presence alongside Hindu temples. Sanskrit and Tamil literature both flourished at court; the Bhakti movement's early Shaiva and Vaishnava saints were Pallava contemporaries.
