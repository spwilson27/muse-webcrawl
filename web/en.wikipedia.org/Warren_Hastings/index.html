Warren Hastings

Warren Hastings (6 December 1732 – 22 August 1818) was a British colonial administrator who served as Governor of Bengal and then as the first Governor-General of India from 1774 to 1785, under the British East India Company. He laid many of the foundations of the British Empire in India, and on his return to England became the central figure in one of the most famous political trials in British history, his impeachment by Parliament, which ended in acquittal.

Career in India

Hastings arrived in Calcutta in 1750 as a clerk ("writer") of the East India Company and rose through its service, surviving the Black Hole incident's aftermath and working closely with Robert Clive. After a first return to England, he came back as a member of the Madras council and in 1772 became Governor of Bengal. He abolished the dual system of administration, brought revenue collection under direct Company control, reorganized the courts and police, and moved the treasury to Calcutta. The Regulating Act of 1773 made him the first Governor-General, though it also saddled him with a hostile council majority led by Philip Francis, with whom he fought a duel in 1780 (Francis was wounded).

Externally, Hastings steered the Company through the First Anglo-Maratha War and the Second Anglo-Mysore War against Hyder Ali, preserving British positions at great financial strain. His fundraising measures—including the affair of the Raja Chait Singh of Benares and the seizure of treasure from the Begums of Oudh—later formed the core of the charges against him. A patron of Indian learning, he supported the founding of the Asiatic Society (1784), a madrasa in Calcutta, and the first English translation of the Bhagavad Gita (1785), for which he wrote a prefatory letter.

Impeachment

Hastings resigned and returned to England in 1785. Led by Edmund Burke, with support from Richard Brinsley Sheridan and Charles James Fox, the House of Commons impeached him for "high crimes and misdemeanours" in 1787. The trial in Westminster Hall opened in February 1788 with celebrated speeches by Burke and Sheridan and became a national spectacle, turning on how Britain should govern its growing Indian empire and whether colonial officials were accountable for abuses. It dragged on for seven years; in 1795 the House of Lords acquitted Hastings on all charges. The East India Company granted him a pension, and he lived quietly at Daylesford, the estate he had repurchased, until his death in 1818.

Assessment

Historians continue to debate Hastings: an able, personally incorruptible administrator who consolidated British India and respected Indian institutions, or an agent of an extractive system that extracted wealth and used coercive methods against Indian rulers. His trial remains a landmark in debates over empire, accountability, and human rights.
