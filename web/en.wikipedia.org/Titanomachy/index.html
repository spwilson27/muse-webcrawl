In Greek mythology, the Titanomachy (Greek: Τιτανομαχία, "War of the Titans") was the ten-year series of battles between the two camps of deities that preceded the Olympian order: the Titans, based on Mount Othrys and led by Cronus, and the younger Olympians, based on Mount Olympus and led by Zeus. Zeus and his siblings waged war from Olympus against the generation of their father Cronus, who had himself overthrown his father Uranus (Sky).

The war

The fullest account is in Hesiod's Theogony. Zeus freed his siblings, whom Cronus had swallowed, and released the Cyclopes and the hundred-handed Hecatoncheires from Tartarus; in gratitude the Cyclopes forged Zeus's thunderbolt. The Hecatoncheires hurled boulders that overwhelmed the Titans. Oceanus, Themis, Mnemosyne and some other Titans did not fight against Zeus. After victory, Zeus imprisoned the defeated Titans in Tartarus, guarded by the Hecatoncheires, while Atlas was condemned to hold up the sky.

Significance and parallels

The myth explains the succession of divine rulers—Uranus, Cronus, Zeus—and the establishment of Zeus's cosmic order. It belongs to a wider eastern Mediterranean pattern of "succession myths", paralleled in the Hurrian-Hittite Kumarbi cycle and Babylonian Enuma Elish, in which a younger storm god defeats elder powers. A lost epic poem, the Titanomachy attributed to Eumelus of Corinth, treated the same theme.
