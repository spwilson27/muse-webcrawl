Ecological validity

Ecological validity, in psychology, is a form of validity concerning whether the findings of a study can be generalized to real-life settings. A study is said to have high ecological validity if its methods, materials, and setting approximate the real-world situation being investigated, so that its results can be applied to everyday life.

Definition and usage

The term was introduced by Egon Brunswik in the 1940s in connection with his concept of representative design, which held that the situations sampled in experiments should represent the situations to which results are generalized. Urie Bronfenbrenner later defined ecological validity as the extent to which the environment experienced by subjects in an investigation has the properties it is supposed or assumed to have by the investigator.

Ecological validity is often contrasted with internal validity: tightly controlled laboratory experiments may have strong internal validity but poor ecological validity, whereas naturalistic studies may generalize well at the cost of experimental control. Modern usage also distinguishes "mundane realism" — superficial resemblance between the experiment and real life — from the deeper question of whether the psychological processes engaged are the same.

Criticism

Some methodologists note that the term is used inconsistently, sometimes referring to generalizability across settings, sometimes to the realism of tasks, and sometimes to the representativeness of stimuli. Critics recommend specifying exactly which aspect of generalization is meant rather than invoking ecological validity as a single property.
