Frege–Geach Problem

The Frege–Geach problem is a problem in meta-ethics concerning the apparent inconsistency between expressivism about moral statements and the fact that such statements can appear in logically valid arguments. It takes its name from the philosopher Gottlob Frege's insights about embedded contexts and from Peter Geach, who first drew attention to the difficulty in the 1960s.

The problem

Expressivist theories hold that a moral statement such as "Stealing is wrong" does not primarily assert a fact but expresses the speaker's attitude of disapproval toward stealing. The problem arises because moral statements can occur in unasserted contexts, as parts of compound sentences. Consider the argument:

  1. If stealing is wrong, then getting your brother to steal is wrong.
  2. Stealing is wrong.
  3. Therefore, getting your brother to steal is wrong.

The argument appears valid by modus ponens. But for it to be valid, the phrase "stealing is wrong" must have the same meaning in both premises. In the second premise it is asserted and can express an attitude; in the first premise, within the antecedent of a conditional, nothing is asserted and no attitude toward stealing is expressed — one can utter the conditional while remaining neutral about stealing. If the meaning of the moral term differs between the two occurrences, the argument commits the fallacy of equivocation.

Background

The problem builds on Frege's distinction between asserted and unasserted occurrences of a sentence, sometimes called the Frege point: a thought can occur as part of a larger thought without being asserted. Geach argued that any adequate semantics for moral language must explain how moral sentences function uniformly in both contexts, something that simple expressivist accounts struggle to do.

Responses

Philosophers have proposed various responses. Simon Blackburn developed quasi-realism, aiming to earn the right to talk of moral truth and valid inference while retaining an expressivist foundation. Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism attempts to give a compositional semantics using systems of norms. Others, such as Mark Schroeder, have argued the problem generalizes to all expressivist theories and requires sophisticated machinery to solve. Critics maintain that the problem shows expressivism cannot accommodate ordinary logical reasoning about morality.
