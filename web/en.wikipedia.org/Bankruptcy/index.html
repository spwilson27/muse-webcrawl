Bankruptcy

Bankruptcy is a legal process through which people or other entities who cannot repay debts to creditors may seek relief from some or all of their debts. In most jurisdictions, bankruptcy is imposed by a court order, often initiated by the debtor. The word derives from Italian banca rotta, "broken bench", referring to the medieval custom of breaking the bench of a defaulting money-changer.

Purpose and principles

Modern bankruptcy law generally pursues two goals: giving honest debtors a "fresh start" by discharging most debts, and ensuring equitable, orderly treatment of creditors. Filing typically triggers an automatic stay halting most collection actions. A trustee may be appointed to administer the estate, liquidate non-exempt assets, or supervise a repayment plan or corporate reorganisation.

United States

In the United States, bankruptcy is governed by the federal Bankruptcy Code (Title 11 of the United States Code). Common chapters include Chapter 7 (liquidation), Chapter 11 (reorganisation, often for businesses), Chapter 12 (family farmers and fishermen) and Chapter 13 (individual repayment plans). The Bankruptcy Reform Act of 1978 modernised the framework, and the Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005 introduced a means test for Chapter 7 consumer filings. Notable corporate cases include Lehman Brothers (2008), the largest bankruptcy filing in US history, and General Motors (2009).

Other jurisdictions

In the United Kingdom, bankruptcy applies to individuals, while companies undergo liquidation, administration or company voluntary arrangements under the Insolvency Act 1986. Canada uses the Bankruptcy and Insolvency Act, and most European countries operate civil-law insolvency regimes, harmonised in part by EU regulation on cross-border insolvency proceedings.

History

Debt relief concepts appear in ancient law; the Hebrew Bible's Jubilee and Deuteronomic release, and Athenian and Roman procedures, addressed insolvent debtors. England's first bankruptcy statute dates to 1542. Early laws treated bankruptcy as a remedy for creditors and a quasi-criminal status for debtors; discharge of debts only became widely available in the nineteenth century.
