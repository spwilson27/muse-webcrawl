Sediment transport

Sediment transport is the movement of solid particles (sediment), typically due to a combination of gravity acting on the sediment and the movement of the fluid in which the sediment is entrained. Sediment transport occurs in natural systems where the particles are clays, silts, sands, gravels, or larger material, the fluid is air, water, or ice, and the force of gravity acts to move the particles along the sloping surface on which they rest. Sediment transport due to fluid motion occurs in rivers, oceans, lakes, seas, and other bodies of water due to currents and tides; transport is also caused by glaciers as they flow and on terrestrial surfaces under the influence of wind.

Modes of transport

Geologists distinguish several modes. In traction, coarse particles roll or slide along the bed. In saltation, particles bounce along the bed in short hops. Finer particles travel in suspension, held aloft by turbulence, while the finest material moves as wash load. The material carried along or near the bed is collectively the bed load; particles also move in solution as dissolved load. Aeolian (wind) transport uses similar modes and builds dunes and loess deposits.

Initiation of motion

Whether sediment moves depends on the balance between fluid shear stress on the bed and the resisting weight of the grains, classically summarized by the Shields parameter and the Hjulström curve, which relates flow velocity to erosion, transport, and deposition for different grain sizes.

Applications

Understanding sediment transport is essential to river engineering, prediction of erosion and deposition, design of canals and dams, maintenance of navigation channels by dredging, beach nourishment, and interpreting the sedimentary rock record. Classic transport-rate formulas include the Meyer-Peter and Müller equation for bed load.
