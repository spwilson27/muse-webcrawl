Colossally abundant number

In number theory, a colossally abundant number (sometimes abbreviated to CA) is a natural number that, in a particular rigorous sense, has many divisors relative to its size. The concept was introduced by Srinivasa Ramanujan in his 1915 paper on highly composite numbers, and the class generalises his notion of "superior highly composite numbers".

Definition

A natural number n is colossally abundant if there exists a real number ε > 0 such that, for every natural number k,

σ(n) / n1+ε ≥ σ(k) / k1+ε,

where σ denotes the divisor summatory function, the sum of all positive divisors of the number. In other words, n maximises σ(k)/k1+ε for some positive exponent.

Examples

The first colossally abundant numbers are 2, 6, 12, 60, 120, 360, 2520, 5040, 55440, 720720, 1441440, 4324320 and 21621600. Every colossally abundant number is also a highly abundant number and a superabundant number, and all colossally abundant numbers greater than 6 are divisible by 12.

Relation to the Riemann hypothesis

Colossally abundant numbers are closely connected with the Riemann hypothesis. In 1984, Guy Robin proved that the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the inequality σ(n) < eγ n log log n holding for all n > 5040, and the potential counterexamples to Robin's inequality, if any exist, would be found among the colossally abundant numbers.
