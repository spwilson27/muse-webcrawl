Sergeant at Arms of the United States Senate

The Sergeant at Arms of the United States Senate is the Senate's chief law enforcement and protocol officer, and one of the chamber's principal administrative officers. The office was established on 7 April 1789, when the Senate elected James Mathers, previously its doorkeeper, as the first Sergeant at Arms.

Duties

The Sergeant at Arms maintains order in the Senate chamber and is empowered to compel the attendance of absent senators — on rare occasions arresting them. The office supervises the United States Capitol Police jointly with the House Sergeant at Arms, manages the Senate's computer and telecommunications services, and oversees the Senate Page Program. As a member of the Capitol Police Board and the Capitol Guide Board, the Sergeant at Arms plays a central role in Capitol security.

Ceremonial role

The Sergeant at Arms carries the Senate's silver and ivory mace, a symbol of the office's authority dating from 1789, and leads the Senate in processions and at joint sessions. The incumbent also serves as a senior protocol officer during inaugurations and state funerals.
