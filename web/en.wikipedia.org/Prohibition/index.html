Prohibition

Prohibition is the act or practice of forbidding something by law; more specifically, the term usually refers to the banning of the manufacture, storage in barrels or bottles, transportation, sale, possession, and consumption of alcoholic beverages. The most famous example is the period in the United States from 1920 to 1933 when a nationwide constitutional ban on alcohol was imposed by the Eighteenth Amendment and enforced by the Volstead Act — an era often called "the Noble Experiment."

Origins

The American temperance movement, driven by organisations such as the Woman's Christian Temperance Union and the Anti-Saloon League and by campaigners like Carrie Nation, had pressed against alcohol for a century, blaming it for poverty, domestic violence, and political corruption. Many states and counties went "dry" locally before the First World War; wartime grain conservation and anti-German sentiment aimed at brewers helped secure the federal amendment, ratified in 1919 and effective from January 1920.

Effects

Alcohol consumption and cirrhosis deaths initially fell, but enforcement was underfunded and widely evaded. Illegal production ("bootlegging") and smuggling ("rum-running") flourished; clandestine bars called speakeasies numbered in the tens of thousands in New York alone. Organised crime syndicates — most notoriously Al Capone's Chicago Outfit — grew wealthy and violent on the liquor trade, and corruption of police and officials was pervasive. Illicit liquor was sometimes lethally adulterated. Public opinion turned against Prohibition as the Great Depression increased the appeal of legal jobs and tax revenue.

Repeal

The Twenty-first Amendment, ratified on 5 December 1933, repealed the Eighteenth — the only constitutional amendment ever repealed, and the only one ratified by state conventions rather than legislatures. Alcohol regulation devolved to the states; some remained dry for decades, Mississippi until 1966, and many counties remain dry today.

Other prohibitions

Other countries experimented with prohibition in the same period, including Canada (briefly, at the provincial level), Finland (1919–1932), Iceland, Norway, and the Russian Empire/Soviet Union. Several countries today prohibit alcohol wholly or partly, and the word "prohibition" is also used for bans on other substances, notably narcotics.
