Geoengineering

Geoengineering
Also calledClimate engineering
Main branchesSolar radiation modification; carbon dioxide removal
StatusLargely theoretical; small-scale research only

Geoengineering (or climate engineering) is the deliberate, large-scale intervention in the Earth's climate system, proposed as a possible response to anthropogenic climate change. It is usually divided into two broad categories: solar radiation modification, which aims to reflect a small fraction of sunlight back into space to cool the planet, and carbon dioxide removal, which seeks to reduce the atmospheric concentration of greenhouse gases.

Solar radiation modification

Proposed techniques include stratospheric aerosol injection — mimicking the cooling effect of large volcanic eruptions — marine cloud brightening, and space-based reflectors. These methods could reduce global temperatures quickly and relatively cheaply, but they do not address ocean acidification, they may disrupt regional precipitation, and stopping them abruptly after deployment could cause rapid "termination shock" warming.

Carbon dioxide removal

Removal approaches include afforestation and reforestation, bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS), direct air capture, enhanced weathering of silicate rocks, and ocean fertilization. Most operate slowly or at high cost, and many face limits of land, energy, or storage, though they address the root cause of warming.

Governance and debate

Geoengineering raises difficult questions of governance: who would decide on deployment, at what temperature target, and who would be liable for side effects. Critics warn of moral hazard — that the prospect of a technical fix could weaken efforts to cut emissions — while supporters argue research is prudent given the risks of severe warming. Scientific bodies such as the Royal Society and the U.S. National Academies have called for governed research programmes, and no technique has been deployed at climate-altering scale.
