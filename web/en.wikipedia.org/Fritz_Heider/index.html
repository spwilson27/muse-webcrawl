Fritz Heider (19 February 1896 – 2 January 1988) was an Austrian psychologist whose work founded the fields of attribution theory and the study of interpersonal relations within social psychology. His 1958 book The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations is one of the most influential works in the discipline.

Life

Heider studied at the University of Graz, where he was influenced by the Gestalt school and the philosopher Alexius Meinong. After work in Berlin and Hamburg he emigrated to the United States in 1930, taught at Smith College, and from 1947 spent the rest of his career at the University of Kansas.

Contributions

Heider proposed that people are "naive psychologists" who explain behaviour by attributing it either to internal dispositions or to external situations — the distinction at the heart of attribution theory. His balance theory described how people prefer consistency ("balance") among their sentiments toward other people and objects, typically modelled with triads of relations. With Marianne Simmel he produced a famous 1944 film study in which viewers spontaneously attributed motives and personalities to moving geometric shapes, demonstrating the power of social perception. Earlier, his 1926 distinction between "thing and medium" contributed to ecological perception theory.
