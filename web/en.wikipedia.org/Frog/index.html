Frog

Tailless amphibians of the order Anura

KingdomAnimalia
PhylumChordata
ClassAmphibia
OrderAnura
DiversityMore than 7,800 described species

A frog is any member of the order Anura, a diverse and largely carnivorous group of short-bodied, tailless amphibians. Frogs are found on every continent except Antarctica, from tropical rainforests to subarctic wetlands, with the greatest diversity in tropical regions. The oldest fossil "proto-frog", Triadobatrachus, dates to the Early Triassic of Madagascar, about 250 million years ago.

Characteristics

Adult frogs are characterised by long hind legs folded beneath the body, adapted for jumping and swimming; bulging eyes; a stout body without a tail; and moist, glandular skin through which they can absorb water and oxygen. Frogs range in size from the 7.7 mm Paedophryne amauensis of Papua New Guinea to the 32 cm goliath frog of west-central Africa. Many species secrete toxins through the skin, and some — such as the brightly coloured poison dart frogs — advertise this with vivid colouring.

Life cycle

Most frogs lay eggs in water, which hatch into aquatic larvae called tadpoles with gills and tails. Through metamorphosis the tadpole absorbs its tail, develops legs and lungs, and emerges as a terrestrial or semi-aquatic adult. Calls produced by males with vocal sacs are used to attract mates and are distinctive to each species.

Conservation

Frog populations have declined sharply worldwide since the 1950s, from habitat loss, pollution, climate change and the fungal disease chytridiomycosis; more than a third of species are considered threatened. Because of their permeable skin and dual aquatic–terrestrial life, frogs are regarded as sensitive indicators of environmental health.
