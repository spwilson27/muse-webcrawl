Experimental theatre

Experimental theatre (also known as avant-garde theatre) is theatre that departs from conventional dramatic forms, often rejecting realistic dialogue, linear narrative and the traditional division between performers and audience. The term covers a wide range of work produced from the late 19th century onward, much of it in opposition to the dominant commercial and naturalistic stage.

History

Although elements of experimentation can be found throughout theatre history, the modern experimental movement is usually traced to the work of Alfred Jarry, whose play Ubu Roi (1896) scandalised Paris audiences, and to the symbolist and expressionist theatres of the early 20th century. Antonin Artaud's "Theatre of Cruelty", set out in The Theatre and Its Double (1938), proposed a stage language of gesture, sound and spectacle intended to act directly on the audience's senses.

After the Second World War, experimental work flourished in small venues and fringe festivals. The Living Theatre, founded in New York in 1947 by Judith Malina and Julian Beck, became famous for participatory productions such as Paradise Now. Jerzy Grotowski's Laboratory Theatre in Poland developed a "poor theatre" stripped of scenery and technology, while Peter Brook's work in London and Paris, described in The Empty Space (1968), explored intercultural performance. Richard Schechner and the Performance Group pioneered environmental theatre, in which the performance space surrounds or encloses the spectators.

Characteristics

Experimental productions commonly abandon the well-made play structure, use improvisation or devised material, mix media such as film and recorded sound, and invite audience participation. Many companies work collectively, with the ensemble creating the piece through workshops rather than performing a pre-written script. Site-specific performance, staged in found spaces rather than theatres, is a frequent extension of these ideas.

Legacy

Techniques once considered radical — direct address, non-linear time, open staging — have been absorbed into mainstream theatre and musical productions. The fringe and festival circuits that nurtured experimental work, notably the Edinburgh Festival Fringe, remain important venues for new companies.
