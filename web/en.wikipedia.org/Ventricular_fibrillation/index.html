Ventricular fibrillation

Ventricular fibrillation (V-fib or VF) is an abnormal heart rhythm in which the ventricles of the heart quiver chaotically instead of contracting in a coordinated way, so that no effective blood is pumped. It is a cause of cardiac arrest and sudden cardiac death. Without immediate treatment it is fatal within minutes.

Signs and symptoms

A person in ventricular fibrillation collapses, becomes unresponsive, stops breathing normally, and has no palpable pulse. The electrocardiogram shows a chaotic, irregular waveform with no recognisable QRS complexes, P waves or T waves.

Causes

VF most often occurs in the setting of coronary heart disease, particularly during or after a heart attack (myocardial infarction). Other causes include cardiomyopathies, valvular heart disease, inherited arrhythmia syndromes such as long QT syndrome and Brugada syndrome, severe electrolyte abnormalities (especially of potassium and magnesium), drug toxicity, and electrocution.

Treatment

Ventricular fibrillation is treated as cardiac arrest: immediate cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and defibrillation with an automated external defibrillator (AED) or manual defibrillator. VF is a "shockable" rhythm, and survival decreases by roughly 10% for each minute defibrillation is delayed. Advanced life support may include adrenaline (epinephrine) and antiarrhythmic drugs such as amiodarone. Survivors are usually investigated for the underlying cause, and many receive an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) to treat future episodes.

Epidemiology

Ventricular fibrillation is the initial recorded rhythm in a substantial proportion of out-of-hospital cardiac arrests and is the most common mechanism of sudden cardiac death in adults, which kills hundreds of thousands of people annually in the United States alone.
