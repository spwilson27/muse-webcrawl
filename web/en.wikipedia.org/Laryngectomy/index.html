Laryngectomy

Laryngectomy is the removal of the larynx, usually performed as a treatment for laryngeal cancer. In a total laryngectomy, the entire larynx (including the vocal cords, epiglottis, hyoid bone, and upper tracheal rings) is removed and the airway is separated from the mouth, nose, and esophagus; the patient breathes thereafter through a stoma (tracheostomy opening) in the neck. Partial laryngectomies remove only part of the larynx and may preserve some voice function.

Indications and procedure

The operation is most often performed for squamous cell carcinoma of the larynx that is advanced or has failed radiation or chemoradiation. The first successful total laryngectomy was performed by Theodor Billroth in Vienna on 31 December 1873. During total laryngectomy the trachea is brought out to the skin of the neck as a permanent stoma, and the pharynx is reconstructed so the patient can still swallow normally.

Speech rehabilitation

Loss of the larynx means loss of the natural voice. Speech can be restored by three main methods: esophageal speech, in which swallowed air is used to vibrate the pharyngoesophageal segment; an electrolarynx, a hand-held vibrating device held against the neck; and a tracheoesophageal voice prosthesis (such as the Blom–Singer valve), a one-way valve placed in a puncture between the trachea and esophagus that shunts air to produce vibration. Modern prosthetic speech achieves fluent speech for many patients.
