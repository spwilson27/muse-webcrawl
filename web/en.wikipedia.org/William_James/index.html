William James

William James (11 January 1842 – 26 August 1910) was an American philosopher and psychologist, one of the founders of the philosophical school of pragmatism and of functional psychology. His works include The Principles of Psychology (1890), The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902) and Pragmatism (1907).

Life

James was the son of the theologian Henry James Sr. and elder brother of novelist Henry James. Educated in Europe and America, he studied painting, chemistry and finally medicine at Harvard, accompanying Louis Agassiz on an expedition to Brazil. After years of depression and ill health, he joined Harvard's faculty in 1872, teaching physiology, then psychology and philosophy, and founded one of the first American psychology laboratories.

Psychology

The Principles of Psychology (1890), twelve years in the writing, surveyed consciousness — famously describing it as a "stream" — habit, attention, emotion and the self. With Carl Lange he proposed the James–Lange theory, that emotion is the perception of bodily changes. His functionalism emphasised what mental processes do, in contrast to structuralism's analysis of their elements.

Philosophy

James developed pragmatism, following Charles Sanders Peirce: the truth of an idea lies in its practical consequences and "cash value". In The Will to Believe (1897) he defended the permissibility of belief where evidence is inconclusive, and in The Varieties of Religious Experience he studied religious life psychologically. His later "radical empiricism" held that relations themselves are part of experience.
