Incentive compatibility

Incentive compatibility is a concept in mechanism design and game theory: a mechanism is incentive-compatible if every participant can achieve their best outcome by acting according to their true preferences, for example by truthfully reporting private information. A strongly incentive-compatible mechanism is one where truth-telling is a dominant strategy (dominant-strategy incentive compatibility, DSIC); a weaker notion requires truth-telling only as a Bayesian Nash equilibrium (Bayesian incentive compatibility, BIC).

In an incentive-compatible mechanism, honesty is each participant's best strategy.

Examples

The classic example is the second-price (Vickrey) auction, in which the highest bidder wins but pays the second-highest bid; bidding one's true value is a weakly dominant strategy. The revelation principle states that for any mechanism there exists an equivalent direct mechanism in which truth-telling is an equilibrium. Mechanism designers such as Leonid Hurwicz, Eric Maskin, and Roger Myerson — who shared the 2007 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics — developed the theory of incentive-compatible design.

Notation and types

Incentive compatibility constraints appear throughout contract theory, auction theory, and voting theory. Ex post incentive compatibility requires no regret after learning others' types, while interim and ex ante versions impose the condition at earlier information stages. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem shows limits on strategy-proof voting rules with three or more outcomes.
