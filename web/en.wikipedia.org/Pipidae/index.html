FamilyPipidae Gray, 1825

The Pipidae are a family of primitive, tongueless frogs. The family includes about 41 species in four or five genera, distributed in tropical South America (genus Pipa) and sub-Saharan Africa (the remaining genera). These frogs are highly aquatic and have strongly flattened bodies; as their common description implies, pipids lack tongues entirely, an unusual trait among frogs.

Characteristics

Pipids have a number of anatomical peculiarities associated with a fully aquatic life. The body is dorsoventrally flattened, the eyes are small and positioned on top of the head, and external ears are absent. The hind feet are large and fully webbed, and in the African clawed frogs (Xenopus) the first three toes bear horny claws. Unlike almost all other frogs, pipids possess a lateral line system as adults, similar to that of fishes, allowing them to detect vibrations in the water. Because they have no tongue, they feed by suction and by shovelling prey into the mouth with their forelimbs.

Reproduction

The Surinam toad (Pipa pipa) has one of the most extraordinary reproductive strategies among vertebrates: during mating, the female releases eggs which the male presses into the spongy skin of her back, where each egg becomes embedded in its own pocket. The young develop there, bypassing a free-swimming tadpole stage, and eventually emerge from the mother's back as fully formed froglets. In Xenopus, by contrast, eggs are laid in water and hatch into filter-feeding tadpoles.

Genera

The living genera generally recognised include Xenopus and Silurana (African clawed frogs), Hymenochirus (African dwarf frogs), Pseudhymenochirus, and the South American Pipa. The African clawed frog Xenopus laevis is one of the most widely used model organisms in developmental biology and was historically used in the first widely available pregnancy tests. Fossil pipids are known from Cretaceous and Cenozoic deposits on both sides of the Atlantic, reflecting the family's ancient Gondwanan distribution.
