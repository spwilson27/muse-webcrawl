Inflammation


Redness and swelling are cardinal signs of inflammation.

Inflammation is a complex biological response of the immune system to harmful stimuli such as pathogens, damaged cells or irritants. It is a protective attempt to remove the injurious stimulus and initiate healing. The classical signs, described since antiquity, are heat, pain, redness, swelling and loss of function (calor, dolor, rubor, tumor, functio laesa). Inflammation is distinct from infarction, which is tissue death from loss of blood supply; an infarct may trigger inflammation, but inflammation itself is a living tissue response.

Acute inflammation

Acute inflammation is the immediate, short-term response, lasting minutes to days. Blood vessels dilate and become more permeable, plasma proteins and fluid exude into the tissue, and white blood cells — principally neutrophils — migrate from the blood to the site of injury, guided by chemical mediators such as histamine, prostaglandins and cytokines. These cells phagocytose microbes and debris. Acute inflammation normally resolves completely once the stimulus is removed.

Chronic inflammation

When the stimulus persists, inflammation becomes chronic, lasting months or years and dominated by macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells, with simultaneous tissue destruction and attempted repair by fibrosis. Chronic inflammation underlies many diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, asthma, inflammatory bowel disease and atherosclerosis, and systemic low-grade inflammation is implicated in metabolic and cardiovascular disorders.

Treatment

Inflammation is treated by removing its cause and with anti-inflammatory drugs, including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroids and targeted biologic agents that block specific cytokines such as tumour necrosis factor.
