Stoma

In botany, a stoma (from Greek στόμα, "mouth", plural stomata) is a pore found in the epidermis of leaves, stems, and other organs, that controls the rate of gas exchange. The pore is bordered by a pair of specialized parenchyma cells known as guard cells that are responsible for regulating the size of the stomatal opening.

The term is also used collectively to refer to the entire stomatal complex, which consists of the guard cells and the pore itself. Carbon dioxide and water vapor enter and exit through stomata; oxygen, which is produced as a byproduct of photosynthesis, also leaves the plant through them. Stomata are present in the sporophyte generation of all land plant groups except liverworts.

Function

Stomata serve two essential and conflicting functions. They allow carbon dioxide to diffuse into the leaf for photosynthesis, but every open stoma also allows water vapour to escape by transpiration. Plants therefore regulate stomatal aperture continuously, balancing carbon gain against water loss. The opening and closing of stomata is driven by changes in turgor pressure within the guard cells: when guard cells take up potassium ions and water, they swell and bow apart, opening the pore; when they lose ions and water, they become flaccid and the pore closes.

Environmental responses

Most plants open their stomata during the day, when light drives photosynthesis, and close them at night. Plants using CAM photosynthesis, such as cacti and pineapples, invert this pattern, opening their stomata at night to reduce water loss in hot, arid environments. Drought stress triggers the hormone abscisic acid (ABA), which causes guard cells to close the stomata. Stomatal density and size also respond over developmental timescales to atmospheric carbon dioxide levels, a relationship used by palaeobotanists to estimate past CO2 concentrations from fossil leaves.

Types

Stomatal complexes are classified by the arrangement of the subsidiary cells surrounding the guard cells. Common types include anomocytic (no distinct subsidiary cells), anisocytic, paracytic, diacytic, actinocytic and cyclocytic. Dicotyledons typically have kidney-shaped guard cells, whereas grasses have dumbbell-shaped guard cells that can open and close especially rapidly.
