The Sena dynasty was a Hindu dynasty that ruled Bengal through the 11th and 12th centuries. The empire at its peak covered much of the modern-day region of Bengal and parts of present-day Bangladesh and the Indian state of West Bengal. The Senas succeeded the Buddhist Pala dynasty as rulers of Bengal.

Origins

The founder of the dynasty was Samanta Sena, said in the Deopara inscription to have descended from a family of Karnataka origin — the Senas traced their ancestry to the Deccan and described themselves as Brahmakshatriyas or Karnata-Kshatriyas. His son Hemanta Sena seized power during the decline of Pala authority around the end of the 11th century.

Major rulers

Vijaya Sena (reigned c. 1095–1158) consolidated Sena power, uniting much of Bengal under one rule during his long reign. His son Ballala Sena (c. 1158–1179) extended the kingdom, is credited with reorganising the caste system in Bengal and with works such as the Danasagara and Adbhutasagara. Lakshmana Sena (c. 1178–1206), the last notable ruler, expanded Sena territory into Assam, Odisha, Bihar and perhaps Varanasi; his court patronised Sanskrit literature, including the poet Jayadeva, author of the Gita Govinda.

Decline

In 1204–1205, the Turkic forces of Muhammad Bakhtiyar Khalji captured the Sena capital at Nadia (Navadwip), reportedly with a small advance party that took the elderly Lakshmana Sena by surprise. The king fled to eastern Bengal, where his descendants continued to rule for some decades as regional power passed to the Delhi Sultanate in the west.

Legacy

The Sena period is remembered for a revival of orthodox Hinduism in Bengal, the codification of social customs attributed to Ballala Sena, and a flourishing of Sanskrit court literature. Sena inscriptions, issued in Sanskrit and recorded on copper plates and stone, are important sources for the social and administrative history of early medieval Bengal.
