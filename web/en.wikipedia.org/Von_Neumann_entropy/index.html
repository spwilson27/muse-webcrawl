Von Neumann entropy

In physics, the von Neumann entropy, named after John von Neumann, is the extension of the classical Gibbs entropy to quantum mechanics. For a quantum system described by a density matrix ρ, it is defined as S(ρ) = −Tr(ρ log ρ), or equivalently as the negative sum of the eigenvalues of ρ times their logarithms. When the state is a classical probability distribution over orthogonal states, the von Neumann entropy reduces to the Shannon entropy of that distribution.

The von Neumann entropy measures the mixedness or uncertainty of a quantum state.

Properties

The von Neumann entropy is zero for a pure state and reaches its maximum, log d for a d-dimensional system, for the maximally mixed state. It is invariant under unitary transformations, so it does not change under the reversible evolution of an isolated system. It is also subadditive and strongly subadditive — the latter, proved by Elliott Lieb and Mary Beth Ruskai in 1973, is one of the deepest basic results in quantum information theory and underlies many capacity theorems.

Applications

Von Neumann entropy quantifies entanglement in pure bipartite states (as the entropy of either reduced density matrix), bounds the efficiency of quantum data compression in Schumacher compression, and appears in quantum channel capacities. The difference between the von Neumann entropy of a joint state and the sum of the marginal entropies defines the quantum mutual information. Conditional von Neumann entropy, unlike its classical counterpart, can be negative for entangled states — a signature of quantum correlations.
