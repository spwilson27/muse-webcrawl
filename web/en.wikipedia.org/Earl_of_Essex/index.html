Earl of Essex is a title in the Peerage of England that has been created several times, taking its name from the historic county of Essex. The current creation dates from 1661 and is held by the Capell family.

Earlier creations

The title was first created around 1139 for Geoffrey de Mandeville by King Stephen (the grant was also recognised by the Empress Matilda), and was forfeited when his son was involved in rebellion. Later creations were held by the Bohun family (from 1239), by Thomas Cromwell briefly in 1540, by William Parr (1543), and then by the Devereux family from 1572.

The Devereux earls

The most famous holder was Robert Devereux, 2nd Earl of Essex (1565–1601), the dashing favourite of Elizabeth I, commander at Cádiz (1596) and Lord Lieutenant of Ireland, whose failed expedition against Tyrone's rebellion and subsequent impulsive coup attempt in London in February 1601 led to his trial and execution for treason. His son, Robert Devereux, 3rd Earl, restored to the title in 1604, became the first commander-in-chief of the Parliamentarian army in the English Civil War, leading it at Edgehill (1642) before resigning under the Self-denying Ordinance in 1645. On his death in 1646 the Devereux creation became extinct.

Capell creation

The title was created again in 1661 for Arthur Capell, 1st Earl of Essex (1631–1683), a Restoration statesman who served as Lord Lieutenant of Ireland and First Lord of the Treasury, and who died in the Tower of London while imprisoned on suspicion connected with the Rye House Plot. His descendants built Cassiobury House in Hertfordshire into a great seat; the earldom continues in the Capell family, whose heirs use the courtesy title Viscount Malden.
