Subtractive color

Subtractive color is a model for predicting the mixed result of colored pigments and colorants. Unlike additive color, which starts with darkness and adds light, the subtractive model starts with light and subtracts wavelengths by absorption. It forms the basis of color printing, painting, and most traditional visual arts media.

Principles

When white light strikes a mixture of pigments, each pigment absorbs (subtracts) some portion of the spectrum and reflects the rest. The color perceived is determined by the wavelengths that survive this process. The ideal subtractive primaries are cyan, magenta, and yellow — the complements of red, green, and blue respectively. Cyan absorbs red light, magenta absorbs green, and yellow absorbs blue.

CMYK printing

In modern color printing, cyan, magenta, and yellow are combined with a key (black) ink, giving the CMYK color model. Black ink is added because real pigments cannot produce a true black when combined, and because black ink is cheaper and dries faster than large amounts of colored ink.

Historical development

Subtractive mixing was described by artists and printers long before its physics was formalized. The traditional red-yellow-blue wheel used in art education is a rough approximation of the subtractive system, though cyan and magenta give a wider and more accurate gamut of mixtures.
