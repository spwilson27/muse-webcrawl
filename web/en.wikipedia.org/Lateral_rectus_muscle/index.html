Lateral rectus muscle

The lateral rectus muscle is one of the six extraocular muscles that control the movements of the eye. It is located on the lateral (temporal) side of the eyeball and is responsible for abduction — moving the eye outward, away from the nose. It is the only extraocular muscle innervated by the abducens nerve (cranial nerve VI).

Structure

The lateral rectus originates from the common tendinous ring (annulus of Zinn) at the apex of the orbit, with an additional smaller head from the spine of the greater wing of the sphenoid bone. It runs forward along the lateral wall of the orbit and inserts into the sclera of the eyeball about 7 mm behind the limbus, on the temporal side. It is the longest of the rectus muscles and is supplied by branches of the ophthalmic artery.

Function

Contraction of the lateral rectus rotates the eyeball laterally (abduction). It works in coordination with the medial rectus of the same eye, which relaxes as the lateral rectus contracts, and with the corresponding muscles of the other eye during horizontal gaze.

Clinical significance

Damage to the abducens nerve — which has the longest intracranial course of any cranial nerve and is vulnerable to raised intracranial pressure, trauma, and vascular disease — causes lateral rectus palsy. The affected eye cannot abduct fully and turns inward (esotropia), producing horizontal double vision that worsens when looking toward the affected side. Weakness of the lateral rectus is also a feature of Duane syndrome and Möbius syndrome.
