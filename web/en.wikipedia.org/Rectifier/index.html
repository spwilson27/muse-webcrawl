Rectifier

A bridge rectifier converts alternating current to pulsating direct current.

A rectifier is an electrical device that converts alternating current (AC), which periodically reverses direction, to direct current (DC), which flows in only one direction. The reverse operation is performed by an inverter. Rectification is most commonly achieved with diodes, which allow current to flow in only one direction, and rectifier circuits are found in virtually every power supply for electronic equipment, in battery chargers, in welding equipment, and in high-voltage direct current power transmission.

Types of rectifier circuit

A half-wave rectifier uses a single diode and passes only one half of the AC cycle, wasting the other. A full-wave rectifier converts both halves of the input to the same polarity: either with a centre-tapped transformer and two diodes, or with four diodes in a bridge rectifier, which needs no centre tap and is the standard arrangement in small power supplies. The output is a pulsating DC which must be smoothed, usually with a reservoir capacitor, and then often regulated to a constant voltage. Three-phase supplies use six-diode bridges giving much lower ripple.

Devices

Early rectifiers included vacuum tube diodes, mercury-arc rectifiers — used for traction and industrial power into the mid-twentieth century — and copper-oxide and selenium rectifiers. Semiconductor diodes, first germanium and then silicon, displaced all of these for most purposes. For controlled rectification, as in DC motor drives and HVDC converter stations, thyristors allow the output to be varied by delaying the firing angle, while modern switch-mode supplies use power transistors switched at high frequency, followed by fast diodes or synchronous rectification with MOSFETs.

Applications

Rectifiers charge the batteries of vehicles (the alternator contains a rectifier pack), power electroplating and aluminium smelting, drive DC railway electrification, and form the front end of consumer electronics. Radio receivers historically used a diode as a rectifier — a detector — to demodulate amplitude-modulated signals.
