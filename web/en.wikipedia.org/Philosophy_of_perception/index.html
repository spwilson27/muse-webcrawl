The philosophy of perception is the study of the nature of perceptual experience and the status of perceptual data, particularly how they relate to beliefs and to knowledge of the world. Its central question is how our experience of a mind-independent world is possible, and whether perception puts us in direct contact with that world or only with inner representations of it.

Theories of perception

Direct (naïve) realism holds that in veridical perception we are directly aware of external objects and their properties. Sense-data theories, defended by Bertrand Russell and others, argue from illusion and hallucination that what we are immediately aware of are private mental items—sense data—from which the external world is inferred. Representationalism (intentionalism) treats experience as a kind of representation of the world, formed by causal processes; adverbialism instead analyzes sensing as a modification of the subject. Disjunctivism denies that veridical perception and hallucination share a common mental core. Enactive approaches, following J. J. Gibson's ecological psychology, emphasize skilled bodily interaction with the environment.

The argument from illusion

A stick half-submerged in water looks bent; a coin seen at an angle looks elliptical. Indirect realists argue that since something is genuinely perceived to be bent or elliptical, and the object is not, the immediate object of awareness must be mental. Direct realists reply that such cases show only that objects can appear differently under different conditions, not that we perceive intermediaries.

Perception and knowledge

Perception is the paradigm source of empirical knowledge, so theories of perception bear directly on epistemology and on skepticism about the external world. Contemporary work connects the debates to vision science, Bayesian models of inference, and predictive processing.
