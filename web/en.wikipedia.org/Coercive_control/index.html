Coercive control

Coercive control is a pattern of behaviour used by one person to dominate and restrict the freedom of another, most often described in the context of intimate partner relationships. The term was developed and popularised by sociologist Evan Stark, whose 2007 book Coercive Control framed such abuse as a liberty crime — an ongoing pattern of intimidation, isolation, and control rather than a series of discrete violent incidents.

Characteristics

Coercive control can include monitoring a partner's movements and communications, controlling finances, isolating them from friends and family, dictating what they wear or eat, threats, degradation, and micromanagement of everyday life. Physical violence may be present but is not necessary; the harm arises from the cumulative restriction of the victim's autonomy and sense of safety.

Legal responses

Several jurisdictions have created specific offences of coercive or controlling behaviour. England and Wales criminalised controlling or coercive behaviour in an intimate or family relationship in the Serious Crime Act 2015, with similar laws in Scotland (Domestic Abuse (Scotland) Act 2018) and Ireland, and in some Australian states and other jurisdictions. Enforcement and the identification of patterns of behaviour, rather than single incidents, remain practical challenges for police and courts.
