Freeway

A freeway is a type of controlled-access highway designed for high-speed vehicular traffic, with all traffic flow and ingress and egress regulated. Freeways have no direct access from adjacent properties, no at-grade intersections or traffic signals, and opposing traffic separated by a median or barrier; entry and exit occur only at interchanges via ramps. Pedestrians, bicycles, and slow vehicles are generally prohibited. The term is used mainly in the United States, Canada, and Australia; equivalent terms include motorway in the United Kingdom, autobahn in Germany, and expressway in parts of Asia.

Characteristics

Freeways are distinguished from expressways in North American usage by complete access control: an expressway may have partial access control with occasional at-grade intersections, while a freeway has none. Design standards specify wide lanes (typically 3.6 m / 12 ft), paved shoulders, gentle grades and curves for design speeds of 90–130 km/h (55–80 mph), and full grade separation at interchanges such as stack, cloverleaf, and diamond configurations.

History

Precursors included parkways in New York and Germany's AVUS (1921), followed by Italy's autostradas and Germany's autobahn network in the 1930s. In the United States, the Pennsylvania Turnpike (1940) demonstrated the modern design, and the Interstate Highway System, authorized in 1956, built more than 75,000 km (46,876 miles) of freeways, transforming American transport and urban form. Freeway construction also provoked "freeway revolts" in cities such as San Francisco, Boston, and Washington, D.C., where neighborhood opposition halted or rerouted planned urban freeways.

Effects

Freeways greatly increased freight capacity and personal mobility, but have been criticized for inducing demand, encouraging sprawl, dividing urban neighborhoods, and contributing to air pollution and greenhouse gas emissions. Some cities have removed waterfront or downtown freeways, replacing them with boulevards and public space.
