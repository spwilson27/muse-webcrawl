Poet laureate

The laurel wreath, symbol of poetic honour since antiquity.

A poet laureate is a poet officially appointed by a government or institution and often expected to compose poems for special events and occasions. The title derives from the laurel wreath awarded to poets in ancient Greece and Rome, a tradition associated with Apollo, the god of poetry.

History

The custom of crowning poets with laurel dates to the ancient world; the Italian scholar and poet Petrarch was crowned poet laureate in Rome in 1341, reviving the classical practice. In England, the title became an official court appointment: John Dryden was appointed the first official Poet Laureate of England in 1668 by Charles II. The post later became an appointment in the gift of the monarch on the advice of the prime minister.

Historically, British laureates were appointed for life; since 1999 the tenure has been fixed at ten years. Carol Ann Duffy, appointed in 2009, was the first woman to hold the post; she was succeeded by Simon Armitage in 2019.

Other countries

Many other nations have adopted the office. The United States appoints a Poet Laureate Consultant in Poetry to the Library of Congress, a position created in 1937 and renamed in 1985. Canada, Scotland, Wales, Ireland, and numerous other countries and regions maintain their own laureateships, as do many cities and U.S. states.
