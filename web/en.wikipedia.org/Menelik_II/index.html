Menelik II (Ge'ez: ዳግማዊ ምኒልክ; baptized as Sahle Maryam; 17 August 1844 – 12 December 1913) was King of Shewa (1865–1889) and then Emperor of Ethiopia from 1889 until his death in 1913. He dramatically expanded Ethiopia's territory, founded Addis Ababa as his capital, began the country's modernization, and led the army that crushed an Italian invasion at the Battle of Adwa in 1896 — the most famous African victory over a European colonial power of the era, securing Ethiopia's independence during the Scramble for Africa.

King of Shewa

Born in Ankober, Menelik was the son of Haile Melekot, King of Shewa. As a boy he was held at the court of Emperor Tewodros II, escaping in 1865 to reclaim the Shewan throne. As king he built Shewa into the wealthiest and best-armed Ethiopian kingdom, importing modern firearms through the port of Djibouti, while outwardly acknowledging the emperors in the north. When Emperor Yohannes IV was killed fighting the Mahdists at Gallabat in 1889, Menelik claimed the imperial crown and was crowned in November 1889.

Treaty of Wuchale and Adwa

Seeking Italian support, Menelik had signed the Treaty of Wuchale (1889) with Italy; the Italian text, however, made Ethiopia an Italian protectorate, while the Amharic text made recourse to Italian diplomacy optional. When Italy moved to enforce its claim, Menelik denounced the treaty and mobilized a national army of some 100,000 men. On 1 March 1896 his forces annihilated the Italian army at Adwa. The Treaty of Addis Ababa that followed recognized Ethiopia's complete independence, and Adwa became a symbol for anti-colonial movements worldwide — though Menelik's own armies simultaneously conquered and incorporated vast southern territories into the empire.

Modernization

Menelik founded Addis Ababa (1886) as capital, promoted the railway from Djibouti (reaching Dire Dawa in 1902), introduced the telephone, telegraph, and modern coinage, established the first government schools and hospitals, created a ministerial cabinet in 1907, and founded the Bank of Abyssinia. His expansion doubled the empire's size, incorporating Oromo, Sidama, Wolayta, and other peoples — a legacy debated for its violence and lasting ethnic tensions.

Death and succession

Incapacitated by strokes from 1909, Menelik died on 12 December 1913. His grandson Iyasu V succeeded briefly before being deposed in 1916; Menelik's daughter Zewditu became empress, with Ras Tafari (later Haile Selassie) as regent. Menelik's tomb stands in the Ba'eta Le Mariam church in Addis Ababa.
