The Human Genome Project (HGP) was an international scientific research project with the goal of determining the base pairs that make up human DNA and of identifying and mapping all of the genes of the human genome. Launched in 1990 and declared complete in April 2003, it remains the world's largest collaborative biological project.

History

The project was coordinated by the U.S. National Institutes of Health and Department of Energy, with major contributions from the UK's Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute and laboratories in France, Germany, Japan, and China. A working draft was jointly announced in June 2000 by the public consortium and the private company Celera Genomics, whose rival whole-genome shotgun approach, led by Craig Venter, spurred the public effort to accelerate.

Results

The project determined that the human genome contains roughly 3 billion base pairs and an unexpectedly modest number of protein-coding genes — around 20,000. The finished sequence covered about 99 per cent of the gene-containing (euchromatic) portion. Remaining gaps in repetitive regions were finally closed by the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium in 2022.

Legacy

The HGP revolutionised biology and medicine, launching genomics, driving down sequencing costs from billions of dollars to a few hundred, and enabling personalised medicine, ancestry testing, and large-scale studies of disease. Its commitment to rapid public data release (the Bermuda Principles) became a model for open science.
