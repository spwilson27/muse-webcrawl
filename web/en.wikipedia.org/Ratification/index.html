Ratification

Ratification is a principal's approval of an act of its agent that lacked the authority to bind the principal in the first place. In public international law, ratification is the formal process by which a state confirms its consent to be bound by a treaty it has signed, making the treaty legally binding for that state. In constitutional law, ratification can also refer to the formal approval of a constitution or constitutional amendment by the people or by state legislatures.

International law

Under the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969), signature of a treaty subject to ratification merely authenticates the text and obliges the signatory not to defeat the treaty's object and purpose; the state becomes bound only upon depositing its instrument of ratification with the depositary. Many treaties enter into force only after a specified number of ratifications have been deposited.

Constitutional ratification

The Constitution of the United States was ratified in 1787–1788 by conventions in the states, taking effect when nine of the thirteen states had ratified. Amendments to the U.S. Constitution require ratification by three-fourths of the states, either through their legislatures or through conventions, as Congress specifies.

Private law

In agency law, a principal may ratify an unauthorised contract made on its behalf, in which case the contract is treated as if it had been authorised from the outset. Ratification generally requires knowledge of the material facts and must encompass the whole transaction.
