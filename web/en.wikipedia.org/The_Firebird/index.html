The Firebird


Léon Bakst's costume design for the title role (1910).

The Firebird (French: L'Oiseau de feu; Russian: Жар-птица, Zhar-ptitsa) is a ballet and orchestral concert work by the Russian composer Igor Stravinsky. It was written for the 1910 Paris season of Sergei Diaghilev's Ballets Russes company and premiered at the Opéra de Paris on 25 June 1910, conducted by Gabriel Pierné, with choreography by Michel Fokine and Tamara Karsavina in the title role. The ballet launched Stravinsky's international fame and began his collaboration with Diaghilev, followed by Petrushka and The Rite of Spring.

Synopsis

The scenario, drawn from Russian folk tales, tells of Prince Ivan, who wanders into the enchanted garden of the ogre Kashchei the Immortal and captures the magical Firebird. In exchange for her freedom she gives him a feather with which to summon her in need. Ivan falls in love with a princess held captive by Kashchei; when the ogre's monsters seize him, Ivan waves the feather, and the Firebird appears, bewitching Kashchei's court into a frenzied dance and revealing the secret of his soul, hidden in an egg. Ivan smashes the egg, Kashchei's realm vanishes, and the ballet ends with a solemn wedding celebration.

Music

The score is famous for its brilliant orchestration, showing the influence of Stravinsky's teacher Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov, and contains celebrated numbers including the Firebird's dance, the "Infernal Dance of King Kashchei", the Berceuse, and the hymn-like Finale. Stravinsky later extracted three concert suites from the ballet, in 1911, 1919, and 1945; the 1919 suite is the most often performed.

Legacy

The Firebird remains a staple of the ballet and concert repertories. Its success made the 27-year-old Stravinsky famous overnight and marked a decisive moment in early modernist music.
