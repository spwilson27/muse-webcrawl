William Paley

William Paley (14 July 1743 – 25 May 1805) was an English Anglican clergyman, Christian apologist, philosopher, and utilitarian. He is best remembered for his natural theology and the watchmaker analogy in his book Natural Theology (1802), a classic statement of the argument from design.

Works

Paley, a fellow of Christ's College, Cambridge, published The Principles of Moral and Political Philosophy (1785), a long-standard Cambridge textbook setting out a form of theological utilitarianism in which moral obligation rests on expediency and obedience to God's will. His Evidences of Christianity (1794), an examination of the historical testimony for the Bible, was likewise required reading — the young Charles Darwin found its logic powerful and "admired" its structure even while later undermining its conclusions.

The watchmaker analogy

Natural Theology opens with the famous image: finding a watch upon a heath, we infer a watchmaker from its contrivance; so too the intricate adaptations of living things, such as the eye, must imply an intelligent Designer. After Darwin's theory of natural selection, the argument's biology was largely superseded, but the analogy remains a touchstone in philosophy of religion, invoked and critiqued down to Richard Dawkins's The Blind Watchmaker (1986).
