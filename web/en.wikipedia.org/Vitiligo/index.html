Vitiligo

Depigmentation of the hand in vitiligo

Symptoms: Patches of skin lose pigment

Usual onset: Before age 30

Causes: Autoimmune destruction of melanocytes

Frequency: ~1% of people

Vitiligo is a chronic autoimmune disorder that causes patches of skin to lose their pigment. The patches occur when melanocytes — the cells that produce melanin — die or stop functioning. Vitiligo affects about 1% of the world's population, occurs in all races, and is most noticeable in people with darker skin. It usually begins before the age of 30 and is not contagious or life-threatening, though it can cause significant psychological distress.

Cause and types

Vitiligo is believed to result from a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers, in which the immune system attacks melanocytes. It is associated with other autoimmune diseases such as Hashimoto's thyroiditis and type 1 diabetes. Nonsegmental vitiligo, the most common form, appears symmetrically and can spread over time; segmental vitiligo affects one area of the body, often in children, and tends to stabilize.

Treatment

There is no cure, but treatments can restore some colour: topical corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors, narrowband ultraviolet B phototherapy, and, since 2022, the topical JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib cream, the first approved repigmentation therapy in the United States. Surgical grafting can help stable patches, and depigmentation therapy may even out skin tone in widespread cases. Sun protection of affected skin is recommended.

Notable cases

Singer Michael Jackson attributed his changing skin colour to vitiligo, a diagnosis supported by his autopsy; model Winnie Harlow has raised the condition's public profile.
