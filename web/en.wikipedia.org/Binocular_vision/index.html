Binocular vision

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Binocular vision is vision in which both eyes are used together to perceive a single three-dimensional image of the surroundings. It is characteristic of predators and of primates, including humans, whose forward-facing eyes have overlapping visual fields. The slight difference between the images projected onto the two retinas — binocular disparity — is used by the visual system to compute depth, a process called stereopsis.

Advantages

Binocular vision confers several advantages. It provides depth perception and the ability to judge distances, useful for grasping and locomotion. It gives a wider field of view than either eye alone and improves visual acuity and contrast sensitivity through binocular summation. It also provides redundancy: vision survives the loss or occlusion of one eye, and a defect in one eye can be partly compensated by the other.

Mechanism

For a single fused image to be perceived, the two eyes must be aligned so that corresponding points of an object fall on corresponding retinal areas. Eye movements controlled by the extraocular muscles keep the eyes converged on the object of interest. Objects lying on the horopter are seen singly; those significantly in front of or behind it may be seen doubly (physiological diplopia), which the brain normally ignores. Disparities within Panum's fusional area are fused and interpreted as depth.

Disorders

Misalignment of the eyes, as in strabismus, can disrupt binocular vision and lead to double vision or, in children, suppression of one eye's image and amblyopia ("lazy eye"). Disorders of binocular vision are assessed and treated within optometry and ophthalmology, sometimes with prisms, vision therapy or surgery.
