The Rwandan genocide (Kinyarwanda: Itsembabwoko ry'u Rwanda) occurred in 1994 during the Rwandan Civil War. Over approximately 100 days, from 7 April to mid-July 1994, members of the Hutu ethnic majority murdered an estimated 800,000 people — primarily members of the Tutsi minority, along with moderate Hutu and Twa who opposed the killings. The genocide was carried out by extremist Hutu militias, soldiers and ordinary civilians, incited by propaganda including the radio station RTLM.

Background and trigger

Rwanda's population comprised Hutu (about 85%), Tutsi (about 14%) and Twa (about 1%) groups whose distinctions had hardened under German and Belgian colonial rule. In 1990 the Tutsi-led Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF) invaded from Uganda, starting a civil war; the 1993 Arusha Accords proposed power-sharing, which Hutu extremists bitterly opposed. On 6 April 1994 the aircraft carrying Presidents Juvénal Habyarimana of Rwanda and Cyprien Ntaryamira of Burundi was shot down over Kigali, triggering the planned extermination.

The killings

Roadblocks went up within hours; Tutsi and moderate Hutu were murdered with machetes, clubs and firearms, often by neighbours. Notable atrocities included massacres in churches and schools where victims had sought refuge. UN peacekeepers (UNAMIR), under General Roméo Dallaire, were hamstrung by a weak mandate and the withdrawal of most contingents after ten Belgian soldiers were killed; the international community failed to intervene. The genocide ended when the RPF captured Kigali and took control of the country in July 1994, prompting some two million Hutu to flee to neighbouring countries.

Aftermath

The International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda (ICTR) prosecuted leading organisers, delivering the first convictions for genocide by an international court. Within Rwanda, community-based gacaca courts processed nearly two million cases. The genocide is commemorated annually during Kwibuka, beginning on 7 April.
