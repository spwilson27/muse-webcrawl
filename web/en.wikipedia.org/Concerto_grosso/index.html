Concerto grosso

A concerto grosso is a form of Baroque music in which the musical material is passed between a small group of soloists (the concertino) and a full orchestra (the ripieno or tutti). The form flourished from the late seventeenth century into the mid-eighteenth century.

Structure

The concertino typically consisted of two violins and a cello, supported by the basso continuo. The contrast between the small solo group and the full ensemble — rather than the virtuosity of a single soloist, as in the later solo concerto — is the form's defining feature. Concerti grossi usually have several movements, alternating fast and slow.

Composers

The form was pioneered by Alessandro Stradella and Arcangelo Corelli, whose twelve Concerti grossi, Op. 6 (published 1714), became models for the genre. George Frideric Handel composed two celebrated sets, Op. 3 and Op. 6, and Johann Sebastian Bach's Brandenburg Concertos employ concerto grosso principles with varied solo groups. Antonio Vivaldi also wrote concerti grossi alongside his many solo concertos.

Later revival

Twentieth-century composers including Ernest Bloch, Bohuslav Martinů, and Alfred Schnittke revived the form in neoclassical works, quoting and reworking its Baroque textures.
