Soil conservation

Soil conservation
ScopePrevention of soil erosion, degradation, and fertility loss
Key practicesContour ploughing, terracing, cover crops, no-till farming, windbreaks, crop rotation
Key figures/agenciesHugh Hammond Bennett; US Soil Conservation Service (1935)

Soil conservation is the prevention and reduction of soil loss through erosion and of soil degradation through chemical and physical deterioration, in order to maintain soil fertility and productivity. It combines agricultural practices, land management, and policy; globally, erosion by water and wind is estimated to affect well over a billion hectares of land.

History

Ancient farmers practised terracing and contour farming for millennia, from the rice terraces of Asia to Andean and Mediterranean hillsides. Modern soil conservation emerged in the United States in response to the Dust Bowl of the 1930s, when Hugh Hammond Bennett—"the father of soil conservation"—persuaded Congress to found the Soil Conservation Service in 1935.

Techniques

Standard methods include contour ploughing and strip cropping to slow runoff; terracing on steep slopes; cover crops and mulching to protect bare ground; conservation tillage and no-till farming, which leave residue on the surface; windbreaks and shelterbelts against wind erosion; crop rotation and managed grazing to maintain soil structure and organic matter; and engineering works such as check dams and grassed waterways. Contemporary approaches emphasise soil health—building organic matter and biological activity—as well as erosion control.

Significance

Topsoil forms over centuries but can be lost in a single storm season. Conserving soil protects crop yields, water quality (by reducing sediment and nutrient runoff), flood resilience, and the large stock of carbon stored in soils, making it a concern of food security and climate policy alike.
