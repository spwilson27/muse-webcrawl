Eclogue
Genre: Pastoral poetry
Form: Short poem, often a shepherd's monologue or dialogue
Key examples: Virgil's Eclogues (Bucolics)

An eclogue is a short pastoral poem, typically in the form of a soliloquy or dialogue between shepherds. The word derives from the Greek eklogē, "selection", as used for selected short poems.

History

The genre originates in the Idylls of the Greek poet Theocritus (3rd century BC). Its canonical Roman form is Virgil's collection of ten Eclogues (also called the Bucolics, c. 42–39 BC), which range from singing contests and love laments to political allegory; the Fourth Eclogue, prophesying a golden age, was famously read in later centuries as a messianic prediction.

Later tradition

Eclogues were revived by Renaissance poets including Mantuan, Sannazaro, Spenser (The Shepheardes Calender, 1579) and Pope, whose Pastorals appeared in 1709. The form continued into the modern period, with examples by W. H. Auden and Seamus Heaney using the shepherd dialogue to engage contemporary themes.
