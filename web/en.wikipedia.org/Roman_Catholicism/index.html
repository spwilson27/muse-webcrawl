Roman Catholicism

Roman Catholicism is the form of Christianity practised by the Catholic Church under the authority of the Bishop of Rome, the pope. It is the world's largest Christian tradition, with roughly 1.4 billion baptised members. The term "Roman Catholic" distinguishes the church in communion with Rome from other Catholic traditions and gained currency after the Protestant Reformation; the Church itself formally uses "Catholic Church."

Beliefs

Roman Catholic teaching rests on Scripture and sacred Tradition, interpreted by the magisterium. Core doctrines include the Trinity, the Incarnation, salvation through grace and the sacraments, the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist (transubstantiation), veneration of Mary and the saints, purgatory, and the pope's primacy and, in defined circumstances, infallibility. The Church celebrates seven sacraments: baptism, confirmation, the Eucharist, penance, anointing of the sick, holy orders, and matrimony.

History

The Latin Church of the West diverged from the Greek East in the Great Schism of 1054. Medieval Catholicism shaped European law, universities, and art. The sixteenth-century Protestant Reformation prompted the Catholic Counter-Reformation, defined by the Council of Trent. The First Vatican Council (1869–70) defined papal infallibility; the Second Vatican Council (1962–65) undertook sweeping liturgical and ecumenical reforms under Popes John XXIII and Paul VI.

Structure and practice

The Church comprises the Latin Church and 23 Eastern Catholic Churches in full communion with Rome, organised into dioceses led by bishops. Its central administration, the Roman Curia, is based in Vatican City. Religious orders, schools, hospitals, and charities make it the largest non-governmental provider of education and health care worldwide.
