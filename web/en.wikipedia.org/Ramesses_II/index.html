Ramesses II (c. 1303 – 1213 BC), commonly known as Ramesses the Great, was the third pharaoh of the Nineteenth Dynasty of Egypt. His 66-year reign — the second longest in Egyptian history — is often regarded as the high point of New Kingdom power and glory, and he is remembered as a warrior king, a prolific builder and, in later tradition, a candidate for the pharaoh of the Exodus (an identification historians treat with caution).

Military career

Crowned in 1279 BC, Ramesses campaigned in the Levant to restore Egyptian influence lost under Akhenaten. In his fifth regnal year (1274 BC) he fought the Hittite king Muwatalli II at the Battle of Kadesh in present-day Syria — among the largest chariot battles ever fought. Though Ramesses proclaimed a great victory in inscriptions across Egypt, the outcome was strategically indecisive; prolonged rivalry ended in 1259 BC with the Treaty of Kadesh, the earliest surviving international peace treaty, later sealed by a diplomatic marriage.

Building programme

Ramesses founded a new capital, Pi-Ramesses, in the Nile Delta and covered Egypt with monuments: the twin temples of Abu Simbel in Nubia (relocated by UNESCO in the 1960s to escape the Aswan High Dam's lake), the Ramesseum mortuary temple at Thebes, additions at Karnak and Luxor, and temples at Abydos. He fathered around a hundred children; his chief queen, Nefertari, received one of the most beautiful tombs in the Valley of the Queens.

Death and mummy

Ramesses died in his early nineties, outliving many of his heirs, and was buried in tomb KV7; his mummy, later reburied by priests at Deir el-Bahari, was rediscovered in 1881 and is now displayed in Cairo. The broken colossus of the Ramesseum inspired Percy Bysshe Shelley's sonnet "Ozymandias," from the Greek form of Ramesses' throne name.
