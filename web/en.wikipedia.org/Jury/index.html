Jury

A jury is a sworn body of people (jurors) convened to hear evidence in a trial and render an impartial verdict or a finding of fact. Juries are a characteristic feature of the common law tradition, used in both criminal and civil cases in many jurisdictions.

History

The modern jury descends from practices in medieval England, where the Assize of Clarendon (1166) used local men to report crimes, and the trial jury gradually replaced trial by ordeal after 1215. The principle that a juror could not be punished for an acquittal was established in Bushel's Case (1670). Jury trial spread throughout the British Empire and is guaranteed in the United States by the Sixth and Seventh Amendments.

Types

A trial jury (petit jury) decides guilt or liability; a grand jury, used mainly in the United States, decides whether to indict. A coroner's jury assists in inquests. Criminal juries traditionally consist of 12 members and in many systems must be unanimous, though some jurisdictions allow majority verdicts.

Selection and function

Jurors are selected from the community, subject to challenges by the parties (voir dire in the US). The jury judges the facts while the judge rules on the law. Jury nullification — acquitting despite proof of guilt — remains a debated de facto power.
