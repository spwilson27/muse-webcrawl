Nickel titanium

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nickel titanium, also known as nitinol, is a metal alloy of nickel and titanium in roughly equal atomic proportions, notable for two closely related properties: the shape-memory effect and superelasticity (pseudoelasticity). The name combines its composition with its place of discovery, the Naval Ordnance Laboratory (Ni-Ti-NOL), where William J. Buehler and Frederick Wang recognised its shape-memory behaviour in the 1960s.

Properties

Nitinol exists in two temperature-dependent crystal structures: a high-temperature phase called austenite and a low-temperature phase called martensite. When deformed in the martensitic state and then heated, the alloy recovers its original shape as it transforms back to austenite — the shape-memory effect. Above the transformation temperature, it is superelastic, able to undergo strains many times larger than ordinary metals and spring back completely. Transformation temperatures can be tuned from below −100 °C to above 100 °C by adjusting composition and processing.

Applications

The largest uses are in medicine: superelastic nitinol is used for self-expanding cardiovascular stents, orthodontic archwires that apply gentle constant force, guidewires, and surgical instruments. Engineering applications include temperature-activated actuators and couplings, eyeglass frames that recover from bending, mobile phone antennas, and components in aerospace and robotics. Processing nitinol is difficult because it is reactive at high temperatures and its properties are highly sensitive to composition; concerns about nickel release in the body are addressed by the stable titanium oxide layer that forms on its surface.
