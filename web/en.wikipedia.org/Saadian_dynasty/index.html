Saadian dynasty


Image placeholder: Saadian dynasty

The Saadian dynasty (Arabic: السعديون, romanized: as-Saʿdiyyūn) was a Sharifian dynasty that ruled Morocco from 1549 to 1659. Claiming descent from the Islamic prophet Muhammad through Hassan ibn Ali, the Saadians rose to power in southern Morocco, defeated the Wattasid dynasty, and reunified the country. They are remembered for resisting both Ottoman expansion and Portuguese incursions, and for the wealth generated by trans-Saharan trade in gold and by the sugar industry of the Sous valley.

Rise and reign

Muhammad al-Shaykh established Saadian control over most of Morocco by 1554. His son Abdallah al-Ghalib consolidated the dynasty. At the Battle of Alcácer Quibir (the Battle of the Three Kings) in 1578, the Portuguese king Sebastian I was killed and the Saadian sultan Abd al-Malik died, but the Moroccan army triumphed; Ahmad al-Mansur ("the Golden") succeeded and reigned until 1603. Under al-Mansur, Morocco enjoyed great prosperity: he built the El Badi Palace in Marrakesh, maintained diplomatic relations with England and the Dutch Republic, and in 1591 sent an expedition under Judar Pasha across the Sahara that destroyed the Songhai Empire and seized the gold and salt resources of Timbuktu and Gao.

Decline

After al-Mansur's death in 1603, the empire fragmented in a civil war among his sons, and Saadian power contracted to Marrakesh and Fez. The last Saadian sultan, Ahmad al-Abbas, was killed in 1659, and the Alaouite dynasty subsequently reunified Morocco. The Saadian Tombs in Marrakesh, built under al-Mansur and rediscovered in 1917, are a celebrated monument of the era.
