Chromaticism

Chromaticism is a compositional technique in which a piece of music uses notes outside the prevailing diatonic scale, drawing on the full twelve-note chromatic scale. Chromatic notes may colour a melody, enrich harmony through chords borrowed from other keys, or undermine tonal stability altogether.

Development

Renaissance composers such as Carlo Gesualdo experimented with intense chromatic writing in madrigals, and chromatic passages became common in Baroque music as expressive devices. In the Classical era chromaticism was used sparingly for dramatic effect — Mozart's "Dissonance" Quartet opens with a famously chromatic introduction. The nineteenth century saw a dramatic expansion: Chopin, Liszt and especially Wagner, in works such as Tristan und Isolde, pushed chromatic harmony to the point where traditional tonal resolution was indefinitely delayed.

Toward atonality

The harmonic language of late-Romantic composers including Wagner, Mahler and Strauss strained the major-minor system so severely that early twentieth-century composers such as Arnold Schoenberg abandoned tonality entirely, developing free atonality and later the twelve-tone technique, in which all twelve chromatic pitch classes are treated as equal.

In other genres

Chromaticism also flourishes outside classical music: jazz harmony relies on chromatic passing chords and altered dominants, blues uses chromatic approach notes, and bebop scales incorporate chromatic passing tones between chord tones.
