Dark matter

Gravitational lensing in a galaxy cluster reveals mass far exceeding the visible matter.

In astronomy, dark matter is an invisible and hypothetical form of matter that does not interact with light or other electromagnetic radiation. Dark matter is implied by gravitational effects that cannot be explained by general relativity unless more matter is present than can be seen. Such effects occur in the context of the formation and evolution of galaxies, gravitational lensing, the observable universe's current structure, mass position in galactic collisions, galaxy motions within galaxy clusters, and cosmic microwave background anisotropies.

In the standard Lambda-CDM model of cosmology, dark matter accounts for about 26.5% of the total mass–energy of the universe (ordinary matter ~4.9%, dark energy ~68.5%), and roughly 85% of all matter. The name refers to the fact that it does not emit, absorb or reflect radiation appreciably — unlike the luminous gases that produce phenomena such as the aurora — and is detected only through gravity.

Evidence

  • Galaxy rotation curves — Vera Rubin and Kent Ford showed in the 1970s that stars orbit too fast for the visible mass; Fritz Zwicky had found the same anomaly in the Coma Cluster in 1933.
  • Gravitational lensing — including the Bullet Cluster, where lensing mass is displaced from the X-ray gas after a collision.
  • Cosmic microwave background — Planck satellite measurements fix the matter budget precisely.
  • Structure formation — simulations require cold, non-baryonic matter to reproduce the cosmic web.

Candidates and searches

Proposed candidates include WIMPs (weakly interacting massive particles), axions, and sterile neutrinos; primordial black holes are a non-particle alternative. Direct-detection experiments (e.g. LUX-ZEPLIN, XENONnT) have so far found no confirmed signal, while indirect searches look for annihilation products. Alternatives such as MOND (modified Newtonian dynamics, proposed by Mordehai Milgrom in 1983) attempt to explain the observations by changing gravity rather than adding matter, but struggle with clusters and the microwave background. Modern analyses of rotation-curve and lensing datasets increasingly rely on statistical calculus-based modelling and deep learning classification.
