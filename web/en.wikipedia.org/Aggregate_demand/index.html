Aggregate demand

In macroeconomics, aggregate demand (AD) or domestic final demand (DFD) is the total demand for final goods and services in an economy at a given time. It is sometimes called effective demand, though at other times this term is distinguished. This is the demand for the gross domestic product of a country. It specifies the amount of goods and services that will be purchased at all possible price levels.

Components

Aggregate demand is conventionally decomposed as AD = C + I + G + (X − M): consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports. Consumption is usually the largest component, driven by disposable income; investment depends on interest rates and business expectations.

The aggregate demand curve

The AD curve slopes downward for three reasons: the Pigou (real balance) effect, the Keynes interest-rate effect, and the Mundell–Fleming exchange-rate effect. Together with aggregate supply, it determines equilibrium output and the price level in the AD–AS model.

Policy

Fiscal and monetary policy act largely by shifting aggregate demand. Keynesian economics, developed by John Maynard Keynes in response to the Great Depression, assigns demand management a central role in stabilising output and employment.
