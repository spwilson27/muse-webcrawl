Smith normal form

A matrix in Smith normal form: diagonal entries d1 | d2 | … | dr

In mathematics, the Smith normal form (sometimes abbreviated SNF) is a normal form that can be defined for a matrix over a principal ideal domain (PID), such as the integers or a polynomial ring over a field. Any matrix over a PID can be transformed, by multiplication on the left and right by invertible (unimodular) matrices, into a diagonal matrix whose nonzero diagonal entries d1, d2, …, dr satisfy the divisibility chain d1 | d2 | … | dr. These entries, called the invariant factors, are unique up to multiplication by units.

History and computation

The result is named after the Irish mathematician Henry John Stephen Smith, who introduced it in 1861 for matrices over the integers. It can be computed by a sequence of elementary row and column operations — swapping rows or columns, adding an integer multiple of one row to another, and negating a row — analogous to Gaussian elimination but with extra steps using greatest common divisors to enforce the divisibility condition. Naive algorithms can suffer from severe intermediate expression swell, and much research has gone into efficient computation.

Applications

Over the integers, the Smith normal form classifies finitely generated abelian groups: the cokernel of an integer matrix is a direct sum of cyclic groups whose orders are its invariant factors, which yields the fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups. It is also used to compute the homology groups of a chain complex (the matrices being the boundary maps), to solve systems of linear Diophantine equations, and in control theory for polynomial matrices.
