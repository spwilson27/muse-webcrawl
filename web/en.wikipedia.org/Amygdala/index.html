Amygdala

The amygdala (plural: amygdalae or amygdalas) is a pair of almond-shaped clusters of nuclei located deep within the medial temporal lobes of the brain in complex vertebrates, including humans. It is a key component of the limbic system and plays a central role in the processing of emotions, especially fear, and in the formation of emotional memories.

Anatomy

Each amygdala consists of several subnuclei, including the basolateral complex, the central nucleus and the cortical nucleus. The basolateral complex receives sensory input from the thalamus and cortex and is critical for assigning emotional value to stimuli; the central nucleus connects to brainstem and hypothalamic systems that generate behavioural and autonomic responses.

Function

Research using lesion studies, functional neuroimaging and animal models shows the amygdala is involved in fear conditioning, threat detection, reward processing, and the modulation of memory consolidation — emotional events are remembered more strongly partly through amygdala activation of the hippocampus and other memory systems. The amygdala also participates in social cognition, including face recognition and the evaluation of facial expressions.

Clinical significance

Abnormal amygdala activity is associated with anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder, depression and autism spectrum conditions. Rare bilateral damage to the amygdala, as in Urbach–Wiethe disease, can markedly reduce the experience of fear.
