The treaties of the European Union are a set of international treaties between the member states of the European Union that set out the EU's constitutional basis. They establish the various EU institutions, their powers and procedures, and the policy areas in which the Union may act. Because the EU has no single codified constitution, the treaties — above all the Treaty on European Union (TEU) and the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU) — function as its supreme law, taking precedence over the national law of member states within the EU's competences.

Founding treaties

The process began with the Treaty of Paris (1951), which created the European Coal and Steel Community, and the Treaties of Rome (1957), which created the European Economic Community and Euratom. Subsequent amending treaties deepened and widened integration: the Merger Treaty (1965), the Single European Act (1986), which set the goal of a single market by 1992, and the Maastricht Treaty (1992), which formally created the European Union and laid the path to the euro. The treaties of Amsterdam (1997) and Nice (2001) reformed the institutions ahead of enlargement to central and eastern Europe.

Treaty of Lisbon

After the proposed Treaty establishing a Constitution for Europe was rejected in French and Dutch referendums in 2005, its substance was largely carried into the Treaty of Lisbon, signed in 2007 and in force from 1 December 2009. Lisbon renamed the founding texts the TEU and TFEU, gave the Charter of Fundamental Rights legal force, created the permanent President of the European Council and the High Representative for foreign affairs, extended qualified-majority voting and co-decision for the European Parliament, and introduced a formal withdrawal clause (Article 50 TEU) — the provision under which the United Kingdom left the Union in 2020.

Amending and accession treaties

The treaties change in three ways: ordinary treaty revision, negotiated at an intergovernmental conference and ratified by every member state; accession treaties, by which new states joined (from the first enlargement of 1973 through Croatia in 2013); and simplified revision procedures for limited matters. Related instruments include the Schengen agreements, incorporated into EU law at Amsterdam, and intergovernmental treaties concluded outside the formal EU framework, such as the European Stability Mechanism treaty and the Fiscal Compact of 2012. The treaties are deposited with the government of Italy, a tradition dating from the Treaties of Rome.
