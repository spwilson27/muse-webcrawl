Emerging market

An emerging market (or emerging economy) is a market economy of a country that is in the process of developing and industrialising, with some but not all characteristics of a developed market: a functioning stock exchange, growing income per head, increasing integration with global markets, but also higher political, currency and liquidity risks. The term was coined in 1981 by Antoine van Agtmael of the International Finance Corporation to promote investment in what had been called the "Third World."

Classification

Index providers such as MSCI, FTSE Russell and S&P Dow Jones classify countries using criteria including market size, liquidity, capital controls and institutional framework; classification changes (promotions and demotions between frontier, emerging and developed status) move billions in index-tracking funds. Large emerging markets commonly listed include China, India, Brazil, Mexico, Indonesia, Turkey, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, Poland and Taiwan or South Korea (classified differently by different providers).

Concepts and groupings

The BRICS grouping (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa — a term coined by Jim O'Neill of Goldman Sachs in 2001, later expanded to include additional members) symbolises the rising economic weight of large emerging economies, which together account for a growing share of global GDP, especially measured at purchasing power parity. Investors are drawn by faster growth and diversification but face volatility: emerging markets suffered sharp sell-offs in the 1997 Asian financial crisis, the 2013 "taper tantrum" and other global risk-off episodes, and persistent debates concern debt levels, governance and the "middle-income trap."
