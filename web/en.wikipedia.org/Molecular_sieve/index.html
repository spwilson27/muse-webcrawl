Molecular sieve


Materials: Zeolites, activated carbon, silica gel, porous glass
Designations: 3Å, 4Å, 5Å, 13X
Uses: Drying, gas separation, purification

A molecular sieve is a material with pores of uniform size, measured in ångströms or nanometres, that adsorbs molecules small enough to pass through the pores while excluding larger ones. The effect can be used to separate mixtures by molecular size and to dry gases and liquids extremely thoroughly.

Materials and types

Most commercial molecular sieves are synthetic zeolites — crystalline aluminosilicates with regular cage-like pores. They are designated by nominal pore aperture: 3Å sieves adsorb water but exclude most other molecules and are used to dry ethanol and solvents; 4Å sieves are general-purpose desiccants; 5Å sieves separate straight-chain from branched hydrocarbons; and 13X sieves, with pores around 10Å, are used in air separation and gas purification. Molecular sieves can be regenerated by heating, which drives off the adsorbed material.

Applications

Molecular sieves are used as desiccants in laboratory and industrial drying, in the production of high-purity oxygen by pressure swing adsorption, in removing water and carbon dioxide from natural gas and air before liquefaction, in double-glazed window units to prevent fogging, and as catalysts and catalyst supports in petroleum refining. Sodium zeolite A is also used as a "builder" in laundry detergents.
