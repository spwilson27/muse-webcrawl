Sumo

Sumo (Japanese: 相撲, sumō) is a form of competitive full-contact wrestling where a wrestler (rikishi) attempts to force his opponent out of a circular ring (dohyō) or into touching the ground with any body part other than the soles of his feet. The sport originates in Japan, the only country where it is practiced professionally, and is considered Japan's national sport.

History

Sumo evolved from Shinto rituals in which wrestling bouts were performed at shrines to pray for good harvests. Professional sumo took shape during the Edo period, acquiring its ring, rankings, and ceremonial elements. Modern professional sumo is governed by the Japan Sumo Association, which holds six grand tournaments (honbasho) a year, each lasting fifteen days.

Rules and ritual

Matches are preceded by elaborate ritual: wrestlers throw salt to purify the ring, stamp their legs to drive away demons, and ritually rinse their mouths with water. The bout itself usually lasts only seconds. Wrestlers may push, thrust, slap, throw, and trip, but may not punch, kick, choke, or pull hair. The referee (gyōji), dressed in traditional costume, officiates from within the ring.

Ranks and wrestlers' lives

Rikishi are ranked in a hierarchy crowned by the yokozuna (grand champion) in the top makuuchi division. Wrestlers live communally in stables (heya) under a stablemaster, following strict traditions governing dress, hairstyle, meals — including the protein-rich stew chankonabe — and daily routine. Many top wrestlers in recent decades have come from Mongolia, including the record-setting yokozuna Hakuhō, who won 45 top-division championships.
