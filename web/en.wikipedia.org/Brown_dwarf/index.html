Brown dwarf

Artist's impression of a late-type brown dwarf

A brown dwarf is a substellar object whose mass lies between that of the heaviest gas-giant planets (about 13 Jupiter masses) and the lightest stars (about 75–80 Jupiter masses). Brown dwarfs can fuse deuterium — and the most massive can fuse lithium — but never sustain hydrogen fusion in their cores as true stars do, so they cool and fade steadily over billions of years.

History

The objects were theorised in the 1960s by Shiv Kumar as "black dwarfs": collapsing gas clouds that never ignite hydrogen fusion. The name "brown dwarf" was proposed by Jill Tarter in 1975. Definitive discoveries came in 1995 with Teide 1 in the Pleiades and Gliese 229B, a companion to a nearby red dwarf whose methane spectrum proved it was far cooler than any star. Thousands are now known, including the nearest, the Luhman 16 binary about 6.5 light-years away.

Classification

Brown dwarfs define the coolest stellar spectral classes: late M, then L, T, and Y, with surface temperatures falling from about 2,500 K to below 300 K for the coldest Y dwarfs. The deuterium-burning limit (~13 Jupiter masses) is used by the IAU as the working boundary between brown dwarfs and planets, though formation-based definitions are also debated. In the solar neighbourhood brown dwarfs appear to be roughly as common as stars.

Properties

Despite their wide mass range, all brown dwarfs have radii close to Jupiter's, owing to electron-degeneracy pressure. Their atmospheres show clouds of silicate and iron in L dwarfs, methane absorption in T dwarfs, and ammonia in Y dwarfs, with weather-like variability observed. Some host planetary companions and discs, blurring the line between star formation and planet formation.
