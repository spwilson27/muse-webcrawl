Pella

FoundedLate 5th century BC

Pella (Greek: Πέλλα) was the capital of the ancient kingdom of Macedon from the late 5th century BC until the Roman conquest. It is best known as the birthplace of Philip II and his son Alexander the Great. The modern town of Pella in the Pella regional unit of Central Macedonia, Greece, lies near the archaeological site.

History

Pella was founded, or refounded as capital, by King Archelaus I (413–399 BC), who moved the seat of government from Aigai and invited artists and thinkers such as the painter Zeuxis, the poet Timotheus, and the tragedian Euripides, who wrote the Bacchae there. The city was laid out on a grid plan with wide streets, an agora of about seven hectares, sanctuaries, and wealthy houses decorated with pebble mosaics.

Philip II was born in Pella in 356 BC, and Alexander was born there in the same generation; the young Alexander was tutored by Aristotle at nearby Mieza. After the Roman defeat of Macedon at the Battle of Pydna (168 BC), Pella was sacked and declined, and it was later destroyed, probably by an earthquake, in the 1st century BC.

Archaeology

Excavations since 1957 have uncovered the agora, the sanctuary of Darron, palace complex, and houses famous for their pebble mosaics, including the Lion Hunt and the Abduction of Helen, now displayed in the Archaeological Museum of Pella, opened in 2009.
