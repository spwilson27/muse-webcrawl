Lange model

The Lange model (also known as the Lange–Lerner theorem) is a neoclassical economic model for a hypothetical socialist economy based on public ownership of the means of production and a trial-and-error approach to achieving economic equilibrium and Pareto efficiency. In the model, a federal planning board (called the Central Planning Board) undertakes trial-and-error computations to determine and achieve prices that clear markets, ordering a/firms to price output to equal marginal cost.

Overview

The model was first proposed by Polish economist Oskar R. Lange in 1936–1937 in his essay "On the Economic Theory of Socialism", written in response to the socialist calculation debate and the arguments of Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek that rational economic calculation was impossible under socialism. Abba Lerner later contributed important refinements, including the rule that firms should set price equal to marginal cost.

Basic structure

In the Lange model, all production is carried out by publicly owned firms directed by plant managers. The Central Planning Board sets arbitrary prices for goods and factors of production; managers choose input combinations that minimize costs and produce output at the level where price equals marginal cost. If a market exhibits excess demand, the board raises the price; if there is excess supply, it lowers the price. This iterative process, analogous to a Walrasian auctioneer, is intended to converge on general equilibrium prices.

Consumers remain free to choose their consumption bundles, and workers are free to choose occupations; only the capital stock is socially owned, with citizens receiving a "social dividend" from the publicly owned capital.

Criticism and legacy

Critics, notably Hayek, argued that the model ignored the dispersed, tacit nature of knowledge and the role of entrepreneurship and incentives. Nevertheless, the Lange model has been credited with anticipating elements of mechanism design theory and market socialism, and Lange himself later argued that computers could perform the required calculations directly.
