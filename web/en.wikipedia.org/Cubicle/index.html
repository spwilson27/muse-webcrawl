Cubicle

A cubicle is a partially enclosed office workspace that is separated from neighbouring workspaces by partitions, generally 1.5–1.8 metres (5–6 feet) tall. Its purpose is to isolate office workers and managers from the sights and noises of an open workspace, providing a degree of privacy while saving floor space compared with private offices. Cubicles consist of modular elements such as walls, work surfaces, overhead bins, drawers, and shelving, which can be configured depending on the individual's needs.

History

The cubicle was developed from the Action Office system designed by Robert Propst for Herman Miller and launched in 1968. Propst intended a flexible, humane environment, but cost pressures led employers to pack his modular components into dense grids of identical workstations, the so-called "cubicle farms" or "seas of cubicles".

Criticism and culture

Cubicles became a symbol of corporate conformity and are satirized in the comic strip Dilbert and the film Office Space. Propst himself later denounced the dense cubicle farm as a perversion of his idea. In recent decades many companies have moved toward open-plan offices or hybrid arrangements, though cubicles persist in call centres and government offices.
