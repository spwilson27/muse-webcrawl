Ammonium nitrate disasters

Ammonium nitrate disasters are explosions and fires involving ammonium nitrate (AN), a widely used fertiliser and blasting agent. Although AN is not normally explosive by itself, it can detonate when contaminated, confined, or exposed to intense heat, and it has been responsible for some of the deadliest industrial accidents in history.

Notable incidents

Oppau, Germany (1921)

A silo containing about 4,500 tonnes of a mixture of ammonium sulfate and ammonium nitrate exploded at the BASF plant in Oppau while workers used dynamite to break up the caked fertiliser. More than 500 people were killed.

Texas City, United States (1947)

A fire aboard the SS Grandcamp, loaded with about 2,300 tonnes of ammonium nitrate, led to a detonation that ignited a second ship, High Flyer. The disaster killed at least 581 people and remains one of the deadliest industrial accidents in US history.

AZF factory, Toulouse, France (2001)

An explosion in a hangar storing roughly 300 tonnes of ammonium nitrate destroyed the AZF fertiliser plant, killing 31 people and injuring thousands; the blast was heard tens of kilometres away.

West, Texas, United States (2013)

A fire at the West Fertilizer Company storage and distribution facility caused an explosion that killed 15 people, most of them firefighters, and devastated part of the town of West.

Beirut, Lebanon (2020)

Approximately 2,750 tonnes of ammonium nitrate that had been stored unsafely in a port warehouse for six years detonated after a fire, devastating large parts of Beirut, killing more than 200 people, injuring over 7,000, and leaving an estimated 300,000 homeless. See also ammonia, the feedstock from which ammonium nitrate is made.

Causes and prevention

Common factors include bulk storage near populated areas, contamination with organic material or chlorides, fires raising confined AN to decomposition temperatures, and poor regulatory oversight. Modern regulations limit stack sizes, require separation from fuels, and mandate reporting of stored quantities.
