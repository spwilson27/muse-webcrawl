The International Celestial Reference System (ICRS) is the current standard celestial reference system adopted by the International Astronomical Union (IAU). Its origin is at the barycenter of the Solar System, and its axes are intended to be fixed with respect to distant extragalactic objects, showing no overall rotation relative to them. The ICRS supersedes earlier systems based on the positions of stars and the dynamics of the Solar System, such as the FK5 catalogue.

Realisation: the ICRF

The ICRS is realised by the International Celestial Reference Frame (ICRF), a catalogue of precise positions of extragalactic radio sources — mostly quasars — measured by very-long-baseline interferometry. The first ICRF (1995) contained 608 sources. ICRF2 (2009) expanded this to 3,414 sources, and ICRF3, adopted from 1 January 2019, lists 4,536 sources observed at multiple radio frequencies, with positions accurate to tens of microarcseconds. The Gaia mission provides an optical realisation (Gaia-CRF) aligned to the radio frame.

Coordinates

Positions in the ICRS are given in equatorial coordinates — right ascension and declination — closely aligned with, but more stable than, the mean equator and equinox of J2000.0. Because the axes do not rotate with the Earth or precess with the equinox, ICRS coordinates of fixed distant objects do not change with time, unlike older epoch-based systems.

Use

The ICRS underpins modern astrometry, spacecraft navigation, telescope pointing and the alignment of star catalogues such as Hipparcos and Gaia, whose positions are expressed in the ICRS at their observation epochs.
