Louis XI (3 July 1423 – 30 August 1483), called "the Prudent" (le Prudent) and later nicknamed "the Universal Spider" (l'araignée universelle) for the webs of diplomacy he wove, was King of France from 1461 to 1483. He succeeded his father Charles VII and is credited with strengthening royal authority, curbing the great feudal nobility, and greatly expanding the territory of the French crown.

Dauphin in revolt

As Dauphin, Louis governed the Dauphiné almost independently and repeatedly defied his father, joining the Praguerie revolt of 1440 and eventually fleeing to the court of Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, in 1456. He remained in exile at Genappe until Charles VII's death in 1461.

Struggle with the nobility and Burgundy

Louis's dismissals of his father's advisers and his tax policies provoked the League of the Public Weal in 1465, a coalition of great nobles led by Charles the Bold of Burgundy, which fought him to a draw at Montlhéry. Louis conceded much at first, then patiently recovered ground by dividing his enemies. His great rival was Charles the Bold, whose ambition to build a middle kingdom between France and the Empire Louis countered by subsidies, diplomacy and support for Charles's enemies, the Swiss and René of Anjou. Charles was killed at the Battle of Nancy in 1477, and Louis promptly absorbed the Duchy of Burgundy and Picardy.

Expansion and reform

By inheritance and escheat Louis also gained Anjou, Maine, Provence and Bar (1480–81), so that by his death only Brittany among the great fiefs remained outside direct royal control. He promoted trade and industry — fostering silk manufacture at Tours and Lyon, encouraging fairs and foreign merchants, reforming the posts, and supporting printing — while taxing heavily and dispensing with the Estates General. In foreign affairs he ended English intervention by the Treaty of Picquigny (1475), paying Edward IV to withdraw.

Death and assessment

Louis spent his last years in seclusion at Plessis-lèz-Tours, fearful of death and conspiracy, and died of a stroke in August 1483. Historians view him as a founder of the strong French monarchy of the early modern period: unscrupulous but far-sighted, preferring bribery and negotiation to battle, and leaving his son Charles VIII a consolidated kingdom.
