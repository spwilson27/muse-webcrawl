SETI@home

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

SETI@home ("SETI at home") was a project of the University of California, Berkeley, that used volunteer computing to analyse radio telescope data for signs of extraterrestrial intelligence. Launched to the public on 17 May 1999, it became the most famous distributed computing project in history, enlisting millions of participants whose idle home computers processed data recorded by the Arecibo Observatory and later the Green Bank Telescope.

How it worked

The project recorded wide-band radio data from the sky at frequencies around the 1.4 GHz hydrogen line. The data were divided into small "workunits," which volunteers' computers downloaded and searched for narrowband signals, spikes, triplets, and pulses that might indicate an artificial transmitter. Results were returned to Berkeley, where persistent candidate signals were checked against terrestrial interference. No confirmed extraterrestrial signal was found.

Impact

SETI@home demonstrated the power of volunteer computing and led directly to the BOINC platform, which now hosts projects in astronomy, medicine, mathematics, and climate science. At its peak, the project aggregated more computing power than any supercomputer of its era, and it set a Guinness record as the largest computation in history. The project entered hibernation on 31 March 2020 so the team could focus on analysing two decades of accumulated results.
