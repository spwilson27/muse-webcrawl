Flash point

The flash point of a material is the lowest liquid temperature at which, under certain standardised conditions, a liquid gives off vapours in a quantity such as to be capable of forming an ignitable vapour/air mixture. The flash point is sometimes confused with the autoignition temperature, the temperature at which the vapour ignites spontaneously without an ignition source, and the fire point, the lowest temperature at which the vapour continues to burn after being ignited.

Examples
Gasoline−43 °C
Ethanol13 °C
Kerosene38–72 °C
Diesel fuel52–96 °C
Vegetable oil327 °C

Mechanism

Every flammable liquid has a vapour pressure, which is a function of its temperature. As the temperature increases, the vapour pressure increases. As the vapour pressure increases, the concentration of evaporated flammable liquid in the air increases. Hence, temperature determines the concentration of evaporated flammable liquid in the air. Each flammable liquid requires a particular concentration of its vapour in air to sustain combustion, and that concentration is somewhat fixed for each liquid — the lower flammable limit. The flash point is the lowest temperature at which there will be enough flammable vapour to induce ignition when an ignition source is applied.

Measurement

There are two basic types of flash point measurement: open cup and closed cup. In open cup devices, the sample is contained in an open cup which is heated, and at intervals a flame is brought over the surface. The measured flash point will actually vary with the height of the flame above the liquid surface. Closed-cup testers, such as the Pensky-Martens apparatus, give lower and more reproducible values and are used in most regulatory classifications.

Classification

Flash points are used to classify liquids for transport and storage regulations. Under the Globally Harmonized System (GHS), flammable liquids are divided into categories with flash points below 23 °C, between 23 °C and 60 °C, and between 60 °C and 93 °C. See also flammability.
