Bomb

A bomb is an explosive weapon that uses the exothermic reaction of an explosive material to provide an extremely sudden and violent release of energy. Detonations inflict damage principally through ground- and atmosphere-transmitted mechanical stress, the impact and penetration of pressure-driven projectiles, pressure damage, and fire. Bombs have been used since the 11th century, beginning in East Asia.

History

The earliest known bombs were incendiary and explosive devices used in China by the Jin dynasty in the 13th century, iron casings filled with gunpowder. In Europe, bombs — shells fired by mortars — became standard siege weapons by the 17th century. The 20th century brought aerial bombs dropped from aircraft, first used in the Italo-Turkish War of 1911, and developed enormously in the two world wars, from blockbusters and bouncing bombs to the first guided weapons.

Types

  • General-purpose bombs — cased explosives relying on blast and fragmentation.
  • Guided bombs ("smart bombs") — fitted with laser, GPS or electro-optical guidance for precision.
  • Cluster bombs — dispensing submunitions over an area; widely restricted by the 2008 Convention on Cluster Munitions.
  • Incendiary bombs — designed to set fires, using substances such as napalm or thermite.
  • Nuclear bombs — deriving energy from fission and/or fusion; used in war only in 1945, against Hiroshima and Nagasaki.
  • Improvised explosive devices (IEDs) — homemade bombs, including car bombs and suicide vests, used by insurgents and terrorists.

Effects

Damage is caused by the blast wave (overpressure), fragmentation of the casing and surroundings, heat, and in some cases ground shock. Military bombs are designed for specific targets — runways, bunkers, armour, or area targets — while bomb disposal technicians (EOD) work to render unexploded ordnance safe; unexploded bombs from the Second World War are still regularly found and defused in cities such as London and Berlin.
