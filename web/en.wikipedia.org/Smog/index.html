Smog is a type of intense air pollution, originally a portmanteau of the words smoke and fog, coined in the early 20th century to describe the mixture of smoke and fog that afflicted London. In modern usage the term covers visible air pollution generally, including the photochemical haze produced when sunlight reacts with vehicle and industrial emissions.

Types

Sulfurous smog

Sulfurous or "London-type" smog results from burning sulfur-rich fossil fuels, especially coal, in cool, humid air. It consists of smoke, sulfur dioxide, and particulate matter. The Great Smog of London in December 1952, which killed thousands, led to the United Kingdom's Clean Air Act of 1956.

Photochemical smog

Photochemical or "Los Angeles-type" smog forms when nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds react in strong sunlight, producing ground-level ozone and peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN). It is characteristic of warm, sunny cities with heavy automobile traffic.

Causes

Major sources include coal-fired power plants, industrial furnaces, motor vehicle exhaust, residential heating, and agricultural and wildfire smoke. Temperature inversions, in which a layer of warm air traps cooler polluted air near the ground, can intensify episodes.

Effects

Smog irritates the eyes and airways, aggravates asthma and chronic lung disease, and is associated with cardiovascular illness and premature mortality. It also damages crops and ecosystems through ozone exposure and acid deposition, and reduces visibility.

Control

Measures include emission standards for vehicles and industry, cleaner fuels, catalytic converters, public transport, and air-quality alert systems. Cities such as London and Los Angeles have seen dramatic improvements since the mid-20th century, while severe smog remains a major problem in parts of Asia.
