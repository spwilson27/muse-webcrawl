W. D. Hamilton

William Donald Hamilton
Born: 1 August 1936, Cairo, Egypt
Died: 7 March 2000 (aged 63), London, England
Known for: Kin selection, Hamilton's rule, inclusive fitness, Red Queen hypothesis
Awards: Darwin Medal (1988), Crafoord Prize (1993)

William Donald Hamilton (1 August 1936 – 7 March 2000) was a British evolutionary biologist widely regarded as one of the most influential evolutionary theorists of the twentieth century. His theory of kin selection resolved the puzzle of altruism in nature and laid the foundation for the gene-centred view of evolution.

Kin selection and inclusive fitness

In two landmark papers in 1964, "The Genetical Evolution of Social Behaviour", Hamilton showed that a gene causing costly helping behaviour can spread if the beneficiaries are relatives, because they share copies of the gene. The condition, known as Hamilton's rule, states that altruism evolves when rB > C — where r is genetic relatedness, B the fitness benefit to the recipient, and C the cost to the actor. The concept of inclusive fitness elegantly explained the sterile castes of ants, bees, and wasps (whose haplodiploid sex-determination makes sisters unusually closely related) and reframed social evolution.

Other work

Hamilton proposed that sex is maintained because parasites constantly evolve to attack common host genotypes — the "Red Queen" idea developed with others — and built influential models of sex ratios, senescence, and parasite-mediated sexual selection, arguing that bright plumage and elaborate ornaments signal genetic resistance to parasites. His collected papers appeared as Narrow Roads of Gene Land (1996–2005).

Death and legacy

Hamilton contracted malaria during fieldwork in the Congo investigating the origin of HIV and died in London in March 2000 from complications. Richard Dawkins, whose The Selfish Gene popularised Hamilton's ideas, wrote his obituary. The Royal Society's Hamilton Lecture commemorates him.
