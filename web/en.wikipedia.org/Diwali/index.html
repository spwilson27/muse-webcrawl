Diwali (also called Deepavali, from Sanskrit dīpāvali, "row of lights") is a festival of lights and one of the major festivals celebrated by Hindus, Jains, Sikhs and some Buddhists. The festival usually lasts five days and is celebrated during the Hindu lunisolar months of Ashvin and Kartik (between mid-September and mid-November). It symbolises the spiritual victory of light over darkness, good over evil and knowledge over ignorance.

Religious significance

Hindus associate Diwali with several legends, varying by region: the return of Rama to Ayodhya after fourteen years of exile and his defeat of Ravana, welcomed home with rows of lamps; Krishna's victory over the demon Narakasura; and the worship of Lakshmi, goddess of wealth and prosperity, who is believed to visit homes on the main night, which falls on the dark new-moon night of Kartik. For Jains, Diwali marks the nirvana of Mahavira in 527 BC. Sikhs celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas, commemorating Guru Hargobind's release from imprisonment. Some Buddhists, notably Newar Buddhists, also observe the festival.

The five days

The festival spans five days: Dhanteras, devoted to wealth and health; Naraka Chaturdashi (Choti Diwali); the main Diwali day with Lakshmi Puja; Govardhan Puja; and Bhai Dooj, celebrating the bond between brothers and sisters. Customs include cleaning and decorating homes with rangoli, lighting earthen oil lamps (diyas), exchanging gifts and sweets, wearing new clothes, and setting off fireworks.

Celebrations worldwide

Diwali is an official holiday in India, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Fiji, Singapore, Malaysia and several other countries, and is celebrated by diaspora communities worldwide, with public events in cities such as London, New York and Sydney. Its commercial season is India's largest shopping period, comparable to Christmas in the West. Air pollution from fireworks has prompted regulation and "green cracker" initiatives in recent years.
