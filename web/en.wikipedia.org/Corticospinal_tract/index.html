Corticospinal tract

The corticospinal tract is the principal descending motor pathway of the central nervous system, carrying signals for voluntary movement from the cerebral cortex to the spinal cord. Together with the corticobulbar tract (which supplies the brainstem) it constitutes the pyramidal tract, so named because its fibres pass through the medullary pyramids. It is the longest and largest tract of the human CNS, containing roughly one million axons.

Course

The tract originates mainly from large pyramidal neurons (including the giant Betz cells) of the primary motor cortex, with contributions from premotor, supplementary motor and somatosensory areas. Fibres descend through the corona radiata and the posterior limb of the internal capsule, then through the cerebral peduncle of the midbrain, the pons, and the medulla. At the caudal medulla, about 90 percent of fibres cross to the opposite side at the pyramidal decussation and descend as the lateral corticospinal tract, terminating on interneurons and motor neurons throughout the cord. The remaining uncrossed fibres form the anterior corticospinal tract, which mainly serves axial and proximal muscles and crosses segmentally.

Function and clinical significance

The corticospinal tract is especially important for fine, fractionated movements of the hands and fingers. Damage — from stroke (commonly in the internal capsule), traumatic injury or diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis — produces upper motor neuron signs: weakness, spasticity, hyperreflexia and the Babinski sign. Developmentally the tract matures over the first two years of life; injury before maturation, as in cerebral palsy, profoundly affects motor development.
