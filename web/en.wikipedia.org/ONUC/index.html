The United Nations Operation in the Congo (French: Opération des Nations Unies au Congo, abbreviated ONUC) was a United Nations peacekeeping force established in July 1960, following Security Council Resolution 143, to assist the government of the newly independent Republic of the Congo during the Congo Crisis.

Background

The Congo became independent from Belgium on 30 June 1960. Within days the army mutinied, Belgium intervened militarily, and the mineral-rich province of Katanga seceded under Moise Tshombe with Belgian support. Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba appealed for UN assistance after requests to the United States were declined.

Operations

ONUC's mandate expanded over time: from securing the withdrawal of Belgian forces, to preventing civil war, and eventually — after a February 1961 Security Council resolution — to using force as a last resort to prevent conflict and remove foreign mercenaries. Its operations included fighting to end the Katangese secession, concluded in early 1963 after Operation Grandslam. The mission also became entangled with the fate of Lumumba, who was murdered in January 1961.

Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld died in a plane crash at Ndola in September 1961 while traveling to negotiate a ceasefire with Tshombe — one of the most controversial episodes in UN history.

Withdrawal and legacy

ONUC withdrew on 30 June 1964. At its peak it fielded nearly 20,000 troops drawn from many nations, including large contingents from India, Ethiopia, Sweden, Ireland, and Ghana; 234 UN personnel were killed. The operation was one of the largest and most complex of the early peacekeeping era and stretched the UN's finances and constitutional limits, provoking a major funding dispute with the Soviet Union and France.
