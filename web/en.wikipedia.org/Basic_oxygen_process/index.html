Basic oxygen process

The basic oxygen process (BOP), also known as the Linz–Donawitz process or basic oxygen steelmaking (BOS), is the primary method of mass steel production in the world. Molten pig iron from a blast furnace, together with scrap steel, is charged into a refractory-lined converter vessel, and pure oxygen is blown through the melt at high speed to oxidise excess carbon and impurities. The process accounts for roughly 70 percent of world crude steel output.

Process

The vessel, typically holding 100–350 tonnes, is tilted to receive molten iron and scrap, then turned upright. A water-cooled lance lowers and blows 99%+ pure oxygen at supersonic speed onto the metal surface for about 15–20 minutes. The oxidation of carbon, silicon, manganese and phosphorus is strongly exothermic, raising the bath to about 1,600–1,650 °C without external fuel. Burnt lime and dolomite fluxes form a basic slag that absorbs phosphorus and sulfur. When the blow ends, the steel is tapped into a ladle, alloyed as needed, and the slag is poured off separately. Bottom-blowing variants (OBM/Q-BOP) and combined blowing use tuyères in the vessel base.

History

The process was developed in Austria around 1948–1952 by Voestalpine engineers at Linz and Donawitz — building on the work of Swiss engineer Robert Durrer — and was commercialised in 1952–53. It rapidly displaced the open-hearth furnace because it is far faster (a heat takes about 40 minutes tap-to-tap versus many hours) and needs no external fuel.
