Biblioclasm

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Biblioclasm is the destruction of books, especially the deliberate destruction of religious or cultural texts. It is related to book burning, though biblioclasm can also encompass other forms of destruction, such as tearing, shredding, or pulping. The term derives from the Greek biblion (book) and klan (to break).

History

Instances of biblioclasm are recorded throughout history, often accompanying conquest, religious conflict, or ideological campaigns. The Chinese emperor Qin Shi Huang is said to have ordered the burning of books in 213 BCE. The destruction of the library of Nalanda, the losses of Maya codices during the Spanish conquest of the Americas, and the burning of the Jaffna Public Library in 1981 are frequently cited examples.

Motives

Biblioclasm is commonly motivated by a desire to erase the memory, beliefs, or identity of a group, to impose censorship, or to dramatise a political rupture — as in the Nazi book burnings of 1933. Scholars of cultural heritage treat large-scale destruction of libraries and archives as a form of cultural genocide ("libricide", in the term coined by Rebecca Knuth).

Modern instances and debate

Destruction of books continues in wartime, including the burning of libraries in Bosnia in the 1990s and attacks on collections in Iraq and Mali in the 2000s and 2010s. Librarians also practise routine "weeding" of damaged or obsolete stock, which is not generally regarded as biblioclasm because it lacks the intent to destroy cultural memory.
