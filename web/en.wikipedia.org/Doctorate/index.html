A doctorate (from the Latin docere, "to teach"), or doctoral degree, is an academic degree awarded by universities and some other educational institutions, derived from the ancient formalism licentia docendi ("licence to teach"). In most countries, a doctorate is the highest degree attainable in a field, qualifying the holder to teach at university level or to work in a specific profession.

Types

Research doctorates, of which the Doctor of Philosophy (PhD) is the most common, are awarded for a programme of original research culminating in a thesis or dissertation defended before examiners. Professional doctorates prepare for practice in professions such as medicine (MD), law (JD in the United States), and dentistry. Higher doctorates (such as the DSc or DLitt in the British tradition) recognise a substantial body of published work, and honorary doctorates are conferred without coursework or examination to honour achievement.

History

The doctorate emerged in the medieval European universities of Bologna, Paris, and Oxford, where the doctor's degree in law, medicine, and theology marked admission to the guild of masters. The modern research PhD took shape in nineteenth-century Germany, notably at Berlin, and spread to the United States (first awarded at Yale in 1861) and worldwide.

Requirements

Doctoral study typically follows a master's or strong bachelor's degree and takes three to seven years. Candidates complete advanced coursework or training, pass qualifying examinations, and produce a dissertation contributing new knowledge, defended in a viva voce oral examination in many systems. Requirements and titles vary significantly by country.
