Argo is an international programme that uses profiling floats to observe temperature, salinity, currents, and, increasingly, other properties of the upper ocean. It is a major component of the Global Ocean Observing System. Argo maintains an array of about 4,000 robotic floats distributed over the world's ice-free oceans, each drifting at depth and surfacing every ten days to transmit a vertical profile of the water column by satellite. The name recalls the mythological ship Argo and its partnership with the Jason satellite altimeters.

How the floats work

A standard Argo float is a free-drifting cylinder about 1.3 m tall. It changes its buoyancy with an external bladder and hydraulic pump: after deployment it descends to a parking depth of about 1,000 m, drifts with the currents for nine days, then sinks to 2,000 m before ascending to the surface, measuring temperature, salinity (via conductivity), and pressure on the way up. At the surface it transmits its data and position through satellites (Iridium or Argos) within hours, then repeats the cycle for a lifetime of four to five years and 150–250 profiles. Because a float's drift between surfacings is tracked, Argo also maps subsurface currents.

Development

Proposed in 1998 by an international team of oceanographers and named at a 1999 meeting, Argo built on the ALACE floats of the World Ocean Circulation Experiment. Deployments began in 2000, and the array reached its initial target of 3,000 floats in November 2007 — the first system to observe the global ocean interior in near-real time, in all seasons and beneath storms. Extensions include Deep Argo floats profiling to 4,000–6,000 m, and BGC-Argo floats carrying biogeochemical sensors for oxygen, nitrate, pH, chlorophyll, and particles, aimed at a fully multidisciplinary array of about 4,700 floats.

Data and scientific impact

Argo data are freely available from global data assembly centres (in France and the United States) within 24 hours, under a landmark open-data policy. The dataset has transformed estimates of ocean heat content and sea-level rise, improved seasonal climate forecasts and ocean reanalyses, and supplied the great majority of all ocean temperature and salinity profiles ever collected — by the 2010s Argo was delivering well over one hundred thousand profiles per year, surpassing the cumulative total from a century of research-vessel hydrography. Participating nations, more than thirty of them, contribute floats roughly in proportion to resources; coordination is provided by the Argo Steering Team and the Argo Information Centre.
