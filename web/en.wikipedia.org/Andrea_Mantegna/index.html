Andrea Mantegna

Born: c. 1431, Isola di Carturo, Republic of Venice
Died: 13 September 1506, Mantua
Known for: Painting, fresco, engraving

Andrea Mantegna (c. 1431 – 13 September 1506) was an Italian Renaissance painter, a student of Roman archaeology, and son-in-law of Jacopo Bellini. Renowned for his mastery of perspective and foreshortening, his sculptural figures, and his austere, antiquarian style, he was the leading painter of northern Italy in the later fifteenth century and court artist to the Gonzaga of Mantua for over forty years.

Early career in Padua

Mantegna was apprenticed to Francesco Squarcione in Padua, a centre of humanist learning and archaeology. By seventeen he was independent, painting the Ovetari Chapel frescoes in the Church of the Eremitani (1448–57, largely destroyed in 1944), which display his precocious command of perspective and classical detail. His altarpiece for San Zeno in Verona (1457–59) set a pattern followed across northern Italy.

Mantua

In 1460 Mantegna became court painter to Ludovico III Gonzaga, Marquis of Mantua, serving him and his successors Federico I and Francesco II. His masterpiece of the period is the Camera dei Piccinini, now the Camera degli Sposi (1465–74), in the Palazzo Ducale — the first fully illusionistic ceiling decoration of the Renaissance, with figures gazing down from a painted balustrade against open sky. For Isabella d'Este he painted mythologies including the Triumphs of Caesar (1480s–1506, now at Hampton Court), nine large canvases celebrating ancient Rome. His Lamentation over the Dead Christ (c. 1480s), viewed in extreme foreshortening from the feet, is among the most famous images of the Renaissance. Mantegna was also the first great Italian engraver.
