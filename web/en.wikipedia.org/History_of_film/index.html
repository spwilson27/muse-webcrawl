History of film

The history of film chronicles the development of motion pictures from 19th-century optical toys to the global digital industry of today. Precursors included the phenakistiscope, zoetrope and Eadweard Muybridge's sequential photographs of a galloping horse (1878). Thomas Edison's Kinetoscope (1891–93) offered peephole viewing, while the Lumière brothers' Cinématographe enabled projection to audiences; their first public screening took place in Paris on 28 December 1895.

Early cinema (1895–1918)

Early films were short novelties screened in music halls and nickelodeons. Georges Méliès pioneered special effects and narrative fantasy with films such as A Trip to the Moon (1902), while Edwin S. Porter's The Great Train Robbery (1903) advanced cross-cutting and location shooting. As feature films lengthened, national industries grew in France, Italy, Denmark and the United States. D. W. Griffith's The Birth of a Nation (1915) and Intolerance (1916) consolidated film grammar — and, in the former case, controversy over its racism.

The silent era matures

The 1920s saw artistic flowering: German Expressionism (The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari, 1920), Soviet montage (Eisenstein's Battleship Potemkin, 1925), and Hollywood comedy from Chaplin, Keaton and Lloyd. The industry consolidated in Hollywood, California, whose studios dominated world markets after the First World War.

The coming of sound

Warner Bros.' The Jazz Singer (1927) popularised synchronised dialogue, and within a few years sound had transformed production, genres and stardom. Early talkies were static, but by the early 1930s filmmakers restored camera mobility. The musical, gangster film and screwball comedy flourished.

The studio system and golden age

From the 1930s to the 1950s the major studios — MGM, Paramount, Warner Bros., 20th Century Fox and RKO — operated a factory system with actors under long contracts, mass-producing genre films. 1939 is often called Hollywood's greatest year, producing Gone with the Wind and The Wizard of Oz. Technicolor became standard, and film noir flourished in the 1940s. The Hays Code enforced moral censorship from 1934. India's film industries, centred later on Mumbai ("Bollywood"), also grew into mass producers.

Postwar challenges and new waves

Television eroded cinema audiences after 1945. Antitrust decisions broke up studio theatre chains, and widescreen formats (CinemaScope, 1953) and colour lured audiences back. Italian neorealism (Bicycle Thieves, 1948) influenced world cinema; the French New Wave (Godard, Truffaut) and directors such as Kurosawa, Satyajit Ray, Bergman and Fellini elevated the auteur. Japan's Rashomon (1950) and Seven Samurai (1954) won international acclaim.

New Hollywood and the blockbuster

In the late 1960s and 1970s a generation of American directors — Coppola, Scorsese, Altman, Kubrick — made artistically ambitious films. Jaws (1975) and Star Wars (1977) inaugurated the summer blockbuster era, with wide releases, merchandising and franchises. Home video and cable soon became major revenue streams.

Home video, digital and streaming

Computer-generated imagery transformed production from Jurassic Park (1993) and Pixar's Toy Story (1995), the first fully computer-animated feature. Digital cameras and projection replaced film stock in the 2000s. In the 2010s subscription streaming services such as Netflix reshaped distribution, financing original films and series, while global box office — led by Hollywood franchises and Chinese and Indian industries — reached record levels before the disruption of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020.
