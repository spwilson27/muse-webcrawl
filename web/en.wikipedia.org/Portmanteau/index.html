Portmanteau

A portmanteau (plural portmanteaus or portmanteaux) is a word formed by blending the sounds and meanings of two or more words, such as smog (from smoke and fog), brunch (breakfast + lunch), or motel (motor + hotel). In linguistics the phenomenon is also called a blend.

Origin of the term

The word comes from the French portmanteau, a suitcase or valise that opens into two equal compartments — itself formed from porter ("to carry") and manteau ("cloak"). Lewis Carroll coined the linguistic usage in Through the Looking-Glass (1871), where Humpty Dumpty explains to Alice the strange words of the poem "Jabberwocky": "You see it's like a portmanteau — there are two meanings packed up into one word." Carroll's own examples include slithy (lithe + slimy) and mimsy (flimsy + miserable).

Formation

Blends typically combine the beginning of one word with the end of another, often where the source words share a sound, as in Spanglish or Eurasia. Some blends fuse whole syllables or overlap heavily (alphanumeric), while others splice at arbitrary points. Portmanteaus are especially productive in naming: businesses (Microsoft = microcomputer + software), hybrid animals (a liger is a lion–tiger cross), celebrity couples in popular media ("Brangelina"), and internet or technology vocabulary (email, webinar, podcast, from iPod + broadcast).

Distinctions

Portmanteaus differ from compounds, which join whole words intact (bookshelf), and from contractions, which merely abbreviate. A blend is generally considered successful when it is phonetically natural and its source words remain recognizable, allowing speakers to decode the new word without explanation.
