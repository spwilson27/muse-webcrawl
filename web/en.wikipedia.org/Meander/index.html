Meander

A meander is one of a series of regular sinuous curves in the channel of a river or other watercourse. It is produced as a watercourse erodes the sediments that compose an outer, concave bank (cut bank or river cliff) and deposits sediments on an inner, convex bank, which is typically the point bar. The result of this coupled erosion and sedimentation is the formation of a sinuous course as the channel migrates back and forth across the axis of a floodplain.

Meanders and oxbow lakes on a river floodplain.

Formation

Meanders form in flat, low-gradient landscapes where the river carries fine sediment. Even a slight initial bend is amplified: water flows fastest around the outside of a bend, eroding the outer bank, while slower water on the inside deposits sediment, building a point bar. Over time the bend grows more pronounced and migrates downstream and laterally across the floodplain.

When two adjacent meander bends erode toward each other, the river may cut through the narrow neck between them during a flood, abandoning the old loop. The abandoned loop, cut off from the main channel, forms an oxbow lake.

Geometry

The geometry of meanders is remarkably regular. The wavelength of a meandering channel is typically about 10 to 14 times the channel width, and the radius of curvature of a bend is usually two to three times the channel width. The term "meander" derives from the Meander River (now the Büyük Menderes River) in Turkey, famous in antiquity for its winding course.

Related features

  • Incised meanders — deep, steep-walled meanders cut into bedrock, formed when a meandering river experiences land uplift.
  • Scroll bars and meander scars — ridges and depressions left on the floodplain as the channel migrates.
  • Slip-off slope — the gently sloping inner bank of a meander where deposition occurs.
