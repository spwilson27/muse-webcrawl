Law of large numbers

Field: Probability theory
First stated: Jacob Bernoulli, Ars Conjectandi (1713)
Related: Central limit theorem, gambler's fallacy

In probability theory, the law of large numbers (LLN) is a theorem that describes the result of performing the same experiment a large number of times. According to the law, the average of the results obtained from a large number of independent random trials should be close to the expected value, and will tend to become closer to the expected value as more trials are performed.

Forms

The weak law of large numbers states that the sample average converges in probability to the expected value: for independent, identically distributed random variables with finite mean, the probability that the sample mean deviates from the true mean by more than any fixed amount approaches zero as the sample size grows. The strong law of large numbers, proved in mature form by Kolmogorov, states that the sample average converges almost surely — short-run deviations are eventually and permanently overwhelmed.

History

Gerolamo Cardano noted the empirical regularity in the 16th century; Jacob Bernoulli proved the first version for binary outcomes after twenty years of work, published posthumously in 1713. Poisson, who coined the name "law of large numbers," Chebyshev, Markov, Khinchin, and Kolmogorov successively generalised the result in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Applications and misconceptions

The law underpins insurance, statistical sampling, quality control, and Monte Carlo simulation: averages over large samples are stable and predictable even when individual outcomes are not. It does not imply that departures will be "corrected" — the gambler's fallacy. Proportions converge while absolute differences may grow, and outcomes remain independent: a fair coin does not owe anyone tails.
