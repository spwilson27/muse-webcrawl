Counterforce

In nuclear strategy, a counterforce target is one that has a military value, such as a launch silo for intercontinental ballistic missiles, an airbase where nuclear bombers are based, a port for ballistic missile submarines, or a command and control installation. A counterforce strike is an attack intended to destroy an enemy's nuclear weapons and delivery systems before they can be used, in contrast to a countervalue strike aimed at an enemy's cities and population.

Strategic implications

Counterforce capability is inherently destabilizing in the logic of deterrence theory: a state that fears its arsenal could be destroyed in a first strike faces "use it or lose it" pressure in a crisis, and may adopt launch-on-warning postures. Because hardened silos require accurate, high-yield, or multiple warheads to destroy, counterforce arsenals tend to encourage the proliferation of warheads and multiple independently targetable reentry vehicles (MIRVs).

History

Counterforce ideas entered United States planning in the early 1960s, when Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara articulated a "no-cities" doctrine in 1962, proposing that American forces target Soviet military assets rather than populations. The concept underpinned later doctrines of flexible response and limited nuclear options. During the 1970s and 1980s, improvements in missile accuracy — notably with the Minuteman III, MX/Peacekeeper, and Trident II — made hard-target counterforce strikes technically plausible, fuelling arms-race competition and arms-control efforts.
