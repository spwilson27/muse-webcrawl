Electrical resistivity and conductivity


A resistive material with contacts; resistance depends on geometry, resistivity does not

Electrical resistivity (also called volume resistivity or specific electrical resistance) is a fundamental specific property of a material that measures how strongly it resists electrical current. A low resistivity indicates a material that readily allows electric current. Resistivity is commonly represented by the Greek letter ρ (rho). The SI unit of electrical resistivity is the ohm-metre (Ω⋅m).

Electrical conductivity is the reciprocal of electrical resistivity. It represents a material's ability to conduct electric current. It is commonly signified by the Greek letter σ (sigma), but κ (kappa) and γ (gamma) are sometimes used. The SI unit of electrical conductivity is siemens per metre (S/m).

Definition

For a uniform material, resistivity is defined by the relation between the electric field E and the current density J: ρ = E/J. For a specimen of length l and cross-sectional area A, the resistance is R = ρl/A. Conductivity is simply σ = 1/ρ.

Causes

In metals, current is carried by delocalised electrons; resistivity rises with temperature because lattice vibrations scatter the electrons. In semiconductors and electrolytes, charge carriers include holes and ions, and conductivity generally increases with temperature. Superconductors exhibit zero resistivity below a critical temperature.

Temperature dependence

For many metals near room temperature, resistivity varies approximately linearly with temperature: ρ = ρ0[1 + α(T − T0)], where α is the temperature coefficient of resistivity. This property is exploited in resistance thermometers.
