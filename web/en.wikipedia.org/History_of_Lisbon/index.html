History of Lisbon

The history of Lisbon, the capital of Portugal, stretches from Neolithic settlement through Phoenician, Roman and Moorish periods to its role as the hub of a global maritime empire, its destruction in the earthquake of 1755, and its modern revival.

Origins and antiquity

The Lisbon area was inhabited in Neolithic times, and Phoenicians established a trading post known as Alis Ubbo around 1200 BCE near the sheltered estuary of the Tagus. Under Rome the city, called Olisipo, was a prosperous port of Lusitania, granted municipal status and Roman citizenship for its inhabitants. After the fall of Rome it passed through Suebi and Visigoth hands.

Moorish and medieval Lisbon

Muslims conquered the city in 714; as al-Ushbuna it was fortified with the castle that survives as the Castelo de São Jorge. In 1147 Afonso Henriques, aided by northern crusaders en route to the Holy Land, besieged and captured Lisbon, making it part of the new Kingdom of Portugal. It formally became the capital in 1255. The university, founded in 1290, was later moved to Coimbra.

Age of Discoveries

From the fifteenth century Lisbon was the departure point of the Portuguese voyages of exploration. The spice trade with India and later gold and sugar from Brazil made it one of Europe's richest ports, with the Manueline Belém Tower and Jerónimos Monastery built from pepper profits. Its cosmopolitan merchant community included a large Jewish population until the forced conversions of 1497.

The 1755 earthquake and Pombaline rebuilding

On 1 November 1755 a massive earthquake, followed by a tsunami and fires, destroyed much of Lisbon and killed tens of thousands. The Marquis of Pombal directed a pioneering reconstruction: the Baixa district was rebuilt on a grid plan with anti-seismic timber cage construction (gaiola pombalina), one of the earliest examples of earthquake-resistant building.

Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Lisbon suffered during the Peninsular War and the liberal civil wars of the 1830s. The nineteenth century brought boulevards such as the Avenida da Liberdade, trams and funiculars. The monarchy fell in 1910; from 1932 António de Oliveira Salazar's authoritarian Estado Novo governed Portugal, overseeing colonial wars in Africa. The Carnation Revolution of 25 April 1974, begun in Lisbon, restored democracy. Expo 98 regenerated the eastern waterfront, and Lisbon has since become a major tourist and technology centre.
