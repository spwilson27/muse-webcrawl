Copenhagen interpretation

The Copenhagen interpretation is a collection of views about the meaning of quantum mechanics, largely stemming from the work of Niels Bohr and Werner Heisenberg in Copenhagen in the 1920s. It remains among the most commonly taught interpretations of the theory.

Principles

Key ideas include Bohr's principle of complementarity—quantum objects exhibit wave-like and particle-like properties depending on the experimental arrangement—and Heisenberg's uncertainty principle. The Born rule gives the probability of a measurement outcome from the wavefunction, and measurement is held to cause an indeterministic collapse of the wavefunction to a definite state. On this view, it is meaningless to speak of a quantum system's properties before measurement.

Criticism and alternatives

Albert Einstein and others objected to the interpretation's indeterminism and its treatment of measurement, debates captured in the Bohr–Einstein debates and the EPR paradox. Alternative interpretations include many-worlds, de Broglie–Bohm theory, and objective collapse models, none of which has displaced Copenhagen as the working orthodoxy in textbooks.
