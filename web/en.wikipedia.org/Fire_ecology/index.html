Fire ecology

Post-fire regeneration: many ecosystems depend on periodic fire.

Fire ecology is a scientific discipline concerned with natural processes involving fire in an ecosystem and the ecological effects, the interactions between fire and the abiotic and biotic components of an ecosystem, and the role of fire as an ecosystem process.

Fire regimes

Ecosystems are characterised by their fire regime: the pattern of fire frequency, intensity, seasonality, and type (surface fire, crown fire, or ground fire) that typically occurs in an area over long periods. Many ecosystems, particularly savanna, chaparral, and coniferous forests, have evolved with fire as an essential contributor to habitat vitality and renewal. Fire is a natural process, and many organisms are specifically adapted to it, contributing to characteristic properties of the habitat.

Plant adaptations

Plants in fire-prone environments show a range of adaptations. Serotiny is the retention of seeds in closed cones or fruits — as in many pines, banksias, and lodgepole pine — which open only under the heat of a fire. Other species resprout from protected buds in bark, lignotubers, or roots (epicormic growth in eucalypts). Some plants are stimulated to flower or germinate by smoke and heat; chemical compounds in smoke called karrikins trigger germination in many fire-following annuals. Thick bark protects the cambium of trees such as ponderosa pine and giant sequoia from surface fires.

Effects and management

Fire recycles nutrients, clears undergrowth, maintains open habitats for fire-dependent species, and shapes landscape mosaics. Conversely, decades of fire suppression in some regions have allowed fuel to accumulate, leading to less frequent but much more destructive fires. Modern land management uses prescribed burning to restore natural fire regimes, and the traditional burning practices of Indigenous peoples — long used to manage country in Australia and North America — are increasingly recognised and revived. Climate change, by increasing heat and drought, is altering fire regimes worldwide.
