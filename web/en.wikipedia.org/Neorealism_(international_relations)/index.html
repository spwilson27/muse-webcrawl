Neorealism (international relations)

Neorealism, or structural realism, is a theory of international relations that holds that the structure of the international system — above all its anarchic ordering and the distribution of capabilities among states — is the principal determinant of state behaviour. The theory was systematised by Kenneth Waltz in his 1979 book Theory of International Politics.

Core assumptions

Waltz argued that the international system is anarchic: there is no authority above the state capable of enforcing rules. States, as the system's principal units, must therefore provide for their own security ("self-help"). Differences in state behaviour arise mainly from differences in relative power, since the system's structure — unipolar, bipolar, or multipolar — constrains and socialises states into similar patterns: balancing against concentrations of power, emulating successful practices, and prioritising survival above other goals.

Unlike classical realism, which traced conflict to human nature and statesmen, neorealism deliberately brackets domestic politics and ideology, seeking a parsimonious, systemic explanation of recurrent patterns such as war, balancing, and the persistence of power competition.

Branches and debates

Later scholars divided neorealism into defensive realism, which holds that states seek an appropriate amount of power and that excessive expansion is counterproductive, and offensive realism, associated with John Mearsheimer, which holds that great powers relentlessly maximise power and seek regional hegemony. Neorealism has been criticised by liberals, constructivists, and others for neglecting institutions, economic interdependence, ideas, and domestic politics, and for struggling to explain peaceful systemic change such as the end of the Cold War.
