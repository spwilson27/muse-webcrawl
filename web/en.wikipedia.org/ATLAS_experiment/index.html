ATLAS (A Toroidal LHC ApparatuS) is the largest general-purpose particle detector experiment at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC), a particle accelerator at CERN in Switzerland. The experiment is designed to take advantage of the unprecedented energy available at the LHC and observe phenomena that involve highly massive particles which were not observable using earlier lower-energy accelerators. ATLAS was one of the two LHC experiments involved in the discovery of the Higgs boson in July 2012.

Design

The detector is a cylindrical onion-like layered system around the collision point. From the inside out it consists of an inner tracker immersed in a solenoid magnetic field, electromagnetic and hadronic calorimeters, and a large muon spectrometer built around eight superconducting barrel toroid magnets — the distinctive feature that gives ATLAS its name. ATLAS is 46 metres long and 25 metres in diameter and weighs about 7,000 tonnes, making it the largest-volume particle detector ever constructed.

Physics programme

ATLAS investigates the fundamental constituents of matter and the forces between them. Its goals include measuring the properties of the Higgs boson, precision tests of the Standard Model, searches for supersymmetry and extra dimensions, and studies of top quarks, B-physics and heavy-ion collisions (quark–gluon plasma). On 4 July 2012, ATLAS and the CMS experiment jointly announced evidence of a new particle consistent with the Higgs boson at a mass of about 125 GeV; the discovery led to the 2013 Nobel Prize in Physics for François Englert and Peter Higgs.

Collaboration and data

The ATLAS collaboration comprises around 3,000 scientific authors from some 180 institutions worldwide. Collisions occur up to a billion times per second; a multi-level trigger system reduces this to roughly a thousand recorded events per second, producing tens of petabytes of data per year analysed on the Worldwide LHC Computing Grid.
