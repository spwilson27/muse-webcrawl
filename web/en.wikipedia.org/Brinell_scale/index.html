The Brinell scale characterises the indentation hardness of materials through the penetration of an indenter loaded onto the material. It was proposed by Swedish engineer Johan August Brinell in 1900 and was the first widely used and standardised hardness test in engineering and metallurgy.

Method

In the test, a ball of hardened steel or tungsten carbide — commonly 10 mm in diameter — is pressed into the test surface under a specified load (typically 500–3000 kgf) for a set dwell time. After removing the load, the diameter of the circular impression is measured with a low-power microscope. The Brinell hardness number (BHN, now designated HBW when a carbide ball is used) is the load divided by the curved surface area of the indentation.

Characteristics

Because the Brinell impression is relatively large, it averages over local structural variations such as grains and inclusions, making the test well suited to castings, forgings, and materials with coarse or heterogeneous structure. It is commonly used for aluminium alloys, copper alloys, and soft steels, and for checking the hardness of large components. Typical values range from about 15 HBW for soft lead alloys to roughly 650 HBW for hardened steels tested with a carbide ball.

Limitations

The test is slower than the Rockwell test, leaves a comparatively large mark, and is unsuitable for very hard materials (where the ball itself deforms), very thin specimens, or finished surfaces. The impression diameter must fall within a valid range relative to the ball diameter for the result to be reliable, so the load-to-ball-diameter ratio is standardised for each material class.
