Wide-body aircraft
DefinitionAirliner with a fuselage wide enough for two passenger aisles
Typical seating200–850 passengers
First typeBoeing 747 (entered service 1970)

A wide-body aircraft, also known as a twin-aisle aircraft, is an airliner with a fuselage wide enough to accommodate two passenger aisles with seven or more seats abreast. Wide-body aircraft were developed in the late 1960s to carry large numbers of passengers on long routes, and to haul containerized cargo in the hold below the passenger deck.

History

The Boeing 747, entering service with Pan Am in 1970, was the first wide-body; its fuselage width of 6.5 metres allowed ten-abreast economy seating. It was followed by the McDonnell Douglas DC-10 and Lockheed L-1011 TriStar trijets. Airbus entered the market with the A300 in 1974, the first twin-engine wide-body. Later types include the Boeing 767, 777 and 787 Dreamliner, and the Airbus A330, A340, A350 and the double-deck A380 — the largest passenger aircraft ever built, seating up to 853 passengers in an all-economy layout.

Characteristics

Wide-bodies are used mainly on long-haul and high-density routes. Most have two aisles, large underfloor cargo holds accepting LD3 containers side by side, and ranges from about 8,000 to over 16,000 km. Modern designs use two fuel-efficient engines thanks to ETOPS rules permitting twinjets far from diversion airports, ending production of three- and four-engine types; the last 747 was delivered in 2023 and A380 production ended in 2021.
