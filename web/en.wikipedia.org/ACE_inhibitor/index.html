ACE inhibitor

Angiotensin-converting-enzyme inhibitors (ACE inhibitors) are a class of medication used primarily for the treatment of high blood pressure and heart failure. They work by inhibiting the angiotensin-converting enzyme, a component of the renin–angiotensin system, thereby relaxing blood vessels and reducing blood volume.

Mechanism

ACE converts angiotensin I to the potent vasoconstrictor angiotensin II and also breaks down bradykinin, a vasodilator. Blocking the enzyme lowers angiotensin II levels while raising bradykinin, producing vasodilation and reduced aldosterone secretion, which decreases sodium and water retention.

Examples and uses

Commonly prescribed ACE inhibitors include lisinopril, enalapril, ramipril, and benazepril, most ending in the suffix "-pril". Beyond hypertension and heart failure, they are used after myocardial infarction and to slow kidney damage in diabetes (diabetic nephropathy). The first drug of the class, captopril, was developed in the 1970s based on peptides found in the venom of the Brazilian pit viper Bothrops jararaca.

Adverse effects

Characteristic side effects include a persistent dry cough (attributed to bradykinin accumulation), hyperkalemia, dizziness, and, rarely, angioedema. ACE inhibitors are contraindicated in pregnancy because of fetotoxicity and should not be combined with ARBs in most patients.
