A bog, also called a quagmire in some contexts, is a wetland that accumulates peat, a deposit of dead plant material — often mosses, principally sphagnum. Bogs are one of the four main types of wetland and are characterized by being ombrotrophic (fed only by precipitation), acidic, and nutrient-poor. They occur mainly in the Northern Hemisphere, in boreal and cool temperate regions such as Canada, Russia, Scandinavia, Ireland, and Britain.

Formation and ecology

Bogs form where water at the ground surface is acidic and low in nutrients — typically where precipitation is high and drainage slow. Sphagnum mosses dominate: as they grow, they acidify their surroundings and exchange hydrogen ions for nutrients, suppressing competing plants and decay organisms. Partially decayed vegetation accumulates as peat, sometimes many metres deep over thousands of years. Raised bogs grow up into domes of peat above the water table; blanket bogs mantle the landscape in wet oceanic climates. Characteristic plants include cotton grass, bog rosemary, cranberry, and insectivorous plants such as sundews and pitcher plants, which supplement the nutrient-poor soil with captured insects.

Carbon and conservation

Peatlands cover only about 3% of Earth's land surface but store roughly a third of the world's soil carbon, making bogs among the most important terrestrial carbon stores. Draining bogs for agriculture, forestry, or peat extraction (for fuel and horticulture) releases that carbon and destroys a specialized habitat. Many countries now protect and restore bogs by re-wetting, which also helps water storage and flood control.

Archaeology

The acidic, anaerobic, antiseptic conditions of bogs preserve organic material extraordinarily well. Hundreds of "bog bodies" — naturally mummified human remains, such as Tollund Man and Grauballe Man in Denmark — have been recovered from European bogs, many dating to the Iron Age, alongside artifacts such as butter kegs, trackways, and weapons.
