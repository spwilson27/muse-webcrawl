French cuisine is the cooking traditions and practices of France, renowned worldwide for its emphasis on technique, quality ingredients, and the codification of culinary practice. In 2010, UNESCO added the "gastronomic meal of the French" to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, recognising the social ritual of the French meal — its sequence of courses, its pairing of food and wine, and its celebration of togetherness around the table.

History

French cooking developed from medieval banquet traditions influenced by Italian Renaissance cuisine, notably after Catherine de' Medici arrived in France in the 16th century bringing cooks and ingredients from Florence. In the 17th and 18th centuries, chefs such as François Pierre La Varenne, whose Le Cuisinier François (1651) marked a break with medieval spicing, laid the foundations of modern French technique.

After the French Revolution, chefs formerly employed by the aristocracy opened restaurants in Paris, democratising fine dining. Marie-Antoine Carême systematised grande cuisine in the early 19th century, and Auguste Escoffier later codified the brigade system and the five "mother sauces" in his Le Guide Culinaire (1903), which remains a cornerstone of professional training.

Nouvelle cuisine and beyond

In the 1970s, chefs including Paul Bocuse, Michel Guérard and the Troisgros brothers promoted nouvelle cuisine — lighter sauces, shorter cooking times, and an emphasis on presentation and fresh produce. French cuisine continues to evolve through bistronomy, fusion influences, and a renewed focus on regional and seasonal cooking.

Regional cuisines

France's regions contribute distinct specialities: butter-rich dishes and crêpes in Brittany; bouillabaisse and ratatouille in Provence; choucroute in Alsace; cassoulet in the south-west; and the charcuterie and quenelles of Lyon, often called the gastronomic capital of France. Cheese and wine appellations (AOC/AOP) protect hundreds of regional products.

Structure of meals

A traditional French meal proceeds from an apéritif through starter, main course, cheese, and dessert, accompanied by wine chosen to complement each course. Everyday meals are simpler but retain the sequence of courses and the convention that good food deserves time and company.
