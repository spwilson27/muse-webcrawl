Nuchal translucency

Nuchal translucency (NT) is the normal fluid-filled space under the skin at the back of a fetus's neck that can be measured using ultrasound in the first trimester of pregnancy. The nuchal translucency scan (NT scan) is a screening test offered between 11 weeks and 13 weeks + 6 days of gestation.

An increased nuchal translucency measurement is associated with a higher likelihood of chromosomal abnormalities such as Down syndrome (trisomy 21), Edwards syndrome (trisomy 18), and Patau syndrome (trisomy 13), as well as congenital heart defects and other structural abnormalities. The test is a screening test rather than a diagnostic one: an increased measurement raises the estimated risk but does not by itself diagnose a condition.

Technique

The scan is performed when the fetal crown–rump length is between 45 mm and 84 mm. The fetus is imaged in a mid-sagittal plane with the head in a neutral position, and the maximum thickness of the translucent space between the skin and the soft tissue overlying the cervical spine is measured with calipers. Accurate measurement requires trained operators and standardized technique, commonly accredited through programs such as that of the Fetal Medicine Foundation.

Interpretation

NT thickness normally increases with crown–rump length; a measurement above the 95th percentile for gestational age is considered increased. The NT result is often combined with maternal age and first-trimester serum markers — pregnancy-associated plasma protein A (PAPP-A) and free beta human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) — in the combined test, which detects roughly 85–90% of fetuses with Down syndrome at a false-positive rate of about 5%.

Follow-up

People with a high-risk screening result may be offered diagnostic testing such as chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis, non-invasive prenatal testing (cell-free DNA screening), a detailed anomaly scan, and fetal echocardiography. An increased NT with normal chromosomes still carries an increased risk of cardiac and other structural anomalies, so further imaging is usually recommended.
