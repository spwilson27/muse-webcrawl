Conditional sentence

Conditional sentences are sentences that express one thing as being dependent on another, e.g. "If it rains, the picnic will be cancelled." They typically consist of a conditional clause (the protasis), often introduced by if, and a main clause (the apodosis) stating the consequence. Conditional sentences are central to grammar teaching, logic and philosophy, where the corresponding logical form is called a material or counterfactual conditional.

Types in English

English-language teaching traditionally distinguishes four main patterns:

TypeProtasisApodosisExample
Zero conditionalpresent simplepresent simpleIf you heat ice, it melts.
First conditionalpresent simplewill + infinitiveIf it rains, we will stay home.
Second conditionalpast simplewould + infinitiveIf I won the lottery, I would travel.
Third conditionalpast perfectwould have + participleIf you had left earlier, you would have caught the train.

The zero and first conditionals generally concern real or likely situations, while the second and third express hypothetical or counterfactual ones — the past-tense forms in the second conditional signal remoteness from reality rather than past time, a usage linguists call the "modal past". Mixed conditionals combine patterns across time, as in "If you had saved money, you would be rich now."

Other forms

Conditions can also be introduced by unless ("unless it rains" = "if it does not rain"), provided that, as long as, in case, and by inversion without if: "Had I known, I would have acted" or "Should you need help, call me." Many languages mark conditionals with dedicated moods; Romance languages, for instance, use a conditional mood in the apodosis and often the subjunctive in the protasis.

In logic and philosophy

Logicians analyse conditionals as connectives between propositions. The material conditional of classical logic is true whenever the antecedent is false or the consequent is true, which produces well-known "paradoxes" at odds with ordinary usage. Counterfactual conditionals — about what would have happened — are studied through possible-worlds semantics associated with David Lewis and Robert Stalnaker.
