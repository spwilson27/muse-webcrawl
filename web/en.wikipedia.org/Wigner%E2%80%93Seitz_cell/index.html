Wigner–Seitz cell

The Wigner–Seitz cell, named after Eugene Wigner and Frederick Seitz, is a type of primitive cell used in solid-state physics to describe a crystal lattice. It is defined as the region of space closer to a given lattice point than to any other lattice point; equivalently, it is the Voronoi cell of the lattice. The Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice is known as the first Brillouin zone.

Construction

The cell is constructed by drawing lines from a lattice point to all nearby lattice points, then erecting perpendicular bisecting planes (in three dimensions) or lines (in two) at the midpoints of those lines. The smallest volume enclosed by these planes is the Wigner–Seitz cell. Unlike conventional primitive cells, it displays the full point-group symmetry of the lattice: for example, it is a square for a square lattice, a hexagon for a triangular lattice, a truncated octahedron for a body-centred cubic lattice, and a rhombic dodecahedron for a face-centred cubic lattice.

Properties and uses

Because it contains exactly one lattice point, the Wigner–Seitz cell has the same volume as any other primitive cell. It is central to the analysis of electronic band structure, where the first Brillouin zone — the Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice — is the fundamental domain for electron wavevectors.
