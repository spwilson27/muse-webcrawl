Location: Gauteng, South Africa
Length: ~56 km (35 mi)
Known for: Gold deposits; Johannesburg

Witwatersrand

The Witwatersrand (Afrikaans for "ridge of white waters"), usually shortened to the Rand, is a 56-kilometre-long, north-facing scarp in South Africa's Gauteng province. It consists of hard, erosion-resistant quartzite rock and forms the watershed between the Vaal River system flowing to the Atlantic and the Limpopo system flowing to the Indian Ocean.

Gold

The Witwatersrand Basin contains the world's largest known gold reserves, hosted in ancient conglomerate "reefs." Gold was discovered on the Rand in 1886 (the main reef near present-day Johannesburg), triggering a gold rush that founded Johannesburg and transformed South Africa's economy. The Witwatersrand has produced roughly a third to a half of all gold ever mined, and its mines — including some of the deepest on Earth, such as Mponeng — remain major producers.

Geology and history

The basin's sediments were deposited around 2.9 billion years ago; the nearby Vredefort impact structure, the largest verified impact crater on Earth, later tilted and preserved the gold-bearing strata. The Rand's wealth drew fortune-seekers from around the world, contributed to tensions leading to the Second Boer War (1899–1902), and shaped South Africa's industrial and political development, including the migrant labour system of the mines.
