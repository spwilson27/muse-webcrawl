Siege of Toulon

The siege of Toulon (29 August – 19 December 1793) was a Republican victory at the pro-royalist port city of Toulon during the French Revolutionary Wars. Royalist insurgents had handed the city and France's Mediterranean fleet to an Anglo-Spanish force. The young artillery officer Napoleon Bonaparte distinguished himself in planning the successful assault, launching his military career.

Background

In the summer of 1793, counter-revolutionary revolts broke out in southern France against the Jacobin government in Paris. Royalists in Toulon seized the city and invited in a British fleet under Admiral Lord Hood, along with Spanish and other allied troops, surrendering the French fleet in the harbour. Control of Toulon and its arsenal was a serious threat to the Republic.

The siege

Republican forces under General Carteaux, later replaced by General Dugommier, besieged the city. Napoleon, appointed to command the artillery, devised a plan to seize the fortified heights of the Petit Gibraltar and other forts commanding the harbour anchorages, from which French guns could drive off the allied fleet. On 17 December 1793, the decisive assault captured the key forts; Napoleon was wounded in the thigh during the attack. The allied fleet evacuated, burning many French ships and the arsenal as it withdrew, and Republican troops retook the city on 19 December.

Aftermath

For his role, Napoleon was promoted to brigadier general at the age of 24. The retaken city suffered reprisals, including mass executions of suspected royalists, and was temporarily renamed Port-la-Montagne. Much of the French Mediterranean fleet was destroyed or carried off, though many ships were later recovered.
