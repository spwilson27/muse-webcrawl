Extremophile

An extremophile (from Latin extremus, "extreme", and Greek philia, "love") is an organism that thrives in physically or geochemically extreme conditions detrimental to most life on Earth. Most extremophiles are single-celled prokaryotes — bacteria and archaea — though multicellular examples exist, including tardigrades and the Pompeii worm.

Types

Thermophiles and hyperthermophiles grow at high temperatures, some above 100 °C in pressurized deep-sea vent water; psychrophiles favour intense cold. Acidophiles and alkaliphiles tolerate extreme pH; halophiles require high salt; piezophiles (barophiles) withstand crushing pressure in the deep ocean; xerophiles survive near-total desiccation; and radioresistant organisms such as Deinococcus radiodurans endure radiation doses thousands of times the lethal human dose. Polyextremophiles combine several tolerances.

Significance

Extremophiles transformed biology's picture of where life can exist and, by extension, where it might exist elsewhere — they are central models in the search for extraterrestrial life on Mars and icy moons. They are also industrially useful: the DNA polymerase Taq, isolated from the hot-spring bacterium Thermus aquaticus, made the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) practical, and extremozymes are used in detergents, biofuels, and food processing.
