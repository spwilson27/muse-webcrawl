Five Emperors

The Five Emperors (Chinese: 五帝; pinyin: Wǔ Dì) are legendary sage-rulers of ancient China, placed in traditional chronology in the third millennium BC, after the Three Sovereigns and before the Xia dynasty.

The five

Lists vary, but the most common grouping — given by Sima Qian in the Records of the Grand Historian — comprises the Yellow Emperor (Huangdi), Zhuanxu, Emperor Ku, Emperor Yao, and Emperor Shun. Other sources substitute figures such as Shaohao or Emperor Zhi.

Role in tradition

The Five Emperors were credited with foundational inventions and institutions of Chinese civilisation: agriculture, writing, the calendar, medicine, and moral government. Yao and Shun in particular became Confucian exemplars of meritocratic succession, each abdicating in favour of the most worthy candidate rather than his own son. Modern historians generally regard the Five Emperors as mythic or euhemerised figures rather than historical kings.
