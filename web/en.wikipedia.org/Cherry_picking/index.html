Cherry picking

Cherry picking, in its figurative sense, is the act of pointing to individual cases or data that seem to confirm a particular position while ignoring a significant portion of related and similar cases or data that may contradict it. It is a kind of fallacy of selective attention, a form of confirmation bias. The term is based on the real process of harvesting fruit: a cherry picker is expected to select only the ripest and healthiest fruits, so an observer who sees only the picked fruit may form a false impression of the quality of the whole crop.

In argument and science

Cherry picking is a common feature of pseudoscientific and denialist argumentation, in which inconvenient data are omitted and only favorable findings are quoted. In scientific debate the practice is related to the fallacy of incomplete evidence and to the Texas sharpshooter fallacy, where patterns are drawn around selected data points after the fact.

In finance and law

In securities trading, cherry picking can describe the practice of allocating profitable trades to favored accounts after the fact, a fraudulent practice prohibited by regulators. In bankruptcy and contract law, the term is used for selectively assuming beneficial contracts while rejecting burdensome ones.

In software development

In version control systems such as Git, a cherry-pick is the operation of applying a specific commit from one branch to another, rather than merging an entire branch.
