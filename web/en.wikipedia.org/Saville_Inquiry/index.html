Saville Inquiry

The Saville Inquiry was a public inquiry in the United Kingdom into the events of Bloody Sunday, 30 January 1972, when British paratroopers shot 26 unarmed civilians during a civil rights march in Derry, Northern Ireland, killing 13 outright and injuring the rest; a fourteenth victim died months later. Chaired by Lord Saville of Newdigate, the inquiry was established in 1998 by Prime Minister Tony Blair and reported on 15 June 2010. At 12 years and roughly £195 million, it was the longest-running and most expensive public inquiry in British legal history.

Background

The original 1972 investigation, the Widgery Tribunal, had largely exonerated the soldiers within weeks, finding that some firing "bordered on the reckless" but broadly accepting the Army's account that it had responded to gunmen and nail bombers. Nationalists denounced the report as a whitewash, and the families of the dead campaigned for decades for a fresh inquiry. Following the Northern Ireland peace process, Blair announced a new inquiry on 29 January 1998, saying the evidence should be re-examined "in accordance with the standards of today."

Proceedings

Mark Saville, Baron Saville of Newdigate, a law lord, chaired the tribunal, sitting with William Hoyt of Canada and John Toohey of Australia. The inquiry heard evidence in Derry's Guildhall and, for the testimony of soldiers and security officials, in London. It took statements from some 2,500 witnesses, called 922 to give oral evidence—including former prime minister Edward Heath—and amassed an archive of 160 volumes of evidence, 5,000 photographs, and 30 million words of testimony.

Findings

The report, published in ten volumes on 15 June 2010, concluded that none of those killed or wounded was armed with a firearm or bomb, that no warning was given before soldiers opened fire, that the first shots were fired by the Army, and that soldiers had "lost control" and subsequently lied about their actions. It exonerated the marchers entirely and found the killings "both unjustified and unjustifiable." Prime Minister David Cameron responded in the House of Commons with an apology on behalf of the government: "On behalf of our country, I am deeply sorry." Prosecutors later charged one former soldier, "Soldier F," with murder, but the case was dropped in 2021; an attempt to revive the prosecution continued in the courts.
