Chorale cantata

A chorale cantata is a church cantata based on a chorale — in its typical form, a Lutheran hymn — in which both the text and the melody of the hymn supply the material for the musical setting. The genre flourished in German Baroque music, especially in the works of Johann Sebastian Bach.

Structure

In a typical chorale cantata, the first and last movements use the first and last stanzas of the hymn verbatim. The opening movement is usually an elaborate chorale fantasia, in which the hymn tune is sung in long notes by one voice part (the cantus firmus) while the other voices and instruments elaborate freely. The closing movement is a straightforward four-part harmonisation of the hymn tune. The inner movements — recitatives, arias, and sometimes duets or choruses — paraphrase the intervening stanzas or present free poetic reflections on them, though the hymn melody may recur in instrumental lines.

History

The chorale cantata developed in the late seventeenth century, in works by composers such as Dieterich Buxtehude and Johann Pachelbel. Its most famous flowering is Bach's second annual cycle of cantatas, composed in Leipzig in 1724–25, often called the chorale cantata cycle, in which nearly every cantata is built on the hymn appointed for the Sunday or feast day. Composers such as Georg Philipp Telemann and Christoph Graupner also wrote numerous chorale cantatas.

Bach's chorale cantatas

Well-known examples among Bach's more than fifty surviving chorale cantatas include Christ lag in Todesbanden, BWV 4 (an early work that sets every stanza verbatim), Wachet auf, ruft uns die Stimme, BWV 140, and Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott, BWV 80, based on Martin Luther's most famous hymn.
