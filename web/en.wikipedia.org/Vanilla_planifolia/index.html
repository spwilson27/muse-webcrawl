Vanilla planifolia

Vanilla planifolia, commonly known as the flat-leaved vanilla, is a species of vanilla orchid native to Mexico and Central America. It is the primary source of the vanilla flavouring known as Bourbon vanilla (when produced on Réunion, formerly Île Bourbon) and Mexican vanilla, and is one of the most economically important orchids in the world.

Description

Vanilla planifolia is a fleshy, evergreen climbing vine that can grow to considerable lengths, scrambling over trees by means of aerial roots. Its stems are green, succulent and branching, with alternate, flat, oval leaves. The short-lived yellow-green flowers are about 10 cm across and open for only a few hours on a single morning. In the plant's native range the flowers are pollinated by stingless bees of the genus Melipona; elsewhere, commercial production depends on hand pollination, a method developed in 1841 by Edmond Albius, an enslaved twelve-year-old boy on Réunion.

Cultivation and production

The fruit, a long capsule commonly called a vanilla bean or pod, takes eight to nine months to mature. Fresh pods have little aroma; the characteristic flavour develops during a lengthy curing process of blanching, sweating and slow drying, during which glucovanillin is converted to vanillin. Major producers include Madagascar, Indonesia, Mexico, and the Comoros. Madagascar typically accounts for the majority of world production.

Uses

Vanilla extract prepared from the cured pods is used worldwide in food flavouring, perfumery and aromatherapy. Synthetic vanillin, produced from lignin or petrochemical precursors, supplies most of the vanilla flavour market, but natural vanilla remains prized for its complex flavour profile of several hundred compounds.
