Cavity quantum electrodynamics

Cavity quantum electrodynamics (cavity QED) is the study of the interaction between light confined in a reflective cavity and atoms or other particles, under conditions where the quantum nature of light photons is significant. It explores how a cavity alters an atom's spontaneous emission and enables coherent exchange of excitation between atom and field.

Principles

Confining electromagnetic modes in a small, high-quality (high-Q) cavity increases the electric field per photon and hence the atom–field coupling. In the strong-coupling regime, an excited atom and a cavity photon exchange energy coherently many times before losses dominate, producing vacuum Rabi oscillations and split spectral lines (vacuum Rabi splitting). In the weak-coupling regime, the cavity instead modifies emission rates — the Purcell effect.

Experiments

Pioneering microwave cavity QED experiments by Serge Haroche's group used Rydberg atoms passing through superconducting cavities; optical cavity QED uses atoms trapped near tiny Fabry–Pérot resonators, and circuit QED extends the ideas to superconducting qubits coupled to microwave resonators. Haroche shared the 2012 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work.

Applications

Cavity and circuit QED are leading platforms for quantum computing, quantum networks, single-photon sources, and precision tests of quantum mechanics.
