Arabic

Arabic (اَلْعَرَبِيَّةُ, al-ʿarabiyyah) is a Central Semitic language spoken primarily across the Arab world. With an estimated 380 million native speakers and many more who use it as a liturgical or second language, it is one of the most widely spoken languages in the world and one of the six official languages of the United Nations.

Arabic is written from right to left in a cursive script of 28 letters. It is the liturgical language of Islam, being the language of the Quran, and has influenced many other languages, lending vocabulary to Persian, Turkish, Urdu, Spanish, and numerous others.

Varieties

Modern Standard Arabic, derived from Classical Arabic, is used in formal writing, education, and media across the Arab world. Spoken Arabic, however, consists of many regional dialects — including Egyptian, Levantine, Gulf, Maghrebi, and Mesopotamian varieties — which can differ substantially from one another, a situation known as diglossia.

History

Arabic emerged in northwestern Arabia in the early centuries CE. Following the early Muslim conquests of the 7th century, it spread across the Middle East and North Africa, eventually supplanting many local languages such as Coptic and Aramaic in everyday use. The standardization of Classical Arabic is traditionally associated with the Quran and the work of grammarians such as Sibawayh.

Grammar

Arabic morphology is based on a system of consonantal roots, typically three consonants, from which words are derived by inserting vowel patterns. The language has two genders, three cases in the classical language, and a dual number in addition to singular and plural.
