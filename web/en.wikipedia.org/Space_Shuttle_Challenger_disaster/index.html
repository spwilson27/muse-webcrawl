The Space Shuttle Challenger disaster occurred on 28 January 1986, when the NASA Space Shuttle orbiter Challenger broke apart 73 seconds into its flight on mission STS-51-L, killing all seven crew members aboard. The disaster was one of the worst accidents in the history of space exploration.

Cause

The Rogers Commission, appointed by President Ronald Reagan, determined that the accident was caused by the failure of rubber O-ring seals in the right solid rocket booster, whose resiliency was compromised by the unusually cold weather on the morning of the launch. Hot gas escaped through the failed joint and breached the external fuel tank, leading to the vehicle's structural breakup. Engineers at contractor Morton Thiokol had warned against launching in such cold temperatures, but their concerns were overridden in a flawed decision-making process that became a famous case study in engineering ethics and organisational failure. Physicist Richard Feynman's televised demonstration of the O-ring's loss of elasticity in ice water became an iconic moment of the investigation.

Crew and aftermath

The crew comprised commander Dick Scobee, pilot Michael Smith, mission specialists Ellison Onizuka, Judith Resnik and Ronald McNair, payload specialist Gregory Jarvis, and Christa McAuliffe, a New Hampshire schoolteacher chosen for the Teacher in Space Project, whose presence had drawn intense public attention and a large live audience of schoolchildren. Shuttle flights were suspended for 32 months while the boosters were redesigned and NASA's safety culture was overhauled; the programme resumed with Discovery on STS-26 in September 1988.
