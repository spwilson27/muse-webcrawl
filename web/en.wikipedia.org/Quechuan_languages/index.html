The Quechuan languages form a family of Indigenous languages of the Andes, spoken from southern Colombia to northern Argentina. With several million speakers, Quechua is the most widely spoken Indigenous language family in the Americas. The varieties are agglutinative, use subject–object–verb word order, and mark evidentiality grammatically.

Classification

Linguists generally divide the family into Central Quechua (Quechua I), spoken in the Peruvian highlands, and Peripheral Quechua (Quechua II), which includes the southern varieties of Ayacucho and Cusco, Bolivian Quechua, Ecuadorian Kichwa, and the extinct Classical Quechua of the Inca and colonial periods. Mutual intelligibility between distant varieties can be limited.

History

The family is thought to have originated in the central Peruvian highlands and expanded under the Wari and later Inca states. The Inca adopted a Cusco-based variety as an administrative lingua franca. Spanish colonial authorities first promoted a standardized Quechua, then later suppressed it after the Túpac Amaru II rebellion of 1780–81.

Status

Quechua is co-official in Peru and Bolivia and recognized in Ecuador. Despite large speaker numbers, many varieties are losing speakers to Spanish, and revitalization efforts include bilingual education, media, and standardized orthographies.
