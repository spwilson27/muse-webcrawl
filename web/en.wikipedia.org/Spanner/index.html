Spanner (wrench)

A spanner (in British English) or wrench (in North American English) is a tool used to provide grip and mechanical advantage in applying torque to turn objects — usually rotary fasteners, such as nuts and bolts — or keep them from turning. The most common shapes are called open-ended spanner and box-ended spanner. In American English, wrench refers to all types, although a monkey wrench usually designates an adjustable wrench.

Types

  • Open-ended spanner — a one-piece tool with a U-shaped opening that grips two opposite faces of a bolt or nut.
  • Box-ended (ring) spanner — an enclosed loop, usually with six or twelve internal points, that grips all faces of the fastener.
  • Combination spanner — open-ended at one end and box-ended at the other, for the same size.
  • Adjustable spanner/wrench — one jaw is movable via a worm gear, allowing one tool to fit many sizes.
  • Socket wrench — a ratchet handle with detachable sockets.
  • Torque wrench — applies a precisely measured torque.
  • Allen key (hex key) — for bolts with hexagonal sockets.
  • Pipe wrench — serrated jaws for gripping round pipe.

History

Wrenches have been made since at least the 15th century. The adjustable wrench with a worm-gear jaw was patented in the 19th century by inventors including the English engineer Richard Clyburn (1842) and the Swedish inventor Johan Petter Johansson, whose 1891–92 patents produced the modern adjustable wrench manufactured by Bahco. Coach wrenches were used for the square nuts on wagon and carriage wheels.

Sizes

Spanners are sized by the width across the flats of the fastener they fit, in millimetres for metric tools or fractions of an inch for imperial (SAE) sizes. Using a correctly sized spanner prevents rounding the corners of the fastener.
