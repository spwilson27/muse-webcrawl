Primary progressive aphasia

Primary progressive aphasia
Specialty: Neurology
Symptoms: Progressive loss of language abilities
Usual onset: Mid-life, typically 50s–60s

Primary progressive aphasia (PPA) is a type of neurological syndrome in which language abilities become slowly and progressively impaired. As with other types of aphasia, the symptoms that accompany PPA depend on what parts of the brain's language network are significantly damaged. However, unlike many other forms of aphasia, PPA results from neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease and frontotemporal lobar degeneration rather than from stroke or head injury.

Classification

Consensus criteria published in 2011 distinguish three main variants:

  • Semantic variant (svPPA) — loss of word meaning and object knowledge, fluent speech with poor naming and comprehension of single words; associated with anterior temporal lobe atrophy.
  • Nonfluent/agrammatic variant (nfvPPA) — effortful, halting speech and agrammatism, often with apraxia of speech; associated with posterior frontal and insular atrophy.
  • Logopenic variant (lvPPA) — word-finding difficulty and impaired sentence repetition with relatively preserved grammar and word meaning; often underpinned by Alzheimer's pathology.

History

The syndrome was described in modern form by the neurologist Marsel Mesulam, who reported patients with slowly progressive aphasia in the early 1980s and championed the term "primary progressive aphasia". Earlier descriptions of similar presentations date to Arnold Pick and others in the late nineteenth century.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis rests on clinical language assessment and neuroimaging showing atrophy or hypometabolism in language-dominant regions. There is no disease-modifying cure; management centres on speech-language therapy, compensatory communication strategies, and support for patients and families.
