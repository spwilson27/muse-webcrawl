Solstice

OccursTwice yearly, around 20–22 June and 20–23 December
CauseMaximum tilt of Earth's axis toward or away from the Sun (23.44°)
EffectsLongest and shortest days of the year
RelatedEquinox; seasons

A solstice is an event that occurs when the Sun reaches its most northerly or southerly excursion relative to the celestial equator on the celestial sphere, owing to the tilt of the Earth's axis. Two solstices occur annually, around 21 June and 21 December. At the June solstice the North Pole is tilted most toward the Sun, giving the Northern Hemisphere its longest day and marking the start of astronomical summer there; the reverse holds in December.

Names and geometry

The word derives from Latin solstitium, "the Sun stands still", because the Sun's apparent north–south movement pauses before reversing. At noon on the June solstice the Sun stands directly over the Tropic of Cancer (23.44° N); on the December solstice over the Tropic of Capricorn. Within the polar circles the Sun may not set at all (midnight sun) or may not rise (polar night).

Cultural significance

Solstices have been marked by festivals and monuments since prehistory. Stonehenge aligns with the solstitial sunrise; midsummer celebrations such as Juhannus and Saint John's Eve honour the June solstice in northern Europe, while the winter solstice is celebrated in festivals including Dongzhi, Yalda Night and the Roman Saturnalia, and is associated with the timing of Christmas.
