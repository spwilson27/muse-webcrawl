EDSAC

The Electronic Delay Storage Automatic Calculator (EDSAC) was an early British computer built at the University of Cambridge Mathematical Laboratory by Maurice Wilkes and his team. Inspired by John von Neumann's draft report on the EDVAC, EDSAC ran its first successful program on 6 May 1949 and is generally recognised as the first practical stored-program computer to provide a regular computing service to researchers.

Design

EDSAC used mercury delay lines for its main memory, storing 512 words of 17 bits (usable as 1,024 words in a later configuration), and used about 3,000 vacuum tubes (valves). Programs were input on paper tape. Instructions were represented in a single-address format, and the machine's "initial orders" — an early form of bootstrap program assembled by David Wheeler — allowed programmers to write symbolic code, making EDSAC a pioneer of practical programming methods. Wheeler, Wilkes and Stanley Gill wrote the influential 1951 book The Preparation of Programs for an Electronic Digital Computer, the first textbook on programming.

Use and legacy

EDSAC supported research across the university, including calculations in astronomy, crystallography and economics. It was the machine on which some of the earliest video games and subroutine libraries were developed, and its team developed the concept of the subroutine library and the debugging techniques of the era. EDSAC was shut down in 1958 after being replaced by EDSAC 2. A working replica project based at The National Museum of Computing at Bletchley Park has reconstructed the machine.
