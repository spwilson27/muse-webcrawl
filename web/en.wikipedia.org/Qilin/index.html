Qilin

The qilin (Chinese: 麒麟; pinyin: qílín) is a legendary hooved chimerical creature from Chinese mythology, said to appear with the imminent arrival or passing of a sage or illustrious ruler. It is one of the Four Auspicious Beasts, alongside the dragon, phoenix, and tortoise, and is often called the "Chinese unicorn," though it is frequently depicted with two horns.

Description

Descriptions vary by era. In older texts the qilin resembles a deer with an ox's tail, a horse's hooves, and a single horn; Ming and Qing depictions give it a dragon-like head, horns, flowing mane, scaled body, and flames or clouds at its joints. The qilin is a gentle, benevolent creature: it is said to walk without harming even grass or insects, to live for a thousand years, and to symbolize serenity, longevity, prosperity, and good government. Its appearance is an omen of a wise ruler; Confucius's mother was said to have encountered one before his birth, and the capture of a qilin in 481 BCE traditionally marks the end of the Spring and Autumn Annals.

Giraffes and the qilin

During the treasure voyages of Zheng He, giraffes brought from East Africa to the Ming court in 1414 were identified as qilin, since the Somali word for giraffe, giri, resembled the name, and their gentle nature and ossicones matched descriptions. Court paintings of the animals as qilin survive.

Variants in other cultures

The creature was adopted into neighboring mythologies as the Korean girin, the Japanese kirin (which ranks foremost among holy beasts and gives its name to a major brewery), and the Vietnamese kỳ lân, one of Vietnam's four sacred animals. In modern popular culture the qilin appears in films, games, and festivals, including the lion-like dances of southern China.
