Enkephalin

The enkephalins are a family of endogenous opioid peptides that bind to the body's opioid receptors and help regulate pain, mood, and other functions. They are pentapeptides — chains of five amino acids — and come in two main forms: Met-enkephalin (with methionine at the C-terminus) and Leu-enkephalin (with leucine). They were discovered in 1975 by John Hughes, Hans Kosterlitz, and colleagues, who isolated them from pig brain while searching for an endogenous ligand for the opiate receptor.

Biology

Both enkephalins are cleaved from the precursor protein proenkephalin, encoded by the PENK gene, which contains several copies of Met-enkephalin and one of Leu-enkephalin. Enkephalins are widely distributed in the central and peripheral nervous systems and the adrenal medulla. They act preferentially at delta opioid receptors (and also at mu receptors) and are rapidly broken down by enzymes called enkephalinases, including neprilysin and aminopeptidase N, which limits their duration of action.

Function

Enkephalin-containing interneurons in the spinal cord gate the transmission of pain signals, and the peptides also modulate stress responses, reward, gastrointestinal motility, and immune function. Inhibitors of enkephalin-degrading enzymes have been investigated as analgesics that might relieve pain with fewer side effects than conventional opioids; the antidiarrheal drug racecadotril acts by this mechanism.
