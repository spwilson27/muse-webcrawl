Paradise Lost

Paradise Lost Author: John Milton
First published: 1667
Language: English
Genre: Epic poem
Form: Blank verse; ten books (1667), twelve books (1674)
Subject: The Fall of Man

Paradise Lost is an epic poem in blank verse by the 17th-century English poet John Milton (1608–1674). The first version, published in 1667, consists of ten books with over ten thousand lines of verse; a second edition followed in 1674, arranged into twelve books (in the manner of Virgil's Aeneid) with minor revisions throughout. It is considered to be Milton's masterpiece, and it helped solidify his reputation as one of the greatest English poets of all time.

The poem concerns the biblical story of the Fall of Man: the temptation of Adam and Eve by the fallen angel Satan and their expulsion from the Garden of Eden. Milton's purpose, stated in Book I, is to "justify the ways of God to men."

Synopsis

The poem opens in Hell, where Satan and his legions, defeated in the war in Heaven, recover from their fall into the burning lake and resolve to corrupt God's new creation. Satan volunteers to journey through Chaos to the newly created world, escapes Hell past Sin and Death, and finds Earth. In Paradise he overhears Adam and Eve discussing God's prohibition against eating from the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil. After a first failed attempt interrupted by angels, Satan returns in the form of a serpent and tempts Eve with flattery and arguments; she eats the fruit, then offers it to Adam, who eats knowingly out of love for her. The final books recount the judgement, the Archangel Michael's revelation to Adam of humanity's future history, and the couple's expulsion from Paradise, "hand in hand, with wandering steps and slow."

Composition and publication

Milton, blind since 1652, composed the poem by dictation to amanuenses and family members. He sold the copyright to the publisher Samuel Simmons in 1667 for £5, with a further £5 contingent on sales of each of three editions — the first known copyright agreement between author and publisher of its kind. Though the poem sold steadily, its critical reputation grew over the following century; by the 18th century it was regarded as a classic, and its characterisation of Satan profoundly influenced the Romantic poets, William Blake declaring Milton "of the Devil's party without knowing it."

Themes

The poem explores free will and predestination, obedience and rebellion, marriage and hierarchy, and the problem of evil. Its theology draws on Milton's heterodox Puritan convictions, including anti-trinitarian leanings and an emphasis on the responsibility of the rational creature. The sequel, Paradise Regained (1671), treats Christ's temptation in the wilderness.
