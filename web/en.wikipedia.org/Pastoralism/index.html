Pastoralism

Pastoralism is a form of animal husbandry where domesticated animals (known as "livestock") are released onto large vegetated outdoor lands (pastures) for grazing, historically by nomadic people who moved around with their herds. Pastoralism generally, though not always, is a subsistence strategy involving the herding of animals such as cattle, sheep, goats, camels, yaks, llamas, and reindeer, and it remains the livelihood of millions of people in arid and mountainous regions where crop agriculture is unreliable.

Forms

Pastoralism varies along a spectrum of mobility. In pastoral nomadism, entire households move with their herds in irregular patterns without permanent dwellings. In transhumance, part of the community moves seasonally between fixed summer and winter pastures while others remain in permanent settlements, as in the Alps and around the Mediterranean. Sedentary ranching, as in North and South America and Australia, keeps herds on large fenced or open-range holdings from a fixed base.

History and distribution

Pastoralism arose in the Neolithic, after the first domestication of sheep and goats roughly 10,000 years ago, and spread through the grasslands of Eurasia and Africa. Pastoral societies produced milk, meat, wool, hides, transport, and mobile wealth, and they frequently interacted — through trade, raiding, and conquest — with neighbouring agricultural states. Famous pastoralist peoples include the Maasai of East Africa, the Mongols of the Central Asian steppe, the Bedouin of Arabia, and the Sami reindeer herders of northern Europe. Today pastoralists face pressure from land enclosure, borders, climate change, and policies favouring sedentarization.
