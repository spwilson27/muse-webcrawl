The Ferriby Boats are three Bronze Age sewn-plank boats discovered on the foreshore of the Humber Estuary at North Ferriby in the East Riding of Yorkshire, England. They are among the oldest known plank-built boats in Europe and provide crucial evidence for early seafaring and boatbuilding technology in prehistoric Britain. The oldest of the three, Ferriby Boat 1, has been dated to around 2000 BC, making it one of the earliest sewn boats found anywhere in the world.

Discovery

The first boat was found in 1937 by Ted Wright, a local man walking on the Humber foreshore, who noticed oak timbers exposed in the mud. His brother, the archaeologist E. V. (Ted) Wright, recognised their significance and organised their excavation and recording. A second boat (Ferriby 2) was found nearby, and a third, fragmentary vessel (Ferriby 3) was discovered in 1963. The boats had been preserved in the anaerobic blue clay of the estuary foreshore.

Construction

The Ferriby boats were built from massive oak planks. Ferriby Boat 1 was constructed from three bottom planks and two side strakes on each side, giving an original length estimated at about 13 to 16 metres (roughly 43–52 feet) and a beam of about 2.6 metres. Individual planks were up to 10 centimetres thick. The planks were joined edge-to-edge with stitches of twisted withies (yew or similar flexible wood), and the seams were caulked with moss and capped with thin oak laths or cleats to make them watertight. There was no keel in the modern sense, no ribs or frames originally, and no evidence of a mast or sail: the boats were probably propelled by paddles, with a crew that may have numbered around eighteen for the largest vessel.

Significance

The Ferriby boats demonstrate that sophisticated plank boatbuilding existed in Britain by the early second millennium BC, centuries earlier than once supposed. Their size and construction suggest they were capable of carrying substantial cargoes — possibly including metalwork, pottery and other goods — and of making crossings of the North Sea or voyages along the coast, linking Britain with continental Europe. They are comparable in technique, though not identical, to the later Dover Bronze Age Boat (c. 1550 BC) and form a key part of the evidence for Bronze Age maritime trade. Radiocarbon dating and dendrochronology have refined the chronology of the boats, with Ferriby 1 dating to approximately 2030–1780 BC.

Preservation and display

Remains of the Ferriby boats are held by the Hull and East Riding Museum in Hull, where timbers from the boats are displayed. Because the fragile waterlogged oak could not all be conserved, much of the archaeological value of the find lies in the detailed records made at excavation. Full-size and scale reconstructions have been proposed and modelled to test the boats' seaworthiness, indicating that they were capable and stable cargo carriers for their time.
