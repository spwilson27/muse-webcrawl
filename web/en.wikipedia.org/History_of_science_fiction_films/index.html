History of science fiction films

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of science fiction films stretches from the earliest days of cinema—Georges Méliès's A Trip to the Moon (1902) is generally regarded as the first science fiction film—through the expressionist masterpieces of the silent era, the monster and invasion films of the 1950s, the genre's elevation to prestige filmmaking in the late 1960s and 1970s, and the blockbuster era dominated by franchises such as Star Wars. Science fiction film has consistently served both as popular spectacle driven by special-effects innovation and as a vehicle for commentary on science, technology, and society.

Silent era

Méliès combined trick photography with stories adapted from Jules Verne and H. G. Wells in films such as A Trip to the Moon (1902) and The Impossible Voyage (1904). The genre's first feature-length landmarks were European: Fritz Lang's Metropolis (1927), a vision of a mechanised future city, and Woman in the Moon (1929); the Soviet Aelita (1924); and Abel Gance's and others' experiments. Lang's work established the visual vocabulary of the cinematic future.

1930s–1940s

Hollywood science fiction leaned on serials (Flash Gordon, 1936; Buck Rogers) and prestige adaptations such as Things to Come (1936), written by H. G. Wells. Horror and science fiction overlapped in Universal's monster films, and the genre contracted during the Second World War, though it produced notable works such as Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde variants and wartime fantasies.

The 1950s boom

The atomic age and the Cold War produced the genre's first golden age: Destination Moon (1950), The Day the Earth Stood Still (1951), The Thing from Another World (1951), The War of the Worlds (1953), Them! (1954), Forbidden Planet (1956), Invasion of the Body Snatchers (1956), and Britain's Quatermass films. Themes of alien invasion, mutation, and nuclear anxiety mirrored public fears, while in Japan Ishirō Honda's Godzilla (1954) channelled the trauma of Hiroshima and the Lucky Dragon incident into the kaiju genre.

1960s–1970s: maturity

Stanley Kubrick's 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968) redefined the genre's artistic and technical ambitions, and Planet of the Apes (1968) demonstrated its allegorical power. The 1970s combined dystopian visions—A Clockwork Orange (1971), Solaris (1972, Tarkovsky), Silent Running (1972), Soylent Green (1973)—with the watershed year 1977, when Star Wars and Close Encounters of the Third Kind transformed science fiction into the dominant blockbuster genre and revolutionised special effects through companies such as Industrial Light & Magic. Ridley Scott's Alien (1979) fused science fiction with horror.

1980s–1990s: blockbuster dominance

The 1980s delivered a string of enduring hits—The Empire Strikes Back (1980), E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial (1982), Blade Runner (1982), The Terminator (1984), Back to the Future (1985), Aliens (1986), RoboCop (1987)—while cyberpunk and anime milestones such as Akira (1988) expanded the genre's range. In the 1990s, CGI transformed the field: Terminator 2: Judgment Day (1991) and Jurassic Park (1993) set new standards, and Independence Day (1996), Men in Black (1997), and The Matrix (1999) dominated the box office.

21st century

Franchise science fiction—superhero crossovers, Star Wars sequels, Avatar (2009, long the highest-grossing film ever)—coexists with a revival of "hard" and auteur science fiction: Children of Men (2006), District 9 (2009), Gravity (2013), Interstellar (2014), Ex Machina (2014), Arrival (2016), and Dune (2021). Non-English-language production, Chinese blockbusters such as The Wandering Earth (2019), and streaming platforms have globalised the genre further.
