Pacific temperate rain forests

Extent: Kodiak Island, Alaska, to northern California

Climate: Oceanic; 1,500–5,000+ mm annual precipitation

Dominant trees: Sitka spruce, western hemlock, Douglas-fir, western redcedar, coast redwood

The Pacific temperate rain forests are the largest temperate rain forest biome in the world, stretching along the Pacific coast of North America from Kodiak Island and Prince William Sound in Alaska, through the coast of British Columbia — including the Great Bear Rainforest — to the redwood forests of northern California. The ecoregions are characterised by very high rainfall, mild oceanic temperatures, and forests dominated by enormous, long-lived conifers.

Ecology

Rainfall commonly exceeds 2,000 mm a year and fog drip adds further moisture, while temperatures are moderated by the ocean — winters are mild and summers cool, with a narrow annual range. The dominant canopy trees include Sitka spruce, western hemlock, Douglas-fir, western redcedar and (in the south) coast redwood, some individuals exceeding 90 metres and living more than a thousand years. The shaded, constantly moist interior supports thick moss mats, ferns such as sword fern, shrubs like salmonberry and huckleberry, and a rich community of epiphytes; fallen "nurse logs" shelter seedling trees in rows. These forests store exceptional amounts of carbon per hectare.

Wildlife includes black and grizzly bears — including the white-phased "spirit bear" (Kermode bear) of the central British Columbia coast — wolves, Sitka black-tailed deer, mountain goats, marbled murrelets (which nest on mossy old-growth branches) and bald eagles, while the region's rivers host all five species of Pacific salmon, whose spawning runs carry marine nutrients deep into the forest.

Subdivisions

The World Wide Fund for Nature divides the belt into ecoregions including the Northern Pacific coastal forests (Alaska panhandle and Haida Gwaii), the British Columbia mainland coastal forests, the Central Pacific coastal forests (Vancouver Island and the Olympic Peninsula, including the Hoh Rainforest), the Puget lowland forests, the Cascades forests and the Klamath-Siskiyou and California coastal forests in the south.

Threats and conservation

Industrial logging converted much of the lowland old growth to second-growth plantations through the 20th century; in the United States, the northern spotted owl controversy led to the 1994 Northwest Forest Plan sharply reducing federal old-growth logging, while in British Columbia conflict over Clayoquot Sound (1993) and the Great Bear Rainforest agreements (2006–2016) moved large areas toward ecosystem-based management and Indigenous-led protection. Remaining threats include fragmentation, climate change and pressure from development; the forests are home to many First Nations and Alaska Native peoples whose stewardship long predates conservation designations.
