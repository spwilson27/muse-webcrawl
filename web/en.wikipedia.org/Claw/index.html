Claw

A claw is a curved, pointed appendage found at the end of a toe or finger in many amniote animals, including most mammals, birds, and some reptiles. An analogous structure is the chela of arthropods. Claws may be adapted for scratching, gripping, digging, climbing, or killing prey, and are made of the hard protein keratin, the same material found in nails, hooves, and horns.

Structure and growth

Claws grow continuously from a vascular bed of tissue at their base, much like fingernails in primates. In many mammals the claw sheathes a bony core, the ungual phalanx. Worn or damaged claws are replaced by growth from below, and animals such as cats keep their claws sharp by scratching, which removes the worn outer sheath.

Functions

Claws serve a wide variety of functions. Predatory mammals and raptorial birds use sharp claws (talons) to seize and subdue prey. Burrowing animals such as moles and aardvarks have broad, spade-like claws for digging. Arboreal species rely on hooked claws for climbing, while ground-dwelling species may have blunter claws suited to traction and defence.

In different groups

In birds of prey the claws are called talons. In arthropods, the pincer-like chelae of crabs and lobsters are often called claws. Among mammals, primates mostly have flat nails instead of claws, though some strepsirrhines retain a grooming claw. The largest claws relative to body size are found in animals such as the giant anteater, which uses them to tear open termite mounds.
