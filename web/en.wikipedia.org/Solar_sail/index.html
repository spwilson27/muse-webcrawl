Solar sail

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Artist's concept of a solar sail spacecraft

Solar sails (also called light sails or photon sails) are a method of spacecraft propulsion using radiation pressure exerted by sunlight on large mirrors. A useful analogy is a sailboat: the difference is that photons rather than air molecules provide the force. Because the thrust is small but continuous and requires no propellant, solar sails are attractive for some long-duration missions.

Principle

Photons carry momentum; when they reflect from a sail, they transfer momentum to it. At Earth's distance from the Sun, radiation pressure on a perfectly reflecting sail is about 9 micronewtons per square metre. Sails are made of very thin, light, reflective materials such as aluminised polyester film, and may extend tens of metres across.

Demonstrations

Japan's IKAROS (2010) was the first successful interplanetary solar-sail demonstration, flying by Venus. The Planetary Society's LightSail 2 (2019) demonstrated controlled solar sailing in Earth orbit. Earlier concepts date to Johannes Kepler, and science-fiction treatments by Jules Verne and Arthur C. Clarke popularised the idea.
