Allier (river)

The Allier (Occitan: Alèir) is a river in central France, one of the principal tributaries of the Loire. It is about 421 kilometres (262 mi) long and gives its name to the Allier department.

Course

The Allier rises on the slopes of Mont Lozère in the Cévennes, in the commune of Chasseradès (Lozère), at an altitude of about 1,485 metres. It flows generally north through the Auvergne, passing Langogne, Brioude, Issoire, and Clermont-Ferrand (to its east), then Vichy and Moulins, before joining the Loire at Bec d'Allier near Nevers. Along much of its middle course it runs through dramatic gorges cut into the Massif Central.

Ecology and character

The Allier is often described as one of the last wild rivers of Western Europe: it is largely undammed, its channel migrates freely across a wide floodplain, and it supports one of France's last significant Atlantic salmon runs, alongside otters, beavers, and rich birdlife along its gravel bars. The upper valley is noted for the basalt landscapes of the Auvergne volcanoes region.

Departments and towns

The river traverses the departments of Lozère, Haute-Loire, Puy-de-Dôme, Allier, Cher, and Nièvre. Towns on its banks include Vichy, famous for its mineral springs, and Moulins, the historic capital of the Bourbonnais.
