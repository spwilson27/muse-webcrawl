Moors

The term Moors (Spanish: Moros) was used in early modern Europe for the Muslim inhabitants of the Maghreb, the Iberian Peninsula, Sicily, and Malta during the Middle Ages. The Moors initially were the indigenous Berber people of Mauretania in northwest Africa; the name later came to be applied to Arabs and Arabised Iberians as well. In 711, troops mostly formed by Berbers from North Africa landed in Iberia, beginning the Muslim rule of al-Andalus that lasted, in diminishing form, until 1492.

Etymology

"Moor" derives from the Latin Mauri, the Roman name for the people of the kingdom of Mauretania in North Africa. During the classical period the term had no religious connotation; it acquired one as medieval Christians applied it broadly to Muslim peoples of the western Mediterranean.

History

The Umayyad conquest of Hispania in 711–718 established Muslim rule over most of the peninsula. The Emirate, and later Caliphate, of Córdoba made al-Andalus one of the wealthiest and most advanced regions of medieval Europe. After the caliphate fragmented into taifa kingdoms in the 11th century, North African Berber dynasties — the Almoravids and then the Almohads — intervened and reunified Muslim Iberia under Maghrebi rule.

The Alhambra in Granada, the last Moorish stronghold in Iberia (conquered 1492).

Al-Andalus

Moorish Iberia produced noted architecture (the Great Mosque of Córdoba, the Alhambra), scholarship in astronomy, medicine, mathematics, and philosophy — figures such as Averroes and Maimonides worked in Córdoba — and advances in agriculture and irrigation. Society included Muslims, Christians (Mozarabs), and Jews under the dhimma system.

Moriscos

After the fall of Granada in 1492 ended the Reconquista, remaining Muslims were compelled to convert to Christianity, becoming the Moriscos; their revolts and continued suspicion led to their expulsion from Spain in 1609–1614.

Legacy

The Moorish period left deep marks on Spanish and Portuguese language, cuisine, music, and architecture, and the image of "the Moor" pervades European literature from medieval romance to Shakespeare's Othello.
