Gaius Plinius Caecilius Secundus, born Gaius Plinius Secundus and better known as Pliny the Younger (61 – c. 113 AD), was a lawyer, author, and magistrate of Ancient Rome. Pliny's uncle, Pliny the Elder, helped raise and educate him, and adopted him in his will. Pliny the Younger wrote hundreds of letters, of which 247 survive, and which are of great historical value. Some are addressed to reigning emperors or to notables such as the historian Tacitus. Pliny served as an imperial magistrate under Trajan (reigned 98–117), and his letters to Trajan provide one of the best surviving sources for the workings of Roman provincial government.

Letters

The ten books of the Epistulae were carefully composed for publication. Famous letters include the detailed account of the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in AD 79, in which his uncle died — written to Tacitus, the source of the term "Plinian eruption"; a description of his villa; a ghost story; and the correspondence with Trajan (Book 10), which includes Pliny's query about how to treat Christians in Bithynia and Trajan's reply forbidding anonymous accusations — a key early document on Roman–Christian relations.

Career

Pliny followed the cursus honorum: military tribune in Syria, quaestor, praetor, prefect of the military and Saturnine treasuries, and consul (suffect) in AD 100, delivering the Panegyricus in praise of Trajan. He was later appointed imperial legate governing Bithynia and Pontus (c. 110–113), where he likely died in office.
