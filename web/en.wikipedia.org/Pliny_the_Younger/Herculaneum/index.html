Herculaneum

Herculaneum (Italian: Ercolano) was an ancient Roman town located in the modern-day comune of Ercolano, Campania, Italy. Herculaneum was destroyed and buried under volcanic ash and pumice in the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD. Unlike nearby Pompeii, the mainly pyroclastic material that covered Herculaneum carbonised and preserved more wood in objects such as roofs, beds, and doors, as well as other organic-based materials such as food and papyrus.

The eruption

On the night of 24–25 August 79 AD, a pyroclastic surge reached the town — which, west of Vesuvius, had largely escaped the pumice fall that buried Pompeii — killing the remaining inhabitants instantly through thermal shock and burying the town under up to 20 metres of volcanic material that hardened into tuff. About 300 skeletons were found at the ancient shoreline and boathouses in the 1980s, overturning the earlier assumption that the population had escaped.

Excavation and finds

The site was rediscovered in 1709 during well-digging, and tunnels driven under Bourbon patronage recovered statues and the Villa of the Papyri, whose carbonised library of some 1,800 papyrus scrolls — many works of the Epicurean philosopher Philodemus — is now being read non-invasively with X-ray tomography and machine learning. Unlike Pompeii, only a fraction of Herculaneum has been excavated, as much of it lies beneath the modern town. Pliny the Younger's letters describe the eruption from across the Bay of Naples.
