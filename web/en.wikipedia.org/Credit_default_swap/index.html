Credit default swap

A credit default swap (CDS) is a financial swap agreement that the seller of the CDS will compensate the buyer in the event of a debt default (by the debtor) or other credit event. The buyer of the CDS makes a series of payments (the CDS "fee" or "spread") to the seller and, in exchange, may expect to receive a payoff if the asset defaults. In effect, a CDS is a form of insurance against non-payment of a reference obligation, typically a bond or loan.

Mechanics

A CDS contract references a notional principal and a reference entity. The protection buyer pays a periodic spread, quoted in basis points per annum on the notional. If a defined credit event occurs — bankruptcy, failure to pay, or (in some contracts) restructuring — the contract is settled either physically, with the buyer delivering the defaulted obligations and receiving par, or in cash, with the seller paying par minus the post-default market value. Since 2009, standardised contracts and auction-based settlement have been coordinated by ISDA, and indices such as CDX and iTraxx trade standardized baskets of reference names.

History

Credit default swaps were developed in the mid-1990s, notably at J.P. Morgan, where a 1994 deal transferred the credit risk of Exxon loans off the bank's balance sheet; Blythe Masters is often credited as a key inventor. The market grew explosively to a peak notional of roughly $62 trillion in 2007. CDS played a prominent role in the 2008 financial crisis: AIG's massive sales of protection on mortgage-related securities required a US government bailout, and "naked" CDS — bought without owning the underlying debt — were criticised as speculative. Post-crisis reforms, including the Dodd–Frank Act and EMIR, pushed standardised CDS into central clearing and trade reporting, shrinking outstanding notionals dramatically.

Uses and criticism

CDS are used to hedge credit exposure, to arbitrage between bonds and derivatives, and to take views on creditworthiness; CDS spreads are widely watched as market indicators of default risk. Critics argue they can amplify systemic risk, encourage lax lending, and create perverse incentives for creditors in restructurings.
