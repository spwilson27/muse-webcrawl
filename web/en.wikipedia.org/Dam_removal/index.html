Dam removal

Dam removal is the process of demolishing a dam, returning a river to a free-flowing condition. Dams are removed for reasons including ecological restoration (especially restoring fish passage for migratory species such as salmon), safety concerns for aging infrastructure, the end of a dam's useful economic life, and recreation. It is distinct from dam decommissioning, in which a structure may be partly retained.

Rationale

Many dams built in the 19th and 20th centuries have outlived their original purposes — powering mills, for example — while continuing to block fish migration, trap sediment, alter water temperature, and fragment river ecosystems. Where restoration benefits exceed the value of the services a dam provides, removal is often the most cost-effective permanent solution.

Process

Typical steps include environmental review, sediment assessment and management, reservoir drawdown, demolition (often by controlled blasting or mechanical excavation), and restoration of the former reservoir area and riverbanks. Sediment accumulated behind a dam is a central concern, since releasing it all at once can harm downstream habitats.

Notable removals

Prominent projects include the removal of the Elwha and Glines Canyon dams on Washington's Elwha River (2011–2014), the largest dam removal undertaken at the time, and the removal of four hydroelectric dams on the Klamath River in Oregon and California (completed in 2024), the largest such project in history, aimed at restoring salmon runs.
