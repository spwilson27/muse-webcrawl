Eleanor Roosevelt

Anna Eleanor Roosevelt (October 11, 1884 – November 7, 1962) was an American political figure, diplomat and activist. She was the longest-serving First Lady of the United States, holding the post from 1933 to 1945 during her husband President Franklin D. Roosevelt's four terms. She transformed the role of First Lady, and later served as the first United States ambassador to the United Nations and chaired the commission that drafted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

Early life

Born into the prominent Roosevelt and Livingston families, Eleanor was a niece of President Theodore Roosevelt. Orphaned by age ten, she was educated at Allenswood Academy in London, whose headmistress Marie Souvestre encouraged independent thought. She married her fifth cousin once removed, Franklin Delano Roosevelt, in 1905.

First Lady

As First Lady during the Great Depression and World War II, Roosevelt held regular press conferences (for women reporters only), wrote a syndicated newspaper column, "My Day", travelled extensively to inspect conditions and report to the president, and championed civil rights — famously resigning from the Daughters of the American Revolution in 1939 when it refused to let the singer Marian Anderson perform at Constitution Hall.

United Nations and later life

After her husband's death in 1945, President Harry S. Truman appointed her a delegate to the United Nations. As chair of the UN Commission on Human Rights she drove the drafting of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted in 1948, which Truman called her the "First Lady of the World" for championing. She remained a leading figure in the Democratic Party and in humanitarian causes until her death in 1962.
