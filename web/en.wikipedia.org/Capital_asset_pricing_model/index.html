Capital asset pricing model

In finance, the capital asset pricing model (CAPM) is a model used to determine a theoretically appropriate required rate of return of an asset, to make decisions about adding assets to a well-diversified portfolio. It describes the relationship between systematic risk (beta) and expected return for assets, particularly stocks.

E(Ri) = Rf + βi ( E(Rm) − Rf )

Here E(Ri) is the expected return of the investment, Rf the risk-free rate, βi the beta of the investment, and E(Rm) − Rf the market risk premium.

History

The model was introduced independently by Jack Treynor, William F. Sharpe, John Lintner, and Jan Mossin in the 1960s, building on Harry Markowitz's work on diversification and modern portfolio theory. Sharpe, Markowitz, and Merton Miller later shared the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for this work.

Assumptions and use

CAPM assumes investors are rational and risk-averse, markets are efficient, there are no taxes or transaction costs, and all investors share the same expectations and can borrow or lend unlimited amounts at the risk-free rate. The model's key insight is that only non-diversifiable (systematic) risk is rewarded; firm-specific risk can be diversified away. CAPM remains widely used to estimate the cost of equity in discounted cash flow valuations.

Criticisms

Empirical tests have found that low-beta stocks often earn more, and high-beta stocks less, than CAPM predicts, and that factors such as size and value also explain returns — findings that led to multi-factor models such as the Fama–French three-factor model.
