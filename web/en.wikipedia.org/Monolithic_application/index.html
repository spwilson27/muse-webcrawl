Monolithic application

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In software engineering, a monolithic application is a self-contained application in which the user interface, business logic, and data access are combined into a single programme — typically built, deployed, and scaled as one unit. The term contrasts with distributed architectures such as microservices, in which an application is composed of many independently deployable services.

Characteristics and trade-offs

A monolith's components call one another in-process, which makes development, debugging, testing, and deployment straightforward at small scale: there is one codebase, one build, and one artefact to release, and transactions and consistency are easy to manage. As the system and team grow, however, the architecture's weaknesses emerge: the whole application must be redeployed for any change; a fault in one module can bring down the entire system; scaling requires replicating the whole application even if only one function is hot; and the codebase can become difficult to understand and to change, slowing development ("the big ball of mud").

Monoliths and microservices

Advocates of microservices argue that decomposing a monolith improves independent deployability and team autonomy, at the cost of distributed-systems complexity. Many practitioners therefore recommend starting with a well-modularised "modular monolith" and extracting services only where justified — sometimes summarised as "monolith first". Large technology companies illustrate both paths: Amazon and Netflix famously decomposed into services, while others, such as Shopify, operate very large modular monoliths successfully.
