Chalcopyrite

FormulaCuFeS₂
Crystal systemTetragonal
ColorBrass yellow; may tarnish iridescent
Mohs hardness3.5–4
StreakGreenish black
Specific gravity4.1–4.3

Chalcopyrite is a copper iron sulfide mineral with the chemical formula CuFeS₂. It has a brassy to golden yellow color and is the most important ore of copper. The mineral crystallizes in the tetragonal system and commonly occurs in massive form or as botryoidal aggregates; well-formed crystals are relatively rare and resemble distorted tetrahedra.

Identification

Chalcopyrite is often confused with pyrite and gold. It is softer than pyrite and can be scratched by a steel knife, whereas pyrite cannot. Its streak is greenish black, and unlike gold it is brittle rather than malleable. On exposure to air, chalcopyrite tarnishes to dull and iridescent hues of blue, purple, and red, in which state it is sometimes sold as "peacock ore", a name more properly applied to bornite.

Occurrence

Chalcopyrite occurs in many types of copper deposits, including porphyry copper deposits, hydrothermal veins, contact metamorphic deposits, and disseminated grains in sedimentary rocks. Major producers include Chile, Peru, the United States, Canada, Zambia, and Australia. It frequently occurs alongside pyrite, bornite, sphalerite, galena, and quartz.

Extraction

Copper is recovered from chalcopyrite ores primarily by froth flotation, which produces a concentrate that is then smelted and refined. Because chalcopyrite is refractory, hydrometallurgical extraction is difficult, and most production still relies on pyrometallurgical routes despite their energy demands and sulfur dioxide emissions.

History

The name comes from the Greek chalkos, "copper", and pyrites, "striking fire". Chalcopyrite has been mined since the Bronze Age and has been the principal source of copper throughout recorded history.
