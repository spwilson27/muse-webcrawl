Mystery religion

Redirect note: this article is about the singular concept of a mystery religion. For the broader survey of such cults in the Greco-Roman world, see Mystery religions.

Initiation into a mystery cult, as depicted in ancient art

A mystery religion is a religion whose central beliefs and practices are kept secret from non-believers and are revealed only to those who have been initiated into the cult. The term derives from the Greek mysterion (μυστήριον), meaning a secret rite or doctrine, from mystes, one who has been initiated, and ultimately from myein, "to close" — referring to the closing of the eyes and mouth of the initiate, who was bound to secrecy.

A mystery religion, in contrast to a public or state religion, offers participation by voluntary association: the worshipper chooses to be initiated, undergoes a ritual experience that often involves a symbolic death and rebirth, and receives secret knowledge promising a transformed relationship with the divine and frequently a blessed afterlife.

Characteristics

Scholars commonly identify the following traits in a mystery religion:

  • Secret rituals and doctrines, revealed only to initiates under oath
  • Voluntary, personal membership cutting across kinship and civic ties
  • A myth of a deity who dies and returns to life, or descends to the underworld and returns
  • Ritual identification of the initiate with the deity's suffering and triumph
  • The promise of salvation, blessings in this life, and a happier fate after death

Examples

The archetypal mystery religion of the ancient world was the cult of Demeter and Persephone at Eleusis, whose Eleusinian Mysteries were celebrated for nearly two thousand years. Other examples include the mysteries of Isis, the cult of Mithras, the cult of Cybele and Attis, the Dionysian mysteries, and the mysteries of Samothrace.
