Sequent calculus

Sequent calculus is a style of formal logical argumentation in which proof is conducted through statements called sequents, of the form Γ ⊢ Δ, read as "the assumptions Γ together imply the conclusions Δ". It was introduced by Gerhard Gentzen in the 1930s as part of his investigation into the foundations of mathematical reasoning.

History

Gentzen introduced the systems LK (for classical logic) and LJ (for intuitionistic logic) in his 1934–35 papers, alongside the natural deduction formalism. His main motivation was to prove the consistency of arithmetic, and his central technical result, the cut-elimination theorem (Hauptsatz), showed that any proof can be transformed into one that does not use the cut rule.

Structure

Rules of sequent calculus are divided into logical rules, which introduce a connective on the left or right of the turnstile ⊢, and structural rules such as weakening, contraction, and exchange. The cut rule combines a proof of Γ ⊢ Δ, A with a proof of A, Σ ⊢ Π to conclude Γ, Σ ⊢ Δ, Π.

Importance

Cut elimination yields the subformula property, from which consistency of the logic follows directly, and underlies automated proof search. Sequent calculi have been developed for modal, substructural, and many other non-classical logics, and are closely related to type systems in the Curry–Howard correspondence.
