Lexan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lexan is a brand name for the polycarbonate resin thermoplastic developed by General Electric's plastics division and now produced by SABIC (Saudi Basic Industries Corporation), which acquired GE Plastics in 2007. "Lexan" is frequently used generically to refer to polycarbonate sheet, although the name remains a registered trademark.

History

Polycarbonate was discovered nearly simultaneously in 1953 by Daniel Fox at General Electric in the United States and by Hermann Schnell at Bayer in Germany (whose product is sold as Makrolon). Fox's discovery story is often told in company lore: he was working on a wire-coating resin when he found that a sample in a beaker had hardened into a tough, unbreakable mass that could only be removed by breaking the beaker. GE began marketing the resin under the Lexan trademark in 1960, initially in sheet and film form.

Properties

Lexan polycarbonate is characterized by very high impact resistance — it is often described as roughly 250 times stronger than glass of the same thickness — combined with optical clarity, light weight (about half the weight of glass), and good thermal stability, with a glass transition temperature around 147 °C. It can be cold-formed, thermoformed, and machined, and it transmits up to about 90% of visible light. Polycarbonate is vulnerable to scratching and to degradation by ultraviolet light and some solvents, so glazing products are often hard-coated or UV-stabilized.

Applications

Because of its toughness and transparency, Lexan sheet is used for bullet-resistant and security glazing, machine guards, aircraft and vehicle windows, riot shields, safety goggles and sports eyewear, greenhouse panels, skylights, and electronic housings. Compact discs and DVDs are made from polycarbonate resin of the same family. Molded Lexan has also been used for water bottles, headlamp lenses, and the housings of computers and mobile phones.
