Sumbel

A drinking horn, passed from person to person during the sumbel.

Sumbel (Old English symbel, Old Norse sumbl) is a ritual drinking ceremony in Germanic paganism, in which participants drank ale or mead from a common horn and made toasts, oaths and boasts in rounds. The word refers both to the feast and to the formal sequence of toasts that structured it. The ceremony is described in Old English and Old Norse literature, most famously in Beowulf, and in the sagas.

The ritual

A sumbel typically proceeded in rounds, the horn being carried round the hall, often by a woman of the household or a designated cup-bearer. Participants might toast the gods (such as Odin, or in Christianised settings God and the saints), drink to the memory of ancestors and dead heroes (the minni toast), and finally make personal boasts or swear binding oaths (heit) over the horn — words spoken in the sumbel were felt to carry special weight and to shape the speaker's fate. In Beowulf, Queen Wealhtheow carries the cup through Heorot, and Unferth's flyting with the hero takes place within the symbel setting.

Modern practice

The sumbel has been revived by modern Heathen movements (Germanic neopaganism), where rounds of toasting to gods, ancestors and personal deeds or oaths remain a central rite, often conducted with three formal rounds followed by an open round.
