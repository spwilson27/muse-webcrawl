Proteome

Proteome
FieldProteomics, molecular biology
Coined1994, by Marc Wilkins
Related termsGenome, transcriptome, metabolome

The proteome is the entire set of proteins that is, or can be, expressed by a cell, tissue, or organism at a particular time and under defined conditions. The term is a blend of protein and genome, coined by Marc Wilkins in 1994 while a PhD student at Macquarie University in Australia. Unlike the genome, which is largely constant for an organism, the proteome varies between cell types, over time, and in response to environmental stimuli.

Proteins are the principal functional molecules of the cell, carrying out catalysis, signalling, transport, and structural roles. Because alternative splicing and post-translational modifications can generate many distinct protein species from a single gene, the proteome is typically far larger and more complex than the genome that encodes it.

Scope and complexity

The human genome contains roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes, yet the human proteome is estimated to contain hundreds of thousands to over a million distinct protein forms, or "proteoforms", once splice variants and modifications such as phosphorylation, glycosylation, and ubiquitination are taken into account. A single cell may express around 10,000 different proteins at any one time, with abundances spanning many orders of magnitude.

Study

The large-scale study of proteomes is called proteomics. The principal technologies are mass spectrometry, which identifies and quantifies proteins from their peptide fragments, and two-dimensional gel electrophoresis, an earlier separation method. Large collaborative efforts such as the Human Proteome Project (HPP), launched by the Human Proteome Organization (HUPO) in 2010, aim to map the entire human proteome; by the early 2020s, high-confidence detections had been reported for more than 90% of predicted human proteins.
