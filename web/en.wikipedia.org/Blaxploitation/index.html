Blaxploitation

Blaxploitation is a film genre that emerged in the United States in the early 1970s, featuring Black actors in leading roles and Black audiences as their primary target, often set in urban environments with themes of crime, action and empowerment. The term, a portmanteau of "Black" and "exploitation", was coined by NAACP official Junius Griffin.

History

Melvin Van Peebles's independently produced Sweet Sweetback's Baadasssss Song (1971) and MGM's Shaft (1971), directed by Gordon Parks, launched the cycle, followed by Super Fly (1972), Coffy and Foxy Brown with Pam Grier, and Black Caesar. Studios, struggling financially, found the films cheap to make and highly profitable.

Characteristics and music

The films are noted for funk and soul-jazz soundtracks by artists such as Isaac Hayes (Shaft) and Curtis Mayfield (Super Fly), which often outlasted the films in popularity. Genres spanned crime, horror (Blacula), martial arts and westerns.

Criticism and legacy

Civil-rights organisations criticised the genre for perpetuating stereotypes even as it created unprecedented opportunities for Black actors, directors and musicians. Later filmmakers including Quentin Tarantino (Jackie Brown) and Mario Van Peebles revisited and reassessed the era.
