Laws of war

The emblem of the Red Cross, symbol of protection under the laws of war

The laws of war are the rules of international law that govern the conduct of armed conflict. They are intended to limit suffering, protect civilians, prisoners, and the wounded, and preserve a measure of humanity even in warfare. The body of law is also known as international humanitarian law and the law of armed conflict.

Major treaties

Foundational instruments include the Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907, which regulate means and methods of warfare, and the Geneva Conventions of 1949, which protect wounded and sick combatants, shipwrecked persons, prisoners of war, and civilians. The 1977 Additional Protocols extended these protections, and later treaties banned specific weapons such as chemical weapons, landmines, and cluster munitions.

Customary law and enforcement

Much of the law also exists as customary international law binding on all states. Serious violations, termed grave breaches or war crimes, may be prosecuted by domestic courts exercising universal jurisdiction or by international tribunals such as the International Criminal Court.

History

Codes restraining warfare are ancient, appearing in many civilizations, but modern codification began with the Lieber Code of 1863 during the American Civil War and the first Geneva Convention of 1864, inspired by Henri Dunant's account of the Battle of Solferino.
