Agent-based model

Agents interacting in a spatial simulation environment.

An agent-based model (ABM) is a computational model for simulating the actions and interactions of autonomous agents (both individual and collective entities such as organizations or groups) in order to understand the behaviour of a system and what governs its outcomes. It combines elements of game theory, complex systems, emergence, computational sociology, multi-agent systems and evolutionary programming. Monte Carlo methods are used to understand the stochasticity of outcomes.

Principles

In an agent-based model, a system is modelled as a collection of autonomous decision-making entities called agents. Each agent individually assesses its situation and makes decisions according to a set of rules, which may range from simple thresholds to adaptive learning algorithms. Agents interact with one another and with an environment, and repetition of these micro-level interactions can generate emergent macro-level patterns that were not explicitly programmed — for example, flocking behaviour in Craig Reynolds's Boids model of 1986, or the segregation patterns that arise in Thomas Schelling's segregation model of 1971 even when agents have only mild preferences.

Applications

Agent-based models are used in biology (ecosystems, epidemics where they complement equation-based SIR models), economics (agent-based computational economics, where markets emerge from interacting traders), social science (social networks, opinion dynamics), traffic and evacuation simulation, land-use planning, epidemiology, archaeology, and supply chains. During the COVID-19 pandemic, large-scale agent-based models informed public-health policy by representing heterogeneous contacts among individuals.

Tools

Widely used ABM toolkits and platforms include NetLogo, Repast, MASON, Mesa (for Python), Agents.jl, and general-purpose environments that support discrete-event simulation. Because ABMs are stochastic, results are typically reported as distributions over many runs, and validation — comparing model output with empirical data — is a central methodological concern.
