Goldman equation

FieldElectrophysiology / membrane biophysics
Named afterDavid E. Goldman
Developed1943, with contributions context from Hodgkin and Katz (1949)

The Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz voltage equation, more commonly known as the Goldman equation, is used in cell membrane physiology to determine the reversal potential across a cell's membrane, taking into account all the ions that are permeant through that membrane. It generalises the Nernst equation, which treats a single ion species.

The equation

For a membrane permeable to a monovalent cation (such as Na+ or K+) and a monovalent anion (such as Cl−), the membrane potential Em is given by:

Em = (RT/F) · ln[ (PK[K+]o + PNa[Na+]o + PCl[Cl−]i) / (PK[K+]i + PNa[Na+]i + PCl[Cl−]o) ]

where P denotes the membrane permeability for each ion, subscripts i and o denote intracellular and extracellular concentrations, R is the gas constant, T the absolute temperature, and F Faraday's constant. Note that the chloride terms appear with inside and outside concentrations reversed, because of its negative charge.

Assumptions and use

The equation rests on the constant-field assumption introduced by David E. Goldman in 1943 — that the electric field within the membrane is uniform. Alan Hodgkin and Bernard Katz applied it in 1949 to explain the resting potential of the squid giant axon as a weighted compromise between the equilibrium potentials of the permeant ions, dominated at rest by potassium permeability. The equation underlies the standard explanation of resting membrane potentials and of the changes that occur during the action potential.
