Picts

The Picts were a group of peoples who lived in what is now northern and eastern Scotland during Late Antiquity and the Early Middle Ages. They are known from Roman and early medieval sources and from their distinctive carved symbol stones. The name, from Latin Picti ("painted ones"), first appears in a Roman panegyric of AD 297 and may refer to a practice of tattooing or body painting.

History

The Picts resisted Roman incursions beyond Hadrian's Wall and later formed powerful kingdoms, notably Fortriu. In the 7th century the Northumbrian expansion into Pictland was halted at the Battle of Dun Nechtain (685). The Pictish church produced notable art and scholarship. During the 9th century, under the kings of the House of Alpin beginning with Kenneth MacAlpin, the Pictish and Gaelic (Scots) realms merged into the Kingdom of Alba, the origin of the Kingdom of Scotland, and Pictish identity and language faded from the record.

Language and culture

The poorly attested Pictish language is generally thought to have been a Brittonic Celtic language, related to Welsh and Cumbric. The Picts are famous for their symbol stones, bearing enigmatic carved symbols such as the crescent and V-rod, the double disc and Z-rod, and the "Pictish beast", as well as later Christian cross-slabs of great artistry. Their society, kingship, and the debated tradition of matrilineal succession remain subjects of scholarly discussion.
