Sophonisba


Sophonisba Receiving the Poisoned Chalice, attributed to Rembrandt

Sophonisba (Punic: Saphanba'al; died 203 BC) was a Carthaginian noblewoman of the Second Punic War, the daughter of Hasdrubal Gisco, a leading Carthaginian general. Renowned in ancient sources such as Livy, Polybius, Appian, and Cassius Dio for her beauty and patriotism, she became a celebrated subject of tragedy, opera, and painting.

Life

Sophonisba was first betrothed to Masinissa, king of the eastern Numidians, but for political reasons her father instead married her to Syphax, king of the western Numidians, to secure his alliance with Carthage. After Syphax was defeated and captured at the Battle of Cirta in 203 BC, Masinissa — now allied with Rome — entered Cirta and married Sophonisba, in part to keep her from Roman captivity.

Death

The Roman commander Scipio Africanus, fearing her influence over Masinissa and determined to display her in his triumph at Rome, demanded her surrender. Rather than annul the marriage by force, Masinissa sent her poison, and Sophonisba drank it, declaring that she died as a free Carthaginian rather than live as a Roman prisoner.

Cultural legacy

Her story inspired plays by Trissino (the first regular tragedy of the Renaissance, 1515), Corneille, and Voltaire, an opera, and numerous paintings by artists including Rembrandt, Guercino, and Tiepolo. She is often depicted at the moment of receiving or drinking the poisoned cup, as an exemplar of patriotic self-sacrifice.
