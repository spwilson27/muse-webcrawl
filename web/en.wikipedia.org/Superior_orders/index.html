Superior orders

Superior orders, also known as the Nuremberg defense or "just following orders", is a plea in a court of law that a person, whether a member of the military or a civilian, should not be considered guilty of committing actions ordered by a superior officer or an official. Under modern international criminal law, the plea generally does not relieve a subordinate of responsibility for war crimes or crimes against humanity, particularly where a moral choice was possible.

The Nuremberg principle

Article 8 of the Charter of the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg (1945) provided that the fact a defendant acted pursuant to an order "shall not free him from responsibility, but may be considered in mitigation of punishment". Principle IV of the Nuremberg Principles, formulated by the International Law Commission in 1950, added the condition "provided a moral choice was in fact possible to him". Major war criminals who argued they had merely obeyed Hitler's orders were convicted nonetheless.

Modern law

Article 33 of the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court allows superior orders as a defence only in narrow circumstances: the person was under a legal obligation to obey, did not know the order was unlawful, and the order was not manifestly unlawful. Orders to commit genocide or crimes against humanity are deemed manifestly unlawful by definition. National military codes likewise impose a duty to disobey manifestly illegal orders, as in the Israeli Kafr Qasim ruling and US doctrine applied after the My Lai massacre.

Historical background

Earlier practice was more permissive but not absolute: at the Leipzig trials after the First World War, German officers who sank a hospital ship's lifeboats were convicted despite pleading orders. Debates over obedience and responsibility also figured in the trials of Adolf Eichmann and Lt. William Calley, and in psychological research on obedience such as the Milgram experiment.
