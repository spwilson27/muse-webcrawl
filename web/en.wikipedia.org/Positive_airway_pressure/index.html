Positive airway pressure

Positive airway pressure (PAP) is a form of respiratory support in which a machine maintains the pressure in the patient's airway above atmospheric pressure throughout the breathing cycle. A blower delivers pressurised air through a hose to a mask worn over the nose, or over the nose and mouth, acting as a pneumatic splint that holds the upper airway open. It is the standard treatment for obstructive sleep apnea and is also used for other forms of sleep-disordered breathing and respiratory failure.

Types

The principal variants are: CPAP (continuous positive airway pressure), a single constant pressure, described by Colin Sullivan in 1981 and still the most common form; BPAP or bilevel PAP, which gives a higher pressure on inhalation (IPAP) and a lower pressure on exhalation (EPAP), useful for patients with hypoventilation or who cannot tolerate CPAP; and APAP (automatic or auto-titrating PAP), which adjusts pressure breath by breath in response to detected apneas, hypopneas, flow limitation, and snoring. Related devices include adaptive servo-ventilation for central sleep apnea and, in hospital practice, non-invasive ventilation for acute respiratory failure.

Benefits and adherence

Effective PAP therapy eliminates apneas, restores normal sleep architecture, reduces daytime sleepiness, and modestly lowers blood pressure; evidence for prevention of cardiovascular events is less conclusive. Its main limitation is adherence: mask discomfort, nasal congestion, and claustrophobia lead many patients to abandon therapy. Heated humidification, mask fitting, and pressure-relief features improve acceptance, and usage data from modern machines allow clinicians to monitor compliance.
