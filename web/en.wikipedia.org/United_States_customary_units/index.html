United States customary units form the system of measurement used for everyday purposes in the United States and its territories. Derived from the English units in use in the American colonies, they are closely related to — but not identical with — the British imperial system created in 1824. The principal units include the inch, foot, yard, and mile for length; the ounce, pint, quart, and gallon for volume; the ounce, pound, and ton for mass and weight; and degrees Fahrenheit for temperature.

History

The young United States retained the British units used before independence. Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson's 1790 report proposed a decimal system but was not adopted. In 1866, Congress legalized the use of the metric system without making it mandatory. Crucially, since the Mendenhall Order of 1893, U.S. customary units have been defined in terms of metric standards; the 1959 international yard and pound agreement fixed the yard at exactly 0.9144 metres and the pound at exactly 0.45359237 kilograms, harmonizing definitions across English-speaking countries.

The Metric Conversion Act of 1975 declared metric the "preferred" system but made conversion voluntary; the Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988 designated it the preferred system for federal government use. The United States is one of only a few countries that have not adopted the metric system as the primary system for trade and commerce, though metric units are standard in science, medicine, the military, and much of industry.

Length, area, and volume

  • Length: 12 inches = 1 foot; 3 feet = 1 yard; 5,280 feet = 1 mile. Surveying historically used the U.S. survey foot, retired at the end of 2022.
  • Area: the acre (43,560 square feet) remains standard for land.
  • Volume: the U.S. liquid gallon equals 231 cubic inches (3.785 litres), differing from the imperial gallon of 4.546 litres. Separate measures exist for dry goods (dry gallon, bushel, peck).

Mass and temperature

The avoirdupois pound of 16 ounces is used for most goods; the short ton equals 2,000 pounds. Troy weights survive for precious metals. Temperature in daily life is measured in Fahrenheit, where water freezes at 32 °F and boils at 212 °F.

Differences from imperial units

The most significant divergences are in volume measures (the U.S. gallon and pint are smaller than imperial), while length and mass units are nearly identical after the 1959 standardization. Labels on U.S. packaged goods typically show both customary and metric quantities, as required by the Fair Packaging and Labeling Act.
