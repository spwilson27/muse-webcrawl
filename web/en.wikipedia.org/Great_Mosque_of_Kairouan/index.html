Great Mosque of Kairouan

Location: Kairouan, Tunisia

Founded: 670 CE by Uqba ibn Nafi

Current form: mainly Aghlabid, 9th century

Style: Aghlabid / early Islamic (hypostyle)

The Great Mosque of Kairouan, also known as the Mosque of Uqba, is a mosque situated in the UNESCO World Heritage city of Kairouan, Tunisia. Established by the Arab general Uqba ibn Nafi in 670 CE at the founding of Kairouan, it is one of the oldest places of worship in the Islamic world and a masterpiece of Islamic art and architecture.

History

The mosque was first built in 670 on the site chosen by Uqba ibn Nafi for his garrison city. It was rebuilt and enlarged several times, notably under the Umayyads and around 724–728 under the governor Bishr ibn Safwan, and again under the Abbasids. Its definitive form dates largely from the Aghlabid period in the 9th century, when Emir Ziyadat Allah I demolished and rebuilt most of the structure around 836, and later Aghlabid rulers added the monumental minaret, the dome over the entrance bay, and the celebrated carved wooden minbar — the oldest surviving minbar in the Islamic world — together with precious lustre tiles imported from Baghdad around the mihrab.

Architecture

The mosque occupies a large, slightly irregular quadrilateral of about 9,000 m². Its massive stone walls give it a fortress-like appearance. The prayer hall is a hypostyle space with rows of columns — many of them reused Roman and Byzantine spolia — supporting horseshoe arches. The courtyard is paved with marble and contains sundials and rainwater collection systems feeding an underground cistern. The three-tiered square minaret, about 31.5 m high, is the oldest surviving minaret in North Africa and a model for later Maghrebi minarets.

Significance

Kairouan is considered by many Tunisians the fourth holiest city of Islam, and the Great Mosque was for centuries a centre of learning in theology, law and science that drew scholars from across the Muslim West. Its architecture served as a reference for mosques throughout the Maghreb.
