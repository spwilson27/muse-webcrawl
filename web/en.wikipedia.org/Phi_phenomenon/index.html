Phi phenomenon

The phi phenomenon is the optical illusion of perceiving a series of still images as continuous motion, when the images are viewed in rapid succession. More strictly, in Max Wertheimer's original experiments, observers shown two stationary lights flashed in alternation at suitable timing perceived a single light moving back and forth, and under certain conditions perceived "pure motion" — movement without any object seen to move.

History

Wertheimer, a founder of Gestalt psychology, published his experiments on the perception of apparent motion in 1912 while at the University of Frankfurt, working with assistants Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Köhler. The results challenged the structuralist view that perception is built purely from elementary sensations, since the perceived motion could not be reduced to the two stationary stimuli.

Relation to beta movement and cinema

The phi phenomenon is often confused with beta movement, in which a sequence of still images is perceived as a smoothly moving object. Film and animation depend on apparent motion: a rapid series of frames (typically 24 per second in cinema) fuses into continuous movement. Older explanations invoked "persistence of vision", but modern accounts explain the effect through motion-detection processes in the visual system that respond to spatiotemporal patterns of stimulation.
