Capitalism

Capitalism is an economic system based on the private ownership of the means of production and their operation for profit. Central characteristics include capital accumulation, competitive markets, a price system, private property, property rights recognition, voluntary exchange, and wage labour. In a capitalist economy, decisions regarding investment, production, and distribution are largely determined by markets rather than by state planning.

History

Capitalist practices developed out of European mercantilism and agrarian commercialization between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries. The Industrial Revolution, beginning in Britain, massively expanded factory production and wage labour. Thinkers such as Adam Smith, in The Wealth of Nations (1776), provided its classical liberal defence, while Karl Marx produced its most influential critique in Das Kapital. Capitalism spread globally through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, surviving the Great Depression, competition with communist states during the Cold War, and periodic financial crises.

Forms

Varieties of capitalism range from laissez-faire free-market systems to mixed economies with extensive welfare states, such as the Nordic model, and to state-directed variants in parts of Asia. Analysts contrast liberal market economies with coordinated market economies in the ways firms, banks, and labour are organized.

Criticism and defence

Supporters credit capitalism with unprecedented growth in productivity, living standards, innovation, and personal freedom. Critics fault it for economic inequality, instability, exploitation of labour, environmental damage, and the commodification of social life. Debate continues over the extent to which markets should be regulated and public goods provided collectively.
