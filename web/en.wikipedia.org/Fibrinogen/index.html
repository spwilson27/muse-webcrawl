Fibrinogen

Fibrinogen, clotting factor I

Fibrinogen (coagulation factor I) is a soluble plasma glycoprotein synthesised by the liver that is essential for blood clotting. It is a 340 kDa protein composed of three pairs of polypeptide chains (Aα, Bβ and γ) joined by disulfide bonds.

Role in coagulation

During coagulation, the enzyme thrombin converts fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin by cleaving small fibrinopeptides from its chains. The resulting fibrin monomers polymerise into a mesh that, stabilised by factor XIII, forms the structural basis of a blood clot. Fibrinogen also supports platelet aggregation by bridging activated platelets.

Clinical significance

Normal plasma fibrinogen concentration is about 1.5–4 g/L, rising as an acute-phase reactant during inflammation. Inherited disorders include afibrinogenemia and dysfibrinogenemia, which cause bleeding or, paradoxically, thrombosis. Elevated fibrinogen is an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease.
