A smart grid is an electrical grid that uses digital communications technology, sensors, and automated control systems to detect and react to local changes in usage and generation. Unlike the traditional one-way power grid, a smart grid enables two-way flows of both electricity and information between utilities and consumers, allowing the network to balance supply and demand in near real time.

The concept emerged from efforts to modernize aging electrical infrastructure, integrate variable renewable energy sources such as wind and solar power, and improve resilience against outages and physical or cyber attacks. Smart grids are a central element of many national energy transition strategies.

Background and history

The conventional alternating-current grid developed over the twentieth century delivered power from large central generating stations to passive consumers. By the early 2000s, limitations of this architecture—including poor visibility into distribution networks, vulnerability to cascading failures such as the 2003 Northeast blackout in North America, and difficulty integrating distributed generation—spurred investment in digitalization. The United States Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007 formally promoted smart grid development, and the 2009 American Recovery and Reinvestment Act funded large deployment programs. The European Union, China, Japan, and South Korea launched parallel initiatives.

Features

Advanced metering infrastructure

Smart meters record consumption at short intervals and communicate readings automatically to the utility, enabling time-of-use pricing, remote connection and disconnection, and faster outage detection.

Distribution automation

Sensors and automated switches on feeders can isolate faults and reroute power without human intervention, a capability sometimes called a "self-healing" grid. Phasor measurement units provide synchronized, high-resolution measurements of voltage and current across wide areas.

Demand response and distributed resources

Smart grids coordinate demand response programs, rooftop solar installations, battery storage, and electric vehicles, treating flexible loads and small generators as active grid resources. Vehicle-to-grid systems can return stored energy from parked electric vehicles during peak demand.

Benefits and challenges

Advocates cite improved efficiency, reduced peak demand, lower operational costs, better integration of renewables, and improved reliability. Challenges include high capital costs, interoperability among vendors, privacy concerns arising from detailed consumption data, and cybersecurity risks created by a larger digital attack surface. Standards bodies such as the IEEE and NIST have developed frameworks and protocols to address interoperability and security.
