Parathyroid gland

Parathyroid glands are small endocrine glands in the neck of humans and other tetrapods. Humans normally have four parathyroid glands, located on the back of the thyroid gland. They vary considerably in size — each is roughly the size of a grain of rice — and in exact position.

The glands secrete parathyroid hormone (PTH), the principal regulator of calcium and phosphate levels in the blood. When blood calcium falls, PTH is released and acts to raise it: it stimulates the release of calcium from bone, increases calcium reabsorption in the kidneys, and promotes the activation of vitamin D, which in turn increases calcium absorption from the gut. The secretion of PTH is controlled by calcium-sensing receptors on the gland's chief cells.

Clinical significance

Overactivity of one or more glands — hyperparathyroidism, most often caused by a benign adenoma — raises blood calcium and can cause kidney stones, bone loss, and fatigue; treatment is usually surgical removal of the abnormal gland. Underactivity, hypoparathyroidism, lowers blood calcium and may cause tingling, muscle cramps, and tetany; it is most commonly a complication of thyroid surgery. The glands were the last major endocrine organ to be recognised, described in humans by Ivar Sandström in 1880.
