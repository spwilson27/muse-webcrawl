Enterocyte

An enterocyte, or intestinal absorptive cell, is the principal cell type of the epithelium lining the small and large intestines. These tall, columnar cells are specialized for absorbing nutrients, electrolytes, and water from the intestinal lumen and transferring them to the blood and lymph, while also forming a barrier against microorganisms and toxins.

Structure

The apical (lumen-facing) surface of an enterocyte is covered by dense microvilli forming the "brush border," which enormously increases surface area and carries digestive enzymes such as lactase, sucrase-isomaltase, and peptidases, together with nutrient transporters. Adjacent enterocytes are joined by tight junctions that regulate paracellular permeability. Enterocytes are continuously renewed: they arise from stem cells in the crypts of Lieberkühn, migrate up the villus over a few days, and are shed at the tip.

Function

Enterocytes absorb monosaccharides (via transporters such as SGLT1 and GLUT5), amino acids and small peptides, fatty acids and monoglycerides (which they re-esterify and package into chylomicrons), vitamins, minerals such as iron and calcium, and bile salts. They secrete immunoglobulin A transport machinery, antimicrobial peptides, and hormones, contributing to gut immunity and signalling. Damage to enterocytes — as in coeliac disease, infections, or chemotherapy — impairs absorption and causes malabsorption syndromes.
