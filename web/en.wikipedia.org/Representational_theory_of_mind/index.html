Representational theory of mind

The representational theory of mind (RTM) is a family of theories holding that mental states such as beliefs, desires, intentions, and perceptions are relations between a thinker and internal mental representations — states or structures that bear content, i.e. that are about things in the world. On this view, thinking is a form of computation performed over these representations.

Core claims

RTM is usually combined with the language of thought hypothesis, associated above all with Jerry Fodor, on which mental representations have a combinatorial, language-like syntax and semantics: complex representations are built from simpler ones, and mental processes are sensitive to their structure. This explains the productivity and systematicity of thought — anyone who can think "John loves Mary" can in principle think "Mary loves John". Propositional attitudes are then treated as computational relations to sentences in this internal code ("Mentalese").

Historical background

Representationalism has roots in Descartes and Locke, but its modern form arose with cognitive science and the computational theory of mind in the 1960s–70s, influenced by the success of digital computers and generative linguistics. It underpins much of classical cognitive science and contrasts with behaviourism, which avoided positing internal content-bearing states.

Criticisms and alternatives

Critics challenge RTM from several directions: connectionism proposes distributed, sub-symbolic representations; embodied and enactive approaches deny that cognition fundamentally consists in manipulating internal symbols; eliminativists doubt that folk-psychological states will survive scientific scrutiny. Debates continue over how the content of representations is determined (the naturalisation of content) and whether phenomenal consciousness can be explained representationally.
