Framing (social sciences)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the social sciences, framing comprises a set of concepts and theoretical perspectives on how individuals, groups and societies organize, perceive and communicate about reality. A frame selects and highlights some aspects of a perceived reality and makes them more salient in a communicating text, in such a way as to promote a particular problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation, or treatment recommendation.

Origins

The concept is commonly traced to Gregory Bateson's work on psychological frames (1955) and to Erving Goffman's Frame Analysis (1974), which examined the "schemata of interpretation" by which people make sense of experience. In sociology, frame analysis was developed by social movement scholars such as David Snow and Robert Benford, who studied how movements construct "collective action frames" — diagnostic, prognostic and motivational — to mobilize supporters.

Media and communication

In media studies, framing refers to the way news outlets present events and issues: the choice of words, images, sources and story structure that shapes audiences' understanding. Robert Entman's influential definition (1993) describes framing as selection and salience. Experimental research in political science, notably by Shanto Iyengar, distinguishes episodic framing (issues shown through individual cases) from thematic framing (issues shown in broader social context), which affects how audiences assign responsibility.

Framing effects in psychology and economics

In cognitive psychology and behavioral economics, framing effects are changes in people's choices when the same options are presented differently — famously in Tversky and Kahneman's experiments on gains versus losses, which showed that describing outcomes as losses (rather than equivalent gains) makes people more risk-seeking.

Criticism

Scholars note that "framing" is used so broadly across disciplines that it risks becoming a "fractured paradigm", and debates continue over the distinction between framing, priming and agenda-setting effects.
