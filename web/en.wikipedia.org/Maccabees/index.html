The Maccabees (Hebrew: מכבים, Makkabim) were a group of Jewish rebel warriors who took control of Judea, which at the time was part of the Seleucid Empire. They founded the Hasmonean dynasty, which ruled from 167 BC until 37 BC, being a fully independent kingdom from 104 to 63 BC. They reasserted the Jewish religion, expanded the boundaries of Judea by conquest, and reduced the influence of Hellenism and Hellenistic Judaism.

Judas Maccabeus, the revolt's most famous commander.

The revolt

The revolt began in 167 BC when the priest Mattathias of Modein refused to worship Greek gods and killed a Hellenistic Jewish apostate and a Seleucid officer, then fled to the hills with his five sons. After Mattathias's death, his son Judas—nicknamed Maccabeus, "the Hammer"—led a guerrilla campaign, winning victories at Beth Horon, Emmaus and Beth Zur. In 164 BC Judas liberated Jerusalem and rededicated the Temple, an event commemorated by the festival of Hanukkah.

Aftermath

Judas fell at Elasa in 160 BC, but his brothers Jonathan and Simon continued the struggle, winning full independence; Simon was recognised as high priest and ethnarch in 142 BC, founding the Hasmonean dynasty.

Sources

The revolt is described in the books of 1 and 2 Maccabees, preserved in the Septuagint and regarded as deuterocanonical by the Catholic and Orthodox churches, and in the works of the historian Josephus.
