Internal combustion engine

An internal combustion engine (ICE) is a heat engine in which the combustion of a fuel occurs with an oxidizer (usually air) inside a combustion chamber that is an integral part of the working fluid flow circuit. The expansion of the high-temperature, high-pressure gases produced by combustion applies direct force to a component of the engine — typically a piston, turbine blade, rotor or nozzle — converting chemical energy into mechanical work.

Principles of operation

In a piston engine, the cycle proceeds through intake, compression, power and exhaust strokes. Four-stroke engines complete the cycle in four piston movements (two crankshaft revolutions), while two-stroke engines complete it in two. Petrol engines ignite the fuel–air mixture with a spark plug (the Otto cycle); diesel engines ignite by compression heating alone (the Diesel cycle), allowing higher compression ratios and greater efficiency.

History

Practical internal combustion engines emerged in the nineteenth century. Étienne Lenoir built a gas engine in 1860; Nicolaus Otto patented the four-stroke cycle in 1876; Karl Benz patented his motor car in 1886; and Rudolf Diesel demonstrated his compression-ignition engine in the 1890s. The twentieth century saw the ICE become the dominant prime mover for road transport, aviation (until jets) and much of industry.

Configurations and fuels

Engines are classified by cylinder arrangement (inline, V, flat, radial), by cycle, by ignition method and by fuel. Rotary Wankel engines, gas turbines and jet engines are also internal combustion engines. Fuels include gasoline, diesel, liquefied petroleum gas, compressed natural gas, ethanol blends and, increasingly in trials, hydrogen and synthetic e-fuels.

Emissions and the energy transition

Combustion produces carbon dioxide, nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide, unburned hydrocarbons and particulates. Catalytic converters, particulate filters and exhaust gas recirculation have greatly reduced toxic emissions, but CO2 output is intrinsic to carbon fuels. Since the 2010s, battery-electric vehicles have displaced ICE cars in a growing number of markets, and several jurisdictions have announced phase-out dates for new petrol and diesel car sales.
