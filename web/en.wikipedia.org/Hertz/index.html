Hertz

Unit system: SI derived unit
Quantity: Frequency
Symbol: Hz
Named after: Heinrich Hertz
In SI base units: s−1

The hertz (symbol: Hz) is the unit of frequency in the International System of Units (SI), defined as one event (or cycle) per second. It is named after the German physicist Heinrich Hertz (1857–1894), who conclusively proved the existence of electromagnetic waves. The hertz is equivalent to the reciprocal second, s−1.

Definition and use

The hertz is used for periodic phenomena: the frequency of a sound wave, an alternating current, a radio signal, or a computer clock is expressed in hertz. Multiples are formed with SI prefixes — kilohertz (kHz), megahertz (MHz), gigahertz (GHz), terahertz (THz). The unit was established by the IEC in 1935 and adopted internationally at the 11th General Conference on Weights and Measures in 1960, replacing the earlier "cycles per second" (cps).

Examples

Human hearing spans roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz; the musical note A above middle C is tuned to 440 Hz. Mains electricity alternates at 50 or 60 Hz. FM radio broadcasts use frequencies around 88–108 MHz, while Wi-Fi commonly operates at 2.4 and 5 GHz. Processor clock speeds are measured in gigahertz.

Relation to other units

For rotating machines, frequency in hertz equals revolutions per minute divided by 60. Angular frequency ω in radians per second is 2π times the frequency in hertz. The becquerel, though also equal to s−1, is reserved for stochastic radioactive decay and should not be interchanged with the hertz.
