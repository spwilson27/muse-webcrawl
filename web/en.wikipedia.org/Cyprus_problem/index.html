Cyprus problem

The Cyprus problem, also known as the Cyprus dispute, is an ongoing dispute between the Greek Cypriot and Turkish Cypriot communities over the governance and territorial status of the island of Cyprus. It has its roots in intercommunal tensions of the 1950s and culminated in the island's de facto division in 1974.

Background

Cyprus gained independence from Britain in 1960 under a constitution that shared power between the Greek Cypriot majority and Turkish Cypriot minority, with Greece, Turkey and the United Kingdom as guarantor powers. Constitutional breakdown and intercommunal violence in 1963–64 led to the deployment of a United Nations peacekeeping force, UNFICYP, which remains on the island.

Events of 1974

In July 1974, the Greek military junta sponsored a coup d'état aimed at enosis (union with Greece). Turkey responded with a military intervention, citing its rights under the Treaty of Guarantee. Turkish forces occupied the northern third of the island, and large numbers of Greek Cypriots and Turkish Cypriots were displaced to the south and north respectively.

Division and negotiations

In 1983 the Turkish Cypriot side unilaterally declared the "Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus", recognised only by Turkey. The Republic of Cyprus joined the European Union in 2004, shortly after Greek Cypriot voters rejected the Annan Plan for reunification in a referendum while Turkish Cypriot voters approved it. Repeated rounds of UN-sponsored talks, most recently at Crans-Montana in 2017, have failed to produce a settlement. The most widely discussed framework is a bizonal, bicommunal federation.
