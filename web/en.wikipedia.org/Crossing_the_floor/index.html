Crossing the floor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Crossing the floor is a term used in some parliamentary systems for a member of parliament who changes political party allegiance, moving from the government side to the opposition, or vice versa. The phrase comes from the physical layout of Westminster-style chambers, in which government and opposition members sit on opposite sides and a defecting member literally crosses the floor of the house.

Practice

Famous examples include Winston Churchill, who crossed from the Conservatives to the Liberals in 1904 and back ("re-ratted") in 1924. Crossing the floor is legal in the United Kingdom, Canada, and Australia, but is controversial: critics argue that voters elected the member under one party's banner, while defenders cite the representative's freedom of conscience and judgment. In some countries, notably India and Bangladesh, anti-defection laws disqualify legislators who switch parties. In New Zealand, "waka-jumping" legislation has periodically sought to prevent the practice.

Related concepts

The term differs from merely voting against the party line while remaining a member, and from floor-crossing at the level of entire local councils, an issue that led South Africa to abolish the practice. A member who leaves to sit as an independent is sometimes described as crossing the floor as well.
