Native American self-determination refers to the policy era and movement, beginning in the 1970s, in which the United States federal government recognised the right of Native American tribes to govern themselves and to administer federal programmes on their own lands. It replaced the earlier policies of termination and assimilation, under which tribal governments were dissolved and Native people pressured to relocate to cities.

Background

From the 1940s to the 1960s, federal termination policy ended recognition of more than one hundred tribes and sold off tribal lands, while relocation programmes moved Native families to urban centres. Native activism of the 1960s — including the founding of the American Indian Movement in 1968, the occupation of Alcatraz Island (1969–1971), and the Trail of Broken Treaties march on Washington in 1972 — demanded restoration of treaty rights and tribal sovereignty.

The self-determination era

President Richard Nixon's 1970 message to Congress formally repudiated termination and endorsed self-determination. The Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 allowed tribes to contract with federal agencies to run programmes such as health care, education, and road building themselves. Later laws, including the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988 and amendments enabling tribal self-governance compacts, expanded tribal authority. Tribes have used these tools to rebuild governments, operate schools and clinics, revitalise languages, and develop enterprises including gaming.

Ongoing issues

Debates continue over the scope of tribal jurisdiction, federal funding shortfalls, land-into-trust decisions, and the recognition of unrecognised tribes. Nevertheless, self-determination remains the stated framework of federal Indian policy across administrations.
