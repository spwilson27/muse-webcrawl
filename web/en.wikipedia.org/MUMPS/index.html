MUMPS ("Massachusetts General Hospital Utility Multi-Programming System"), also abbreviated as M, is a high-performance transaction processing key–value database and programming language that supports the development and execution of transaction processing applications. Originally developed in 1966 at Massachusetts General Hospital for managing hospital laboratory information, it is now used across healthcare, banking, and other transaction-intensive industries.

Overview

MUMPS is unusual in that the programming language and the database are tightly integrated: its central data structure is the global, a persistent, sparse, multidimensional array stored in a hierarchical (tree-structured) database. Programs refer to globals with the caret character, as in ^PATIENT(123,"NAME"). This design allowed interactive multi-user applications to run on the very small minicomputers of the late 1960s.

History

The system was developed under Octo Barnett at Massachusetts General Hospital by Neil Pappalardo, Curtis Marble, and Robert Greenes, among others. It spread through the United States Veterans Administration hospital system, where its descendant VistA became one of the largest electronic health record systems in the world, and through commercial vendors including InterSystems, whose Caché and IRIS platforms embed an M implementation. Epic Systems' Chronicles database, used by its widely deployed electronic health records, is also based on M technology.

Language features

M code is famously terse, historically permitting single-letter commands and heavy abbreviation. A classic "Hello, World" program is:

 Write "Hello, World!",!

Commands include SET, KILL, DO, GOTO, IF, FOR, and MERGE. The language has a single data type: all values are strings that are interpreted numerically or as text according to context. MUMPS was standardized by ANSI (X11.1) and later by ISO.

Criticism

Critics cite its cryptic abbreviated syntax, limited error handling in classic implementations, and idiosyncratic scoping rules (variables are dynamically scoped by default) as obstacles to maintainability; defenders point to its speed, reliability, and extraordinary longevity in mission-critical systems.
