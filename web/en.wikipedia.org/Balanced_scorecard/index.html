Balanced scorecard

The balanced scorecard is a strategic planning and management framework used to align business activities with an organisation's vision and strategy, improve communications, and monitor performance against strategic goals. It was introduced by Robert S. Kaplan and David P. Norton in a 1992 Harvard Business Review article and a subsequent book.

Perspectives

The framework supplements traditional financial measures with three non-financial perspectives, each framed by objectives, measures, targets and initiatives: (1) Financial — how the organisation appears to shareholders; (2) Customer — how customers see the organisation; (3) Internal business processes — what the organisation must excel at; and (4) Learning and growth — how the organisation can continue to improve and create value. Kaplan and Norton later added strategy maps to show cause-and-effect links among objectives across the perspectives.

Adoption and criticism

The balanced scorecard became one of the most widely adopted management tools in large companies and public sector organisations. Critics note implementation difficulties, the risk of proliferating metrics, disputes about which perspectives matter, and the lack of rigorous causal validation; supporters respond that its value lies in forcing balanced, strategy-linked measurement rather than in any fixed set of indicators.
