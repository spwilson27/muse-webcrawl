Eicosanoid

Class: Signaling molecules (lipid mediators)

Precursor: 20-carbon fatty acids, chiefly arachidonic acid

Families: Prostaglandins, thromboxanes, leukotrienes, lipoxins

In biochemistry, eicosanoids are signaling molecules made by the enzymatic or non-enzymatic oxidation of arachidonic acid or other polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) that are, similar to arachidonic acid, around 20 carbon units in length. Eicosanoids are a sub-category of oxylipins, which include molecules derived from other PUFAs.

Biosynthesis

Arachidonic acid is liberated from membrane phospholipids by phospholipase A2. It is then metabolized along three main enzymatic routes: the cyclooxygenase (COX) pathway, yielding prostaglandins and thromboxanes; the lipoxygenase (LOX) pathways, yielding leukotrienes and lipoxins; and cytochrome P450 epoxygenase routes, yielding epoxyeicosatrienoic acids.

Functions

Eicosanoids act mainly as autocrine and paracrine mediators, exerting powerful effects at very low concentrations. They mediate inflammation, fever, pain, blood clotting, blood pressure, bronchial tone, and aspects of reproduction such as labor. Prostaglandins sensitize pain receptors and promote fever; thromboxane A2 promotes platelet aggregation; leukotrienes are potent bronchoconstrictors involved in asthma.

Clinical relevance

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as aspirin and ibuprofen inhibit the COX enzymes, reducing prostaglandin formation and thereby inflammation, pain, and fever. Leukotriene receptor antagonists are used in asthma, and prostaglandin analogues are used in glaucoma and to induce labor.
