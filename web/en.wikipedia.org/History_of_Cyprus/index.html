The history of Cyprus reflects the island's strategic position in the eastern Mediterranean, at the crossroads of Europe, Asia and Africa. Settled since the Neolithic, famed in antiquity for copper (whose Latin name, cuprum, derives from Cyprus), it passed through Greek, Phoenician, Assyrian, Egyptian, Persian, Hellenistic, Roman and Byzantine hands.

Medieval and Ottoman periods

Arab raids from the 7th century were followed by full Byzantine recovery in 965. Richard I of England seized Cyprus in 1191 during the Third Crusade; sold to the Templars and then Guy de Lusignan, it became a Frankish kingdom, later Venetian (1489). The Ottoman conquest of 1570–71, sealed at Famagusta, ruled the island for three centuries, during which a Muslim Turkish Cypriot community took root alongside the Greek Orthodox majority.

British rule and independence

Britain administered Cyprus from 1878 and annexed it in 1914. Greek Cypriot demands for enosis (union with Greece) produced the EOKA guerrilla campaign of 1955–59. The Zurich–London agreements produced an independent republic in 1960 under Archbishop Makarios, with a power-sharing constitution between Greek and Turkish Cypriots and Britain, Greece and Turkey as guarantors.

Division

Intercommunal violence in 1963–64 collapsed the partnership; UN peacekeepers arrived in 1964. In July 1974 a Greek junta-backed coup aimed at enosis triggered a Turkish military intervention; the island was partitioned, with mass displacements on both sides. In 1983 the Turkish Cypriot north unilaterally declared independence, recognised only by Turkey. The Republic of Cyprus joined the EU in 2004, its authority effectively limited to the south, and reunification talks — most recently at Crans-Montana in 2017 — have yet to produce a settlement.
