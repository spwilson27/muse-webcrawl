John Hawkins

Born1532, Plymouth, England
Died12 November 1595, at sea off Puerto Rico
OccupationNaval commander, privateer, slave trader
AllegianceKingdom of England

Sir John Hawkins (also spelled Hawkyns; 1532 – 12 November 1595) was an English naval commander, shipbuilder and merchant, and one of the earliest English slave traders. A cousin of Francis Drake, he played a major role in building the Elizabethan navy and in defeating the Spanish Armada.

Slave trading voyages

Born into a Plymouth seafaring family, Hawkins led three slaving expeditions in the 1560s (1562–63, 1564–65 and 1567–68), capturing or purchasing enslaved Africans in Guinea and Sierra Leone and selling them — in defiance of Spanish colonial law — to settlers in the Spanish Caribbean. Queen Elizabeth I invested in the second voyage and lent ships, including the Jesus of Lübeck. The third voyage ended in disaster at San Juan de Ulúa (1568), where Spanish forces attacked his fleet in the Mexican port; Hawkins and Drake escaped with few survivors, an episode that embittered Anglo-Spanish relations.

Naval administrator and Armada

As Treasurer and Comptroller of the Navy from the late 1570s, Hawkins reformed naval administration and ship design, promoting longer, lower, faster, race-built galleons with heavier broadside armament — innovations credited with helping England against Spain. In 1588 he served as a vice admiral and squadron commander against the Spanish Armada, fighting in the battles off Plymouth, Portland and Gravelines, and was knighted at sea by Lord Howard of Effingham.

Death and legacy

In 1595 Hawkins sailed with Drake on a final expedition against Spanish possessions in the Caribbean and died of fever off Puerto Rico on 12 November 1595; Drake died on the same voyage weeks later. Hawkins's legacy is controversial: celebrated in his own age as a naval hero and administrator, he is now also recognised as a pioneer of the English transatlantic slave trade.
