Greek yogurt is a style of yogurt that has been strained to remove most of its whey, resulting in a thicker consistency than normal unstrained yogurt, while preserving the distinctive sour taste. In Greece it is made traditionally from sheep's or cow's milk and strained through cloth; similar strained yogurts are found across the Balkans, Turkey (where it is called süzme yoğurt) and the Middle East, where a further-strained, salted version is labneh. In many countries there is no legal definition of "Greek yogurt", and yogurts thickened with additives are often marketed as "Greek-style".

Production

Greek yogurt is made by fermenting milk with Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus, then straining the curd through cloth or fine filters — industrially, through centrifuges — to remove liquid whey (which contains much of the lactose, sodium and water). About three to four times as much milk is required to make a given quantity of strained yogurt as regular yogurt, which explains its higher cost and its concentrated protein: a typical serving contains roughly 15–20 grams of protein and less sugar than conventional yogurt. The acidic whey by-product ("acid whey") presents a disposal challenge for large producers.

History and popularisation

Strained yogurt has been eaten in Greece and neighbouring regions for centuries and is the basis of dishes such as tzatziki. Its global commercial breakthrough came in the United States in the 2000s: the Greek company Fage had exported to the US since the 1990s and opened a plant in New York in 2008, while Chobani, founded by Turkish immigrant Hamdi Ulukaya in 2005, grew explosively to become the best-selling yogurt brand in the US by 2011. Greek yogurt's share of the US yogurt market rose from about 1% in 2007 to roughly half by the mid-2010s, prompting all major brands to launch competing lines.

Naming disputes

In the United Kingdom, a 2013 court ruling (upheld on appeal in 2014) held that "Greek yogurt" sold in Britain must be made in Greece, after Fage sued Chobani, whose US-made product was relabelled "Greek strained yogurt" and then "cultured Greek strained yogurt". In the United States and European Union, "Greek yogurt" is generally treated as a style designation, though Greek producers have campaigned for protected status comparable to feta cheese.

Uses

Besides being eaten plain or with honey, fruit or granola, Greek yogurt is used in cooking as a substitute for sour cream or mayonnaise, in marinades, dips, sauces and baking, and as the base for tzatziki and raita-style condiments.
