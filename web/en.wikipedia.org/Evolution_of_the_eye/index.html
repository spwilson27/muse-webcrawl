Evolution of the eye

The evolution of the eye has attracted study since Charles Darwin admitted in On the Origin of Species that the eye's perfection made his theory "absurd in the highest possible degree" — though he went on to outline a plausible gradual path. Eyes are now among the best-documented examples of natural selection producing complex organs, and are thought to have evolved independently dozens of times.

Early photoreception

The simplest precursors are photoreceptor cells that merely detect light intensity, useful for circadian rhythms and shadow responses. Clusters of such cells in a shallow pit (as in planaria) add crude directionality; a deeper pit, and eventually a pinhole opening (as in the nautilus), sharpens the image. A transparent covering, then a lens — in many lineages formed from crystallin proteins recruited from other functions — dramatically improves focus and brightness.

Modelling and genetics

A famous 1994 computer model by Dan-Eric Nilsson and Susanne Pelger showed that a camera-type eye could evolve from a flat light-sensitive patch in under 400,000 generations, given plausible selection pressures. Genetic work revealed deep homology: the Pax6 gene family orchestrates eye development across bilaterians, suggesting that while eyes evolved convergently many times, they built on an ancient shared toolkit.

Diversity

Major eye designs include the camera eyes of vertebrates and cephalopods (a classic case of convergence, differing in retinal wiring), the compound eyes of arthropods, mirror eyes of scallops, and the simple eyespots of many larvae. Fossil trilobites with calcite-lens compound eyes date to the early Cambrian, over 520 million years ago.
