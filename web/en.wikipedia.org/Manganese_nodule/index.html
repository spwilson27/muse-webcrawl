Manganese nodule

Manganese nodules, also called polymetallic nodules, are mineral concretions on the sea floor formed of concentric layers of iron and manganese hydroxides around a core. They occur on vast areas of the abyssal plains of the world's oceans, typically at depths of 4,000 to 6,000 metres.

Composition

As well as manganese and iron, the nodules contain economically interesting metals such as nickel, copper and cobalt, adsorbed from seawater over millions of years. A typical nodule grows only a few millimetres per million years, making them among the slowest-growing geological formations known.

Distribution

The most studied region is the Clarion-Clipperton Zone in the north-east Pacific Ocean, where nodules lie scattered across the seabed in densities high enough to attract commercial interest. Other significant fields occur in the Peru Basin, the Cook Islands exclusive economic zone and the Indian Ocean.

Deep-sea mining

Since the 1970s, companies and states have explored collecting nodules for their metal content. The activity is regulated internationally by the International Seabed Authority, and it remains controversial because of the potential damage to poorly understood deep-sea ecosystems.
