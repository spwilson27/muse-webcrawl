Semantic internalism

Semantic internalism (also called semantic individualism) is a position in the philosophy of language and philosophy of mind holding that the meanings of a speaker's words, and the contents of their mental states, depend only on what is inside the speaker—their internal, intrinsic properties—and not on the external environment or the linguistic practices of their community.

Contrast with externalism

Semantic internalism is the opposite of semantic externalism, which holds that meaning and mental content are at least partly determined by factors outside the individual. The classic arguments for externalism are Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment, which concluded that "meanings just ain't in the head", and Tyler Burge's arthritis thought experiment, which emphasised the role of the linguistic community in fixing the content of a speaker's beliefs.

Internalist responses

Internalists have responded in several ways. Some distinguish two kinds of content: a narrow content determined internally, which tracks the psychological role of a state, and a wide content determined externally. Others argue that the Twin Earth intuitions track reference rather than meaning, or that internal descriptive states suffice to fix reference in ways that preserve internalism. Philosophers associated with internalist or descriptivist positions include David Chalmers (with his two-dimensional semantics) and Frank Jackson.

Motivations

Internalism is motivated by considerations about privileged access—the apparent ability of thinkers to know their own thoughts without investigating the environment—and by the causal explanation of behaviour, which seems to depend on factors internal to the agent. Critics reply that internalism struggles to account for the success of communication and the phenomenon of deference to experts.
