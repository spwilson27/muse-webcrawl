Yagul

Yagul is an archaeological site and former city-state associated with the Zapotec civilisation, located in the Tlacolula arm of the Valley of Oaxaca in the Mexican state of Oaxaca, near the modern town of Tlacolula de Matamoros. Together with the prehistoric caves of the central valley — including Guilá Naquitz — the site forms the cultural landscape inscribed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site in 2010 under the name "Prehistoric Caves of Yagul and Mitla in the Central Valley of Oaxaca."

The site

The fortress-city occupies a rocky hill overlooking the valley floor. It rose to prominence after the abandonment of Monte Albán, around 750 CE, and flourished in the Late Classic and Postclassic periods. Excavated in the 1950s and 1960s, Yagul contains a large ball court — one of the largest in Mesoamerica — palace complexes with patios and cruciform tombs, temples and extensive defensive works. The Palace of Six Patios is a labyrinthine residential complex of courts and rooms associated with the city's rulers.

Guilá Naquitz and early agriculture

The Yagul landscape is equally famous for its caves. Guilá Naquitz, a small rock shelter excavated by Kent Flannery in 1966, yielded some of the earliest evidence for plant domestication in the Americas, including squash remains dated to around 10,000 years ago and early maize cobs. Nearby caves preserve pictographs and evidence of occupation by hunter-gatherers through the Archaic period, documenting the transition from foraging to farming in the valley.
