Ethnographic film

Ethnographic film is a non-fiction film, often similar to a documentary film, historically shot by Western filmmakers and dealing with non-Western peoples. The genre has its origins in the early twentieth century, when explorers, anthropologists and travellers filmed the customs, rituals and daily lives of communities they encountered, frequently in colonial contexts.

History

Early landmark works include Robert J. Flaherty's Nanook of the North (1922), a portrait of Inuit life that blended observation with staged sequences, and Margaret Mead and Gregory Bateson's studies of Balinese culture in the 1930s, which produced films such as Trance and Dance in Bali.

In the postwar period, French filmmaker Jean Rouch pioneered an approach he called cinéma vérité and later "ethnofiction", in which subjects participated in shaping the film. The Harvard Film Study Center, founded in 1957, became an important institutional base, and in the 1960s and 1970s the development of lightweight synchronised-sound equipment transformed fieldwork filming.

Criticism

Ethnographic film has been criticised for reproducing colonial power relations, presenting cultures as timeless and exotic, and denying subjects a voice in their own representation. Since the 1980s, indigenous media movements and collaborative filmmaking practices have challenged the outsider's gaze, and the field has increasingly emphasised reflexivity and shared authorship.
