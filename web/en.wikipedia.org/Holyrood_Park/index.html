Holyrood Park, also called the King's Park or Queen's Park depending on the reigning monarch, is a royal park in central Edinburgh, Scotland, about a mile east of Edinburgh Castle. It is a striking landscape of hills, crags, lochs, and glens covering 260 hectares (650 acres), adjoining the Palace of Holyroodhouse. The park's dramatic topography is the remnant of a Carboniferous volcano that erupted around 340 million years ago.

Features

The park's highest point is Arthur's Seat (251 m), the plug and summit of the ancient volcano, one of the most popular walks in Edinburgh and the site of an Iron Age hill fort. The basalt cliffs of Salisbury Crags form a sweeping escarpment along the park's western edge, beneath which runs the Radical Road, built by unemployed weavers in the 1820s. The Hutton's Section exposures along the crags helped James Hutton develop his theory of geological unconformity in the eighteenth century. The park contains three lochs — St Margaret's Loch, Dunsapie Loch, and Duddingston Loch, the last a bird sanctuary and the site of the historic Duddingston village on the park's southern edge.

History

The area was enclosed as a royal hunting park in the twelfth century by David I, founder of Holyrood Abbey. The ruins of St Anthony's Chapel, dating from the fifteenth century, stand above St Margaret's Loch. Charles II had the park wall rebuilt in the seventeenth century; the boundary wall and gates remain prominent features. In the nineteenth century Queen Victoria and Prince Albert landscaped parts of the park and created Queen's Drive, the road that circles Arthur's Seat.

Archaeological remains in the park include hill forts on Arthur's Seat and Dunsapie, cultivation terraces on the eastern slopes, and the well of St Anthony. The park is protected as a Scheduled Ancient Monument and a Site of Special Scientific Interest for its geology and grassland habitats.
