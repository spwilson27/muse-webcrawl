Baroque architecture


St. Peter's Basilica in Rome, a landmark of Baroque architecture

Baroque architecture is a highly decorative and theatrical style which appeared in Italy in the early 17th century and gradually spread across Europe. It was originally introduced by the Catholic Church, particularly by the Jesuits, as a means to combat the Reformation and the Protestant church with a new architecture that inspired awe and emotion. It reached its height in the "High Baroque" (1625–1675), when it was used in churches and palaces in Italy, Spain, Portugal, France, Bavaria and Austria. In the Late Baroque period (1675–1750), it reached as far as Russia and the Spanish and Portuguese colonies in Latin America. Around 1730, an even more elaborately decorative variant called Rococo appeared and flourished in Central Europe.

Baroque architects took the basic elements of Renaissance architecture, including domes and colonnades, and made them higher, grander, more decorated, and more dramatic. The interior effects were often achieved with the use of quadratura, or trompe-l'œil painting combined with sculpture; the eye is directed to the most important feature and creates a sense of movement and drama.

Characteristics

Typical features of Baroque architecture include:

  • Giant orders and large-scale colonnades
  • Oval and undulating wall surfaces, replacing the flat planes of the Renaissance
  • Dramatic use of light and shadow (chiaroscuro)
  • Rich ornamentation, gilding, and coloured marble
  • Large ceiling frescoes integrating painting, sculpture and architecture
  • Long, narrow naves in churches with elliptical domes
  • Broken pediments and exuberant sculptural decoration

Origins in Italy

The style emerged in Rome in the early 1600s. Carlo Maderno extended the nave of St Peter's Basilica and designed its facade. Gian Lorenzo Bernini created the great elliptical piazza and colonnades before St Peter's, the baldacchino over the high altar, and the Church of Sant'Andrea al Quirinale with its oval plan. Francesco Borromini, Bernini's rival, designed San Carlo alle Quattro Fontane, whose undulating facade and oval dome epitomised the Baroque concern with movement. Pietro da Cortona contributed dramatic facades such as that of Santa Maria della Pace.

Spread across Europe

In France, Baroque classicism shaped the Palace of Versailles under Louis Le Vau, Jules Hardouin-Mansart and André Le Nôtre. In Spain, the style took the ultra-ornate form known as Churrigueresque. In southern Germany, Austria and Bohemia, architects such as Johann Bernhard Fischer von Erlach and Balthasar Neumann created pilgrimage churches and palaces of great dynamism, culminating in the Würzburg Residence. In England, Christopher Wren's St Paul's Cathedral and the work of John Vanbrugh and Nicholas Hawksmoor represented a restrained Baroque. In colonial Latin America and the Philippines, local craftsmen merged European Baroque with indigenous motifs.
