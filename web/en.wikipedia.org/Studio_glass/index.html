Studio glass is the modern use of glass as an artistic medium, in which artists design and produce one-of-a-kind or limited-edition works in independent studios rather than in industrial factories. The movement began in the United States in the early 1960s and spread worldwide, transforming glass from a primarily functional and industrial material into a medium of fine art sculpture.

Origins

The movement is generally dated to 1962, when ceramics professor Harvey Littleton, working with glass researcher Dominick Labino, held two workshops at the Toledo Museum of Art. Labino had developed a small furnace capable of melting glass at temperatures low enough for a studio, together with a glass formula that melted at those temperatures — technical breakthroughs that freed artists from factory facilities. Littleton went on to found the first university glass program at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, training a first generation of studio glass artists.

Development

In 1971 Littleton's student Dale Chihuly co-founded the Pilchuck Glass School in Washington State, which became an international center for glass art. Institutions such as the Corning Museum of Glass and its Steuben studio, and later museums and galleries dedicated to glass, supported the movement's growth. European traditions, including Czech and Venetian glassmaking, cross-fertilized with the American studio movement from the 1970s onward.

Techniques

Studio glass encompasses glassblowing, kiln-formed (fused and slumped) glass, casting, cold working, and lampworking. Because works are made individually or in small teams, studio glass pieces emphasize unique form, color, and surface rather than the repeatability prized in factory production.
