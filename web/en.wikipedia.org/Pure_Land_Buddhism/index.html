Pure Land Buddhism (Chinese: 淨土宗, Jìngtǔzōng; Japanese: 浄土仏教, Jōdo bukkyō) is a broad branch of Mahāyāna Buddhism focused on devotion to the Buddha Amitābha and the aspiration to be reborn in his Pure Land, Sukhāvatī, a realm ideally suited to attaining enlightenment. It is one of the most widely practised forms of Buddhism in East Asia.

Teachings

According to the Pure Land sutras, Amitābha, while still the bodhisattva Dharmākara, made 48 vows, including the "Primal Vow" that anyone who sincerely entrusts themselves to him and calls his name even ten times will be reborn in his land. Pure Land thought contrasts difficult "self-power" (jiriki) practices with reliance on Amitābha's "other-power" (tariki), making liberation accessible to ordinary people in the present degenerate age (mappō).

History

Pure Land ideas developed in India and were systematised in China by figures such as Tanluan, Daochuo and Shandao. In Japan, Hōnen (1133–1212) founded the Jōdo-shū school teaching exclusive recitation of the nembutsu ("Namu Amida Butsu"), and his disciple Shinran (1173–1263) founded Jōdo Shinshū, emphasising faith (shinjin) as itself a gift of Amida. Pure Land practice also remains central in Chinese and Vietnamese Buddhism, often combined with Chan/Zen.

Practices

  • Recitation of Amitābha's name (nianfo/nembutsu), aloud or silently, singly or in groups
  • Visualisation of the Pure Land as described in the Contemplation Sutra
  • Devotional services, chanting of the sutras, and deathbed recitation
