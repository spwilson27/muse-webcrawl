Medigap

Medigap, also called Medicare supplement insurance, refers to various private health insurance plans sold to supplement Medicare in the United States. Medigap policies are standardized and are regulated by state and federal law. They are designed to cover some of the health care costs that Original Medicare (Parts A and B) does not cover, such as copayments, coinsurance, and deductibles. Medigap policies may also cover certain costs not covered by Medicare at all, such as medical care when traveling outside the United States.

Medigap plans fill cost-sharing "gaps" in Original Medicare.

Standardized plans

Since 1992, Medigap policies sold in most states have been standardized into plans identified by letters (A, B, C, D, F, G, K, L, M, and N). Every policy in a given letter category offers the same basic benefits regardless of the insurer; only the premiums differ. Plan F, once the most popular, is no longer available to people who became eligible for Medicare after 1 January 2020; Plan G has become the most comprehensive plan available to new beneficiaries. Massachusetts, Minnesota, and Wisconsin have their own standardization systems.

Enrollment

The best time to buy a Medigap policy is during the six-month Medigap Open Enrollment Period, which begins when a person is 65 or older and enrolled in Medicare Part B; during this period insurers cannot deny coverage or charge more because of pre-existing conditions. Outside this window, insurers in most states may use medical underwriting. Medigap policies only work with Original Medicare and cannot be used with Medicare Advantage (Part C) plans. Since 2006, Medigap policies may not include prescription drug coverage; drug coverage is obtained separately through Medicare Part D.
