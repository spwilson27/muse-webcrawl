Joseph Cornell (24 December 1903 – 29 December 1972) was an American visual artist and filmmaker, one of the pioneers and most celebrated exponents of assemblage. He is best known for his shadow boxes — glass-fronted wooden boxes containing arrangements of found objects, prints and ephemera — that evoke memory, astronomy, birds, childhood and vanished worlds of travel and theatre.

Life

Cornell was born in Nyack, New York, and attended Phillips Academy in Andover. After his father's death he became the family's provider, working as a textile salesman in New York City while living nearly his whole life in a modest house on Utopia Parkway in Flushing, Queens, caring for his mother and his brother Robert, who had cerebral palsy. Largely self-taught, he drew inspiration from Max Ernst's collages, the Surrealists, the ballet, astronomy and his vast home archive of clippings, films and trinkets.

Work

Cornell exhibited at the Julien Levy Gallery from 1932 and was included in the Museum of Modern Art's landmark 1936 exhibition Fantastic Art, Dada, Surrealism. His boxes form series: the Soap Bubble Sets, the Medici Slot Machines, the Aviary parrot boxes, the Hotel boxes and the celestial Navigation boxes. His 1936 collage film Rose Hobart, assembled from a B-movie and projected through blue glass, scandalised Salvador Dalí and became a founding work of found-footage cinema. He corresponded and exchanged works with Marcel Duchamp and many other artists while remaining famously reclusive.

Legacy

Cornell died in Flushing in 1972. His boxes influenced Pop art, Fluxus and generations of assemblage artists, and his studio's contents were preserved and studied through the Joseph Cornell Study Center. Major retrospectives have been held at the Museum of Modern Art and the Royal Academy.
