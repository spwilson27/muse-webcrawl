The Great Sphinx of Giza is a limestone statue of a reclining sphinx, a mythical creature with the head of a human and the body of a lion, standing on the west bank of the Nile at the Giza Plateau in Egypt. It represents the pharaoh Khafre, whose pyramid complex it guards. The face of the Sphinx appears damaged by weathering and iconoclasm. It is the oldest known monumental sculpture in Egypt and one of the most recognizable statues in the world.

Description and construction

Carved from a single ridge of the Mokattam Formation limestone, the Sphinx is 73 meters long from paw to tail, 19 meters wide and 20 meters high from base to head — the largest surviving monolith statue from the ancient world. It faces due east. The head wears the nemes headdress with a uraeus; a fragmentary royal beard and the head of a serpent were found in the sand below and are now in museums. Blocks cut from around the body were used to build the Sphinx Temple in front of it. Mainstream Egyptology dates the statue to Khafre (c. 2558–2532 BC), based on the archaeological context of his pyramid complex and stylistic analysis, though minority theories proposing a much older date based on water-erosion arguments have not gained acceptance.

The missing nose

The statue's nose is famously absent. Drawings by the Danish explorer Frederic Louis Norden (1737) already show it missing, disproving the popular legend that Napoleon's soldiers shot it off. Examination shows the nose was deliberately pried away with chisels, probably in the medieval period; Arab sources attribute the vandalism to the Sufi zealot Muhammad Sa'im al-Dahr in 1378, who is said to have been lynched by locals for the act.

Restoration and the Dream Stela

The Sphinx has been buried up to its neck in sand for much of its history. Between its paws stands the Dream Stela of Thutmose IV (c. 1401 BC), recounting how the young prince fell asleep in the statue's shadow and dreamed the god Horemakhet promised him the crown if he cleared the sand — one of several ancient restorations, followed by Roman, 19th-century (Caviglia, 1817; Mariette, 1850s) and modern Egyptian conservation campaigns. Weathering of the soft limestone layers remains a continuing conservation problem.

Mythology and names

In the New Kingdom the Sphinx was identified with the god Horemakhet ("Horus of the Horizon"). Its Arabic name, Abu al-Hawl ("Father of Terror"), may echo the Coptic name. Greeks applied the word "sphinx" from their own winged lion-woman of Theban legend.
