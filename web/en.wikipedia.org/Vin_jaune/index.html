Vin jaune

Vin jaune (French for "yellow wine") is a special and characteristic type of white wine made in the Jura wine region of eastern France. It is made from the Savagnin grape and is produced by a process of extended oxidative ageing under a veil of yeast, similar in some respects to the making of dry sherry, though vin jaune is not fortified. It is widely regarded as one of the great white wines of France and is noted for its remarkable longevity.

Production

After fermentation, the wine is aged in old oak barrels for a minimum of six years and three months. The barrels are not topped up, so evaporation — the "angels' share" — creates an air space in which a film of yeast, known locally as the voile (veil), develops on the surface of the wine. This veil protects the wine from full oxidation while allowing the slow development of its characteristic flavours. Roughly a third of the volume is lost to evaporation during ageing.

Characteristics

Vin jaune is intensely flavoured, with aromas of walnuts, curry spices, dried fruit and hazelnuts. On the palate it is dry, powerful and very long. Because of its oxidative style and high acidity, well-made examples can age for many decades, and bottles a century old have been reported to remain drinkable. It is traditionally served slightly cool and pairs famously with Comté cheese and with chicken in cream sauce, notably poulet au vin jaune.

Bottling and appellations

Vin jaune is sold in a distinctive squat 62-centilitre bottle called a clavelin, said to represent the volume remaining from one litre of young wine after ageing. It may be produced under the Côtes du Jura appellation and in specific communes, most famously Château-Chalon, which has its own appellation reserved exclusively for vin jaune. Each year the wine is celebrated at the Percée du Vin Jaune festival, held in a different Jura village.
