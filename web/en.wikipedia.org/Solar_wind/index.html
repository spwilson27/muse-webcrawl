The solar wind is a stream of charged particles released from the upper atmosphere of the Sun, called the corona. This plasma consists mostly of electrons, protons, and alpha particles with kinetic energy between 0.5 and 10 keV. The wind varies in density, temperature, and speed over time and solar latitude, and it carries the Sun's magnetic field outward in a spiral pattern, filling the heliosphere.

History

The idea that the Sun emits a particle stream was suggested in the 19th century to explain comet tails and auroras. In 1958 Eugene Parker predicted theoretically that the hot corona must expand supersonically into space; Soviet and American spacecraft, beginning with Luna 1 and Mariner 2, confirmed the wind's existence in the early 1960s. NASA named its Parker Solar Probe (launched 2018) in his honour; it has flown through the corona itself.

Properties

Fast wind (~750 km/s) streams from coronal holes at high latitudes, while slow wind (~400 km/s) emerges near the solar equator. At Earth's orbit the wind typically contains about 5 protons per cubic centimetre and takes roughly four days to arrive from the Sun. The wind's ram pressure is small but sculpts comet tails, planetary magnetospheres, and the cavity of the heliosphere, whose boundary the Voyager spacecraft crossed in 2012 and 2018.

Effects on Earth

Where the wind meets Earth's magnetosphere it drives auroras and, during coronal mass ejections, geomagnetic storms. Over geological time the wind strips atmosphere from unmagnetized bodies such as Mars, a process it could not accomplish on Earth because of our magnetic shield.
