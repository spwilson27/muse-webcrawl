Garamantes

The Garamantes were an ancient people of Berber origin who lived in the Fezzan region of present-day southwestern Libya, deep in the Sahara Desert. They flourished from roughly 500 BCE to about 700 CE, building a remarkable desert civilization based on an elaborate underground irrigation system.

History

Herodotus mentioned the Garamantes in the 5th century BCE as a numerous people who herded cattle and hunted. Their capital was Garama, near modern-day Germa. At their height they controlled trade routes across the Sahara, exchanging gold, slaves, and other goods between sub-Saharan Africa and the Mediterranean world.

The Garamantes came into conflict with the Roman Empire; Cornelius Balbus campaigned against them in 19 BCE, and their territory was eventually absorbed into the Roman sphere, though they retained considerable autonomy. With the Arab conquests of the 7th century CE their civilization faded.

Irrigation and agriculture

The Garamantes are famous for their foggaras — underground aqueduct channels, similar to Persian qanats, that tapped fossil water from sandstone aquifers and carried it to oases. More than 600 foggaras, extending over a thousand kilometres in total, have been identified. This system supported wheat, barley, millet, dates, grapes, and cotton. Modern scholarship suggests the eventual depletion of the accessible aquifer contributed to their decline.

Archaeology

Excavations by Charles Daniels and later by the Fazzan Project under David Mattingly uncovered fortified settlements, cemeteries with tens of thousands of burials, and evidence of a literate, stratified society with crafts, imports from the Roman world, and a written script related to Tifinagh.
