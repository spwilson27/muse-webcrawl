History of the Quakers

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Friends meeting house; Quaker worship traditionally takes place in silence.

The history of the Quakers, or the Religious Society of Friends, began in England in the mid-seventeenth century during the upheavals of the English Civil War. George Fox (1624–1691), considered the movement's principal founder, began preaching in 1647 that Christ taught his people himself, without need of ordained clergy — the doctrine of the Inner Light. In 1652, after Fox's vision on Pendle Hill and his preaching at Firbank Fell, the movement spread rapidly across northern England.

Persecution and expansion

Early Friends were mocked as "Quakers" — a nickname Fox recorded after telling a magistrate to "tremble at the word of the Lord". Their refusal of oaths, tithes, hat honour and military service, and their unprogrammed silent worship, brought severe persecution under the Commonwealth and after the Restoration; thousands were imprisoned under the Quaker Act 1662 and the Conventicle Acts. Missionaries, including the "Valiant Sixty", carried the movement across Britain and to the American colonies and the Caribbean, where Quakers such as Mary Dyer were executed in Boston.

William Penn, who had joined Friends in 1667, founded Pennsylvania in 1681 as a "holy experiment" in religious toleration, and Quakers became prominent in colonial America.

Quietism and reform

The eighteenth century saw a turn inward ("quietism"), strict discipline and endogamy, alongside growing involvement in humanitarian causes. Quakers were among the earliest organized opponents of slavery; by the 1750s London and Philadelphia Yearly Meetings condemned the slave trade, and Friends such as John Woolman campaigned against slaveholding itself. In 1783 British Quakers presented the first abolitionist petition to Parliament.

In the nineteenth century, Friends became noted industrialists and philanthropists in Britain (the Cadbury, Rowntree and Fry families) and reformers in prison reform (Elizabeth Fry), education and mental health care (the York Retreat). The movement also split: the Hicksite–Orthodox separation of 1827–28 in America and later Gurneyite and Conservative branches reflected theological tensions.

Twentieth century onward

During both world wars, many Quakers were conscientious objectors and served in relief work, such as the Friends Ambulance Unit. The American Friends Service Committee and the Friends Service Council shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 1947. Quakers have remained active in peace, human rights and development work, and in moves toward reconciliation. Today Friends number roughly 380,000 worldwide, with the largest populations in Kenya and the Americas, worshipping in both unprogrammed (silent) and programmed (pastoral) traditions.
