Invisible hand

The invisible hand describes how self-interested behaviour can promote the public interest.
Key figureAdam Smith
Key worksThe Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759); The Wealth of Nations (1776)
FieldEconomic theory
Related conceptsLaissez-faire, market equilibrium, spontaneous order

The invisible hand is a metaphor used by the Scottish economist and philosopher Adam Smith to describe the unintended social benefits of an individual's self-interested actions. Introduced in The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759) and developed most famously in An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (1776), the concept holds that individuals pursuing their own gain in a competitive market are led, as if by an invisible hand, to promote the interests of society more effectively than if they had directly intended to do so.

Origins in Smith's work

Smith used the phrase only three times in his published writings. In The Theory of Moral Sentiments, he argued that the rich, in consuming luxury goods, are led by an invisible hand to distribute the necessaries of life among the poor. In The Wealth of Nations, he used it in a narrower context: a merchant who prefers domestic investment over foreign trade for reasons of security unintentionally increases the annual revenue of his society.

The famous butcher, brewer, and baker passage illustrates the underlying idea: "It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker, that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest."

Later interpretations

In the 20th century the invisible hand became a central emblem of laissez-faire economics and of the efficiency of markets. Economists connected it to the price mechanism, which coordinates the decisions of millions of buyers and sellers, and to the first fundamental theorem of welfare economics, which states conditions under which competitive equilibria are Pareto efficient. Critics note that Smith himself assigned government important roles—in defence, justice, public works, and education—and that the metaphor can fail in the presence of externalities, monopoly, or information asymmetries.
