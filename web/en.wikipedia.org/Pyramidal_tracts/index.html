Pyramidal tracts

The pyramidal tracts are the great descending motor pathways of the central nervous system. They carry signals from the cerebral cortex to the brainstem and spinal cord and comprise two systems: the corticospinal tract and the corticobulbar tract. Their name comes from the pyramid-shaped bundles they form on the ventral surface of the medulla oblongata.

Pyramidal tracts
Components: Corticospinal, corticobulbar
Origin: Motor cortex, layer 5
Function: Voluntary movement
Upper motor neurons: Pyramidal cells

Corticospinal tract

Axons of upper motor neurons, mainly pyramidal cells of the primary motor cortex, descend through the internal capsule, cerebral peduncle, and pons into the medullary pyramids. There, about 90% of the fibres cross (decussate) to the opposite side, forming the lateral corticospinal tract, which governs skilled movements of the limbs; the remainder descend uncrossed as the anterior corticospinal tract, supplying axial and proximal muscles.

Corticobulbar tract

The corticobulbar tract projects to the motor nuclei of the cranial nerves in the brainstem, controlling muscles of the face, jaw, tongue, and pharynx. Most nuclei receive bilateral cortical input, a notable exception being the part of the facial nucleus supplying the lower face.

Clinical significance

Damage to the pyramidal tracts produces upper motor neuron signs: weakness, spasticity, hyperreflexia, and a positive Babinski sign. Causes include stroke, multiple sclerosis, and spinal cord injury.
