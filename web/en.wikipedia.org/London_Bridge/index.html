London Bridge refers to several historic crossings that have spanned the River Thames between the City of London and Southwark, in central London, England. The current crossing, a prestressed concrete box girder bridge opened in 1973, replaced a nineteenth-century stone-arched bridge designed by John Rennie, which in turn succeeded a medieval stone structure built between 1176 and 1209.

History

Roman engineers probably built the first wooden bridge near the site around AD 50, serving the new port and settlement of Londinium. The medieval bridge, begun under Peter of Colechurch, carried houses, shops, and a chapel of St Thomas Becket; for centuries it was the only bridge across the Thames in London. Its nineteen arches and a drawbridge section, with traitors' heads displayed on spikes at the southern gate, made it a landmark of the city.

Rennie's granite bridge (1831) proved too narrow for modern traffic. In 1968 it was famously sold to the American entrepreneur Robert P. McCulloch for US$2.46 million and re-erected, stone by stone, at Lake Havasu City, Arizona — giving rise to the (incorrect) urban legend that McCulloch believed he was buying Tower Bridge.

In culture

The bridge is celebrated in the nursery rhyme "London Bridge Is Falling Down", whose origins and meaning remain debated. The present bridge is a comparatively plain structure and must not be confused with Tower Bridge (1894), the bascule bridge with twin Gothic towers downstream.
