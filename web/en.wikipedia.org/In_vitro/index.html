In vitro

In vitro (Latin for "in glass"; often not italicised in English) is a term used in science, especially biology and medicine, for experiments, procedures and processes performed outside a living organism, in a controlled artificial environment such as a test tube, culture dish or flask. Studies conducted in vitro allow scientists to isolate specific components — cells, tissues, organs, proteins or DNA — and examine their behaviour under precisely controlled conditions that would be impossible or unethical to reproduce inside a living body.

The term is contrasted with in vivo (within the living organism) and ex vivo (tissue removed from an organism and kept alive outside it), and in computational work with in silico.

Definition and uses

In vitro methods are fundamental to molecular biology, biochemistry, pharmacology and toxicology. Common examples include:

  • Cell culture — growing cells derived from multicellular organisms in nutrient medium, used to study cell physiology, test drugs and produce vaccines and recombinant proteins such as insulin.
  • In vitro fertilisation (IVF) — fertilising an egg with sperm in a laboratory dish to treat infertility.
  • Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) — amplifying DNA sequences in a tube.
  • Enzyme and protein assays — measuring the activity of purified proteins in solution.
  • In vitro toxicology testing — screening chemicals on cultured cells as an alternative or preliminary step to animal testing.

Advantages and limitations

The chief advantages of in vitro work are control and simplicity: temperature, pH, nutrient supply and exposure to test substances can be manipulated one variable at a time, experiments are comparatively fast and inexpensive, and results are often easier to measure and reproduce. In vitro studies also reduce the use of experimental animals.

The central limitation is extrapolation. An isolated cell in a dish lacks the hormonal signals, circulation, immune interactions and three-dimensional tissue architecture of a whole organism, so a substance that is effective or toxic in vitro may behave quite differently in vivo. Drug candidates, for example, routinely fail in animal or human trials despite promising in vitro results because of poor absorption, metabolism or distribution — a gap sometimes summarised by the warning that "in vitro success does not equal clinical success".

History

The practice grew out of nineteenth-century physiology and bacteriology: Robert Koch's pure cultures on solid media, Ross Harrison's 1907 tissue-culture experiments, and Alexis Carrel's cell-culture work established that living cells could be maintained and studied outside the body. The establishment of immortal cell lines, most famously HeLa cells in 1951, made in vitro research routine across the life sciences.
