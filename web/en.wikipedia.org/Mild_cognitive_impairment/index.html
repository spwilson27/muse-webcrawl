Mild cognitive impairment

SpecialtyNeurology, psychiatry, neuropsychology
SymptomsMemory or thinking decline beyond normal aging, daily function preserved
TypesAmnestic, non-amnestic
RiskIncreased risk of dementia, esp. Alzheimer's disease

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is a condition in which a person experiences measurable decline in memory or other cognitive abilities beyond what is expected for their age, but not severe enough to interfere substantially with daily life and independence — distinguishing it from dementia. MCI is considered a transitional stage between normal aging and dementia, although many people with MCI remain stable or even revert to normal cognition.

Classification

Amnestic MCI primarily affects memory and is most often an early manifestation of Alzheimer's disease. Non-amnestic MCI affects other domains — language, attention, executive function, or visuospatial skills — and may precede other dementias. Each type may involve a single domain or multiple domains.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on a cognitive concern from the patient or an informant, objective impairment on neuropsychological testing (typically 1–1.5 standard deviations below norms), preserved independence in daily activities, and exclusion of dementia and other causes such as depression, medication effects, or sleep apnea. Biomarkers of amyloid and tau pathology can identify MCI due to Alzheimer's disease.

Progression and management

People with MCI progress to dementia at roughly 10–15 percent per year in clinic-based samples, higher than the general older population. No drug is approved specifically for MCI, though anti-amyloid therapies have been authorized for early Alzheimer's disease including its MCI stage in some jurisdictions. Management emphasizes cardiovascular risk control, exercise, cognitive engagement, treatment of hearing loss and mood, and periodic monitoring.
