Case law

Also calledJudge-made law; common law; precedent
Legal systemsCommon law jurisdictions
Key doctrineStare decisis

Case law, also used synonymously with common law in some contexts, is law that is established by the outcome of former court cases. It is the body of law derived from judicial decisions of courts and similar tribunals, as opposed to law created by legislatures (statutes) or by executives through regulations. In common law jurisdictions, case law is a primary source of law.

Precedent and stare decisis

Case law operates under the principle of stare decisis ("to stand by things decided"), under which courts are bound to follow the legal principles — the ratio decidendi — of earlier decisions by equal or higher courts. The binding precedent system gives the law consistency and predictability while still allowing development: a court may distinguish a precedent on its facts, and a higher court may overrule an earlier decision that is considered wrong or obsolete.

Case law versus statute

Where a statute governs a question, it prevails over conflicting case law, but judges interpret statutes and their interpretations themselves become case law. Much of the traditional common law — contract, tort, and property principles in England, the United States, Canada, Australia, and other jurisdictions — developed principally through case law before being partly codified.

Reporting

Decisions are collected in law reports and databases (such as Westlaw and LexisNexis) and cited by name and citation, for example Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562. In civil law systems, by contrast, judicial decisions do not generally have formally binding precedential force, though consistent lines of decisions (jurisprudence constante) may be highly persuasive.
