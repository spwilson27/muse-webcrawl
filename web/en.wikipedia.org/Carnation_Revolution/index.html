Carnation Revolution

Civilians placing carnations in soldiers' rifles, Lisbon, 25 April 1974 (placeholder)

The Carnation Revolution (Portuguese: Revolução dos Cravos), also known as the 25 April (25 de Abril), was a military coup by the Armed Forces Movement (MFA) in Lisbon on 25 April 1974 that overthrew Portugal's authoritarian Estado Novo regime, ending nearly five decades of dictatorship and thirteen years of colonial wars in Africa. It is named for the carnations that civilians placed in the muzzles of soldiers' rifles and on their uniforms; despite the upheaval, the coup was almost bloodless, with the only deaths caused by the regime's political police firing on a crowd.

Background

The Estado Novo, founded by António de Oliveira Salazar in 1933 and continued after 1968 by Marcelo Caetano, kept Portugal poor, censored, and policed by the PIDE secret police, while conscripting a generation of young men for unwinnable colonial wars in Angola, Mozambique, and Portuguese Guinea. In February 1974 General António de Spínola published Portugal and the Future, arguing the wars could not be won militarily. Junior officers, many of them war veterans aggrieved by career grievances and the unwinnable wars, organised the MFA to seize power.

The coup

The operation was triggered by two songs broadcast on the radio: "E depois do adeus" late on 24 April (the alert signal) and, at 00:20 on 25 April, the banned song "Grândola, Vila Morena" by José Afonso (the go signal). Troops led by officers such as Captain Salgueiro Maia occupied key points in Lisbon; crowds flooded the streets, fraternising with soldiers and handing them carnations. Caetano surrendered to General Spínola at the Carmo barracks, and the regime collapsed within hours.

Aftermath

The revolution unleashed a turbulent transition (the PREC) with land occupations, nationalisations, and decolonisation that granted independence to Portugal's African colonies in 1975. After a failed leftist coup attempt on 25 November 1975, moderate forces consolidated; a democratic constitution followed in 1976. 25 April is now Portugal's national Freedom Day holiday.
