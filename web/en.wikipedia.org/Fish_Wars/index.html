Fish Wars

Date1960s–1970s (direct actions)
LocationPacific Northwest, United States
IssueNative American treaty fishing rights
OutcomeBoldt Decision (1974)

The Fish Wars were a series of protests and civil disobedience actions in the Pacific Northwest of the United States during the 1960s and early 1970s, in which Native American tribes asserted their treaty rights to fish in their usual and accustomed places.

Background

Under the treaties of the 1850s, such as the Treaty of Medicine Creek, tribes in what became Washington State ceded vast lands but reserved the right to fish "in common with" settlers. As commercial and sport fishing grew and salmon runs declined, state authorities increasingly arrested tribal fishers, including members of the Nisqually and Puyallup tribes, for fishing off-reservation.

Fish-ins

Inspired by the sit-ins of the civil rights movement, tribal activists staged "fish-ins" — deliberately fishing in defiance of state regulations. Figures such as Billy Frank Jr. and Hank Adams organised the campaigns, which drew support and publicity from celebrities including Marlon Brando and Dick Gregory.

Boldt Decision

The protests culminated in the federal court case United States v. Washington. In 1974 Judge George Hugo Boldt ruled that the treaties entitled the tribes to up to half of the harvestable fish and recognised them as co-managers of the resource — a landmark victory for tribal sovereignty.
