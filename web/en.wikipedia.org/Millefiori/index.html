Millefiori

Millefiori (Italian: [ˌmilleˈfjoːri]) is a glasswork technique which produces distinctive decorative patterns on glassware. The term is a combination of the Italian words mille ("thousand") and fiori ("flowers"). The technique involves fusing together rods of coloured glass — called canes or murrine — so that the cross-section of each rod shows a flower-like or geometric pattern; slices of the bundled cane are then embedded in clear glass to form beads, paperweights, pendants and vessels covered in a field of tiny "flowers".

A millefiori paperweight displays dozens of patterned cane slices.

History

Mosaic glass of this kind was made in ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, and reached a high level in Hellenistic and Roman workshops, producing bowls of fused coloured cane sections. The knowledge faded in the early medieval period but survived or was reinvented in Islamic glasshouses. Venetian glassmakers on the island of Murano revived and perfected the technique in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, guarding the methods as trade secrets. The name millefiori itself was popularised in the nineteenth century.

Revival and paperweights

From the 1840s, French factories at Baccarat, Saint-Louis and Clichy made millefiori famous in glass paperweights, arranging concentric rings of patterned canes in mushroom or garland designs; Scottish maker Paul Ysart and later studios continued the tradition. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, Murano and studio glass artists have used murrine to build complex pictorial canes — including portraits and landscapes — by assembling thousands of tiny coloured rods before fusing and slicing them.

Technique

A cane begins as a gather of molten glass layered or bundled in the desired pattern, encased in clear or contrasting glass, then heated and pulled to a thin rod, shrinking the pattern while preserving it. Cooled rods are cut into short slices (murrine), which are arranged on a surface, reheated and picked up on a gather of clear glass, then shaped into the final object. Related techniques include millefiori beads made in Murano and traded worldwide, and modern polymer-clay work imitating the same cane method.
