Marasmus is characterised by severe wasting without oedema.

Marasmus is a form of severe malnutrition characterised by energy deficiency. It can occur in anyone with severe malnutrition but usually occurs in children. Body weight is reduced to less than 62% of the normal (expected) body weight for the age. Marasmus occurrence increases prior to age 1, whereas kwashiorkor occurrence increases after 18 months. The prognosis is better than for kwashiorkor, but half of severely malnourished children die as a result of unavailability of adequate treatment.

The word "marasmus" comes from the Greek μαρασμός (marasmós), meaning "withering" or "wasting away". Marasmus is one of the forms of severe acute malnutrition, which remains a major cause of child death worldwide, typically in settings of famine, food insecurity, and chronic poverty compounded by infection.

Signs and symptoms

The child with marasmus appears severely wasted — "skin and bones" — having lost subcutaneous fat and muscle mass, with prominent ribs and a wizened "old man" face. Unlike kwashiorkor, there is no pitting oedema, and the hair is usually less affected. Body temperature may be low, the heart rate slow, and the child is often irritable when handled but listless; appetite may be surprisingly preserved. Weight-for-height is extremely low (below −3 standard deviations by WHO criteria), and mid-upper arm circumference is under 115 mm in severe cases.

Causes and physiology

Marasmus results from a grossly inadequate intake of energy — from overall starvation, early weaning onto dilute foods, chronic disease, or malabsorption. The body adapts by reducing energy expenditure and catabolising fat stores and then muscle protein to maintain essential functions and plasma protein levels; because this adaptation largely preserves serum albumin, oedema does not develop. This "adapted" pattern contrasts with kwashiorkor, in which adaptation fails and albumin falls.

Treatment

Treatment follows WHO protocols for severe acute malnutrition: stabilisation with cautious feeding (low-protein F-75 therapeutic milk), correction of hypothermia, hypoglycaemia, dehydration, and electrolytes, antibiotics for infection, and micronutrients (with iron withheld initially), followed by a rehabilitation phase with F-100 or ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF) for catch-up growth. Rapid refeeding must be avoided because of the danger of refeeding syndrome — potentially fatal shifts in phosphate, potassium, and magnesium.
