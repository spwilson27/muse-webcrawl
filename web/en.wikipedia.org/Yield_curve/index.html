Yield curve

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In finance, the yield curve is a graph that depicts the yields (interest rates) of bonds having equal credit quality but differing maturity dates. The slope of the yield curve gives an indication of future interest rate changes and economic activity. The most closely watched yield curve is that of United States Treasury securities, plotted from maturities ranging from one month to thirty years.

Shape of the curve

There are three main shapes. A normal yield curve slopes upward: longer-maturity bonds have higher yields, compensating investors for the greater risk of locking up money for longer and for expected inflation. An inverted yield curve slopes downward, with short-term yields above long-term yields; inversions of the Treasury curve have historically preceded recessions and are closely monitored as an economic indicator. A flat yield curve shows little difference between short- and long-term yields, often occurring in transitions between normal and inverted regimes.

Theories

Several theories explain the curve's shape. The expectations hypothesis holds that long-term rates reflect the average of expected future short-term rates. Liquidity preference theory adds a premium demanded for holding longer-term bonds. Market segmentation theory treats different maturities as separate markets driven by distinct supply and demand. In practice all three influences are thought to operate together.

Uses

Yield curves are used to price bonds, set rates for mortgages and loans, and gauge market expectations of monetary policy and growth. Central banks influence the short end directly through policy rates, and may influence the long end through operations such as quantitative easing. Analysts also construct yield curves for corporate bonds and other currencies, and spreads between curves are used as credit-risk indicators.
