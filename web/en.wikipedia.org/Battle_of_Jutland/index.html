The Battle of Jutland (German: Skagerrakschlacht) was a naval battle fought between Britain's Royal Navy Grand Fleet and the Imperial German Navy's High Seas Fleet on 31 May and 1 June 1916 in the North Sea near the Jutland peninsula. It was the largest naval battle of the First World War and the only full-scale clash of battleships in that war. Germany sought to lure out and destroy a portion of the Grand Fleet in order to break the British blockade; instead, thanks to signals intelligence, the entire Grand Fleet put to sea. In two fleet engagements and a confused night action, the Germans inflicted heavier losses but twice turned away from the British battle line, and escaped to port during the night.

Background

The British blockade was slowly strangling Germany's war economy, while the German fleet, inferior in numbers, avoided a decisive action. In 1916 Admiral Reinhard Scheer adopted a more aggressive strategy of raids intended to draw British forces onto U-boats and minefields. British codebreakers in Room 40, however, read the German signals, and Admiral Sir John Jellicoe's Grand Fleet sailed before the Germans had even left port.

The battle

The action began with a battlecruiser engagement: Vice-Admiral Franz von Hipper's scouting group drew Vice-Admiral David Beatty's battlecruisers south onto the German battle fleet, destroying HMS Indefatigable and Queen Mary by magazine explosions. When Beatty sighted Scheer's battleships he reversed course — the "run to the north" — and in turn drew the Germans onto Jellicoe's approaching Grand Fleet. Jellicoe deployed his fleet across the German line of advance, "crossing the T"; Scheer executed two brilliantly handled 180-degree battle turns away, covered by torpedo attacks and his battlecruisers' sacrificial charge (in which Lützow was fatally damaged). In the night action that followed, German ships broke through the British destroyer screens astern of the Grand Fleet and reached Horns Reef by morning, losing the pre-dreadnought Pommern among others; the British lost the battlecruiser Invincible — blown up in the main action — and several cruisers and destroyers.

Outcome and significance

Britain lost more ships (14, totalling about 113,000 tons) and more men (6,094) than Germany (11 ships, about 62,000 tons, 2,551 men), and both sides claimed victory. Strategically, however, the result was a British success: the blockade remained intact, and the High Seas Fleet never again risked a fleet action, eventually turning to unrestricted submarine warfare. The battle prompted reforms in British shell design, flash protection, and signalling, and remains one of the most debated naval engagements in history.
