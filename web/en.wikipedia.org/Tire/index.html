Tire

A tire (North American English) or tyre (Commonwealth English) is a ring-shaped component that surrounds a wheel's rim to transfer a vehicle's load from the axle through the wheel to the ground and to provide traction on the surface over which the vehicle travels. Most modern tires are pneumatic — filled with compressed air — and made chiefly of synthetic and natural rubber, fabric and steel cord.

History

Early wheels used iron or steel bands (also called tires) shrunk onto wooden rims. Solid rubber tires followed in the 19th century. John Boyd Dunlop's practical pneumatic bicycle tire (1888), building on Robert William Thomson's earlier 1845 patent, revolutionised cycling and was soon adapted to automobiles. Bias-ply construction dominated until Michelin's radial tire (1946) gradually took over the car market; most car tires today are tubeless radials.

Construction and markings

A tire consists of the tread (the patterned contact surface), the carcass plies, steel belts, sidewalls and beads that seal against the rim. Sidewall markings encode size — e.g. 205/55 R 16 — load index, speed rating, manufacture date and whether the tire is a summer, winter or all-season design. Specialised types include run-flat, off-road, racing slick and studded tires.

Maintenance and impact

Correct inflation pressure is vital for safety, wear and fuel economy; tires are major sources of microplastic pollution through tread wear, and waste tires are a significant recycling challenge. Tire wear, aging and damage are leading factors in road accidents and are regulated in most jurisdictions.
