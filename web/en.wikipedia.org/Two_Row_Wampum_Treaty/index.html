Two Row Wampum Treaty

The Two Row Wampum Treaty, known in the Seneca language as Kaswentha, is a mutual treaty agreement between the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy) and Dutch settlers in what is now New York, traditionally dated to 1613. It is symbolized by a wampum belt of shell beads bearing two purple rows on a white background.

Meaning

The two parallel rows represent two vessels traveling down the same river together: the Haudenosaunee canoe, containing their laws, customs, and government, and the Dutch ship, containing theirs. The rows run side by side in peace and friendship, but neither vessel attempts to steer the other — a principle of non-interference and mutual respect between sovereign peoples. The three white rows between them are said to stand for peace, friendship, and respect, and the belt records the agreement that the relationship would last "as long as the grass grows, the river flows, and the sun shines."

Historical debate and legacy

Haudenosaunee oral tradition holds that the agreement was made in 1613, the year the Dutch established a trading post at Fort Nassau near present-day Albany. Some historians have questioned whether a formal treaty document from 1613 survives, pointing to a purported treaty paper whose authenticity is disputed; others note that the relationship it describes is well attested in later Dutch and English records of diplomacy conducted through wampum. The Two Row Wampum remains a foundational symbol for Haudenosaunee sovereignty and is frequently invoked in negotiations with the governments of the United States, Canada, and the Netherlands. In 2013, the 400th anniversary was marked by an Onondaga-led campaign and a symbolic journey down the Hudson River by Indigenous and non-Indigenous paddlers.
