Whisky

Whisky
Type: Distilled spirit
Alcohol by volume: typically 40–50%
Origin: Ireland and Scotland
Ingredients: Fermented grain mash; aged in wooden casks

Whisky or whiskey is a type of distilled alcoholic beverage made from fermented grain mash. Various grains — barley, corn, rye and wheat — are used for different varieties, and whisky is typically aged in wooden casks, generally made of charred white oak. The word derives from the Gaelic uisce beatha (Irish) or uisge-beatha (Scottish), meaning "water of life".

History

The art of distillation spread to Ireland and Scotland by the late Middle Ages; the earliest Scottish record is in the Exchequer Rolls of 1494, noting malt given to Friar John Cor to make aqua vitae. Irish and Scottish traditions each claim the invention. Whisky-making spread worldwide with emigration, and major producing nations now include Canada, the United States and Japan, whose whiskies have won top international awards since the 2000s.

Styles

Scotch whisky must be distilled and matured in Scotland for at least three years in oak and falls into categories including single malt, single grain, blended malt and blended whisky; producing regions include Speyside, the Highlands, Islay — famed for peaty, smoky malts — and the Lowlands. Irish whiskey is usually triple-distilled and spelled with an "e", as is American whiskey, which includes bourbon (at least 51% corn, aged in new charred oak), Tennessee whiskey and rye whiskey. Regulations in most countries require a minimum of 40% alcohol by volume.

Production

Production involves malting (for barley), mashing, fermentation, distillation in pot or column stills, maturation in casks, and bottling. Much of a whisky's flavour comes from the cask and the length of ageing, though age statements refer only to the youngest whisky in the bottle.
