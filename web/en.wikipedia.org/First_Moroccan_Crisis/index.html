The First Moroccan Crisis, also known as the Tangier Crisis, was an international crisis between March 1905 and May 1906 over the status of Morocco. Germany attempted to use the issue of Moroccan independence to break up the Anglo-French Entente Cordiale of 1904, under which Britain had conceded France a free hand in Morocco in exchange for a free hand in Egypt. Instead, the crisis strengthened the Entente and deepened Germany's diplomatic isolation.

Background

French influence in Morocco had grown steadily, and French foreign minister Théophile Delcassé moved to establish a protectorate, negotiating loans and a program of "peaceful penetration" without consulting Germany, whose commercial interests in Morocco were also substantial. Chancellor Bernhard von Bülow and the foreign ministry official Friedrich von Holstein decided to challenge France and test the new Entente.

The Kaiser's visit

On 31 March 1905, German Emperor Wilhelm II landed at Tangier from his yacht and, riding through the city, declared that he regarded the Sultan as an independent sovereign, expected Germany's trade to be respected, and would not accept reforms compromising Moroccan independence. The speech was understood across Europe as a direct challenge to France and raised the prospect of war.

Resolution

Delcassé was forced to resign in June 1905 under German pressure, but at the Algeciras Conference (January–April 1906) Germany found itself supported only by Austria-Hungary and Morocco. The resulting Act of Algeciras nominally affirmed Moroccan independence and the open door for trade, but confirmed French and Spanish policing roles and French predominance in Moroccan finance — essentially the outcome Germany had sought to prevent. Tensions would flare again in the Agadir Crisis of 1911, after which France established a full protectorate in 1912.
