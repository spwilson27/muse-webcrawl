Vein

In the circulatory system, a vein is a blood vessel that carries blood toward the heart. Most veins carry deoxygenated blood from the tissues back to the heart; the exceptions are the pulmonary veins, which carry oxygenated blood from the lungs to the heart, and the umbilical veins in the fetus. Veins contrast with arteries, which carry blood away from the heart.

Structure

Veins have thinner walls and larger, less regular lumens than arteries of comparable size, and they operate at much lower pressure. Many veins — especially in the limbs — contain one-way valves that prevent backflow. Venous return is aided by skeletal muscle contractions (the muscle pump) and by respiratory pressure changes.

Function and classification

Veins act as a capacitance reservoir: at any moment roughly two-thirds of the body's blood volume resides in the venous system. Superficial veins lie close to the skin; deep veins run alongside arteries; perforating veins connect the two. Examples include the inferior vena cava, the jugular veins, and the great saphenous vein of the leg.

Clinical significance

Venous disorders include varicose veins, deep vein thrombosis, chronic venous insufficiency and phlebitis. Veins are also routinely used for venipuncture and intravenous therapy.
