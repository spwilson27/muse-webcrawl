Sulforaphane is an organosulfur compound of the isothiocyanate group, obtained from cruciferous vegetables such as broccoli, Brussels sprouts, and cabbage. It is not present as such in the intact plant; it forms when the enzyme myrosinase acts on its precursor, the glucosinolate glucoraphanin, after plant cells are damaged by chewing, chopping, or digestion. Broccoli sprouts are an especially concentrated source.

Biological activity

Sulforaphane is best known as a potent activator of the Nrf2 pathway, the master regulator of cellular antioxidant and phase II detoxification defenses: by modifying the sensor protein Keap1, it frees Nrf2 to switch on genes such as NQO1, heme oxygenase-1, and glutathione S-transferases. It also exhibits anti-inflammatory effects, inhibits histone deacetylases in vitro, and interferes with carcinogen metabolism.

Research

Interest in sulforaphane intensified after a 1992 Johns Hopkins study (Paul Talalay's group) identified it as a major inducer of protective enzymes in broccoli. Subsequent research has explored it in cancer prevention models, Helicobacter pylori infection, air-pollution detoxification, type 2 diabetes, and autism spectrum disorder, with human trials generally small and results preliminary. Regulatory agencies have not approved sulforaphane for the treatment of any condition, and dietary-supplement preparations vary widely in actual content, since stability and myrosinase activity strongly affect the delivered dose.
