Luminous efficacy

Luminous efficacy is a measure of how well a light source produces visible light. It is the ratio of luminous flux to power, measured in lumens per watt (lm/W) in the SI system. Depending on context, the power may be either the radiant flux of the source's output (giving the luminous efficacy of radiation) or the total power consumed by the source, including losses (giving the luminous efficacy of a source, or wall-plug efficacy).

Limits

The maximum possible luminous efficacy of radiation is 683 lm/W, for monochromatic light at 555 nm, where the photopic eye response peaks. For white light the practical theoretical ceiling is far lower — around 240–300 lm/W depending on the spectrum and colour rendering required — because energy at wavelengths the eye sees poorly cannot contribute many lumens.

Examples

Candlelight yields about 0.3 lm/W; incandescent lamps 10–17 lm/W; halogen lamps up to about 24 lm/W; compact fluorescent lamps 45–70 lm/W; and modern white LEDs commonly 80–200 lm/W, with laboratory devices exceeding 200 lm/W. High-pressure sodium street lighting reaches roughly 100–150 lm/W with poor colour rendering, while low-pressure sodium lamps approach 180 lm/W but emit nearly monochromatic orange light.
