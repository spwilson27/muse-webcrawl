Penal laws

Penal laws were laws that were passed against a particular group or class, most famously against Roman Catholics and Protestant Nonconformists in the British Isles. They imposed civil disabilities—restrictions on worship, education, office-holding, land ownership, and the franchise—on those who refused to conform to the established church.

England and Wales

English penal laws grew from the Elizabethan settlement. The Acts of Supremacy and Uniformity (1559) required attendance at Anglican worship; fines for recusancy followed, and after the Gunpowder Plot (1605) further statutes barred Catholics from law, the army, and Parliament. The Test Acts (1673, 1678) required office-holders to receive Anglican communion and renounce transubstantiation, excluding Catholics and, in practice, many dissenters. These laws were progressively repealed from 1828 (repeal of the Test and Corporation Acts) and 1829 (Catholic Emancipation), though a few restrictions, including on the monarch, survive.

Scotland

In Scotland, penal statutes targeted Catholics after the Reformation and Episcopalians after 1689 and the Jacobite risings, including restrictions on clergy and schools, most lifted by relief acts in the late 18th and 19th centuries.

Ireland

The harshest code operated in Ireland, where the Penal Laws of 1695–1728 sought to destroy the political and economic power of the Catholic majority and underpinned the Protestant Ascendancy.

Elsewhere

The term is also applied to discriminatory statutes in other contexts, such as the laws against Catholics and Huguenots in early modern France and against religious minorities in various European states.
