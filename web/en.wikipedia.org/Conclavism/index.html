Conclavism

Conclavism is the practice, associated with some sedevacantist groups, of holding an alternative papal conclave to elect a rival claimant to the papacy. Conclavists hold that the see of Rome is vacant and that the college of cardinals as constituted by the recognised Church is unable or unwilling to elect a valid pope, so that the duty of election falls to faithful clergy and laity.

Background

Conclavism arises from sedevacantism, the position that recent popes — usually from John XXIII or Paul VI onward — have been invalid because of alleged heresy, particularly in connection with the Second Vatican Council. Where most sedevacantists simply await a resolution, conclavists proceed to elect their own pope, whose claim is recognised only by his followers.

Claimants

Several conclavist elections have taken place since the 1970s, mostly in the United States and Spain. Claimants have included Clemente Domínguez y Gómez, who claimed to have been mystically crowned as Pope Gregory XVII in 1978 (the Palmarian movement), and David Bawden, elected as Pope Michael in 1990 by a small group of laypeople in Kansas. Others, such as Victor Von Pentz (Linus II) and Oscar Michaelli (Valerian I), led very small communities.

Status

Conclavist claimants are regarded by the Catholic Church and by most traditionalist Catholics as antipopes. Their followings are small, and their elections lack recognition by any significant body of clergy.
