Doctor of Philosophy

A Doctor of Philosophy (PhD, DPhil; Latin Philosophiae Doctor or Doctor Philosophiae) is a research doctorate, normally the highest academic degree awarded by universities in most countries. PhDs are awarded for research in a wide range of academic fields. Because it is an earned research degree, those studying for a PhD are required to produce original research that expands the boundaries of knowledge, normally in the form of a dissertation, and defend their work before a panel of other experts in the field. The completion of a PhD is often a requirement for employment as a university professor, researcher, or scientist in many fields.

History

The research doctorate developed in Germany in the 19th century, where the PhD at universities such as Berlin became the model for advanced scholarly training. Yale University awarded the first American PhDs in 1861, and the degree spread through the English-speaking world and beyond during the 20th century, displacing older doctorates in many systems.

Requirements

Requirements vary by country and institution, but typically include:

  • A bachelor's or master's degree with strong academic standing
  • Advanced coursework and comprehensive or qualifying examinations
  • An original research project written up as a dissertation
  • An oral defence (viva) before examiners

In the United States, completion commonly takes four to seven years; in the United Kingdom, three to four years of full-time study is typical. Funding may come from teaching or research assistantships, fellowships, or employment.

Value and careers

PhD holders pursue careers in academia, industry research, government, and the professions. Admission to doctoral programs is competitive, and academic posts for graduates are limited in many fields, prompting longstanding debate about the structure of doctoral training.
