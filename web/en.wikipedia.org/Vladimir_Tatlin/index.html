Vladimir Tatlin

Vladimir Yevgrafovich Tatlin (December 28, 1885 – May 31, 1953) was a Russian and Soviet artist, architect and stage designer, and a central figure of the Russian avant-garde. He is best known for his design for the Monument to the Third International (1919–20) — "Tatlin's Tower" — a vast spiralling structure that was never built but became an icon of Constructivism, and for his pioneering "counter-reliefs", abstract constructions of industrial materials.

Early work and counter-reliefs

Tatlin trained as a painter and worked as a merchant seaman in his youth. A 1913 visit to Picasso's Paris studio, where he saw Cubist relief constructions, inspired his own "painterly reliefs" and then the radical corner counter-reliefs of 1914–15: assemblages of wood, metal, glass and rope suspended across the corners of rooms, abandoning the picture plane for real materials in real space — the doctrine he called the "culture of materials".

Monument to the Third International

Commissioned in 1919 by the Soviet Department of Fine Arts, Tatlin's design proposed a 400-metre twin spiral of iron and glass leaning across the Neva in Petrograd — taller than the Eiffel Tower. Inside, geometric glass volumes (a cube, pyramid, cylinder and hemisphere) were to rotate at different speeds — yearly, monthly, daily and hourly — housing the legislature, executive, press and radio of the Communist International. A model was exhibited in 1920 to great acclaim; the tower's cost and engineering demands, amid civil war, made construction impossible, but the design became the emblem of constructivist architecture.

Later years

At Vkhutemas and in Petrograd, Tatlin turned constructivism toward useful design — stoves, clothing, furniture — under the banner of "art into life". His final major project was the Letatlin (1929–32), a graceful human-powered ornithopter. As Socialist Realism became compulsory, Tatlin worked mainly in theatre design and painting; he died in Moscow in 1953.
