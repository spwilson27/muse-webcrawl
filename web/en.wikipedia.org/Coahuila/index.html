Coahuila

Coahuila de Zaragoza
Capital: Saltillo
Largest city: Torreón
Area: approx. 151,600 km²
Population: approx. 3.1 million

Coahuila (officially Coahuila de Zaragoza) is one of the 32 federal entities of Mexico, located in the north-east of the country. It borders the Mexican states of Nuevo León to the east, Zacatecas to the south, Durango and Chihuahua to the west, and shares a long border along the Rio Grande with the United States state of Texas. The capital is Saltillo, and the largest metropolitan area is La Laguna, centred on Torreón. With an area of about 151,600 square kilometres, it is the third-largest Mexican state.

Geography

Much of Coahuila lies within the Chihuahuan Desert, with the Sierra Madre Oriental running along its eastern edge. The region of Cuatro Ciénegas is famous for its spring-fed pools (pozas) harbouring endemic fish, turtles, and stromatolites of great scientific interest. The state includes part of the Bolsón de Mapimí and a segment of the Rio Grande plain used for irrigated agriculture.

History

Before Spanish colonisation the region was inhabited by nomadic hunter-gatherer peoples collectively called Chichimeca by the Mexica; the state name derives from a Nahuatl word for the indigenous Coahuiltecan peoples. Saltillo was founded in 1577, and during the colonial era the province formed part of Nueva Vizcaya and later the Provincias Internas. After independence, the 1824 constitution joined Coahuila with Texas as the state of Coahuila y Tejas, a union ended by the Texas Revolution of 1835–36. During the Porfiriato the state became a centre of mining and ranching wealth under families such as the Madero, who launched the Mexican Revolution from Coahuila with the Plan of San Luis Potosí in 1910; Venustiano Carranza, author of the Constitution of 1917, was also a coahuilense.

Economy

Coahuila is one of Mexico's most industrialised northern states. Saltillo and Ramos Arizpe host major automotive plants; Torreón and La Laguna are centres for metallurgy, dairy production, and textiles; Monclova was long known for steel (Altos Hornos de México); and the Carbonífera region around Sabinas and Piedras Negras produces most of Mexico's coal. The state is also noted for palaeontology — including the hadrosaur Velafrons coahuilensis — and for the vineyards of Parras de la Fuente, among the oldest wine-producing areas in the Americas.
