Behistun Inscription

The Behistun Inscription (also Bisotun, Bisitun or Bisutun; Persian: بیستون, Old Persian: Bagastana, meaning "the place of god") is a multilingual inscription and large rock relief on a cliff at Mount Behistun in the Kermanshah Province of Iran, established by Darius the Great (r. 522–486 BC). It was crucial to the decipherment of cuneiform script, as it includes three versions of the same text, written in three different cuneiform script languages: Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian (a variety of Akkadian).

Contents

The inscription begins with a brief autobiography of Darius, including his ancestry and lineage. It describes the events following the deaths of Cambyses II and Smerdis, and the revolts that broke out throughout the empire — nineteen battles in a single year — ending with the defeat of the "lying kings" who had rebelled against Darius. The text, approximately 15 m (49 ft) high by 25 m (82 ft) wide and 100 m (330 ft) up a limestone cliff, states that the inscription was composed in a form of writing ("Aryan script") that Darius himself introduced — Old Persian cuneiform.

Decipherment

The inscription was copied by European travellers from C. Niebuhr onwards, but was only fully transcribed — at great personal risk, dangling from ropes and ladders — by Henry Rawlinson between 1835 and 1847. Using the Old Persian version as a key, Rawlinson and others (notably Grotefend before him) deciphered first Old Persian, then Elamite and Babylonian, making the inscription the "Rosetta Stone" of cuneiform studies. An Aramaic translation was later found among the Elephantine papyri in Egypt.

The relief

The rock relief depicts a life-size Darius holding a bow — a sign of sovereignty — and treading on the chest of a figure lying before him, identified as Gaumata, the pretender to the throne. Behind Darius stand two attendants, and nine captured rebel kings approach in a row, bound by ropes around their necks.
