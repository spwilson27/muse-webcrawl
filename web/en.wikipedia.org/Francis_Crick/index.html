Francis Crick

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Francis Harry Compton Crick (8 June 1916 – 28 July 2004) was an English molecular biologist, biophysicist and neuroscientist. With James Watson, Maurice Wilkins and Rosalind Franklin's data, he is best known for proposing the double helix structure of DNA in 1953. He, Watson and Wilkins shared the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

The structure of DNA

Crick, trained as a physicist and working on naval mines during the Second World War, moved to biology and joined the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge. There he and Watson built models informed by Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling's X-ray diffraction data (notably "Photo 51") and Erwin Chargaff's base-composition rules. Their paper "Molecular Structure of Nucleic Acids: A Structure for Deoxyribose Nucleic Acid" appeared in Nature on 25 April 1953, famously closing with the observation that the pairing they proposed suggested a copying mechanism for genetic material.

Later molecular biology

Crick went on to formulate the sequence hypothesis and the "central dogma" of molecular biology, and, with Sydney Brenner and others, to crack the genetic code's triplet nature and establish that it was non-overlapping and read from a fixed starting point. He also proposed the adaptor hypothesis, anticipating transfer RNA.

Neuroscience

In 1976 Crick moved to the Salk Institute in California and turned to neuroscience, particularly the problem of consciousness. With Christof Koch he promoted the search for the neural correlates of consciousness, arguing that consciousness could be studied empirically, and he published his views in The Astonishing Hypothesis (1994).
