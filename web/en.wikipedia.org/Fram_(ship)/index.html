Fram ("Forward") is a ship that was used in expeditions of the Arctic and Antarctic regions by the Norwegian explorers Fridtjof Nansen, Otto Sverdrup, Oscar Wisting, and Roald Amundsen between 1893 and 1912. It was designed and built by the Scottish-Norwegian shipwright Colin Archer for Fridtjof Nansen's 1893 Arctic expedition, in which the plan was to freeze Fram into the Arctic ice cap and float with it over the North Pole.

Fram is preserved in the Fram Museum in Oslo, Norway. The ship is said to have sailed farther north (85°57′N) and farther south (78°41′S) than any other wooden ship.

Design and construction

Nansen's ambition was to build a ship that could survive the pressure of the pack ice — not merely resist it, but be designed so that the ice would squeeze the hull upward rather than crush it. Colin Archer designed Fram with an unusually rounded, strengthened hull of exceptional thickness, with few protrusions that the ice could grip. The ship was launched at Larvik on 26 October 1892.

Expeditions

Nansen's North Pole attempt, 1893–1896

Fram was deliberately frozen into the Arctic Ocean ice near the New Siberian Islands and drifted across the polar basin for nearly three years. When it became clear the drift would not carry the ship over the Pole itself, Nansen and Hjalmar Johansen left the vessel in March 1895 and made a dash for the Pole by dog sledge, reaching a record farthest north of 86°13.6′N before retreating to Franz Josef Land. Fram, under Otto Sverdrup's command, emerged from the ice north of Svalbard in August 1896, shortly before Nansen's own return to Norway.

Sverdrup's Canadian Arctic expedition, 1898–1902

Otto Sverdrup led a second expedition in Fram to the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, charting some 260,000 km² of previously unmapped islands, including Axel Heiberg Island, Ellef Ringnes Island, and Amund Ringnes Island — more new territory than any previous Arctic expedition.

Amundsen's South Pole expedition, 1910–1912

Roald Amundsen used Fram on his Antarctic expedition, reaching the Bay of Whales in January 1911. From there Amundsen's sledging party reached the South Pole on 14 December 1911, beating Robert Falcon Scott's expedition by just over a month.

Museum ship

After years of neglect, Fram was restored in the 1930s and installed in a purpose-built museum at Bygdøy, Oslo, which opened in 1936. The ship is preserved whole inside the Fram Museum, where visitors can go aboard and explore the cabins, saloon, and engine room.
