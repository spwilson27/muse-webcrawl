Þingvellir

Þingvellir National Park Location: South-western Iceland
Althing assembly site: 930 – 1798
National park established: 1930
UNESCO World Heritage Site: inscribed 2004
Þingvellir, where the North American and Eurasian plates drift apart.

Þingvellir ("assembly fields") is a national park in south-western Iceland, about 40 km north-east of Reykjavík, of immense historical, cultural and geological significance. It is the site of the Althing, the national parliament of Iceland, founded here in 930 AD and generally regarded as the world's oldest surviving parliamentary institution.

For two weeks each summer, chieftains and free men from across the Icelandic Commonwealth gathered at the Law Rock (Lögberg), where the law-speaker recited the law aloud and disputes were settled. The assembly met at Þingvellir until 1798. It was here, in the year 1000, that Iceland peacefully adopted Christianity, and here that the Republic of Iceland was proclaimed on 17 June 1944.

Geology

Þingvellir lies in a rift valley on the boundary between the North American and Eurasian tectonic plates, which are pulling apart by a few millimetres to centimetres each year. The sunken plain is cut by fissures such as Almannagjá and Silfra — the latter filled with crystal-clear glacial water popular with divers. Lake Þingvallavatn, Iceland's largest natural lake, adjoins the plain. The park was designated in 1930 and inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2004.
