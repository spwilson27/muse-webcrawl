The Althing (Icelandic: Alþingi) is the national parliament of Iceland. Founded in 930 at Þingvellir, the "assembly fields" some 45 km east of present-day Reykjavík, it is one of the oldest surviving parliamentary institutions in the world, alongside the Tynwald of the Isle of Man and the Løgting of the Faroes.

History

Norwegian settlers established the Althing as the general assembly of the Icelandic Commonwealth: chieftains (goðar), their followers, and a lawspeaker who recited the law met for two weeks each summer at the Law Rock (Lögberg). Legislation and judgments were handled by the Law Council (Lögrétta) and quarter courts. The assembly famously adopted Christianity by legal decision in the year 1000.

After Iceland submitted to the Norwegian, then Danish, crown, the Althing dwindled into a court and was abolished in 1800. It was re-established as an advisory assembly in 1843 and regained legislative power with home rule in 1904 and sovereignty in 1918. Since 1991 it has been unicameral, with 63 members elected for four-year terms by proportional representation.

Modern role

The Althing sits in the Alþingishúsið in Reykjavík, built of Icelandic dolerite in 1880–81. Celebrations of the assembly's millennium were held at Þingvellir in 1930, and the republic itself was proclaimed there on 17 June 1944.
