Gentry

Gentry (from Old French genterie, from gentil, "high-born, noble") are "well-born, genteel and well-bred people" of high social class, especially in the past. In the United Kingdom, the term landed gentry refers to members of land-owning social classes who lived wholly or largely on rental income from their estates and did not need to work for a living — yet who, unlike the peerage, bore no hereditary titles (other than baronets, knights, and dames). The gentry occupied a position between the aristocracy above and the yeomanry, tenant farmers, and merchants below.

The landed gentry in Britain

The landed gentry formed the backbone of local government in rural England for centuries. Its members served as justices of the peace, lord lieutenants, high sheriffs, and members of Parliament for county seats, and they dominated the social life of the countryside from their country houses. Status derived from landownership, lineage, education at public schools and the ancient universities, and adherence to a code of manners rather than from wealth alone.

From the late nineteenth century the economic position of the gentry declined sharply under the agricultural depression, falling rents, and new death duties. Many great estates were broken up after the First World War, though the social cachet of "county" families persisted well into the twentieth century.

Gentry in other societies

The term has been applied to comparable classes elsewhere. In China, the "gentry" (shenshi) were the scholar-officials and degree-holding landowners who mediated between the imperial state and local society. In Poland the szlachta formed an unusually numerous noble estate; in colonial and early national America, planter and merchant elites such as the First Families of Virginia were styled gentry. In Ireland the Anglo-Irish landowning class was sometimes called the Protestant Ascendancy.

Terminology

Within the British gentry a gentleman's rank might be recognised through a grant of arms recorded by the heralds' visitations. The phrase "landed gentry" was popularised by reference works such as Burke's Landed Gentry, first published in the 1830s alongside Burke's Peerage. "Gentrification" derives from the word, coined by sociologist Ruth Glass in 1964 to describe the displacement of working-class residents by wealthier newcomers.
