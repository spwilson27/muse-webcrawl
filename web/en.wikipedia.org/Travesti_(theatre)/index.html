Travesti (theatre)

In theatre and opera, travesti (from the Italian travestire, "to disguise") refers to a role performed by a performer of the opposite sex, or more specifically a character who cross-dresses within the performance. The term overlaps with, but is distinct from, traditions of female impersonation and drag.

In opera and drama

In opera, travesti (or "trouser roles", German Hosenrolle) most often denotes a male character sung by a woman, typically a mezzo-soprano or contralto: examples include Cherubino in Mozart's The Marriage of Figaro, Octavian in Richard Strauss's Der Rosenkavalier, and the title role of Handel's Giulio Cesare (originally written for castrato voices). The practice arose from a mixture of vocal practicality, historical bans on women performing in some places, and dramatic convention.

In spoken drama, travesti roles and productions in which women played male heroes were especially popular in 19th-century theatre: actresses such as Charlotte Cushman played Romeo, and in British pantomime the "principal boy" — the young male hero — is traditionally played by a woman, while the dame is played by a man. In Shakespeare's time all female roles were played by boys; modern all-male or all-female productions deliberately revive or invert the convention.

Related senses

The word also denotes a character within a plot who disguises themselves as the opposite sex — a staple device of comedy from Shakespeare's Twelfth Night onwards — and, in some languages, a broader category of gender-crossing performance and identity.
