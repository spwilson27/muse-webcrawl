The Second Vatican Council, commonly known as Vatican II, was the 21st ecumenical council of the Catholic Church, held in St Peter's Basilica in Vatican City from 1962 to 1965. Announced by Pope John XXIII in January 1959 to widespread surprise, it pursued aggiornamento — an "updating" of the Church — and an opening to the modern world and other Christians. John XXIII died in June 1963, and the council was continued and concluded by Pope Paul VI. It produced sixteen documents that reshaped Catholic liturgy, ecclesiology, ecumenism, and relations with other religions and the modern state.

Background and opening

John XXIII announced the council on 25 January 1959 at the Basilica of Saint Paul Outside the Walls, only three months into his pontificate. After extensive preparation by commissions that surveyed bishops worldwide, the council opened on 11 October 1962 with more than 2,500 bishops in attendance — the largest gathering of bishops in history — plus observers from other churches, a novelty. In his opening address, Gaudet Mater Ecclesia, John XXIII rejected the "prophets of gloom" and directed the Church to present its doctrine in dialogue with modern culture rather than by condemnation.

Effects and controversies

The council's implementation transformed Catholic life: Mass celebrated facing the people and largely in local languages, renewed religious orders, expanded roles for the laity, ecumenical prayer and dialogue, and a new relationship with Judaism. Interpretation of the council remains contested — between readings emphasising continuity ("hermeneutic of reform," in Benedict XVI's phrase) and those emphasising rupture — and a minority of traditionalists, most visibly Archbishop Marcel Lefebvre's Society of Saint Pius X (founded 1970), rejected aspects of the reforms, leading to the 1988 Écône consecrations and excommunications (remitted in 2009). The council's texts remain the authoritative reference point for Catholic teaching in the modern era.
