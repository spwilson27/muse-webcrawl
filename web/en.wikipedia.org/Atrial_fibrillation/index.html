Atrial fibrillation (AF or A-fib) is an abnormal heart rhythm (arrhythmia) characterised by rapid and irregular beating of the atrial chambers of the heart. It often begins as brief periods of abnormal beating, called paroxysmal episodes, which become longer or continuous over time. Often, episodes have no symptoms; occasionally there may be heart palpitations, fainting, lightheadedness, shortness of breath, or chest pain.

Atrial fibrillation is associated with an increased risk of heart failure, dementia, and stroke, because blood may pool in the poorly contracting atria and form clots that can embolise. It is a type of supraventricular tachycardia.

Signs and symptoms

Many episodes are asymptomatic. When symptoms occur they commonly include palpitations, exercise intolerance, fatigue, lightheadedness, dyspnoea and chest discomfort. The pulse is classically described as "irregularly irregular".

Causes and risk factors

Risk increases with age. Major risk factors include high blood pressure, heart failure, coronary artery disease, valvular heart disease (especially mitral stenosis), obesity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, hyperthyroidism and heavy alcohol use. Hypertension and valvular disease account for the majority of cases.

Pathophysiology

The normal electrical impulse generated by the sinoatrial node is overwhelmed by disorganised impulses usually arising from the roots of the pulmonary veins. This leads to irregular conduction of impulses to the ventricles, which generate the heartbeat. Structural remodelling of the atria, with fibrosis and dilatation, perpetuates the arrhythmia — a process summarised as "AF begets AF".

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by electrocardiogram, which shows absent P waves, an irregular baseline and an irregularly irregular QRS response. Episodes may be captured with ambulatory monitoring devices. Blood tests, echocardiography and thyroid function tests are used to identify causes and complications.

Management

Treatment goals include rate control (typically with beta blockers, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers or digoxin), rhythm control (cardioversion, antiarrhythmic drugs, or catheter ablation), and stroke prevention with anticoagulants such as warfarin or direct oral anticoagulants, guided by risk scores such as CHA2DS2-VASc. Healthy lifestyle changes, including weight loss and reduced alcohol intake, reduce recurrence.

Epidemiology

Atrial fibrillation is the most common serious abnormal heart rhythm worldwide. In the developed world it affects about 2.5% of the population, with prevalence rising steeply with age; in 2010 it was estimated to affect about 33.5 million people globally.
