Gondwana (India)

Gondwana is a region of central India named after the Gonds, the largest tribal (Adivasi) group in the country, who are primarily concentrated there. The region comprises parts of the modern states of Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra (Vidarbha), Chhattisgarh, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh and Odisha, roughly corresponding to the hills and forests drained by the Godavari and its tributaries, including the Satpura range and the Maikal hills.

History

From roughly the 14th to the 18th centuries, the region was ruled by Gond kingdoms — notably Garha-Mandla, Deogarh, Chanda and Kherla — which built forts, tanks and temples and at their height controlled extensive territories. The Gond queen Durgavati of Garha-Mandla, who died resisting Mughal expansion in 1564, is a celebrated figure of the region. Gondwana later came under Maratha and then British rule, and the name was used for a proposed administrative province during the colonial period.

Geological legacy

The region's name became globally famous through geology: the Austrian geologist Eduard Suess used the rock formations of the Gondwana region — coal-bearing strata containing the distinctive Glossopteris flora — to name the ancient supercontinent Gondwana (or Gondwanaland), which once joined India to Africa, Australia, Antarctica and South America.

Modern significance

The region is rich in coal, manganese and other minerals, and its large Gond population has inspired demands for a separate Gondwana state within India. Forest rights and land alienation remain major political issues for its tribal communities.
