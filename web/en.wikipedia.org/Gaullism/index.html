Gaullism (French: Gaullisme) is a French political stance based on the thought and actions of Second World War French Resistance leader and later President Charles de Gaulle, who founded the Fifth French Republic. Gaullism emerged as the dominant strand of the French political right in the post-war era.

Principles

Core Gaullist ideas include a strong, independent French state; national sovereignty and suspicion of supranational authority; an assertive foreign policy — de Gaulle withdrew France from NATO's integrated military command in 1966 and developed an independent nuclear deterrent (the force de frappe); a strong executive presidency under the 1958 constitution; dirigiste economic policy with state planning and national champions; and a "certain idea of France" combining republican values with grandeur. Socially, Gaullism has often been conservative, though it accommodates both liberal-conservative and more socially-minded ("left Gaullist") strands.

History

After leading the Free French and the provisional government (1944–46), de Gaulle founded the Rally of the French People (RPF) in 1947. Returning to power during the May 1958 crisis over Algeria, he established the Fifth Republic and served as president until 1969. His successors Georges Pompidou and Jacques Chirac governed in the Gaullist tradition through the UNR, UDR and RPR parties. The RPR merged into the UMP in 2002, renamed Les Républicains in 2015. Modern Gaullists remain divided over European integration and economic liberalism, debating how much of the original doctrine survives.
