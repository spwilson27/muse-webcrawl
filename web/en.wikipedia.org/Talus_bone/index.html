The talus (Latin for ankle or ankle bone; plural tali), also historically called the astragalus, is one of the tarsal bones of the foot. It transmits the entire weight of the body from the tibia downward to the foot, sitting between the leg bones above and the calcaneus (heel bone) below.

Structure

The talus is divided into a rounded head, a short neck, and a body with a pulley-shaped upper surface, the trochlea, which fits into the mortise formed by the tibia and fibula at the ankle joint. Unusually, no muscles attach to the talus; its position is maintained entirely by its shape and the ligaments around it. More than half of its surface is covered by articular cartilage.

Function and clinical significance

Together with the calcaneus, the talus forms the subtalar joint, which allows inversion and eversion of the foot, while the ankle (talocrural) joint permits dorsiflexion and plantarflexion. The talus has a precarious, largely retrograde blood supply, so fractures of the talar neck — classically from forced dorsiflexion, as in falls or aviation accidents — carry a significant risk of avascular necrosis of the body.

In other animals and culture

In many hoofed animals the talus (astragalus) has a double-pulley shape restricting motion to one plane. Knucklebones made from the tali of sheep and goats were used as dice and gaming pieces in the ancient world, a practice echoed in the word "astragaloi" for such gaming bones.
