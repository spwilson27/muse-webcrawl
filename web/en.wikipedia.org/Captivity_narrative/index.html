Captivity narrative

Captivity narratives are a genre of literature, usually written by Europeans and European Americans, recounting their capture by and life among non-European peoples. The genre flourished in colonial North America from the seventeenth to the nineteenth century, where narratives of capture by Native Americans became bestsellers and helped shape American cultural identity.

Characteristics

Typical narratives describe a violent capture, a journey into the wilderness, hardships and encounters with an alien culture, and eventual ransom, escape, or rescue. Early examples, such as Mary Rowlandson's The Sovereignty and Goodness of God (1682), frame the experience as a Puritan spiritual trial. Later narratives became more sensational and novelistic, and the genre fed into the American frontier novel.

Related forms

Related traditions include Barbary captivity narratives by Europeans and Americans enslaved in North Africa, slave narratives by African Americans, and modern hostage memoirs. Scholars study the genre for its portrayal of race, gender, religion, and cross-cultural encounter.
