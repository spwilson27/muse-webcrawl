Boris and Gleb

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Boris and Gleb (Old East Slavic: Борисъ и Глѣбъ; Russian: Борис и Глеб; Ukrainian: Борис і Гліб), Christian names Roman and David, respectively, died 1015, were the first saints canonized in Kievan Rus' after the Christianization of the country. They were sons of Vladimir the Great and were murdered during the internecine wars that followed his death, traditionally at the instigation of their elder half-brother Sviatopolk "the Accursed."

The Passion

According to the chronicle accounts and the hagiographic "Tale and Passion and Encomium of Boris and Gleb," Boris, prince of Rostov, learned of a plot against his life while campaigning against the Pechenegs. Refusing to resist his elder brother or raise his hand against him, Boris dismissed most of his retainers and was slain in his tent on the Alta River on 24 July 1015 while praying. Gleb, prince of Murom, was lured from his domain and murdered on the Dnieper near Smolensk weeks later.

Passion-bearers

Boris and Gleb were venerated not as martyrs who died for the faith but as "passion-bearers" (strastoterptsy) — saints who accepted death in imitation of Christ's non-resistance, without malice toward their killers. Their cult became immensely popular in Rus'; their relics were enshrined at Vyshhorod, numerous churches were dedicated to them, and their feast day is 24 July (with a second feast, the translation of their relics, on 2 May).

Sources

The story survives in the Primary Chronicle and in Nestor the Chronicler's "Reading on the Life and Murder of the Blessed Passion-Bearers Boris and Gleb," as well as the anonymous "Tale." Scholars debate how far the accounts of Sviatopolk's sole responsibility reflect the propaganda of Yaroslav the Wise, the brother who ultimately prevailed.
