Adenosine

Adenosine
Formula: C10H13N5O4
Type: Purine nucleoside
Components: Adenine + ribose
Related: ATP, ADP, AMP, cAMP

Adenosine is an organic compound composed of the purine base adenine attached to a ribose sugar. It is a nucleoside and a fundamental building block of nucleic acids and of the cell's energy currency: its phosphorylated forms are adenosine monophosphate (AMP), adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Free adenosine also acts as a signalling molecule throughout the body, particularly in the cardiovascular and nervous systems.

Physiological roles

Adenosine accumulates during metabolic stress and wakefulness. Acting on four G-protein-coupled receptors (A1, A2A, A2B and A3), it slows heart rate and atrioventricular conduction, dilates blood vessels, inhibits neurotransmitter release, and promotes sleep — the pressure to sleep that caffeine relieves by blocking adenosine receptors. In the heart, adenosine released during ischemia helps match coronary blood flow to demand.

Medical use

Intravenous adenosine is a first-line drug for terminating paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia, because its brief blockade of the AV node interrupts re-entrant circuits. Its half-life in blood is only a few seconds, so it is given as a rapid bolus. Adenosine (or the related agent regadenoson) is also used as a pharmacological stress agent in myocardial perfusion imaging. Side effects such as flushing, chest tightness and a sense of doom are intense but transient.

Metabolism

Adenosine is removed by adenosine kinase (converting it to AMP) and by adenosine deaminase (converting it to inosine). Inherited deficiency of adenosine deaminase causes a severe combined immunodeficiency (ADA-SCID), one of the first diseases treated by gene therapy.
