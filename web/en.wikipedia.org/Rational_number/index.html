Rational number

In mathematics, a rational number is a number that can be expressed as the quotient or fraction p/q of two integers, a numerator p and a non-zero denominator q. The set of rational numbers, denoted ℚ (from the German Quotient), includes all integers, since any integer z can be written as z/1. Rational numbers can be positive, negative or zero.

Properties

The rational numbers form a field: they are closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication and division (except by zero). They are densely ordered — between any two rationals there is another — yet the set is countably infinite, as shown by Cantor's enumeration argument. Numbers that are not rational, such as √2, e and π, are called irrational numbers; together the two sets make up the real numbers.

Decimal expansions

Every rational number has a decimal expansion that either terminates (1/4 = 0.25) or eventually repeats (1/3 = 0.333…, 1/7 = 0.142857142857…). Conversely, every terminating or repeating decimal is rational — a standard test distinguishing them from irrationals.

Formal construction

Formally, ℚ can be constructed as equivalence classes of ordered pairs of integers (p, q) with q ≠ 0, where (p, q) ~ (r, s) when ps = qr. Fractions are usually written in lowest terms, with numerator and denominator coprime and the denominator positive. The ancient Egyptians used unit fractions, and Greek mathematicians systematised ratios of integers before the Pythagoreans' discovery of incommensurable magnitudes.
