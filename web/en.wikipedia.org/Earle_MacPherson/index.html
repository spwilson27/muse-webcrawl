Earle Steele MacPherson (August 6, 1891 – January 26, 1960) was an American automotive engineer best known for inventing the MacPherson strut, a widely used form of car suspension that combines a shock absorber and a coil spring into a single unit. His design, patented in the late 1940s, made independent front suspension cheaper and more compact, and it remains standard on a large share of the world's passenger cars.

Early life and education

MacPherson was born in Highland Park, Illinois. He served in the United States Army during the First World War and studied engineering, graduating from the University of Illinois. His early career included work with several American manufacturers, giving him broad experience in chassis design before he joined the major Detroit automakers.

Career

MacPherson worked for General Motors, where by the mid-1940s he was chief engineer on the Chevrolet Cadet project, a proposed lightweight car. For the Cadet he devised a compact front suspension in which the coil spring surrounded the damper and the strut itself formed part of the steering and suspension linkage. The Cadet was cancelled, but the suspension concept survived.

In 1947 MacPherson moved to the Ford Motor Company. Ford first applied his strut suspension in production on the French-built Ford Vedette in 1949, and then on British Fords such as the Consul and Zephyr from 1950–51. The layout's low cost, small footprint and good ride made it attractive for the growing generation of smaller cars.

The MacPherson strut

The MacPherson strut uses a telescopic damper as the upper steering pivot, with the coil spring mounted coaxially around it. A transverse lower arm and the anti-roll bar complete the location of the wheel. Because the strut carries suspension loads into the body shell, it suits unitary (monocoque) construction and front-wheel-drive packaging, and variants are used on rear suspensions as well, sometimes called Chapman struts after Colin Chapman.

Later life and legacy

MacPherson rose to senior engineering positions at Ford before retiring. His suspension design went on to be adopted by manufacturers worldwide, including Volkswagen, Toyota, Honda, BMW and Porsche, and it is still one of the most common front suspension layouts in production.
