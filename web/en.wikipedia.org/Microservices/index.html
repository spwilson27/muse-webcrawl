Microservices

Microservices — also known as the microservice architecture — is an architectural style in software engineering that structures an application as a collection of small, loosely coupled, independently deployable services, each organized around a business capability and communicating over a network, typically via lightweight protocols such as HTTP APIs or message queues.

Characteristics

In a microservices architecture, each service is owned by a small team, can be developed and scaled independently, manages its own database, and is deployed through automated pipelines. The style emerged in the early 2010s from practices at companies such as Amazon and Netflix, building on service-oriented architecture (SOA) but rejecting heavyweight standards like SOAP and enterprise service buses in favor of simplicity — "smart endpoints and dumb pipes."

Advantages

Microservices allow independent scaling of hot components, faster and safer deployments, technology diversity between services, and organizational alignment with small autonomous teams (related to Conway's law). Fault isolation means a failure in one service need not bring down the whole system if circuit breakers and retries are used.

Challenges

The style introduces distributed-systems complexity: network latency and partial failure, data consistency across services (sagas instead of transactions), testing and observability across many services, and operational overhead. Critics note that many organizations adopt microservices prematurely and would be better served by a well-structured monolith — an approach sometimes called "monolith first."
