Lyman series

In physics and chemistry, the Lyman series is a hydrogen spectral series of transitions and resulting ultraviolet emission lines of the hydrogen atom as an electron goes from n ≥ 2 to n = 1 (or the corresponding absorption lines). The series is named after its discoverer, the American physicist Theodore Lyman, who identified its first lines between 1906 and 1914.

Description

The wavelengths of the Lyman series all lie in the ultraviolet band. The first line, Lyman-alpha (Ly-α), has a wavelength of 121.57 nanometres; successive lines converge on the series limit at 91.18 nm. The wavelengths are described by the Rydberg formula with the lower principal quantum number fixed at 1.

Astronomical importance

The Lyman-alpha line is one of the most important spectral features in astronomy. Because neutral hydrogen is abundant in the universe, Ly-α absorption reveals interstellar and intergalactic gas. The "Lyman-alpha forest" of absorption lines in quasar spectra traces clouds of hydrogen along the line of sight, and Lyman-break techniques use the series limit to identify very distant galaxies. Lyman-alpha emitters are young, star-forming galaxies in the early universe.

Other series

Transitions to the n = 2 level form the Balmer series (visible light), to n = 3 the Paschen series (infrared), and further series include Brackett, Pfund, and Humphreys, all in the infrared.
