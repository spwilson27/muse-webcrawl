Grand Secretariat

The Grand Secretariat (Chinese: 內閣, Nèigé) was the highest institution of government in imperial China during the Ming and Qing dynasties. It functioned as a coordinating agency and the emperor's chief secretariat, staffed by Grand Secretaries (大學士) who advised the emperor and supervised the paperwork of the central government.

Origins under the Ming

The institution grew out of reforms of the Hongwu Emperor, founder of the Ming dynasty. In 1380 Hongwu abolished the office of Chancellor and began ruling directly with the help of low-ranking academicians. Under the Yongle Emperor, a body of grand secretaries was formally organized; by the mid-Ming they had become the de facto heads of the civil administration, drafting rescripts and responses to memorials in a process known as piaoni (票擬, "drafting proposals").

Function

Grand secretaries reviewed memorials submitted to the throne, drafted proposed imperial replies, and coordinated the work of the Six Ministries. The Senior Grand Secretary was often compared to a prime minister, although the office formally remained advisory, and its holders depended entirely on imperial favour. Famous senior grand secretaries included Yan Song, Xu Jie, Gao Gong and Zhang Juzheng, the powerful reformer of the Wanli era.

Under the Qing

The Qing dynasty retained the Grand Secretariat but gradually shifted real power elsewhere: first to the Deliberative Council of Princes and Ministers, and after 1730 to the Grand Council (軍機處), established by the Yongzheng Emperor to handle military campaigns. The Grand Secretariat survived as a prestigious but largely formal body until the end of the dynasty in 1912.
