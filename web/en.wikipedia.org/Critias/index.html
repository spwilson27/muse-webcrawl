Critias

Critias (Greek: Κριτίας)
Born: c. 460 BC, Athens
Died: 403 BC, near Piraeus, Athens
Occupation: Tyrant, philosopher, writer
Known for: Leader of the Thirty Tyrants; Plato's dialogues

Critias (c. 460 – 403 BC) was an ancient Athenian political figure and author. Born in Athens, Critias was the son of Callaeschrus and a first cousin of Plato's mother Perictione; he appears as a character in several of Plato's dialogues, most notably the Timaeus and the unfinished dialogue that bears his own name, Critias, which recounts the story of Atlantis.

Life

Critias was associated with Socrates in his youth and was a prolific author of tragedies, elegies, prose treatises and political pamphlets; only fragments survive, including the controversial “Sisyphus fragment” which presents religion as a human invention for social control and has often been attributed to him. Exiled from Athens under the democracy, he spent time in Thessaly.

The Thirty Tyrants

After Athens' defeat in the Peloponnesian War, Critias returned and in 404 BC became the leading figure among the Thirty Tyrants, the oligarchic government installed by Sparta. The Thirty carried out confiscations and executions of citizens and metics, including the orator Lysias's brother Polemarchus; Theramenes, a more moderate colleague, was put to death after falling out with Critias. Critias was killed in 403 BC at the Battle of Munychia, fighting the democratic exiles led by Thrasybulus, and the democracy was restored.

Legacy

In later tradition Critias became a byword for tyrannical cruelty, while Plato's use of him as a speaker in the Timaeus–Critias preserved the memory of his family's claimed descent from Solon's circle and its role in transmitting the Atlantis story.
