The 400 Blows (French: Les Quatre Cents Coups) is a 1959 French coming-of-age drama film, and the directorial debut of François Truffaut. The film, shot in black-and-white anamorphic format, stars Jean-Pierre Léaud as Antoine Doinel, a misunderstood adolescent in Paris who struggles with unresponsive parents and teachers and descends into a life of petty crime. One of the defining films of the French New Wave, it displays the movement's hallmarks: location shooting, improvised-feeling dialogue, and a rejection of polished studio craftsmanship.

Title

The French title comes from the expression faire les quatre cents coups, "to raise hell" — literally, "to make the four hundred blows." The English title is a literal translation that loses the idiom.

Plot

Antoine Doinel lives in a cramped Paris apartment with his indifferent mother and weak stepfather. Scolded at school and misunderstood at home, he plays truant with his friend René, steals milk and money, and, after stealing a typewriter from his stepfather's office and failing to sell it, is arrested and sent to a juvenile observation centre near the sea. In the film's famous final sequence he escapes during a football game, runs to the shore of the sea he has never seen, and turns to face the camera in a freeze-frame as he reaches the water's edge — one of the most celebrated shots in cinema history.

Legacy

The film won Truffaut the Best Director award at the 1959 Cannes Film Festival and was a commercial success. Léaud returned as Antoine Doinel in four further films over twenty years, from Antoine and Colette (1962) to Love on the Run (1979), forming an autobiographical cycle closely mirroring Truffaut's own troubled childhood. The 400 Blows is routinely ranked among the greatest films of all time.
