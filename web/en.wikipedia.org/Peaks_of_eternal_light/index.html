Peak of eternal light


Terrain near the lunar poles can remain sunlit almost continuously.

A peak of eternal light (PEL) is a point on a body within the Solar System that is almost always in sunlight. The concept was first proposed in the 19th century, notably by the astronomer Camille Flammarion, for locations near the poles of the Moon, where the small axial tilt of the Moon means that elevated terrain can catch sunlight nearly all the time.

Lunar peaks

Orbital imagery from missions such as SMART-1, Kaguya, and the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has been used to map illumination near the lunar poles. Peaks along the rims of craters such as Peary and Shackleton receive sunlight for a large fraction of the lunar year, though no point is illuminated literally 100% of the time. Such sites are attractive for lunar bases because they offer nearly constant solar power and moderate temperature swings, and lie close to permanently shadowed craters that may hold water ice.

Elsewhere

Similar conditions may exist near the poles of Mercury, where radar and MESSENGER data suggest water ice in shadowed craters beside long-lit high ground.
