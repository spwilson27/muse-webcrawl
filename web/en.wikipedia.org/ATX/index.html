ATX

ATX (Advanced Technology Extended) is a motherboard and power supply configuration specification developed by Intel in 1995 to improve on the earlier AT design. It defines the physical dimensions of the motherboard, the location of mounting points and the I/O panel, and the electrical characteristics of the power supply and its connectors. ATX and its derivatives (MicroATX, Mini-ITX derivatives notwithstanding) became the dominant standard for desktop personal computers.

History

Intel released the ATX specification in July 1995. It rotated the board layout 90 degrees relative to Baby-AT, moved the CPU and memory slots to improve airflow and access, integrated the external I/O connectors into a fixed rear panel with a standardized cutout, and introduced a power supply with a single keyed connector, +3.3 V and +5 V standby rails, and soft power control from the motherboard. The standard evolved through revisions (2.01–2.2), including the addition of the 4-pin P4 CPU connector, the shift to the 24-pin main connector, and SATA power connectors. Intel's attempt to replace ATX with BTX (2004) failed, and ATX remains current, with the ATX 3.0/3.1 power supply revisions (2022–2023) adding the 12V-2x6 connector for high-power graphics cards.

Standard ATX boards have up to seven expansion slots. MicroATX, introduced in 1997, is backward compatible with ATX cases and mounting holes. EATX extends board width for servers and high-end desktops.

Power supply

An ATX power supply converts AC mains to +3.3 V, +5 V, and +12 V DC, provides a +5 V standby rail so the system can wake on events, and uses the PS_ON# signal for software-controlled power. Later revisions strengthened the +12 V rails as CPU and GPU demand grew, and ATX12VO, an Intel variant, removes the minor rails from the supply and generates them on the motherboard for greater efficiency.
