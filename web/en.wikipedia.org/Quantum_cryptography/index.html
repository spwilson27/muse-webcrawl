Quantum cryptography

Quantum cryptography is the science of exploiting quantum mechanical properties to perform cryptographic tasks. The best known example is quantum key distribution (QKD), which enables two parties to produce a shared random secret key known only to them, which can then be used to encrypt and decrypt messages — with the distinctive property that any eavesdropping attempt necessarily disturbs the quantum states used and can be detected.

Quantum key distribution

QKD protocols such as BB84, proposed by Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard in 1984, encode key bits in quantum states of photons (e.g., polarization). By the no-cloning theorem and the disturbance caused by measurement, an eavesdropper cannot copy the states undetected. Artur Ekert's 1991 protocol instead uses entangled photon pairs and Bell inequalities. The raw exchange is followed by classical error correction and privacy amplification to distill a shorter, secure key.

Advantages and limitations

QKD's security rests on the laws of physics rather than computational assumptions, so it is not threatened by future quantum computers. However, practical systems face distance limits in optical fiber (a few hundred kilometers without quantum repeaters), the need for an authenticated classical channel, implementation side-channel vulnerabilities, and high cost. Satellite-based QKD, demonstrated by China's Micius satellite (2016), extends range through free-space links.

Relation to post-quantum cryptography

Quantum cryptography is distinct from post-quantum cryptography, which develops classical algorithms believed resistant to quantum attacks. Security agencies have generally favored the latter for general deployment, while QKD networks have been built for specialized government and banking links in China, Europe, and elsewhere.
