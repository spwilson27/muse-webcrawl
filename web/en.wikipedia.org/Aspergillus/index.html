Aspergillus

KingdomFungi
PhylumAscomycota
OrderEurotiales
SpeciesSeveral hundred described

Aspergillus is a genus of moulds consisting of several hundred species found in almost every environment worldwide. First catalogued in 1729 by the Italian priest and biologist Pier Antonio Micheli, the genus takes its name from the aspergillum, a device for sprinkling holy water, whose shape its spore-bearing structures resemble. Members of the genus grow as filamentous hyphae and reproduce asexually by conidia produced on characteristic conidiophores topped by a swollen vesicle.

Importance

Aspergillus species are of major economic and medical importance. Aspergillus niger is used industrially to produce citric acid and enzymes; A. oryzae (koji) is essential to sake, miso and soy sauce production in East Asia; and A. terreus produces the cholesterol-lowering drug lovastatin. Others are spoilage organisms and contaminants of food and indoor environments.

Pathogenicity

Several species cause disease in humans and animals, collectively called aspergillosis. Aspergillus fumigatus is the most common cause of invasive aspergillosis in immunocompromised people and of allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis. A. flavus contaminates grain and nuts with aflatoxins, among the most potent known carcinogens. Aspergillus spores are ubiquitous in air, so exposure is constant; disease occurs mainly when immunity is impaired or spore loads are very high.
