History of photography

The history of photography begins in the 1820s with the first permanent photographs and develops through the daguerreotype, film and colour processes to the digital image sensors of today.

Precursors

The camera obscura had been known for centuries, and chemists such as Johann Heinrich Schulze (1727) observed that silver salts darken in light. The missing piece was a way to fix the image permanently.

Invention

Nicéphore Niépce produced the earliest surviving photograph, View from the Window at Le Gras, around 1826 using bitumen-coated pewter. His partner Louis Daguerre developed the daguerreotype — a sharp image on a silvered copper plate — announced to the public in 1839. In England, William Henry Fox Talbot's calotype used paper negatives, allowing multiple prints.

Expansion

The wet collodion process (1851) combined detail with reproducibility and dominated for decades; photographers such as Roger Fenton and Mathew Brady documented war. Dry plates, then George Eastman's roll film and the Kodak camera of 1888 ("You press the button, we do the rest"), brought photography to amateurs.

Colour and photojournalism

James Clerk Maxwell demonstrated a colour photograph in 1861; practical colour came with Autochrome (1907) and Kodachrome (1935). The 35 mm Leica (1925) enabled modern photojournalism, and agencies such as Magnum spread documentary photography worldwide.

Digital era

Steven Sasson built the first digital camera at Kodak in 1975. CCD and CMOS sensors, camera phones and social media made photography ubiquitous in the 2000s, transforming both the art and the evidentiary status of the image.
