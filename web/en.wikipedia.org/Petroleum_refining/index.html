Petroleum refining

Fractionating towers at a petroleum refinery.

Petroleum refining is the process of transforming crude oil into useful products such as gasoline (petrol), diesel fuel, asphalt base, fuel oils, heating oil, kerosene, liquefied petroleum gas and petroleum naphtha. Petrochemical feedstocks such as ethylene and propylene can also be produced directly by cracking crude oil without the need for refined products of crude oil such as naphtha.

The crude oil distillation unit is the first processing step in virtually all refineries. The main refining processes can be grouped into separation, conversion, and treatment.

Separation: distillation

In atmospheric distillation, preheated crude oil enters a fractionating column, where components vaporize and condense at different heights according to their boiling ranges. Light products (gases, naphtha, gasoline) rise to the top; middle distillates (kerosene, diesel) are drawn from the middle; and heavy residue leaves the bottom. The residue is further processed in a vacuum distillation unit, where reduced pressure allows heavy gas oils and bitumen to be recovered without thermal decomposition.

Conversion

Cracking breaks large hydrocarbon molecules into smaller, more valuable ones. Fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) converts heavy gas oil into gasoline and olefins; hydrocracking uses hydrogen and catalysts for similar purposes; thermal processes such as coking convert residue into petroleum coke and lighter products. Reforming (catalytic reforming) rearranges naphtha molecules to raise octane and produces aromatics and hydrogen. Alkylation combines isobutane with butenes to make high-octane alkylate.

Treating and blending

Hydrotreating removes sulfur, nitrogen and metals using hydrogen over catalysts, producing cleaner fuels that meet environmental standards. Finished products are blended from multiple streams to meet specifications for octane, vapor pressure, sulfur content and other properties.

History

Early refining in the 19th century focused on kerosene for lighting, extracted by simple distillation; gasoline was initially a waste product. The invention of the internal combustion engine and the Burton thermal cracking process (1913) reshaped refining around motor fuels, and catalytic cracking (1930s–40s) and reforming further increased gasoline yield and quality.
