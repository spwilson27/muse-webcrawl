Voting

Voting is a method by which a group, such as a meeting or an electorate, can make a collective decision or express an opinion, usually following discussions, debates, or election campaigns. Democracies elect holders of high office by voting. A vote is an individual voter's formal expression of preference for a candidate or a proposed resolution, recorded — in modern elections typically by secret ballot — and combined according to an electoral system to produce a result.

Methods

Votes may be cast by show of hands, voice, paper ballot, voting machine, post, or electronically. Counting rules vary widely: plurality (first-past-the-post), majority runoffs, proportional representation, ranked-choice (instant-runoff) voting, approval voting, and score voting weight preferences differently, and social choice theory studies their properties and paradoxes — Arrow's impossibility theorem shows that no ranked system can satisfy a set of seemingly natural fairness criteria simultaneously.

Who may vote

Suffrage has expanded over two centuries from narrow property- and sex-based franchises to near-universal adult suffrage; the voting age is 18 in most countries, and debates continue over prisoner voting, expatriate voting, and lowering the age to 16. Some countries, including Australia and Belgium, make voting compulsory. Turnout varies with the competitiveness and salience of the election and is a major subject of political science.

Integrity

Secret ballots protect voters from coercion; electoral administration, observer missions, recounts, and audits aim to ensure that results reflect the votes cast. Concerns over fraud, voter suppression, misinformation, and the security of electronic voting recur in many democracies.
