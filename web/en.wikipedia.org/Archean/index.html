The Archean Eon (also spelled Archaean or Archæan), from the Ancient Greek arkhē meaning "beginning" or "origin", is the second of the four geologic eons of Earth's history, preceded by the Hadean and followed by the Proterozoic. It spans from the formation of the oldest preserved rocks, about 4,031 million years ago (the age of the Acasta Gneiss of Canada), to 2,500 million years ago.

Earth's environment

During the Archean, Earth's crust had cooled enough to allow the formation of continents and the first oceans, but the planet was still far hotter internally than today, with mantle temperatures possibly twice modern values. The atmosphere lacked free oxygen and consisted mainly of nitrogen, carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour. Volcanism was vigorous and widespread, and the Late Heavy Bombardment of meteorite impacts continued into the early Archean.

Geology

Archean rocks are found in cratons — the stable cores of modern continents — including the Canadian Shield, the Pilbara Craton of Australia, the Kaapvaal Craton of southern Africa and the Baltic Shield. Characteristic rock associations include greenstone belts and tonalite–trondhjemite–granodiorite (TTG) complexes. The Jack Hills zircons of Western Australia, dated to as old as 4.4 billion years, are the oldest known terrestrial minerals.

Origin of life

The earliest evidence for life dates to the Archean. Carbon isotope signatures suggestive of biological activity occur in rocks about 3.8–4.1 billion years old, and layered microbial structures called stromatolites are known from about 3.48 billion years ago in the Pilbara. Photosynthesis by cyanobacteria began during the Archean, setting the stage for the Great Oxidation Event at the start of the Proterozoic.
