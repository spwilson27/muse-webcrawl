Optimality Theory

Optimality Theory (OT) is a linguistic model proposing that the observed forms of a language arise from the resolution of conflicts among competing, violable constraints. Proposed by Alan Prince and Paul Smolensky in 1993, OT holds that grammars consist of a universal set of constraints whose language-specific ranking determines which candidate output — generated freely from an input — is optimal, i.e., incurs the least serious constraint violations.


An OT tableau

Architecture

The grammar has three components: GEN, which generates a set of candidate outputs for an input; CON, the universal inventory of constraints; and EVAL, which selects the optimal candidate under the language's ranking. Constraints divide into markedness constraints, which penalize structurally complex or disfavored outputs, and faithfulness constraints, which require outputs to resemble inputs. Languages differ only in how they rank the same constraints, a property called factorial typology.

Example

Whether a language allows words to end in voiced obstruents can be modeled with a markedness constraint *VoicedCoda against a faithfulness constraint IDENT(voice): ranking *VoicedCoda above IDENT yields final devoicing, as in German; the reverse ranking preserves voicing, as in English.

Scope and criticism

OT originated in phonology and has been extended to syntax, morphology, semantics, and language acquisition. Critics have raised issues including opacity effects, computational complexity, and the learnability of rankings, prompting variants such as Harmonic Grammar and stochastic OT.
