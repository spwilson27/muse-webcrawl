Perpetual Peace: A Philosophical Sketch (German: Zum ewigen Frieden. Ein philosophischer Entwurf) is a 1795 essay by the German philosopher Immanuel Kant. Written in the form of a treaty, it sets out the conditions under which wars between states could permanently cease, and it became a foundational text of liberal international relations theory and of cosmopolitan thought.

Contents

Kant proposes six preliminary articles — including the abolition of standing armies, a ban on contracting state debts for war, non-interference in other states' internal affairs, and the rule that no peace treaty is valid if it tacitly reserves grounds for a future war. Three definitive articles follow: the civil constitution of every state should be republican; the law of nations should be founded on a federation of free states; and the rights of individuals as citizens of the world should be limited to conditions of universal hospitality — the right of a foreigner not to be treated with hostility on arriving in another country.

Influence

The essay influenced the democratic peace thesis, the design of the League of Nations and the United Nations, and modern theories of cosmopolitan democracy and global justice. Earlier plans for perpetual peace, such as the Abbé de Saint-Pierre's project (1713) and Rousseau's commentary on it, form part of the tradition Kant transformed into a system of right.
