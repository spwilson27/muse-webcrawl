Equestrian statue

An equestrian statue is a statue of a rider mounted on a horse, from the Latin eques, meaning a knight or horseman. For millennia such monuments have commemorated rulers and military heroes, and they represent one of the most technically demanding forms of sculpture: a multi-ton bronze figure balanced, in the most ambitious examples, on two or three of the horse's legs.

History

The form flourished in ancient Rome; the gilded bronze Marcus Aurelius (c. AD 175) is the only imperial equestrian statue to survive from antiquity, preserved because it was mistaken for the Christian emperor Constantine. The Renaissance revived the type with Donatello's Gattamelata in Padua (1453) and Verrocchio's Bartolomeo Colleoni in Venice. Leonardo da Vinci's colossal horse for Milan was never cast in his lifetime.

Notable examples

Famous equestrian monuments include Étienne-Maurice Falconet's Bronze Horseman of Peter the Great in St Petersburg (1782), the statues of Joan of Arc and Charlemagne in Paris, and the numerous Civil War monuments of the United States. The world's largest equestrian statue is the Chinggis Khan statue near Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia, completed in 2008. A persistent popular myth holds that the position of the horse's hooves encodes how the rider died; the claim is not supported by any consistent convention.
