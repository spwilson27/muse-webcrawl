Tapestry

Tapestry is a form of textile art, traditionally woven by hand on a loom. Unlike ordinary woven cloth, tapestry is weft-faced: all the warp threads are hidden in the finished work, and the weaver creates the image by interlacing coloured weft yarns only where each colour is needed, producing areas of pattern with discontinuous wefts. Tapestries are usually pictorial wall hangings, and historically they were among the most prestigious and expensive forms of decorative art in Europe.

History

Tapestry weaving is ancient, practised in Egypt, Peru, China, and elsewhere. In medieval and Renaissance Europe the art reached its height in France and the Southern Netherlands. Famous workshops included those of Paris, Arras, Tournai, Brussels, and later the Gobelins manufactory in Paris and the Mortlake works in England. Renowned survivals include the Lady and the Unicorn series (c. 1500), the Bayeux Tapestry (technically an embroidery), and the Apocalypse Tapestry at Angers.

Function

Large tapestries insulated draughty castle and palace walls, could be rolled up and transported between residences, and displayed the wealth and lineage of their owners through heraldry, biblical and mythological scenes, and hunting imagery. Sets might cover entire rooms.

Technique

Weavers worked from a full-scale painted design called a cartoon, often created by major artists — Raphael's cartoons for the Sistine Chapel tapestries are celebrated examples. Weaving proceeded slowly; a single large tapestry could take a team of weavers several years. Materials were wool and silk, sometimes with gold and silver thread.

Modern tapestry

The 20th century saw a revival: artists such as Jean Lurçat revived the Aubusson workshops, and the Bauhaus weaving workshop treated tapestry as modern art. Contemporary fibre artists continue to work in the medium.
