Bioinorganic chemistry

Bioinorganic chemistry is a field that examines the role of metals in biology. It studies both natural phenomena — such as the behaviour of metalloproteins — and artificially introduced metals, including non-essential metals and metal-based drugs, in medicine and toxicology. Many biological processes such as respiration depend upon molecules that fall within the domain of inorganic chemistry.

Roles of metal ions in biology

Roughly a third of all known proteins contain metal ions. Metal centres perform functions that organic molecules alone cannot: binding and transporting oxygen (iron in haemoglobin and myoglobin, copper in haemocyanin), catalysing redox reactions (iron–sulfur clusters, the manganese cluster of photosystem II that splits water in photosynthesis), transferring electrons (cytochromes, plastocyanin), and activating substrates in enzymes such as zinc-containing carbonic anhydrase and the molybdenum enzyme nitrogenase, which fixes atmospheric nitrogen. Other metals play structural roles or act in signalling, as with calcium.

Transport, storage and medicine

Because free metal ions can be toxic, organisms tightly regulate their uptake, transport, and storage — for example iron is carried by transferrin and stored in ferritin. Bioinorganic chemistry also underpins metal-based medicine: the anticancer drug cisplatin contains platinum, lithium salts treat bipolar disorder, gadolinium complexes serve as MRI contrast agents, and chelation therapy removes excess or toxic metals in diseases such as Wilson's disease and lead poisoning.
