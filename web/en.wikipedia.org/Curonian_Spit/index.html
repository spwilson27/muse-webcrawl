The Curonian Spit (Lithuanian: Kuršių nerija; Russian: Куршская коса) is a 98-kilometre-long, thin, curved sand-dune spit that separates the Curonian Lagoon from the Baltic Sea coast. It stretches from the Sambian Peninsula in the south (in Russia's Kaliningrad Oblast) to the Lithuanian port city of Klaipėda in the north. In 2000 the spit was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as an outstanding cultural landscape.

Geography

Formed about 5,000 years ago by sand carried on Baltic currents, the spit ranges from just 400 metres to about 3.8 kilometres in width. Its great drifting dunes — among Europe's highest, once reaching 60 metres — repeatedly buried fishing villages in the 18th and 19th centuries, until large-scale reforestation with mountain pine stabilised the sand.

History and settlements

The spit was home to the Curonian (Kursenieki) people, Baltic fishermen whose distinctive wooden houses, weathervanes and burial markers (krikštai) remain in villages such as Nida, Juodkrantė and Preila. In the late 19th century Nida became a celebrated artists' colony, drawing expressionist painters of the Brücke group. After the Second World War the southern half passed to the Soviet Union; today the spit is shared between Lithuania and Russia, with national parks on both sides protecting its dunes, forests and migrating-bird habitat.
