The Heart Sutra (Sanskrit: प्रज्ञापारमिताहृदय, Prajñāpāramitāhṛdaya, "The Heart of the Perfection of Wisdom"; Chinese: 心經, Xīnjīng) is a popular sutra in Mahāyāna Buddhism. In Sanskrit, the title is rendered Prajñāpāramitāhṛdaya, which translates to "The Heart of the Perfection of Wisdom". It is one of the shortest and most widely recited of the Prajñāpāramitā ("Perfection of Wisdom") texts, and is cherished across East Asian Buddhism and in Tibetan traditions.

Content

The sutra is framed as a teaching by the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara addressed to the monk Śāriputra. Its most famous declaration is that "form is emptiness, emptiness is form" (rūpaṃ śūnyatā śūnyataiva rūpam). Avalokiteśvara explains that the five aggregates (skandhas)—form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness—are all empty of inherent existence (svabhāva). The text then systematically negates the categories of Buddhist doctrine itself: no eye, ear, nose, tongue, body or mind; no ignorance and no extinction of ignorance; no suffering, origin, cessation or path; no attainment. Because there is "no attainment", a bodhisattva who relies on the perfection of wisdom dwells without mental obstruction and ultimately attains nirvana.

The sutra concludes with a mantra presented as the great, unsurpassed mantra of prajñāpāramitā:

gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā

often rendered "gone, gone, gone beyond, gone altogether beyond—awakening, hail!"

Origins and transmission

The Heart Sutra exists in short and long versions. The long version, preserved in Sanskrit and Tibetan, includes a conventional narrative frame; the short version, best known through the Chinese translation attributed to the monk Xuanzang (649 CE), omits it. Scholars debate whether the text was composed in India or China; the philological work of Jan Nattier argued that the core passages derive from Kumārajīva's Chinese translation of the Large Prajñāpāramitā Sutra, suggesting the short sutra was assembled in China, then translated back into Sanskrit. Whatever its origin, the text spread throughout East Asia, where it is chanted daily in Zen, Pure Land, and other schools, and it entered Tibet as well, where a long version is used.

Influence

Because of its brevity and depth, the Heart Sutra became one of the most commented-upon texts in Buddhism, generating commentaries in China, Korea, Japan, and Tibet. It is frequently copied as calligraphy as an act of devotion, memorised by monastics and laypeople, and recited at funerals and in daily liturgy. Its doctrine of emptiness is regarded as the distilled essence—the "heart"—of the entire Prajñāpāramitā corpus.
