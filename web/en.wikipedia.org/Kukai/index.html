Kūkai

Died835, Mount Kōya

Kūkai (Japanese: 空海; 774–835), posthumously honoured as Kōbō Daishi, was a Japanese Buddhist monk, calligrapher, poet, engineer, and scholar who founded the esoteric Shingon school of Buddhism. One of the most revered figures in Japanese religious history, he is credited with establishing the monastic complex of Mount Kōya, compiling the first dictionary of Chinese characters in Japan, and — in popular tradition — inventing the kana syllabaries.

Life

Born into the Saeki clan in Sanuki Province (modern Kagawa Prefecture, Shikoku), Kūkai entered government college in the capital but abandoned Confucian studies for Buddhism. In 804 he travelled to Tang China on an official embassy — the same mission that carried Saichō, founder of Tendai — and in Chang'an studied Sanskrit and esoteric (Tantric) Buddhism under the master Huiguo, who initiated him as his dharma heir shortly before Huiguo's death. Kūkai returned to Japan in 806 with a large collection of sutras, mandalas, and ritual implements.

Works and legacy

After years of semi-obscurity, Kūkai gained imperial patronage: Emperor Saga granted him the temple of Tō-ji in Kyoto and, in 816, permission to found a monastic centre on remote Mount Kōya (Kōyasan), today the headquarters of Shingon and one of Japan's most sacred sites, with the vast Okunoin cemetery. In 823 he received charge of Tō-ji, and in 828 opened the Shugei Shuchi-in, a school for laypeople and poor students regardless of rank. His writings, including the Benkenmitsu nikyō ron and the treatises on attaining enlightenment in this very body (sokushin jōbutsu), systematised esoteric doctrine, and he is traditionally credited as one of the "Three Brushes" (Sanpitsu), the great calligraphers of the age. Tradition holds that Kūkai did not die but sits in eternal meditation at Okunoin, awaiting the future Buddha Maitreya. He is a central figure of the Shikoku Pilgrimage of 88 temples.
