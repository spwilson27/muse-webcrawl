Exorcism

PracticeExpulsion of demons or evil spirits
TraditionsChristianity, Islam (ruqya), Judaism, Hinduism, Buddhism, Taoism
Catholic riteDe Exorcismis et Supplicationibus Quibusdam (1999)
PractitionerExorcist

Exorcism (from Greek exorkismós, "binding by oath") is the religious or spiritual practice of evicting demons, jinn, or other malevolent spiritual entities from a person, animal, place, or object believed to be possessed. The practice is ancient and appears in many cultures and religions. The person performing the rite — an exorcist — typically employs prayers, invocations, commands, blessed materials such as holy water, and ritual gestures, addressing the entity in the name of a higher power.

In Christianity

The Gospels depict Jesus casting out demons, and the early Church continued the practice; a minor order of exorcists existed in the Latin Church. In Roman Catholicism, solemn ("major") exorcism of a person may be performed only by a priest with the express permission of the diocesan bishop, following medical and psychiatric evaluation to exclude natural illness. The rite was codified in the Rituale Romanum of 1614 and revised in 1999. Catholic teaching distinguishes demonic possession from obsession, vexation, and infestation, and lists traditional signs of possession such as speaking languages never learned, superhuman strength, and knowledge of hidden things. Anglican and Orthodox churches retain deliverance ministries, and many evangelical and Pentecostal churches practise informal deliverance prayer.

In other religions

In Islam, ruqya — recitation of Qur'anic verses, especially over water or the afflicted person — is used to expel jinn. Jewish tradition preserves exorcism of a dybbuk, a possessing spirit of the dead, famously dramatised in S. Ansky's play The Dybbuk (1914). Hindu, Buddhist, and Taoist traditions include their own rites and specialist practitioners, and shamanic cultures worldwide practise forms of spirit extraction.

Modern perspectives

Psychiatry and neurology explain many historical "possessions" through conditions such as epilepsy, schizophrenia, dissociative identity disorder, and Tourette syndrome. Deaths during exorcisms — most notoriously that of Anneliese Michel in Germany in 1976 — have led to criminal prosecutions and tightened church guidelines, and ethicists debate how clinicians should respond to patients who request ritual help within their own belief systems.
