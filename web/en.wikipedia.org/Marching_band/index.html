Marching band

A marching band is a group of instrumental musicians who perform music while marching, often for entertainment or ceremony, and sometimes for competition. Instrumentation typically includes brass, woodwind and percussion instruments, along with a colour guard that performs with flags, rifles and sabres. Marching bands are most commonly associated with American high schools and universities, where they perform at half-time during American football games, in parades, and at pep rallies.

History

Marching bands developed from military bands used to keep soldiers in step and to signal commands on the battlefield. By the late nineteenth century, American colleges adopted the military band tradition, and figures such as John Philip Sousa popularised the concert and marching band in civilian life. In the twentieth century, show-style bands at historically Black colleges and universities added high-stepping choreography and popular music, while corps-style bands influenced by drum and bugle corps emphasised precision drill.

Instrumentation and roles

A typical marching band includes trumpets, mellophones, trombones, baritones, tubas (often carried as sousaphones), flutes or piccolos, clarinets, and saxophones. The percussion section is divided into a battery (snare drums, tenor drums, bass drums and cymbals) that marches on the field and a front ensemble or "pit" of stationary instruments. The drum major conducts the band and leads it on parade. Many modern bands use electronics and amplified front ensembles in field shows.

Performance

Field shows combine music with drill — coordinated movements that form pictures and patterns on the field, usually written with computer software and learned in steps counted in eighths ("eight-to-five" steps of 22.5 inches). Competitive circuits such as Bands of America and Drum Corps International judge bands on musical and visual performance. Marching bands also appear in parades such as the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade and the Tournament of Roses Parade.
