Annealing (metallurgy)

In metallurgy and materials science, annealing is a heat treatment that alters the physical and sometimes chemical properties of a material to increase its ductility and reduce its hardness, making it more workable. It involves heating a material above its recrystallisation temperature, maintaining a suitable temperature for a period of time, and then cooling.

Stages

Annealing proceeds in three stages as temperature rises: recovery, in which internal stresses are relieved and dislocations rearrange; recrystallisation, in which new strain-free grains nucleate and replace deformed grains; and grain growth, in which grains coarsen if heating continues. The result is a softer, more uniform microstructure with restored ductility.

Types

Full annealing of steel heats it above the upper critical temperature followed by slow furnace cooling, producing a coarse pearlite structure. Process annealing relieves work hardening between cold-working steps. Stress-relief annealing uses lower temperatures to reduce residual stresses without major microstructural change. Normalising, a related treatment, uses air cooling for a finer, harder structure. Copper, brass, silver, and aluminium are also routinely annealed to reverse work hardening.

Process details

Annealing is carried out in batch or continuous furnaces, often with controlled atmospheres of nitrogen, hydrogen, or vacuum to prevent oxidation and decarburisation. Temperature and time depend on alloy composition and the desired properties; excessive time or temperature causes unwanted grain growth and loss of strength.

Applications

Annealing prepares metals for further forming (drawing, stamping, machining), restores ductility after cold work, improves electrical conductivity in copper wire, and is essential in glass manufacture, where slow cooling prevents internal stress.
