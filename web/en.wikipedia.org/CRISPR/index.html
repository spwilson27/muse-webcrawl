CRISPR

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (recreated from memory)

CRISPR (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats) is a family of DNA sequences found in the genomes of bacteria and archaea, forming the basis of an adaptive immune system that protects prokaryotes against viruses. Derived from this system, CRISPR-Cas9 and related technologies allow precise, programmable editing of genomes in living organisms, transforming genetics research and leading to the first approved CRISPR-based medicines.

Natural function

In bacteria, CRISPR loci store short fragments ("spacers") of DNA captured from invading bacteriophages between repeated sequences. Transcribed and processed into guide RNAs, these spacers direct Cas proteins to cut matching foreign DNA upon reinfection — a heritable, adaptive immunity unique among known systems.

Genome editing

In 2012, Jennifer Doudna, Emmanuelle Charpentier, and colleagues showed that the Cas9 nuclease could be programmed with a single guide RNA to cut any chosen DNA sequence, and Feng Zhang's and George Church's groups soon demonstrated editing in human and mouse cells. The cell's repair pathways then either disrupt a gene (via error-prone NHEJ) or rewrite it from a supplied template (HDR). Later developments include base editors, prime editing, Cas12 and Cas13 systems, and CRISPR-based diagnostics. Doudna and Charpentier received the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Applications and ethics

Applications span basic research, crop improvement, livestock breeding, gene drives for pest control, and therapies — in 2023 regulators approved the first CRISPR therapy, for sickle cell disease and beta thalassemia. Ethical controversy erupted in 2018 when He Jiankui announced the birth of genome-edited babies, prompting worldwide condemnation and renewed debate over heritable human editing.
