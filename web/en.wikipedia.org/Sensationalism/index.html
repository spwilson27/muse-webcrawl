Sensationalism

Sensationalism is a type of editorial tactic in mass media in which events and topics in news stories are selected and worded to excite the strongest emotions in readers and viewers — shock, fear, outrage or titillation — often at the expense of accuracy and balance. The term is usually pejorative and is associated with tabloid journalism, "yellow journalism" of the 1890s, and modern clickbait.

History

Sensational reporting flourished in the penny press of the 1830s and in the circulation wars between Joseph Pulitzer's New York World and William Randolph Hearst's New York Journal, whose coverage helped inflame public opinion before the Spanish–American War of 1898.

Criticism

Critics argue that sensationalism distorts public priorities, over-emphasises crime and disaster, and rewards emotional arousal over understanding. Defenders note that vivid storytelling can draw audiences to important issues.
