Flake tool

In archaeology, a flake tool is a type of stone tool created by striking a flake from a stone core through lithic reduction. Whereas a core tool (such as a hand axe) is shaped from the core itself, a flake tool is made from the detached piece — the flake — whose sharp edges formed by percussion flaking could be used directly or further shaped by retouch. Flake tools were produced throughout the Stone Age and across the world.

Manufacture

Flakes are struck from suitable stone — commonly flint, chert, obsidian or quartzite — using a hammerstone or a "soft" hammer of antler or wood. The fracture mechanics of these cryptocrystalline materials produce a recognisable bulb of percussion on the flake's ventral surface. Techniques such as the Levallois method, characteristic of Middle Palaeolithic industries associated with Neanderthals and early modern humans, prepared the core so that a flake of predetermined shape could be struck off with a single blow.

Types and uses

Common flake-tool forms include scrapers (for cleaning hides and working wood), points and knives, burins (for engraving bone and antler), denticulates (saw-like edges) and notched flakes. Use-wear analysis — microscopic examination of edge damage and polish — allows archaeologists to infer what materials a tool worked. Unretouched flakes, once dismissed as mere manufacturing debris, are now recognised as expedient cutting tools in their own right.

Significance

Because flake tools and the waste flakes (debitage) of their production survive in enormous numbers, they are a principal source of evidence for hominin behaviour, technology and raw-material movement. The earliest known stone industries, such as the Oldowan (from about 2.6 million years ago), consist largely of simple cores and sharp flakes used for cutting.
