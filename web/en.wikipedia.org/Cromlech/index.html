Cromlech

The term cromlech (from Welsh crom, "bent" or "curved," and llech, "slab") has been used in two distinct senses in archaeology. In Welsh and older British usage it denotes a Neolithic burial chamber or dolmen: a structure of upright stones supporting a large capstone. In continental European usage, and in much modern archaeology, it denotes a circle of standing stones — what English usage usually calls a stone circle.

Usage and examples

Because of this ambiguity, many archaeologists avoid the term or specify which meaning they intend. In the dolmen sense, examples include Pentre Ifan in Wales and the great passage tombs of Brittany. In the stone-circle sense, the Cromlech of the Almendres in Portugal — one of the largest megalithic complexes in Europe, with nearly a hundred standing stones arranged in concentric ovals — is the best-known example.

Megalithic context

Cromlechs date principally to the Neolithic and Early Bronze Age. Like other megalithic monuments, they are thought to have served ritual, ceremonial, astronomical, and territorial functions, though their precise purposes remain debated.
