First transcontinental railroad

The "Golden Spike" ceremony at Promontory Summit, 10 May 1869.

North America's first transcontinental railroad (known originally as the "Pacific Railroad" and later as the "Overland Route") was a 1,912-mile (3,077 km) continuous railroad line built between 1863 and 1869 that connected the existing eastern U.S. rail network at Council Bluffs, Iowa, with the Pacific coast at the Oakland Long Wharf on San Francisco Bay. The line was built by three private companies — the Western Pacific Railroad Company, the Central Pacific Railroad Company of California, and the Union Pacific Railroad Company — under the Pacific Railway Acts of 1862 and 1864.

Construction

The Central Pacific built eastward from Sacramento, California, through the Sierra Nevada, relying heavily on Chinese immigrant labourers, who formed the great majority of its workforce and performed much of the most dangerous work, including tunnelling through granite. The Union Pacific built westward from Omaha across the Great Plains, employing many Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans. The two lines raced to lay as much track as possible, since federal land grants and loans were tied to mileage; on one famous day Central Pacific crews laid ten miles of track.

Completion and impact

The rails were joined at Promontory Summit, Utah Territory, on 10 May 1869, where Leland Stanford drove the ceremonial golden spike. The railroad cut the journey across the continent from months to about a week, transformed trade and settlement in the American West, bound California to the Union, and displaced the wagon trails and the Pony Express. It also had devastating consequences for Native American peoples, accelerating military conquest and the destruction of the bison herds on which Plains cultures depended.
