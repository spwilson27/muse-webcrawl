The United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA) is a free trade agreement between the United States, Mexico and Canada. It is known as CUSMA in Canada (Canada–United States–Mexico Agreement) and as T-MEC in Mexico. The agreement replaced the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) and entered into force on 1 July 2020.

Negotiation and ratification

During the 2016 US presidential campaign, Donald Trump pledged to renegotiate or withdraw from NAFTA, which he criticised as harmful to American manufacturing. Negotiations among the three countries began in August 2017 and concluded with an agreement in principle in the autumn of 2018; the agreement was signed on 30 November 2018 at the G20 summit in Buenos Aires.

Following changes negotiated with Democrats in the US House of Representatives — particularly on labour enforcement, environmental provisions and pharmaceutical patent rules — a protocol of amendment was signed on 10 December 2019. Mexico ratified the agreement first, followed by Canada and the United States, whose Congress approved it with broad bipartisan support in January 2020.

Key provisions

  • Automobiles: rules of origin were tightened so that 75% of a vehicle's content must be made in North America to qualify for duty-free treatment, and a share of content must be produced by workers earning at least US$16 per hour.
  • Dairy: Canada opened a modestly larger share of its supply-managed dairy market to US producers and eliminated certain milk-pricing classes.
  • Labour and environment: enforceable chapters require the parties to uphold core labour rights; Mexico undertook reforms allowing independent unions and secret-ballot votes, backed by a rapid-response enforcement mechanism.
  • Digital trade: new rules prohibit customs duties on electronic transmissions and data-localisation requirements, and protect cross-border data flows.
  • Intellectual property: copyright terms were extended, though a planned extension of data exclusivity for biologic drugs was dropped in the 2019 protocol.
  • Investor–state dispute settlement: ISDS was phased out between the US and Canada and curtailed between the US and Mexico.
  • Sunset and review: the agreement has a 16-year term with a joint review every six years, the first scheduled for 2026.

Economic assessments

Analyses by bodies such as the US International Trade Commission projected a modest positive effect on US GDP and employment relative to NAFTA, while noting that the stricter auto rules of origin could raise vehicle prices and shift some sourcing. Trade among the three countries exceeds US$1.5 trillion annually, and Mexico and Canada rank among the United States' largest trading partners.
