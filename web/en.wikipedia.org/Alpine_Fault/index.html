Alpine Fault

The Alpine Fault is a geological fault running for about 600 kilometres along the western side of New Zealand's South Island, forming the boundary between the Pacific Plate and the Indo-Australian Plate. It is a major dextral (right-lateral) strike-slip fault with a significant component of uplift, which has raised the Southern Alps along its eastern side.

Geology

Motion across the fault accounts for much of the relative movement between the two plates in the South Island. Total horizontal displacement is estimated at roughly 700–800 km since the fault formed in the Miocene, and the uplifted Pacific Plate side exposes deeply buried schist rocks — a classic locality studied by geologists. The fault's slip rate is on the order of 30 mm per year, fast by global standards.

Earthquake hazard

The Alpine Fault ruptures in very large earthquakes, roughly magnitude 8, at intervals of a few centuries; paleoseismic studies identify major ruptures around 1717 CE and earlier. Because more than three centuries have elapsed, scientists judge there is a high probability of another great rupture within the coming decades, a scenario (studied by the AF8 project) that would strongly shake the whole South Island and disrupt roads, power and tourism across the region.
