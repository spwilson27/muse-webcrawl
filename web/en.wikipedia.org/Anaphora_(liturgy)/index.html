Anaphora (liturgy)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The anaphora (Greek: ἀναφορά, "offering up", "carrying back") is the central Eucharistic prayer in the liturgies of Eastern Christianity and in the eucharistic prayers of the Roman Rite of the Catholic Church. It is the part of the Divine Liturgy or Mass in which the bread and wine are consecrated; in the Roman tradition its equivalent component is called the Eucharistic Prayer, of which the Roman Canon is the historic example.

Structure

Although details differ between traditions, an anaphora characteristically includes:

  • an opening dialogue and preface of thanksgiving;
  • the Sanctus ("Holy, holy, holy");
  • a post-Sanctus recalling salvation history;
  • the institution narrative, recounting Christ's words at the Last Supper;
  • the anamnesis, the memorial offering of the sacrifice;
  • the epiclesis, the invocation of the Holy Spirit upon the gifts and the assembly;
  • intercessions for the living and the dead; and
  • a concluding doxology.

Historic anaphoras

Important examples include the Anaphora of the Apostolic Tradition (associated with Hippolytus of Rome), the Alexandrian Anaphora of St. Mark and St. Cyril, the Anaphora of St. James used in Jerusalem, the Anaphora of St. John Chrysostom and of St. Basil the Great in the Byzantine Rite, and the East Syriac Anaphora of Addai and Mari — notable for lacking an explicit institution narrative, whose validity the Catholic Church recognised in 2001. The variety of historic anaphoras illustrates the diversity of early Christian eucharistic praying.
