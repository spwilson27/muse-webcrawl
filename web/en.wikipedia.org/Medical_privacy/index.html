Medical privacy

Medical privacy, or health privacy, is the practice of maintaining the security and confidentiality of patient records. It involves both conversational discretion on the part of health professionals and the security of medical records. The terms can also refer to the physical privacy of patients from other patients and providers while in a medical facility. Modern concerns include the proliferation of electronic medical records and the sharing of data among insurers, employers, researchers, and technology companies.

Confidentiality of patient information has been a professional duty since the Hippocratic Oath.

Ethical foundations

Confidentiality in medicine dates to the Hippocratic Oath, which obliges physicians to keep patients' information private. Confidentiality encourages patients to disclose sensitive information needed for diagnosis and treatment, and protects them from stigma or discrimination. It is limited in practice by duties to third parties — for example, mandatory reporting of certain infectious diseases, gunshot wounds, or credible threats of harm (such as the Tarasoff duty to warn).

Legal frameworks

In the United States, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) sets national standards for protecting "protected health information", granting patients rights of access and amendment while permitting specified uses for treatment, payment, and healthcare operations. The HITECH Act of 2009 strengthened enforcement and extended requirements to business associates. In the European Union, health data are a "special category" under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), subject to heightened protections. Other jurisdictions, including Canada (PIPEDA and provincial health-privacy statutes) and Australia (the Privacy Act and My Health Record system), have their own regimes.

Contemporary issues

Digitization and data breaches have made medical privacy a major policy concern: healthcare organizations are frequent targets of ransomware and data theft, and health data command high prices on black markets. Debates continue over de-identification of data for research, genetic privacy, direct-to-consumer testing, and the use of health apps and wearable data that may fall outside traditional medical privacy laws.
