Blood vessel

Blood vessels are the components of the circulatory system that transport blood throughout the body. The three main types are arteries, which carry blood away from the heart; veins, which return blood to the heart; and capillaries, the tiny vessels in which exchange of water, oxygen, nutrients and waste products with the tissues takes place. Together the vessels of an adult human, laid end to end, would stretch for tens of thousands of kilometres.

Structure

Arteries and veins have walls of three layers: the tunica intima, an inner lining of endothelium; the tunica media, containing smooth muscle and elastic fibres; and the tunica externa (adventitia), of connective tissue. Arteries have thicker, more elastic media to withstand pulsatile pressure. Capillaries consist of a single layer of endothelial cells and a basement membrane, optimised for exchange. Many veins contain valves that prevent backflow.

Function

Blood vessels regulate blood flow and pressure through vasoconstriction and vasodilation of their smooth muscle, under nervous, hormonal and local control. The endothelium also secretes substances such as nitric oxide, participates in clotting, inflammation and the formation of new vessels (angiogenesis).

Disease

Important vascular diseases include atherosclerosis, aneurysm, varicose veins, vasculitis and thrombosis. Rupture or blockage of vessels supplying the heart or brain causes heart attack and stroke respectively.
