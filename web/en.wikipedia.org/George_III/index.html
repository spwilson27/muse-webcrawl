George III (George William Frederick; 4 June 1738 – 29 January 1820) was King of Great Britain and Ireland from 25 October 1760 until his death in 1820, and concurrently King of Hanover from 1814. His 59-year reign was the third longest of any British monarch and saw the union of Great Britain and Ireland (1801), the loss of the American colonies, and the wars against Revolutionary and Napoleonic France.

Reign

The first Hanoverian king born in England and to use English as his first language, George III ascended the throne on the death of his grandfather George II. His reign opened with victory in the Seven Years' War, but disputes over taxation of the American colonies escalated into the American Revolutionary War, ending in British recognition of United States independence in 1783. Britain later led successive coalitions against France during the Napoleonic Wars, culminating in victory at Waterloo in 1815.

Illness and regency

George suffered recurrent bouts of mental illness from 1788–89 onward; historians and medical writers have proposed diagnoses including porphyria and bipolar disorder. In 1811 his eldest son, the Prince of Wales, was made Prince Regent and ruled for the remainder of the reign. Nicknamed "Farmer George" for his interest in agriculture and his simple tastes, the king died at Windsor Castle in 1820, blind and deaf, and was succeeded by George IV.

Legacy

George III's reputation has shifted over time: once caricatured as the tyrant of American independence and as "mad", he is now often portrayed as a conscientious, dutiful monarch constrained by the politics of his age.
