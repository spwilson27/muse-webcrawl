Ligand field theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ligand field theory explains the splitting of metal d-orbitals by surrounding ligands.

Ligand field theory (LFT) describes the bonding, orbital arrangement, and other characteristics of coordination complexes of transition metals. It combines the electrostatic ideas of crystal field theory with molecular orbital theory, allowing for covalent character in metal–ligand bonds. Developed mainly in the 1930s–1950s through the work of John Hasbrouck van Vleck, Leslie Orgel, Carl Ballhausen and others, it remains the standard qualitative framework for understanding the colour, magnetism, and reactivity of transition metal complexes.

Basic ideas

In an octahedral complex, the five metal d-orbitals split into two sets: the lower-energy t2g set (pointing between the ligands) and the higher-energy eg set (pointing at the ligands). The energy gap between them, Δo, depends on the metal ion and on the ligands, which are ordered empirically in the spectrochemical series from weak-field ligands (e.g., I−, Br−) to strong-field ligands (e.g., CO, CN−). If Δo is small, electrons occupy orbitals singly, giving high-spin complexes; if it exceeds the electron-pairing energy, electrons pair up, giving low-spin complexes.

Applications

LFT explains the colours of complexes through d–d electronic transitions, their magnetic moments, crystal field stabilization energies that influence ionic radii, reaction rates and geometries, and effects such as the Jahn–Teller distortion. Pi-bonding between metal and ligand—central to explaining the spectrochemical series and the stability of metal carbonyls—is naturally incorporated in LFT but not in a purely ionic crystal-field picture.
