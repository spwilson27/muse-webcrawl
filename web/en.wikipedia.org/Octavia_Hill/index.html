Octavia Hill

Octavia Hill (3 December 1838 – 13 August 1912) was an English social reformer, whose main field of work was housing for the poor in British cities in the second half of the nineteenth century. She was also a co-founder of the National Trust, an organisation that works to preserve places of historic interest and natural beauty in England, Wales and Northern Ireland.

Early life

Hill was born in Wisbech, Cambridgeshire, into a family influenced by utilitarian and Christian socialist ideas; her father was a corn merchant and banker who went bankrupt, and from her early teens she worked helping to run a co-operative workshop for women. She came under the influence of the theologian F. D. Maurice and the art critic John Ruskin, who later financed her first housing scheme.

Housing reform

From 1865 Hill bought dilapidated properties in London slums, renovated them, and let rooms to poor tenants at fair rents, insisting on prompt payment and careful maintenance. She trained a corps of women housing managers — the forerunners of professional social housing managers — who combined rent collection with personal knowledge of tenants' circumstances. She opposed large-scale municipal clearance and rebuilding, preferring improvement of existing homes, and campaigned for preserving open spaces for city dwellers.

National Trust and later work

In 1895 Hill co-founded the National Trust with Robert Hunter and Canon Hardwicke Rawnsley. She also served on the Royal Commission on the Poor Laws of 1905–1909, signing the majority report that resisted radical reform of the poor law system. She died in London in 1912.
