Socialist feminism
MovementFeminism; socialism
Key figuresClara Zetkin, Alexandra Kollontai, Juliet Mitchell, Silvia Federici
Core claimWomen's liberation requires ending both capitalism and patriarchy

Socialist feminism is a branch of feminism that holds that women's oppression arises from the interlocking systems of capitalism and patriarchy, and that liberation therefore requires both the abolition of class society and the transformation of gender relations. It grew out of the women's liberation movements of the late 1960s and 1970s, synthesising Marxist analysis with radical feminist insights.

Theory

Socialist feminists argue that women's unpaid domestic and caring labour — "social reproduction" — sustains the wage-labour system by reproducing workers daily and generationally. Writers such as Juliet Mitchell ("Women: The Longest Revolution", 1966), Zillah Eisenstein, and Silvia Federici analysed how the family, sexuality, and housework function within capitalist economies; campaigns like Wages for Housework pressed these claims politically.

History

Roots run through nineteenth-century utopian socialists and Marxists such as Clara Zetkin and Alexandra Kollontai, who linked women's emancipation to socialist transformation. The tendency was prominent in second-wave feminism, especially in Britain and North America, and later broadened through intersectional attention to race and colonialism.

Criticism and relations

Socialist feminism distinguishes itself from Marxist feminism (which may subordinate gender to class) and from radical feminism (which centres patriarchy alone). Critics charge it with theoretical eclecticism; defenders reply that dual-systems or unified-systems theory better captures lived oppression.
