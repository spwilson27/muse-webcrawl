Randolph Denard Ornette Coleman (9 March 1930 – 11 June 2015) was an American jazz saxophonist, violinist and composer. One of the founders of free jazz, he freed improvisation from the chord changes of bebop, allowing soloists to follow melodic intuition rather than preset harmony. His album title Free Jazz: A Collective Improvisation (1960) gave the movement its name.

Career

Raised in Fort Worth, Texas, Coleman taught himself saxophone and worked in rhythm-and-blues bands before moving to Los Angeles, where he developed his style while working as an elevator operator. His 1959 engagement at New York's Five Spot with his quartet — Don Cherry, Charlie Haden and Billy Higgins or Ed Blackwell — polarised the jazz world; his plastic alto saxophone and "harmolodic" playing were praised by Leonard Bernstein and attacked by others. Landmark albums include The Shape of Jazz to Come (1959), containing the standard "Lonely Woman", and Free Jazz (1960), recorded with a double quartet.

Harmolodics and later work

Coleman's theory of harmolodics treats harmony, melody and rhythm as equal elements that every player may move freely. In the 1970s he formed the electric band Prime Time, applying the approach to funk-inflected music, and in 1988 recorded Virgin Beauty with Jerry Garcia. He received a Pulitzer Prize for Music in 2007 for the live album Sound Grammar — the first Pulitzer awarded to a recording — and a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award the same year.
