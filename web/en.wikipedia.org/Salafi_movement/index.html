Salafi movement

The Salafi movement or Salafism (Arabic: السلفية, as-Salafiyya) is a revivalist movement within Sunni Islam that emerged in the late nineteenth century. Named after the salaf, the "pious predecessors" — the first three generations of Muslims, comprising the companions of Muhammad (the Sahaba) and their successors — Salafis seek to model religious life on the practice of these earliest generations, and reject later innovations (bid'ah) in worship and doctrine.

Beliefs and method

Salafi methodology (manhaj) centres on adherence to the Quran and the Sunnah (the example of Muhammad) as understood by the first generations, an emphasis on tawhid (the oneness of God), and the purification of worship from what Salafis regard as accretions such as saint veneration, tomb pilgrimage, and Sufi practices. Doctrinally, most contemporary Salafis follow the Athari school of theology, accepting the divine attributes as stated in scripture without figurative reinterpretation, and in jurisprudence they often downplay loyalty to the classical schools of law (madhhabs) in favour of direct evidence from scripture.

History

Intellectual antecedents include the Hanbali school and the reformist theology of Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328) and his student Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya. In the eighteenth century, Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab's movement in Arabia allied with the House of Saud recast these ideas as a political-religious reform programme. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, reformers such as Jamal al-Din al-Afghani, Muhammad Abduh, and Rashid Rida used "Salafiyya" for a modernist reform of Islamic thought; over the twentieth century, however, the label came to be dominated by scripturalist scholars in Saudi Arabia and Yemen and by figures such as Nasiruddin al-Albani, Abd al-Aziz ibn Baz, and Muhammad ibn al-Uthaymin.

Currents within Salafism

Scholars commonly distinguish three broad currents: quietist or "purist" Salafis, who focus on education and preaching and avoid politics; activist (haraki) Salafis, who engage in political parties and movements; and jihadi Salafis, who endorse armed struggle against governments they deem apostate and against foreign powers. The great majority of Salafis belong to the quietist current and reject the jihadi current.

Terminology and relationship to Wahhabism

"Salafism" and "Wahhabism" overlap substantially in doctrine and history, though adherents generally prefer "Salafi" and regard "Wahhabi" — coined by opponents after Ibn Abd al-Wahhab — as pejorative. Salafism has grown rapidly since the 1960s–70s, spread through Saudi-funded publishing, scholarships, and satellite and internet media, and is present across the Muslim world and in Western diaspora communities.
