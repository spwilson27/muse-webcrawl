Hypoparathyroidism

The four parathyroid glands sit behind the thyroid.

Hypoparathyroidism is a disorder of decreased function of the parathyroid glands, causing abnormally low production of parathyroid hormone (PTH). This results in low blood calcium (hypocalcaemia), high blood phosphate, and defective activation of vitamin D. PTH normally raises calcium by acting on bone, kidney, and (through calcitriol) the intestine.

Causes

The most common cause is accidental damage to, or removal of, the parathyroid glands during thyroid or other neck surgery. Other causes include autoimmune destruction (as in autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 1), congenital absence of the glands (as in DiGeorge syndrome), infiltrative diseases (haemochromatosis, Wilson's disease), radiation, and severe magnesium deficiency, which impairs PTH secretion and action. Activating mutations of the calcium-sensing receptor (autosomal dominant hypocalcaemia) cause a genetic form.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms are those of hypocalcaemia: tingling in the fingers, toes, and lips, muscle cramps and tetany, positive Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs, and in severe cases seizures, laryngospasm, or cardiac arrhythmias with prolonged QT interval. Chronic disease may produce cataracts, dry skin, brittle nails, dental defects, and calcification of the basal ganglia.

Treatment

Acute symptomatic hypocalcaemia is treated with intravenous calcium gluconate. Chronic management uses oral calcium supplements with active vitamin D analogues (calcitriol or alfacalcidol), aiming for low-normal calcium while avoiding hypercalciuria and kidney damage—a balance made difficult because PTH's renal calcium-conserving effect is missing. Recombinant PTH therapies (including palopegteriparatide) have been introduced to provide more physiological replacement.
