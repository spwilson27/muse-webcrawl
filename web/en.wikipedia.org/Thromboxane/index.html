Thromboxane

ClassEicosanoid
PrecursorProstaglandin H2, from arachidonic acid
EnzymeThromboxane-A synthase
MembersThromboxane A2, thromboxane B2

Thromboxane is a member of the family of lipids known as eicosanoids. The two major thromboxanes are thromboxane A2 (TXA2) and thromboxane B2 (TXB2). Thromboxane takes its name from its role in blood clot formation (thrombogenesis).

Production

Thromboxane-A synthase, an enzyme found in platelets, converts arachidonic acid-derived prostaglandin H2 to thromboxane A2. TXA2 is highly unstable in aqueous solution, hydrolyzing within about 30 seconds to the biologically inactive thromboxane B2.

Mechanism

Thromboxane acts through the thromboxane receptor, a G protein-coupled receptor on platelet and vascular smooth muscle membranes. TXA2 is a potent vasoconstrictor and a powerful promoter of platelet aggregation, and it facilitates platelet release reactions. It stands in physiological opposition to prostacyclin, an inhibitor of platelet aggregation and a vasodilator produced by the vessel wall; the balance between the two helps regulate clot formation.

Clinical significance

Low-dose aspirin irreversibly inhibits the enzyme cyclooxygenase-1 in platelets, reducing thromboxane A2 production and thereby decreasing the risk of arterial thrombosis, heart attack, and stroke. Thromboxane receptor antagonists and thromboxane synthase inhibitors have been investigated as alternative antithrombotic drugs. Excess thromboxane activity has also been implicated in bronchoconstriction in asthma.
