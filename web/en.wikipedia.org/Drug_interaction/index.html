Drug interaction

A drug interaction is a change in the action or side effects of a drug caused by the concurrent administration of another substance, such as another drug, a food, or a dietary supplement. Interactions can make a drug more or less effective, or produce adverse effects not seen with either substance alone.

Mechanisms

Pharmacokinetic interactions

These affect the absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion of a drug. Many important interactions involve the cytochrome P450 family of liver enzymes: one drug may inhibit an enzyme, slowing the breakdown of a second drug and raising its blood levels, or induce an enzyme, speeding breakdown and reducing effectiveness. Grapefruit juice is a well-known inhibitor of intestinal CYP3A4. Other pharmacokinetic mechanisms include competition for plasma protein binding and alteration of renal clearance or urine pH.

Pharmacodynamic interactions

These occur when drugs act on the same physiological system. Effects may be additive or synergistic — for example, combining alcohol with sedatives increases drowsiness and respiratory depression — or antagonistic, as when naloxone reverses opioid effects.

Risk factors and management

The risk of interactions increases with the number of medications a person takes (polypharmacy) and is higher in older adults. A classic example is the increased bleeding risk when the anticoagulant warfarin is combined with many antibiotics, antifungals, or anti-inflammatory drugs. Clinicians manage interactions by reviewing medication lists, consulting interaction databases, choosing alternative drugs, adjusting doses, and monitoring drug levels or clinical markers.
