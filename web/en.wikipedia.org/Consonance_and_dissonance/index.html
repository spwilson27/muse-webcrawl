Consonance and dissonance

In music, consonance and dissonance are categorisations of simultaneous or successive sounds. Consonant intervals and chords are typically experienced as stable, pleasant and restful, while dissonant ones are experienced as tense and requiring resolution to a consonance.

Acoustic basis

Consonance correlates with simple frequency ratios between tones: the octave (2:1), perfect fifth (3:2) and major third (5:4) are strongly consonant. Where partials of two tones fall close together without coinciding, listeners perceive roughness or beating, a key acoustic component of dissonance described by Helmholtz and quantified in 20th-century psychoacoustics.

Cultural and historical variation

What counts as consonant has shifted through history. The perfect fourth was consonant in medieval music but treated as dissonant in later counterpoint; the major third was once classed among imperfect consonances. Modern harmony emancipated many dissonances, and listeners' judgements are shaped by enculturation and musical context as well as acoustics.

In theory

Western tonal theory classifies intervals into perfect consonances (unison, fifth, octave), imperfect consonances (thirds and sixths) and dissonances (seconds, sevenths, the tritone and most augmented and diminished intervals), with rules of voice leading governing how dissonances are prepared and resolved.
