Convergent series

In mathematics, a series is the sum of the terms of an infinite sequence of numbers. A series is convergent (or converges) if the sequence of its partial sums tends to a limit; that limit is called the sum of the series. If the partial sums do not converge to a finite limit, the series is said to be divergent.

Examples

The geometric series 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + … converges to 2, since its partial sums approach that value. More generally a geometric series with ratio r converges exactly when |r| < 1. By contrast, the harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + … diverges, although its terms tend to zero — showing that terms tending to zero is necessary but not sufficient for convergence.

Convergence tests

Mathematicians have developed many tests to determine convergence: the comparison and limit comparison tests, the ratio and root tests, the integral test, and the alternating series test. A series converges absolutely if the series of absolute values of its terms converges; absolute convergence implies convergence, and such series can be rearranged without changing their sum. Series that converge but not absolutely are conditionally convergent, and by the Riemann series theorem their terms can be rearranged to sum to any value whatsoever.

History

Ideas of convergence date to antiquity — Archimedes summed a geometric series in computing the area of a parabolic segment — and were made rigorous in the 19th century by Cauchy, Abel and others, forming the foundations of mathematical analysis.
