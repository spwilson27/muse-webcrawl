Sichuan cuisine

Sichuan cuisine (Chinese: 四川菜; pinyin: Sìchuān cài) is a style of Chinese cuisine originating from Sichuan Province in southwestern China. It is famed for its bold flavours, particularly the pungency and spiciness resulting from the liberal use of garlic, chili peppers, and especially Sichuan pepper, which produces a characteristic numbing sensation known as málà (麻辣, "numbing-spicy"). It is one of the Four Great Traditions of Chinese cuisine.

History

Sichuan, a landlocked basin with a humid climate, developed cooking techniques such as pickling, salting, and drying to preserve food. Chili peppers, introduced from the Americas via maritime trade routes in the 16th and 17th centuries, were adopted enthusiastically, partly because they suited the local climate and were believed to counteract dampness. In 2010, Chengdu, the provincial capital, was named a UNESCO City of Gastronomy.

Flavour profiles

Traditional descriptions enumerate 23 or 24 complex flavour profiles in Sichuan cooking, including numbing-spicy (málà), fish-fragrant (yúxiāng, which contains no fish), strange-flavour (guàiwèi), sour-spicy, and red-oil flavours. Key seasonings include doubanjiang (fermented broad bean and chili paste) from Pixian, Sichuan pepper, dried chilies, black vinegar, and garlic.
