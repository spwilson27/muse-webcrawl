Kilt

A kilt is a garment resembling a wrap-around knee-length skirt, made of twill-woven worsted wool with a tartan pattern. Originating in the traditional dress of men and boys in the Scottish Highlands in the 16th century, it is most recognisably made with pleats at the back and flat aprons at the front. The kilt has become a symbol of Scottish identity and is worn at formal occasions, Highland games, military ceremonies and weddings.

History

The earliest form, the féileadh mór or great kilt, was a long length of cloth belted at the waist, with the surplus draped over the shoulder. The tailored short kilt with sewn pleats developed in the early 18th century and was adopted by Highland regiments of the British Army. After the Jacobite rising of 1745, the Dress Act 1746 banned Highland dress for civilians (soldiers excepted) until its repeal in 1782. In the 19th century, romantic interest in Highland culture — spurred by the works of Sir Walter Scott and the 1822 visit of George IV to Edinburgh — fixed the kilt's modern form and its association with clan tartans.

The outfit

Traditional accessories include a sporran (a pouch worn at the front), a kilt pin, knee-length kilt hose with flashes, ghillie brogues, and often a skean-dhu, a small knife tucked into the hose. Kilts are also traditional in other Celtic nations, including Ireland, Wales (the cilt) and Cornwall, usually in solid colours or national tartans.
