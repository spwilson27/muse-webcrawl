Jacques Offenbach

Jacques Offenbach (20 June 1819 – 5 October 1880) was a German-born French composer, cellist, and impresario, celebrated as the foremost composer of operetta in the 19th century.

Life and career

Born Jakob Offenbach in Cologne, he moved to Paris as a teenager, studied cello, and played in the orchestra of the Opéra-Comique. In 1855 he opened his own theatre, the Bouffes-Parisiens, where his short comic operas delighted Second Empire Paris. His longer works, including Orphée aux enfers (Orpheus in the Underworld, 1858), with its famous "Infernal Galop" or can-can, La belle Hélène (1864), and La Vie parisienne (1866), satirized contemporary society and myth.

Legacy

Offenbach composed more than 100 stage works. His opera Les contes d'Hoffmann (The Tales of Hoffmann), left unfinished at his death, was completed by others and became a staple of the operatic repertoire. His style strongly influenced the development of operetta in Vienna and the Savoy operas of Gilbert and Sullivan.
