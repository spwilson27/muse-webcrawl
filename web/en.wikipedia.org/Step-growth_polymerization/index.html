Step-growth polymerization
MechanismReaction between functional groups of molecules of all sizes
Contrasts withChain-growth polymerization
ExamplesNylon, polyester (PET), polyurethane, polycarbonate

Step-growth polymerization refers to a type of polymerization mechanism in which bi-functional or multifunctional monomers react to form first dimers, then trimers, longer oligomers, and eventually long polymer chains. Unlike chain-growth polymerization, in which monomers are added one at a time to an active growing chain, in step-growth polymerization any two molecules present in the mixture can react with each other.

Kinetics and molecular weight

Because of this mechanism, high molecular weight is achieved only at very high extents of reaction. The relationship is described by the Carothers equation, developed by Wallace Carothers at DuPont: the number-average degree of polymerization equals 1/(1 − p), where p is the fraction of functional groups that have reacted. Thus a p of 0.99 is required to reach a degree of polymerization of 100. Precise stoichiometric balance of the two functional groups is also essential, and a small imbalance limits molecular weight.

Types

Step-growth reactions include polycondensation, in which each bond-forming step releases a small molecule such as water (as in the formation of nylons and PET), and polyaddition, in which no by-product is lost (as in polyurethane formation from diisocyanates and diols). Monomers with more than two functional groups produce branched or cross-linked networks; the onset of gelation is described by Flory–Stockmayer theory. Important step-growth polymers include polyesters, polyamides, polyurethanes, polycarbonates, epoxy resins, and phenol–formaldehyde resins such as Bakelite.
