Kepi

A French officer's kepi.

The kepi (English: /ˈkɛpiː/ or /ˈkeɪpiː/) is a cap with a flat circular top and a peak, or visor. Etymologically, the word is a borrowing of the French képi, itself a respelling of the Alemannic German Käppi, a diminutive form of Kappe, meaning "cap". In Europe, this headgear is most commonly associated with French military and police uniforms, though versions of it were widely worn by other armies during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. In North America, it is usually associated with the American Civil War, as it was worn by soldiers of both the Union and the Confederacy.

Origins

The kepi originated during France's occupation of Algeria in the 1830s. French troops found their heavy leather shakos impractical in the North African climate, and light cloth caps modelled on local styles were adopted. Early versions were tall and cylindrical; by the Crimean War the design had evolved into the familiar short cap with a stiffened crown and a small leather peak. The kepi's practicality and smart appearance led many armies to copy it, including those of the United States, Austria, Spain, and much of Latin America.

Use in the United States

During the American Civil War (1861–1865), the kepi and its softer cousin, the forage cap, were standard issue in the Union Army and were also widely worn by Confederate troops, often in grey or butternut cloth. A softer, less formal variant with a slouching crown — popularised by officers such as George B. McClellan — is often called the "McClellan cap" or bummer's cap. After the war, kepi-style caps remained part of United States dress uniforms for several decades.

Modern use

The kepi remains the ceremonial headgear of the French Foreign Legion, whose white képi blanc is an iconic symbol of the corps, and it continues to be worn by the French National Gendarmerie and other French uniformed services. Similar caps are worn by ceremonial units in Spain, Monaco, Belgium and several other countries, and the style influenced civilian railway, postal and airline caps worldwide.
