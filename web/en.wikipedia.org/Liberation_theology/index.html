Liberation theology

Liberation theology is a theological approach emphasising the liberation of the oppressed, which emerged in the late 1960s among Latin American Roman Catholic theologians. It interprets the teachings of Jesus Christ in terms of freedom from unjust political, economic and social conditions, and insists on a "preferential option for the poor".

Origins

The movement grew out of the Second Vatican Council and the 1968 Medellín conference of Latin American bishops. Its foundational text is Gustavo Gutiérrez's A Theology of Liberation (1971). Other leading figures include Leonardo Boff, Jon Sobrino, and the Salvadoran archbishop Óscar Romero, assassinated in 1980.

Practice and reception

Liberation theology was practised through base ecclesial communities — small neighbourhood groups reading the Bible in relation to their own struggles. The Vatican's Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith criticised aspects of the movement in 1984 and 1986 for Marxist influence, while many of its themes were later embraced more warmly, notably under Pope Francis. Related movements include Black theology and feminist theology.
