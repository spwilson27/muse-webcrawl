Molecular phylogenetics


Data: DNA, RNA and protein sequences
Methods: Maximum likelihood, Bayesian inference, parsimony
Pioneers: Emile Zuckerkandl, Linus Pauling

Molecular phylogenetics is the branch of phylogeny that analyses genetic, hereditary molecular differences — predominantly in DNA sequences — to gain information on an organism's evolutionary relationships. From these analyses, scientists can estimate the divergence times and relationships among species, producing phylogenetic trees that show how life has diversified.

History

The field began in the 1960s, when Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling proposed the "molecular clock" hypothesis, observing that the number of amino acid differences between proteins such as haemoglobin increased roughly in proportion to evolutionary time. Walter Fitch and Emanuel Margoliash constructed early trees from cytochrome c sequences in 1967. The arrival of DNA sequencing in the 1970s and PCR in the 1980s transformed the field; analyses of ribosomal RNA by Carl Woese led to the recognition of the three domains of life — Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya.

Methods

Work typically involves aligning homologous sequences, choosing a model of molecular evolution, and inferring a tree using methods such as neighbour joining, maximum parsimony, maximum likelihood, or Bayesian inference (as implemented in programs such as MrBayes and BEAST). Statistical support is assessed by bootstrapping. Molecular data have resolved relationships that morphology could not — for example, placing whales within the even-toed ungulates — but analyses must contend with complications such as horizontal gene transfer, incomplete lineage sorting, and convergent evolution.
