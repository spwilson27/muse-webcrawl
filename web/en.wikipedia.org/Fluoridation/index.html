Water fluoridation

Water fluoridation is the controlled adjustment of fluoride to a public water supply purely for the purpose of dental health. Fluoridated water operates on tooth surfaces: in the mouth it creates low levels of fluoride in saliva, which reduces the rate at which tooth enamel demineralizes and increases the rate at which it remineralizes in the early stages of tooth decay.

History

The practice grew out of Frederick McKay's early-20th-century investigation of "Colorado brown stain" — mottled teeth that proved unusually resistant to decay — which H. Trendley Dean traced to naturally high fluoride in drinking water. In 1945 Grand Rapids, Michigan became the first city to deliberately fluoridate its water, beginning a controlled trial that was quickly followed by widespread adoption in the United States, Canada, Australia, Ireland and elsewhere.

Effects and assessment

Reviews, including Cochrane systematic reviews, find that fluoridation reduces decayed, missing and filled teeth in children, while the clearest adverse effect at recommended levels is an increased prevalence of mild dental fluorosis. Major health bodies including the WHO, the US CDC and national dental associations endorse the practice; the CDC named community water fluoridation one of the ten great public health achievements of the 20th century. Fluoridation remains contested politically, and several countries — particularly in continental Europe — rely instead on fluoridated salt, milk or topical fluoride.
