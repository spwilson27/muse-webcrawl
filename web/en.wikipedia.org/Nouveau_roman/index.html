The nouveau roman ("new novel" in French), also called the anti-novel, is a type of French novel of the 1950s and 1960s that broke with the conventions of the traditional nineteenth-century novel. The term was coined by the critic Émile Henriot in 1957, in reviewing Alain Robbe-Grillet's La Jalousie and Nathalie Sarraute's Tropismes.

Characteristics

The nouveaux romanciers rejected the omniscient narrator, linear plot and psychologically rounded characters of the Balzacian tradition. Instead their works emphasise the meticulous, sometimes obsessive description of objects and surfaces (chosisme), fractured chronology, repetition with slight variations, and narrators whose perceptions may be unreliable or incomplete. Meaning is not delivered by an authorial voice but is generated — or withheld — in the reader's encounter with the text.

Writers and theory

The movement was closely associated with the publisher Éditions de Minuit and its editor Jérôme Lindon. Robbe-Grillet set out its theory in the essays collected as Pour un nouveau roman (1963; For a New Novel). Principal works include Sarraute's Tropismes (1939) and Portrait of a Man Unknown, Robbe-Grillet's The Erasers, The Voyeur and Jealousy, Michel Butor's La Modification (1957), Claude Simon's The Flanders Road (1960) and Marguerite Duras's Moderato Cantabile. Simon received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1985, as did the closely associated Samuel Beckett in 1969.

Legacy

The nouveau roman influenced the French New Wave cinema — Robbe-Grillet wrote the screenplay for Alain Resnais's Last Year at Marienbad (1961) — and postmodern fiction internationally. Though never a unified school, and resisted by several of its supposed members, it permanently changed expectations of what a novel could do.
