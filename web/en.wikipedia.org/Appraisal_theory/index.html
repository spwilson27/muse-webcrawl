Appraisal theory

Appraisal theory is the theory in psychology that emotions are extracted from our evaluations (appraisals) of events that cause specific reactions in different people. Essentially, our appraisal of a situation causes an emotional, or affective, response that is based on that appraisal.

History

The modern theory traces to Magda Arnold's work in the 1960s, which proposed that emotion begins with an unconscious appraisal of an event's significance for the individual. Richard Lazarus subsequently developed appraisal into a central framework for the study of emotion, stress, and coping, distinguishing primary appraisal (evaluating relevance and goal congruence) from secondary appraisal (evaluating coping options and resources).

Appraisal dimensions

Researchers such as Klaus Scherer and Phoebe Ellsworth identified recurring appraisal dimensions that differentiate emotions, including novelty, intrinsic pleasantness, goal conduciveness, agency (who caused the event), coping potential, and compatibility with norms and self-ideals. For example, anger typically involves appraising an event as obstructing one's goals and caused blameworthily by another person, whereas guilt involves blame directed at oneself.

Applications

Appraisal theory informs affective computing, where computational models appraise events on behalf of virtual agents, and clinical psychology, where reappraisal — changing how a situation is evaluated — is a core emotion-regulation strategy in cognitive therapy.
