Flashover

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A flashover is the near-simultaneous ignition of most of the directly exposed combustible material in an enclosed area. When certain materials are heated, they undergo thermal decomposition and release flammable gases. Flashover occurs when the majority of surfaces in a space are heated to their autoignition temperature and emit these gases. The phenomenon marks the transition from a fire that is localised ("a fire in a room") to a fire that involves the whole room ("a room on fire").

Flashover normally occurs when the upper layer of hot gases in a room reaches around 500–600 °C (932–1,112 °F), corresponding to a heat flux at floor level of roughly 20 kW/m². It is one of the most dangerous events a firefighter can face, because conditions change from survivable to unsurvivable in seconds.

Mechanism

A fire burning in a compartment produces a buoyant plume of hot smoke and gases that rises and collects at the ceiling, forming a hot upper layer. This layer radiates heat downward onto every surface in the room. As the layer thickens and heats, objects far from the original fire begin to pyrolyse — releasing combustible gases — until the accumulated gases and heated surfaces ignite almost at once, producing flames throughout the volume of the compartment.

Warning signs

Firefighters are trained to recognise precursors: rapidly increasing heat, dense black smoke banking down, rollover (isolated flames flickering in the smoke layer), and "ghosting" flames. The related phenomenon of backdraft involves the sudden ignition of accumulated gases when fresh oxygen is introduced, for example by opening a door.

Dangers and prevention

After flashover, temperatures can exceed 1,000 °C and survival for unprotected occupants is essentially impossible. Ventilation control, early suppression, and compartmentation in building design are the main defences. Full-scale tests, such as those conducted at fire research laboratories, have been used to model flashover timing and to develop the fire-safety engineering criteria embedded in modern building codes.
