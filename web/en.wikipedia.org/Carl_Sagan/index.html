Carl Edward Sagan (November 9, 1934 – December 20, 1996) was an American astronomer, planetary scientist, and science communicator. His best known scientific contributions were research on the possibility of extraterrestrial life, including experimental demonstration of the production of amino acids from basic chemicals by exposure to light — foundational work in chemical evolution and astrobiology. Sagan assembled the first physical messages sent into space, the Pioneer plaque and the Voyager Golden Record, universal messages that could potentially be understood by any extraterrestrial intelligence that might find them.

Career

Sagan studied at the University of Chicago, earning his doctorate in 1960, and taught at Harvard before moving in 1968 to Cornell University, where he founded the Laboratory for Planetary Studies and taught until his death. He contributed to nearly every major American planetary mission of his era, including Mariner, Viking, Voyager, and Galileo, and was among the first to hypothesize that Saturn's moon Titan might have surface oceans of liquid compounds and that Jupiter's moon Europa might possess subsurface water oceans.

His early research on Venus correctly inferred that its high surface temperature results from a runaway greenhouse effect, and he was an early scientific voice warning of the climatic dangers of nuclear war — the "nuclear winter" hypothesis developed with colleagues in 1983 (the TTAPS study).

Science communication

Sagan's television series Cosmos: A Personal Voyage (1980), co-created and hosted by Sagan and written with Ann Druyan and Steven Soter, was watched by more than 500 million people in 60 countries and remains a landmark of science broadcasting. The companion book Cosmos spent 70 weeks on the bestseller list. His novel Contact (1985) was adapted into a 1997 film. In Pale Blue Dot (1994) he reflected on the famous photograph of Earth taken by Voyager 1 from beyond Neptune, and in The Demon-Haunted World (1995) he argued for scientific skepticism and critical thinking, popularizing the "baloney detection kit." His book The Dragons of Eden (1977) won the Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction.

Death and legacy

Sagan died of pneumonia at age 62, after years of treatment for the blood disorder myelodysplasia and three bone-marrow transplants. The landing site of the Mars Pathfinder spacecraft was named the Carl Sagan Memorial Station, and asteroid 2709 Sagan bears his name. He received the NASA Distinguished Public Service Medal twice, the Public Welfare Medal of the National Academy of Sciences, and numerous other honours.
