Teleost

Teleosts (infraclass Teleostei) are the largest infraclass in the class Actinopterygii, the ray-finned fishes. They comprise more than 26,000 species in about 40 orders and 448 families, accounting for about half of all known vertebrate species and the great majority of fish species. Teleosts range from giant ocean sunfish and oarfish to tiny paedocyprids less than a centimetre long, and include most fish of economic importance.

Characteristics

Teleosts are distinguished by several derived features, including a movable maxilla and premaxilla that allow the jaws to protrude when catching prey, a symmetrical (homocercal) tail fin supported by specialized bones, and in most species a swim bladder used for buoyancy control. Their feeding mechanisms are highly varied, from filter feeding to suction feeding and pharyngeal jaws that process food in the throat.

Evolution and diversity

Teleosts originated in the Triassic period, roughly 240 million years ago, and diversified enormously during the Cretaceous and Cenozoic. A whole-genome duplication early in their history is thought to have contributed to their evolutionary success. Major teleost groups include the Ostariophysi (carps, catfishes, and characins, dominant in fresh water), the Euteleostei (including salmon, pikes, and the vast Percomorpha), and the Percomorpha, which include perches, tunas, flatfish, seahorses, and most coral-reef fishes.

Ecology and importance

Teleosts occupy nearly every aquatic habitat, from deep-sea trenches to mountain streams. They are central to global fisheries and aquaculture: species such as anchovies, tunas, cod, and salmon support major industries, while zebrafish and medaka serve as important model organisms in research.
