Samaritans

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Samaritans

Total population: fewer than 1,000 (about 850 in the 2020s)

Regions: Mount Gerizim (Kiryat Luza) and Holon, Israel

Languages: Samaritan Hebrew and Samaritan Aramaic (liturgical); Modern Hebrew and Arabic (everyday)

Religion: Samaritanism

Holy place: Mount Gerizim

The Samaritans (Samaritan Hebrew: שומרים, Shomrim, “Guardians/Keepers [of the Torah]”; Hebrew: שומרונים) are an ethnoreligious group native to Samaria in the Levant. They regard themselves as descendants of the Israelite tribes of Ephraim and Manasseh who remained in the land after the Assyrian conquest of the northern Kingdom of Israel in about 722 BCE, and they preserve a distinct version of the Torah and an ancient liturgy.

Beliefs and practices

Samaritan religion centres on the Samaritan Pentateuch, which differs in some particulars from the Masoretic text, most importantly in commanding an altar on Mount Gerizim near ancient Shechem (modern Nablus). Samaritans accept only the Torah (including their own version of the Book of Joshua) as scripture. Their most distinctive observance is the Passover sacrifice, still offered each year on Mount Gerizim with the roasting of whole lambs, an observance preserved continuously into the present day.

History

A large community in antiquity, the Samaritans suffered catastrophic losses through revolts against Byzantine rule in the 5th and 6th centuries CE and through persecution and forced conversion in later centuries. By the early 20th century the community had dwindled to roughly 150 people, and it was feared to be on the verge of extinction. Under the British Mandate and after the establishment of Israel it slowly recovered; today the community numbers roughly 850 people, divided between Kiryat Luza on Mount Gerizim and the city of Holon. Samaritans hold both Israeli and Palestinian ties and have traditionally sought a neutral, mediating position between their neighbours.

In the New Testament

Samaritans appear in the Gospels in a generally favourable light relative to their portrayal in Jewish sources of the period: the parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10) makes a Samaritan the moral hero, and in John 4 Jesus converses with a Samaritan woman at Jacob’s well near Shechem.
