Moonshine

Moonshine is high-proof distilled alcohol, traditionally produced illicitly without government authorisation or taxation. The word derives from the practice of distilling by night — "by the light of the moon" — to evade detection. In the United States the term is most associated with unaged corn whiskey made in the Appalachian and southern states; analogous illicit spirits exist worldwide under many names.

History

Illicit distilling in America dates to the colonial era and flared after the federal whiskey excise tax of 1791, which helped provoke the Whiskey Rebellion. Moonshining flourished in remote mountain districts where corn was bulky to transport but could be converted into valuable, portable liquor. During national Prohibition (1920–1933), moonshine production industrialised, supplying speakeasies through networks of smugglers — "bootleggers" — whose fast cars, modified to outrun revenue agents, later seeded American stock car racing and NASCAR.

Production and hazards

Traditional moonshine is distilled from a fermented mash of corn, sugar, yeast, and water in pot stills, historically often of copper. Because it is unaged, it is clear and fiery. Illicit production carries real dangers: poorly separated distillation fractions and adulteration can yield methanol poisoning, and lead leached from stills built with automotive radiators or lead-soldered joints has caused chronic lead poisoning among heavy drinkers.

Legal status and modern revival

Home distilling of spirits remains illegal under US federal law without permits, though several states permit licensed "moonshine" brands — legally produced, untaxed-avoiding commercial spirits marketed in mason jars. Since the 2010s, craft distilleries have popularised legal unaged whiskey sold under the moonshine name.
