Root locus

In control theory and stability theory, root locus is a graphical method for examining how the roots of a system change with variation of a system parameter, commonly the gain of a feedback system. Developed by Walter R. Evans in 1948, the technique plots the trajectories of closed-loop poles in the complex s-plane as the parameter varies from zero to infinity.

Method

For a transfer function with open-loop poles and zeros, the root locus consists of branches that begin at the open-loop poles (at zero gain) and terminate at the open-loop zeros or at infinity (at infinite gain). A set of construction rules — concerning the real-axis segments, asymptotes, breakaway points, and angles of departure — allows the locus to be sketched by hand.

Uses

Because stability requires all closed-loop poles to lie in the left half of the s-plane, the root locus shows directly the gain ranges for which a system is stable and predicts transient behaviour such as damping and natural frequency, guiding the design of compensators.
