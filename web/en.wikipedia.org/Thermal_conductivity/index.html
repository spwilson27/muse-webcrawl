Thermal conductivity

Thermal conductivity (often denoted k, λ, or κ) is a material's ability to conduct heat. It is defined by Fourier's law: the heat flux through a material is proportional to the negative temperature gradient. Its SI unit is watts per metre-kelvin (W/(m·K)).

Values

Metals have high conductivities — copper about 400 W/(m·K), silver about 429 — because free electrons carry heat efficiently. Diamond, an electrical insulator, reaches over 2,000 through lattice vibrations (phonons). Insulators such as glass wool and polystyrene foam fall below 0.05, and aerogels lower still.

Measurement and uses

Conductivity is measured by steady-state guarded-hot-plate and transient hot-wire methods. It governs building insulation, heat sink design, and the thermal management of electronics.
