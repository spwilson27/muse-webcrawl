Gunpowder, also commonly known as black powder to distinguish it from modern smokeless powder, is the earliest known chemical explosive. It is a mixture of sulfur, charcoal (traditionally of willow wood) and potassium nitrate (saltpetre). The sulfur and charcoal act as fuels, while the saltpetre is the oxidiser; gunpowder consequently burns rapidly — technically deflagrates rather than detonates — producing hot, expanding gases that propel projectiles from guns and burst shells, rockets and fireworks.

History

Gunpowder was invented in China during the Tang dynasty, apparently arising from alchemists' experiments with saltpetre and sulfur in the search for elixirs of immortality; a mid-9th-century text warns of a mixture that had burned alchemists' faces and hands. The earliest surviving written formula appears in the Song-dynasty military compendium Wujing Zongyao (1044). Chinese armies developed fire lances, bombs, rockets and eventually true guns by the 13th century. The knowledge spread westward along trade routes and through the Mongol conquests: Roger Bacon gave a European formula in the 1260s, and gunpowder weapons — cannon by the 1320s, handguns, and later muskets — progressively transformed warfare in Europe, the Islamic world and Asia, favouring centralised states that could afford artillery and new fortress designs.

Manufacture and properties

Traditional manufacture involved grinding and "incorporating" the ingredients together wet for hours to coat each charcoal particle with saltpetre, then pressing, drying and "corning" the cake into grains of graded sizes — finer for pistols and priming, coarser for cannon. Good powder is hygroscopic and highly flammable, requiring careful storage in magazines. Its combustion yields roughly 55% solid residue (the familiar white smoke) and 45% gases, which limits muzzle velocity compared with smokeless powders and fouls barrels, but black powder remains valued for its reliability of ignition.

Modern use

From the 1880s, smokeless powders (nitrocellulose-based) replaced black powder in nearly all military and sporting firearms. Gunpowder survives in fireworks (of which it remains the essential ingredient), pyrotechnics, safety fuses, quarrying in moisture-sensitive applications, blank charges, and in black-powder shooting sports with reproduction muzzle-loaders. Its manufacture and storage are tightly regulated; historic accidents — from early powder mills to the 1605 Gunpowder Plot, an attempt to blow up the English Parliament — remain famous.
