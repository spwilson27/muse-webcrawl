Earned Income Tax Credit

The Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC), sometimes called the Earned Income Credit (EIC), is a refundable tax credit in the United States federal income tax system for low- to moderate-income working individuals and couples, particularly those with children. Enacted in 1975 and expanded repeatedly, it is one of the largest anti-poverty programs in the United States.

Structure

The credit rises with earned income up to a maximum, remains flat over a plateau range, and then phases out. The maximum credit depends on the number of qualifying children: families with three or more children receive the largest credits, while workers without children qualify for a much smaller amount. Because the credit is refundable, a taxpayer whose credit exceeds their tax liability receives the difference as a payment.

History

The EITC was enacted in the Tax Reduction Act of 1975, championed by Senator Russell Long as a "work bonus" alternative to welfare. Major expansions followed in 1986, 1990 and 1993, the last making it the centerpiece of the Clinton administration's pledge to make work pay and complementing the 1996 welfare reform. The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 added a larger tier for families with three or more children and increased marriage-penalty relief; the American Rescue Plan of 2021 temporarily expanded the credit for childless workers.

Effects

Economic research associates the EITC with increased labor-force participation, especially among single mothers, and with lifting millions of people — roughly half of them children — above the poverty line each year. Studies also link it to improved infant and maternal health and better educational outcomes for children. Criticisms focus on its complexity, an improper-payment rate driven largely by errors in claiming qualifying children, and marriage penalties at some income levels.

State credits

Many states and some cities offer their own earned income credits calculated as a percentage of the federal credit.
