William of Ockham (also Occam; c. 1287 – 1347) was an English Franciscan friar, scholastic philosopher, apologist, and Catholic theologian, widely considered one of the most important figures of medieval thought. He is best known for the methodological principle called Occam's razor, and for his defence of nominalism, the view that universals are names and concepts rather than independently existing realities.

Life

Ockham was born in the village of Ockham, Surrey, around 1287. He joined the Franciscan order and studied theology at Oxford, where he lectured on the Sentences of Peter Lombard, though he never completed the requirements for a master's degree — hence his title Venerabilis Inceptor, the "Venerable Beginner." In 1324 he was summoned to Avignon to answer charges of heresy; while there he became involved in the Franciscan dispute over apostolic poverty. In 1328 he fled Avignon with Michael of Cesena and took refuge with the Holy Roman Emperor Louis IV in Munich, where he spent the rest of his life writing political treatises. He died in 1347, probably of the plague.

Philosophy

Occam's razor

Ockham frequently invoked the principle of parsimony in explanation, formulated in Latin as entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem — "entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity." Though the maxim predates him, his forceful use of it gave it his name. It remains a cornerstone of scientific methodology: given competing explanations, prefer the one requiring fewer assumptions.

Nominalism

Against the realism of his scholastic predecessors, Ockham argued that only individual things exist; universals such as "humanity" are signs in the mind and in language. His nominalism had far-reaching consequences for logic, semantics, and theology.

Political thought

In works such as the Dialogus and the Opus nonaginta dierum, Ockham defended limits on papal power and contributed pioneering arguments about property rights and the separation of temporal and spiritual authority.
