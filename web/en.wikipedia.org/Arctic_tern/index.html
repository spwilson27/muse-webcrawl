The Arctic tern (Sterna paradisaea) is a seabird of the tern family Laridae famous for the longest migration of any animal: it breeds in the Arctic and sub-Arctic regions of Europe, Asia and North America and winters in the Southern Ocean around Antarctica, travelling distances of up to about 70,000–90,000 km per year. In doing so it experiences two summers annually and sees more daylight than any other creature.

Description

The Arctic tern is a medium-sized tern, 28–39 cm long with a wingspan of 75–85 cm, white and pale grey plumage, a black cap, deeply forked tail streamers and a blood-red bill and short legs in breeding plumage. It is very similar to the common tern, from which it is distinguished by its shorter legs, shorter all-red bill and longer tail streamers.

Migration

Tracking studies using miniature geolocators (notably a 2010 study of birds from Greenland and Iceland) showed that Arctic terns follow long, indirect routes down the Atlantic — some along the South American coast, others along Africa — crossing to the Indian Ocean and finally to Antarctic waters, taking advantage of prevailing winds. Over a lifespan that can exceed 30 years, a tern may fly well over two million kilometres, equivalent to several return trips to the Moon.

Breeding and feeding

Arctic terns breed in loose colonies on coasts and islands, laying one to three eggs in a shallow scrape; both parents incubate and fiercely defend the nest, dive-bombing intruders including polar bears and humans. They feed mainly on small fish, such as sand eels and young herring, and marine invertebrates, caught by plunge-diving. Population estimates are around one million individuals, though numbers have declined in parts of the range as food supplies changed.
