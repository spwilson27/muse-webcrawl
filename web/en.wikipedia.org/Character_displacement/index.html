Character displacement

Divergence of beak size reduces competition between finch species
Character displacement
FieldEvolutionary ecology
Key figuresWilliam L. Brown Jr., E. O. Wilson
Coined1956

Character displacement is the phenomenon where differences among similar species whose distributions overlap geographically are accentuated in regions where the species co-occur, but are minimised or lost where the species' distributions do not overlap. This pattern results from evolutionary change driven by natural selection as a consequence of competition between species for limited resources.

The term was coined by William L. Brown Jr. and E. O. Wilson in 1956, who wrote that "the two species in any given region diverge in those characteristics which pertain to food-getting or reproductive functions" where their ranges overlap.

Examples

Classic examples include the beak sizes of Darwin's finches on the Galápagos Islands, where coexisting species differ more in beak depth than populations living alone on separate islands; body sizes of Anolis lizards on Caribbean islands; and bill morphology in coexisting nuthatch and spadefoot toad tadpole populations.

Ecological and reproductive forms

Researchers distinguish ecological character displacement, acting on traits related to resource use, from reproductive character displacement, acting on mating signals and preferences that reduce costly hybridisation between species — a process closely related to reinforcement.
