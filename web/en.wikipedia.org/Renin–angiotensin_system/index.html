Renin–angiotensin system

The renin–angiotensin system (RAS), or renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), is a hormone system that regulates blood pressure, body fluid balance, and systemic vascular resistance. It is one of the body's principal long-term blood pressure control mechanisms and a major target of antihypertensive drugs.

Mechanism

When renal blood flow falls, juxtaglomerular cells in the kidneys release the enzyme renin into the circulation. Renin converts the plasma protein angiotensinogen (produced by the liver) into angiotensin I. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), abundant in the lungs and vascular endothelium, then converts angiotensin I into the biologically active octapeptide angiotensin II.

Angiotensin II raises blood pressure by several actions:

  • Potent vasoconstriction of arterioles
  • Stimulation of aldosterone secretion from the adrenal cortex, increasing renal sodium and water reabsorption
  • Stimulation of antidiuretic hormone (vasopressin) release and thirst
  • Increased sympathetic nervous system activity

Local systems and counter-regulation

Beyond the circulating system, local tissue RAS exist in the heart, brain, vasculature, and other organs. A counter-regulatory axis—ACE2 converting angiotensin II to angiotensin (1–7), which acts on the Mas receptor—produces vasodilatory and anti-fibrotic effects that balance the classical pathway.

Clinical significance

Overactivity of the RAS contributes to hypertension, heart failure, and diabetic kidney disease. Widely used drugs block the system at different points: ACE inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), direct renin inhibitors, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists. ACE2 also serves as the entry receptor for the coronaviruses SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2, giving the system a role in viral infection.
