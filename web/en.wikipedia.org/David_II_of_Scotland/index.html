David II (5 March 1324 – 22 February 1371) was King of Scots from 1329 until his death in 1371. The only surviving son of Robert the Bruce, he succeeded his father at the age of five and was the first Scottish king anointed at his coronation. His long reign was marked by war with England, years of exile in France, and more than a decade spent as a prisoner of the English.

Early reign and exile

David was crowned at Scone in November 1331, with his wife Joan of the Tower, sister of Edward III of England, whom he had married in 1328 as part of the Treaty of Edinburgh–Northampton. After Edward III renewed English claims and supported Edward Balliol, the Scots were defeated at Halidon Hill in 1333, and the young king was sent for safety to France, where he was received by Philip VI at Château Gaillard in Normandy.

Return and capture

David returned to Scotland in 1341 and took personal control of government. In 1346, supporting France during the Hundred Years' War, he invaded northern England but was defeated and captured at the Battle of Neville's Cross near Durham. He was held prisoner in England for eleven years, mostly in London and at Odiham Castle in Hampshire.

Release and later reign

David was released in 1357 under the Treaty of Berwick, in exchange for an enormous ransom of 100,000 marks, payable in instalments. The financial burden shaped the rest of his reign. He quarrelled with the Scottish Parliament over proposals that would have altered the succession in England's favour, and over his second marriage to Margaret Drummond, whom he later divorced. His reign saw efforts to strengthen royal administration and revenue.

David died unexpectedly at Edinburgh Castle in February 1371. As he left no children, the crown passed to his nephew Robert Stewart, who became Robert II, the first Stewart king of Scots and the last of the Bruce line's direct successors.
