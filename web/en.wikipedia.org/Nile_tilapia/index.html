The Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) is a species of tilapia, a cichlid fish native to parts of Africa and the Levant, particularly the Nile Basin. It is one of the most widely farmed fish in the world and a globally important food fish in aquaculture. Because of its hardiness, fast growth, and tolerance of a wide range of environmental conditions, the species has been introduced throughout the tropics and subtropics, where it has often become an invasive species that competes with native fishes.

Characteristics

The Nile tilapia is a deep-bodied fish with a silvery-grey coloration and faint vertical banding. Breeding males develop a reddish flush on the head and fins. Adults commonly reach 40–60 cm in length and can weigh several kilograms, although farmed fish are usually harvested much smaller. The species is primarily herbivorous to omnivorous, feeding on phytoplankton, algae, aquatic plants, and detritus, which makes it well suited to low-input pond culture.

Reproduction

Nile tilapia are maternal mouthbrooders: after spawning in a nest excavated by the male, the female carries the fertilised eggs in her mouth until hatching, and continues to shelter the fry for some time afterward. Under warm conditions the fish breeds repeatedly throughout the year, beginning at a relatively small size — a trait that can cause overpopulation and stunting in unmanaged ponds, but which also underlies its productivity in aquaculture.

Aquaculture

The Nile tilapia ranks among the world's most important farmed finfish, with production concentrated in China, Egypt, Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand, and Brazil. Egyptian fish farmers raised tilapia in ponds as early as ancient times, and the species appears in Egyptian tomb paintings. Modern production uses monosex (all-male) culture, achieved through manual sexing, hybridisation, or hormonal sex reversal, to prevent uncontrolled reproduction. Tilapia is marketed as a mild-flavoured white fish and is sometimes sold as "aquatic chicken" because of its ease of culture.

As an invasive species

Introductions for aquaculture and mosquito or weed control have established feral populations in North and South America, Asia, and Australia. Escapees can dominate local fish communities, disturb aquatic vegetation, and hybridise with native tilapia species, leading several countries to regulate or ban further introductions.
