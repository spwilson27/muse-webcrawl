Pan and scan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pan and scan is a method of adjusting a widescreen film image to fit the proportions of a standard-definition 4:3 television screen, by cropping the sides of the original image and selecting — "panning" across — the most important area of each shot. It was the dominant presentation format for feature films on broadcast television and VHS in the era before widescreen televisions became common.

Technique

In a pan-and-scan transfer, a technician or editor reframes each shot, sometimes adding artificial pans, tilts, zooms, or cuts between speakers to keep the action within the narrower frame. Up to half of the original image can be lost in transfers from wide formats such as CinemaScope (2.39:1), which often noticeably damaged compositions — characters at opposite edges of the frame might disappear entirely, and simultaneous action had to be shown sequentially.

Criticism and decline

Many directors and critics objected to pan and scan as a mutilation of their work; Sidney Lumet, Martin Scorsese and others campaigned for letterboxing, in which the whole width of the image is preserved with black bars above and below. The growth of 16:9 televisions, DVD and Blu-ray releases offering both formats, and streaming has made pan and scan largely obsolete, though cropping controversies continue with vertical video and reframings of older television programmes.

A related technique, "open matte", shoots with extra headroom so a film can be shown in more than one aspect ratio; "tilt and scan" is an analogous reframing for high-definition sources shown in wider formats.
