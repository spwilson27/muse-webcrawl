Infiltration (hydrology)

Infiltration is the process by which water on the ground surface enters the soil. It is commonly used in both hydrology and soil science. The infiltration capacity is the maximum rate at which water can enter the soil in a given condition; the actual infiltration rate falls from this initial capacity toward a steady value as the soil wets up. Water that infiltrates may be stored as soil moisture, taken up by plants, or percolate downward to recharge groundwater; precipitation that exceeds the infiltration capacity becomes surface runoff, contributing to flooding and erosion.

Factors and measurement

Infiltration depends on soil texture and structure (sands admit water far faster than clays), antecedent moisture, vegetation and litter cover, surface crusting and slope, and land use — compaction and impervious urban surfaces greatly reduce it. It is measured with ring infiltrometers and rainfall simulators. Models of infiltration include Horton's equation (an empirical exponential decay of capacity), the Green–Ampt model (based on a sharp wetting front and soil suction), and the Philip and Richards equations derived from soil physics; the SCS curve-number method estimates infiltration implicitly for stormwater and flood prediction.
