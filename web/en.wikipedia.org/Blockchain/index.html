Blockchain

A blockchain is a distributed ledger with growing lists of records (blocks) that are securely linked together via cryptographic hashes. Each block contains a cryptographic hash of the previous block, a timestamp, and transaction data. Since each block contains information about the previous block, they effectively form a chain, with each additional block linking to the ones before it. Consequently, blockchain transactions are irreversible in that, once they are recorded, the data in any given block cannot be altered retroactively without altering all subsequent blocks.

History

Cryptographer David Chaum first proposed a blockchain-like protocol in his 1982 dissertation. The first decentralized blockchain was conceptualized in 2008 by Satoshi Nakamoto, who implemented it in 2009 as the public ledger for the cryptocurrency bitcoin. The invention solved the double-spending problem without a central authority. Ethereum, launched in 2015, extended the concept with programmable smart contracts.

Structure and consensus

Blockchains are managed by peer-to-peer networks in which nodes validate new blocks according to a consensus mechanism, such as proof of work (mining) or proof of stake. Altering recorded data would require collusion of the network majority, which grows prohibitively expensive on large public chains.

Types and uses

Blockchains may be public (permissionless), private, consortium, or hybrid. Beyond cryptocurrencies, proposed and actual applications include supply-chain tracking, trade finance, digital identity, land registries, and non-fungible tokens (NFTs). Critics point to energy consumption in proof-of-work systems, scalability limits, and regulatory uncertainty.
