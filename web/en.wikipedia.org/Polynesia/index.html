Polynesia is a subregion of Oceania made up of more than 1,000 islands scattered over the central and southern Pacific Ocean. The term was first used in 1756 by the French writer Charles de Brosses. Geographically, Polynesia is defined by the Polynesian Triangle, whose corners are the Hawaiian Islands in the north, Easter Island (Rapa Nui) in the southeast, and New Zealand (Aotearoa) in the southwest.

History

Polynesia was settled by Austronesian voyagers — heirs of the Lapita culture — who navigated by stars, swell patterns, and bird flight in double-hulled voyaging canoes. Samoa and Tonga were settled by about 900 BC; after a long pause, voyagers pushed east to the Cook Islands, Tahiti, and the Marquesas, reaching Hawaii and Easter Island by around AD 1000–1200 and New Zealand by about AD 1250–1300, making it one of the last major landmasses settled by humans.

European contact began in the 16th–18th centuries, and in the 19th century missionaries — among them those of the London Missionary Society — spread Christianity across the islands. Most Polynesian territories were later colonised by Britain, France, Germany, and the United States; today the region includes independent states such as Samoa, Tonga, Tuvalu, and New Zealand, alongside territories including French Polynesia, American Samoa, and Hawaii.

Culture

Polynesian cultures share related languages, seafaring traditions, tattooing, tapa cloth, and chiefly social organisation. Concepts such as mana and tapu (the origin of the word "taboo") are widespread. A modern revival of traditional voyaging, led by canoes such as Hōkūleʻa, has reconnected the region since the 1970s.
