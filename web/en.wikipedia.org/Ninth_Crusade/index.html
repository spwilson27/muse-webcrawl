The Ninth Crusade, sometimes grouped with the Eighth Crusade, was the last major crusade to the Holy Land of the medieval period. It was led by the Lord Edward, the future King Edward I of England, who sailed to Acre in 1271 after the failure of Louis IX of France's expedition against Tunis. Edward's small force achieved some local successes against the Mamluk sultan Baibars but lacked the strength to recover Jerusalem, and the crusade ended with a ten-year truce in 1272.

Background

By 1270 the Crusader states had been reduced to a strip of coastal cities. The Mamluk sultan Baibars had captured Antioch (1268) and the great Hospitaller fortress of Krak des Chevaliers fell in April 1271. Edward had taken the cross in 1268 but was delayed by English politics; he arrived at Acre on 9 May 1271 with roughly a thousand men, including some 225 knights.

Campaign

Edward raided the territory around St Georges-de-Lebeyne, attempted a joint campaign with the Mongol Ilkhanate of Persia — a Mongol force under Samagar raided northern Syria in October 1271 — and repelled Baibars's attack on Qaqun. He also strengthened the defences of Acre and intervened in the politics of the Kingdom of Jerusalem, mediating between Hugh III of Cyprus and the military orders. In June 1272 an attempt was made on Edward's life by an assassin, whom Edward killed despite being wounded with a (possibly poisoned) dagger.

Aftermath

Recognising that no major reinforcement would come from Europe, Edward concluded the Truce of Caesarea with Baibars in May 1272, securing the remaining Crusader coast for ten years, and departed for England, where he learned of his father's death and his own accession. The truce expired in the 1280s; Acre, the last Crusader stronghold, fell to the Mamluks in 1291, ending the Crusader presence in the Levant.
