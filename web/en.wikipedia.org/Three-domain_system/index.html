Three-domain system

Proposed byCarl Woese, Otto Kandler, Mark Wheelis
Year1990
DomainsBacteria, Archaea, Eukarya
Basis16S ribosomal RNA sequences

The three-domain system is a biological classification introduced by Carl Woese, Otto Kandler and Mark Wheelis in 1990 that divides cellular life into three domains: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya. It superseded earlier two-empire schemes (prokaryotes versus eukaryotes) by recognising that the archaea, though prokaryotic in cell structure, are evolutionarily distinct from bacteria and in many molecular respects closer to eukaryotes.

Basis

Woese's comparison of 16S ribosomal RNA sequences, begun in the 1970s, revealed that methanogens and other unusual microbes formed a lineage as distant from ordinary bacteria as bacteria are from eukaryotes — overturning the assumption that all prokaryotes form one primary group. In the three-domain tree, the domain Bacteria diverged first, while Archaea and Eukarya share a more recent common ancestor.

Debate

The system has been challenged by the "two-domain" or eocyte hypothesis, in which eukaryotes emerge from within the archaeal Asgard group, reducing the primary divisions to Bacteria and Archaea. The discovery of Asgard archaea has strengthened this view for many researchers, but the three-domain system remains widely taught and used as a working framework.
