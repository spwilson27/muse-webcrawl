Whaling in Japan

Coastal whaling has a long history in towns such as Taiji

Whaling in Japan has been practised since at least the twelfth century, with organised coastal whaling developing in the seventeenth century. In the twentieth century Japan became one of the world's largest whaling nations, operating factory fleets in the Antarctic and North Pacific. Whale meat became an important source of protein in post-war Japan, though consumption has declined sharply in recent decades.

History

Traditional hand-harpoon whaling around the Japanese coast dates to the Jōmon period and earlier, but large-scale commercial net whaling began in the 1670s in Wakayama Prefecture. Modern Norwegian-style whaling with steam catchers and explosive harpoons was adopted in the Meiji era, and from the 1930s Japanese fleets ranged into the Antarctic, taking blue, fin and sperm whales in large numbers.

After the Second World War, the Allied occupation authorities encouraged whaling to relieve food shortages, and whale meat supplied a substantial share of Japan's animal protein into the 1960s, featuring prominently in school lunches.

Moratorium and scientific whaling

Japan initially objected to the International Whaling Commission's 1982 decision for a moratorium on commercial whaling (effective 1986) but withdrew the objection under United States pressure. From 1987 Japan conducted whaling under Article VIII of the International Convention for the Regulation of Whaling, which permits lethal sampling for scientific research (the JARPA and JARPN programmes). Critics, including the governments of Australia and New Zealand and conservation organisations, maintained that these programmes were commercial whaling in disguise.

In 2014 the International Court of Justice ruled that JARPA II did not comply with Article VIII and ordered Japan to revoke its permits. Japan redesigned its Antarctic programme (NEWREP-A) and continued scientific whaling in the North Pacific.

Withdrawal from the IWC

In December 2018 Japan announced its withdrawal from the IWC, effective 30 June 2019, and resumed commercial whaling within its territorial waters and exclusive economic zone, ending Antarctic whaling. Catches — principally minke, Bryde's and sei whales, with fin whales added to the catch list in 2024 — are set according to quotas calculated by the IWC's Revised Management Procedure. The industry remains small and subsidised, with a handful of coastal communities such as Taiji, Ayukawa, Wada and Abashiri involved, while domestic demand for whale meat is a fraction of its 1960s peak.
