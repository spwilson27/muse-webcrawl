Lake Ohrid is a lake that straddles the mountainous border between the southwestern part of North Macedonia and eastern Albania. It is one of Europe's deepest and oldest lakes, with a unique aquatic ecosystem of worldwide importance, hosting more than 200 endemic species. The ancient lake is estimated to be two to three million years old.

Geography and hydrology

The lake covers 358 km² and reaches a maximum depth of 288 metres. Unusually, it is fed mainly by underwater springs and by inflow from higher Lake Prespa through karst channels; it drains northward via the Black Drin River to the Adriatic Sea. With a water retention time of around 70 years and very low nutrient input, the lake is exceptionally clear and oligotrophic.

Biodiversity

Often called a "museum of living fossils", Lake Ohrid preserves relict species from the pre-glacial fauna of the Balkans, including endemic snails, sponges, and the Ohrid trout (Salmo letnica). Endemism reaches over 200 species and is especially high among gastropods, of which the great majority are found nowhere else. Scientific drilling projects have used its sediments to reconstruct Balkan climate history.

Human history

The town of Ohrid, once known as Lychnidos, was a centre of the first Slavic literacy: Saints Clement and Naum, disciples of Cyril and Methodius, worked here in the 9th and 10th centuries. The lake, town, and surrounding region were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site — "Natural and Cultural Heritage of the Ohrid region" — in 1979–1980.
