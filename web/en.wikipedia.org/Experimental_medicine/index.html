Experimental medicine

Experimental methods underpin modern biomedical research.

Experimental medicine is the use of controlled investigation—especially experiments on living systems, tissues, and animal models—to understand the mechanisms of disease and to develop treatments. It stands in contrast to purely observational clinical practice, applying the methods of the laboratory to questions of human health.

History

The field took shape in the 19th century with physiologists such as Claude Bernard, whose Introduction to the Study of Experimental Medicine (1865) argued that medical knowledge should rest on controlled experiment rather than clinical impression alone. Bernard's work on the liver, curare, and the internal environment (milieu intérieur) established the template for laboratory medicine. In the 20th century, experimental medicine expanded with the growth of pharmacology, immunology, and molecular biology.

Methods

Experimental medicine employs in vitro studies (on cells and tissues outside the body), in vivo studies in animal models, and controlled human studies including clinical trials. Randomization, blinding, and replication are used to isolate causal effects. Modern techniques include gene editing, imaging, and high-throughput screening of candidate compounds.

Ethics

Because experiments may impose risks, the field is governed by ethical frameworks such as the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional review boards, which require informed consent, minimization of harm, and justification of animal use under the principles of replacement, reduction, and refinement.
