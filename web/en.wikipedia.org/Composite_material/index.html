Composite material

Composite material
ConstituentsMatrix (binder) + reinforcement (fibres or particles)
ExamplesConcrete, plywood, fiberglass, carbon-fibre-reinforced polymer
AdvantagesHigh strength-to-weight ratio, corrosion resistance, design flexibility

A composite material (also called a composition material or shortened to composite) is a material which is produced from two or more constituent materials. These constituent materials have notably dissimilar chemical or physical properties and are merged to create a material with properties unlike the individual elements; within the finished structure, the constituents remain separate and distinct.

Structure

Most composites consist of a bulk phase called the matrix and a dispersed phase called the reinforcement. The matrix — commonly a polymer resin, metal, or ceramic — binds and protects the reinforcement and transfers loads to it. Reinforcements include glass, carbon, and aramid fibres; particles; and whiskers, arranged to maximize stiffness and strength in the directions of expected load.

History

Humans have used composites for millennia: straw-reinforced mud bricks, Mongol composite bows, and wattle and daub. Concrete — aggregate in a cement matrix — dates to Roman times. Modern polymer composites began with Bakelite and fiberglass in the early 20th century; carbon-fibre composites were developed in the 1960s and transformed aerospace design.

Types and applications

Fibre-reinforced polymers include fiberglass (glass fibres in polyester or epoxy) and carbon-fibre-reinforced polymer (CFRP), used in aircraft structures such as the Boeing 787 fuselage, wind turbine blades, boats, and sporting goods. Metal matrix composites and ceramic matrix composites serve in engines and brakes. Engineered woods (plywood, oriented strand board), reinforced concrete, and asphalt are everyday composites. Sandwich panels — thin strong skins over a light honeycomb or foam core — offer exceptional bending stiffness per unit weight.

Advantages and limitations

Composites offer high specific strength and stiffness, fatigue and corrosion resistance, and the ability to tailor properties by fibre orientation. Drawbacks include high cost, difficult recycling, anisotropic behavior, and susceptibility to delamination and hidden impact damage.
