Jedediah Smith

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jedediah Strong Smith (January 6, 1799 – May 27, 1831) was an American clerk, transcontinental pioneer, frontiersman, hunter, trapper, author, cartographer, and explorer of the Rocky Mountains, the western United States, and the American Southwest during the early 19th century. After 75 years of obscurity following his death, Smith was rediscovered as the American whose explorations led to the use of the 20-mile (32 km)-wide South Pass as the dominant point of crossing the Continental Divide for pioneers on the trails westward.

Early life

Smith was born in Jericho (now Bainbridge), New York, into a family of modest means that later moved west to Pennsylvania and Ohio. In 1822 he answered William H. Ashley's famous advertisement for "enterprising young men" to ascend the Missouri River and join the fur trade, and travelled to St. Louis.

Explorations

With the Ashley-Henry fur company, Smith survived a famous mauling by a grizzly bear in 1823 that scarred his face and cost him part of an ear. In 1824 his party rediscovered South Pass in present-day Wyoming, the key gateway across the Rockies later used by the Oregon, California, and Mormon trails. In 1826–1827 Smith led the first overland journey by a U.S. citizen from the United States into California, crossing the Mojave Desert; on the return in 1827 he made the first crossing of the Sierra Nevada and the Great Basin by a non-Native American. A second California expedition ended in disaster in 1828 when most of his party was killed on the Umpqua River in Oregon; Smith was among the few survivors.

Death and legacy

In May 1831, while scouting for water on a trading expedition along the Santa Fe Trail near the Cimarron River in present-day Kansas, Smith was killed by Comanche warriors; his body was never recovered. His maps and journals, published posthumously in part, contributed greatly to geographic knowledge of the American West, and he is remembered as one of the most accomplished of the mountain men.
