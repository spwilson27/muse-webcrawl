Reliabilism

Reliabilism locates justification in the reliability of the belief-forming process.

Reliabilism is a category of theories in epistemology that analyse knowledge or justification in terms of the reliability of the belief-forming process. In its best-known form, process reliabilism associated with Alvin Goldman, a belief is justified if and only if it is produced by a cognitive process that reliably produces true beliefs — such as perception under normal conditions — and knowledge is (roughly) true belief produced by a reliable process.

Background

Reliabilism rose to prominence after Goldman's "What Is Justified Belief?" (1979) as an externalist alternative to internalist theories: the believer need not have access to, or even be aware of, the grounds that justify the belief. This neatly handles cases of unsophisticated knowers — children and animals — and of expert intuition. It was also offered as a response to Gettier cases, in which justified true belief falls short of knowledge through luck: reliabilists argue the process in such cases is not suitably reliable in the circumstances.

Objections

The generality problem asks how the relevant process type is to be individuated, since any token process instantiates many types of differing reliability. Laurence BonJour's clairvoyance cases attack externalism itself: a subject whose reliable clairvoyance gives them true beliefs they have every reason to distrust, BonJour argued, is not justified. Other problems include the "new evil demon" thought experiment and specifying reliability for unusual environments. Replies include virtue reliabilism (Ernest Sosa, John Greco), which ties justification to the competence of the agent's cognitive faculties.
