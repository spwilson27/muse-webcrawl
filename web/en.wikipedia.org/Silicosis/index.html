Silicosis is a form of occupational lung disease caused by inhalation of crystalline silicon dioxide dust, commonly known as silica. It is a type of pneumoconiosis characterized by inflammation and progressive fibrosis (scarring) of the lungs, which impairs gas exchange and cannot be reversed, though its progression can be slowed if exposure ends.

Forms

Chronic silicosis develops after 10 or more years of exposure to relatively low dust levels. Accelerated silicosis develops within 5–10 years of higher exposure, and acute silicosis — a rapidly fatal form resembling a proteinosis of the airspaces — can arise within months of very heavy exposure, as seen historically among sandblasters and, more recently, workers fabricating engineered-stone countertops.

Symptoms and complications

Early silicosis may cause no symptoms. As fibrosis advances, patients develop a dry cough, breathlessness, chest pain, and fatigue. Silicosis increases susceptibility to tuberculosis, lung cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis and systemic sclerosis.

History

The dangers of silica dust were noted as early as Agricola in the 16th century. Notorious episodes include the Hawks Nest Tunnel disaster in West Virginia (1930–31), in which hundreds of workers died after drilling through high-silica rock with minimal protection.

Prevention and treatment

There is no cure; management focuses on removing the worker from exposure, treating complications, and supportive care. Prevention through wet cutting, exhaust ventilation, dust monitoring, and respiratory protection is regulated in many countries, yet silicosis remains a major global occupational disease burden.
