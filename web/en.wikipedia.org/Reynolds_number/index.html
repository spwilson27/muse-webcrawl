Reynolds number

In fluid dynamics, the Reynolds number (Re) is a dimensionless quantity that helps predict fluid flow patterns in different situations by measuring the ratio between inertial and viscous forces. At low Reynolds numbers, flows tend to be dominated by laminar (sheet-like) flow, while at high Reynolds numbers, flows tend to be turbulent. The turbulence results from differences in the fluid's speed and direction, which may sometimes intersect or even move counter to the overall direction of the flow (eddy currents).

Definition

The Reynolds number is defined as Re = ρuL/μ = uL/ν, where ρ is fluid density, u a characteristic velocity, L a characteristic length (for pipe flow, the diameter), μ the dynamic viscosity, and ν the kinematic viscosity. The concept was introduced by George Stokes in 1851 but is named after Osborne Reynolds, whose 1883 paper and dye-injection experiments in pipe flow established its importance.

Flow regimes

For flow in a pipe, Reynolds numbers below about 2,300 indicate laminar flow and above about 2,900 fully turbulent flow, with a transitional range between. Because flows with the same Reynolds number are dynamically similar, engineers use the number to scale wind-tunnel and towing-tank model tests to full-size aircraft and ships. In biology, micro-organisms live at very low Reynolds numbers, where viscosity dominates and reciprocal swimming motions cannot produce net motion.

Applications

The Reynolds number appears throughout fluid mechanics: in drag correlations, heat-transfer coefficients (via the Nusselt number and the Chilton–Colburn analogy), boundary-layer theory, and weather and ocean modelling. Analogous dimensionless groups include the Mach, Froude, and Prandtl numbers.
