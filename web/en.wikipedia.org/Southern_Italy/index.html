Southern Italy

Southern Italy (Italian: Italia meridionale), also known as the Mezzogiorno (literally "midday"), is a macroregion of Italy consisting of its southern half without the insular regions of Sicily and Sardinia, which are counted separately in Italian statistics. It comprises the regions of Abruzzo, Molise, Campania, Apulia, Basilicata, and Calabria. Naples is its largest city.

History

The south was colonized by the Greeks from the eighth century BC, forming Magna Graecia, and later became part of the Roman world, the Byzantine Empire, and Arab and Norman states. From the twelfth century most of the mainland south was united in the Kingdom of Sicily and then the Kingdom of Naples, ruled for centuries by French and Spanish dynasties before passing to the Bourbons; it joined the unified Kingdom of Italy in 1861.

Economy and the "Southern Question"

Since unification, the Mezzogiorno has lagged behind the industrial north in income, employment, and infrastructure — a persistent disparity debated as the Questione Meridionale ("Southern Question"). Postwar development funds such as the Cassa per il Mezzogiorno narrowed but never closed the gap. The region's strengths include agriculture (olive oil, wine, citrus, durum wheat), tourism along the Amalfi Coast and in Puglia, and growing technology sectors, while organized crime groups such as the Camorra, 'Ndrangheta, and Sacra Corona Unita remain serious social problems.
