Mexican drug war

Date: 2006 – present
Location: Mexico
Parties: Mexican government vs. drug cartels (Sinaloa, CJNG, Gulf, Zetas and successors)

The Mexican drug war is an ongoing asymmetric conflict between the Mexican government and rival drug trafficking cartels competing to supply the United States market. Although cartel violence predates it, the conflict is generally dated from December 2006, when President Felipe Calderón deployed the military to Michoacán against the cartels.

Background

Mexican trafficking organizations rose to dominance after Colombian cartels weakened in the 1990s and cocaine routes shifted through Mexico. Major organizations have included the Sinaloa Cartel (long led by Joaquín "El Chapo" Guzmán, convicted in the US in 2019), the Gulf Cartel and its former enforcers Los Zetas, the Tijuana and Juárez cartels, and the Jalisco New Generation Cartel (CJNG).

Human cost

The war and related organized-crime violence have killed well over 300,000 people since 2006 by common estimates, with more than 100,000 people officially registered as disappeared. Notorious episodes include the 2014 Iguala mass kidnapping of 43 Ayotzinapa students. The militarized "kingpin strategy" fragmented cartels into more violent splinter groups, while corruption and US demand for drugs and supply of firearms have sustained the conflict. Successive administrations — under Enrique Peña Nieto and Andrés Manuel López Obrador ("hugs, not bullets") — have struggled to reduce homicide rates, which remain near historic highs.
