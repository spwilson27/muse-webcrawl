The California Current is a cold Pacific Ocean current that moves southward along the western coast of North America, beginning off southern British Columbia and ending off southern Baja California Sur. It is considered an eastern boundary current and is part of the North Pacific Gyre, driven by the North Pacific High.

Physical characteristics

The current carries cold, relatively low-salinity subarctic water equatorward over a width of up to 1,000 km, at typical speeds of around 25 cm/s. In spring and summer, northerly winds drive surface water offshore through Ekman transport, drawing cold, nutrient-rich deep water to the surface in a process called coastal upwelling. A seasonal inshore countercurrent, the Davidson Current, flows northward in winter.

Ecosystem

Upwelling fertilises sunlit surface waters, supporting rich phytoplankton growth and one of the world's most productive marine ecosystems — the California Current Large Marine Ecosystem. It sustains major fisheries of sardine, anchovy, hake and squid, vast kelp forests, and abundant seabirds, seals, sea lions and whales. The system is, however, sensitive to climate variability: El Niño events warm the water, suppress upwelling and have periodically caused collapses in fish stocks and starvation among seabirds and marine mammals.

Climate influence

The cold current chills the air above it, contributing to the cool summers of coastal California and to its famous advection fog, especially around San Francisco. Together with upwelling, it helps create the Mediterranean and semi-arid coastal climates of the region.
