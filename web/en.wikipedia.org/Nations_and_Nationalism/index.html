Nations and Nationalism is a 1983 book by the philosopher and anthropologist Ernest Gellner, regarded as one of the most influential theoretical works on the origins of nationalism. In it, Gellner argues that nationalism is a distinctly modern phenomenon, produced by the transition from agrarian to industrial society.

Argument

Gellner defines nationalism as the political principle that the political and national unit should be congruent. Agrarian societies, organised into local, self-sufficient communities beneath a ruling elite with its own high culture, had no need for cultural homogeneity. Industrial society, by contrast, requires a mobile, literate workforce able to communicate in a shared, standardised medium. This need is met by mass education in a common "high culture," and nationalism is the ideology that demands each such culture have its own state. Hence Gellner's famous inversion: nationalism does not awaken pre-existing nations; rather, "nationalism invents nations where they do not exist."

Influence

The book, together with Benedict Anderson's Imagined Communities (published the same year), anchored the "modernist" school of nationalism studies, against primordialist and ethnosymbolist critics such as Anthony D. Smith, Gellner's former student. A second edition with a new introduction by John Breuilly appeared in 2006.
