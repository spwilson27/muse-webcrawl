Chemical kinetics

Chemical kinetics, also known as reaction kinetics, is the branch of physical chemistry that is concerned with understanding the rates of chemical reactions. It is to be contrasted with chemical thermodynamics, which deals with the direction in which a reaction occurs but says nothing about its rate.

Rate laws and reaction order

The rate of a reaction is described by a rate law relating the rate to the concentrations of reactants raised to experimentally determined powers, the reaction orders. For a reaction aA + bB → products, rate = k[A]m[B]n, where k is the rate constant. Reactions may be zero-, first- or second-order overall. First-order processes, such as radioactive decay and many drug eliminations, have a constant half-life independent of concentration.

Temperature dependence

The Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius proposed that the rate constant depends exponentially on temperature, k = A·e−Ea/RT, where Ea is the activation energy and A the pre-exponential (frequency) factor. Later frameworks — collision theory and transition state theory — interpret these quantities in terms of molecular encounters and the activated complex at the top of the energy barrier.

Factors affecting reaction rates

Reaction rates depend on the nature of the reactants, concentration, temperature, physical state and surface area, and the presence of a catalyst, which speeds a reaction by providing an alternative pathway of lower activation energy without being consumed. Kinetics is essential in chemical engineering, atmospheric chemistry, pharmacology and enzyme studies, where the Michaelis–Menten equation describes saturable enzyme-catalysed rates.
