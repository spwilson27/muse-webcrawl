Deconstruction (architecture)

Deconstructivist buildings feature fragmentation, skewed geometry, and apparent instability.

Deconstruction in architecture, commonly associated with the movement known as deconstructivism, is a development of postmodern architecture that began in the late 1980s. It is characterised by fragmentation, an interest in manipulating a structure's surface or skin, non-rectilinear shapes which appear to distort and dislocate elements of architecture, and a rejection of the "pure form" of modernism. Its visual appearance is marked by unpredictability and controlled chaos.

Origins

The term gained currency after the Museum of Modern Art's 1988 exhibition Deconstructivist Architecture, curated by Philip Johnson and Mark Wigley, which presented work by Frank Gehry, Zaha Hadid, Daniel Libeskind, Rem Koolhaas, Peter Eisenman, Bernard Tschumi, and Coop Himmelb(l)au. The name drew partly on Jacques Derrida's philosophical concept of deconstruction and partly on Russian Constructivism, from which the movement borrowed diagonal, intersecting geometric forms.

Key works

Important deconstructivist works include the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao (1997) by Frank Gehry, the Jewish Museum Berlin (1999) by Daniel Libeskind, the Wexner Center for the Arts (1989) by Peter Eisenman, the Parc de la Villette in Paris by Bernard Tschumi, and Hadid's Vitra Fire Station (1994) and MAXXI museum in Rome. Critics have disputed whether deconstructivism is a coherent movement, noting its practitioners' divergent aims; its forms were greatly facilitated by computer-aided design.
