Postmodern architecture

Postmodern architecture reintroduced ornament, colour, and historical reference.

Postmodern architecture is a style or movement which emerged in the late 1960s as a reaction against the austerity, formality, and lack of variety of modern architecture, particularly the International Style advocateded by Le Corbusier and Mies van der Rohe. The movement was introduced by the architect and urban planner Denise Scott Brown and the architectural theorist Robert Venturi in their 1966 book Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture and their 1972 book Learning from Las Vegas.

Characteristics

Postmodern buildings combine elements of past styles, often in playful or ironic ways: broken pediments and oversized columns, bright colour, applied ornament, asymmetry, and references understood by both architects and the public ("double coding", in Charles Jencks's phrase). Venturi's Vanna Venturi House (1964) and Guild House (1963) were early exemplars; later landmarks include Philip Johnson and John Burgee's AT&T Building in New York (1984), with its Chippendale-style top, James Stirling's Neue Staatsgalerie in Stuttgart (1984), and Michael Graves's Portland Building (1982).

Development and legacy

Postmodernism flourished in the 1980s and early 1990s, encompassing related strands such as deconstructivism and neo-eclecticism. Jencks's The Language of Post-Modern Architecture (1977) popularised the term. By the late 1990s the movement's commercial dominance waned, though its emphasis on context, pluralism, and historical reference left a lasting mark, and a number of its once-derided buildings have since been listed for preservation.
