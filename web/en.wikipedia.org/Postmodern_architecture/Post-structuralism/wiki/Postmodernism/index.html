Postmodernism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Postmodern architecture rejected modernist purity in favour of ornament, irony, and historical reference.

Postmodernism is a broad movement that developed in the mid-to-late 20th century across philosophy, the arts, architecture, and criticism, characterised by scepticism toward the grand narratives and universal certainties of modernism. The term acquired much of its early public prominence through postmodern architecture, while its theoretical vocabulary was supplied largely by French post-structuralist philosophy — the thought of figures such as Jacques Derrida, Michel Foucault, Gilles Deleuze, and Jean-François Lyotard, whose 1979 book The Postmodern Condition famously defined the postmodern as "incredulity toward metanarratives".

Postmodernism

Emerged: 1960s–1970s

Domains: Architecture, philosophy, literature, art, critical theory

Key texts: Lyotard, The Postmodern Condition (1979); Venturi, Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture (1966)

Preceded by: Modernism

Postmodern architecture

In architecture, postmodernism arose as a reaction against the austerity, functionalism, and universalising ambitions of the International Style. Robert Venturi's Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture (1966) and Learning from Las Vegas (1972, with Denise Scott Brown and Steven Izenour) championed ornament, symbolism, ambiguity, and the vernacular commercial landscape — "less is a bore", Venturi replied to Mies van der Rohe's "less is more". Postmodern buildings quote historical styles, mix materials and scales, and use colour and irony: examples include Venturi's Vanna Venturi House, Philip Johnson's AT&T Building in New York with its "Chippendale" pediment, Michael Graves's Portland Building, and Charles Jencks's writings, which codified the movement. The style dominated corporate and civic architecture in the 1980s before reactions in favour of deconstructivism, high-tech, and a revived modernism set in.

Post-structuralist foundations

Philosophically, postmodernism drew on post-structuralism's critique of structuralism: Derrida's deconstruction argued that texts undermine their own binary oppositions and stable meanings; Foucault analysed how knowledge is bound up with power; Lyotard questioned the legitimating "grand narratives" of Enlightenment progress and Marxist emancipation. Common postmodern themes include the instability of meaning, the social construction of identity, fragmentation, pastiche, intertextuality, and suspicion of objective, context-free truth — ideas also expressed in postmodern literature (Pynchon, Borges, Calvino), art, and film.

Criticism

Critics — among them Jürgen Habermas, Noam Chomsky, and Alan Sokal (whose 1996 hoax paper dramatised the charge) — have accused postmodern thought of obscurantism, relativism, and abandoning the Enlightenment values needed to criticise power itself. Defenders respond that postmodernism usefully exposes hidden assumptions, gives voice to marginalised perspectives, and that in architecture it restored wit, ornament, and historical memory to the built environment; more recent cultural commentary speaks of a successor sensibility, sometimes called metamodernism.
