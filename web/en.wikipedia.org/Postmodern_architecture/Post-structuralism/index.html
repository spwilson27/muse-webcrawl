Post-structuralism

Post-structuralism emerged in France in the 1960s as a critique of structuralism.

Post-structuralism is a philosophical and literary movement that emerged in France in the 1960s. It is closely related to the writings of Jacques Derrida, Michel Foucault, Gilles Deleuze, Roland Barthes, and Jean-François Lyotard, among others. Post-structuralists generally hold that the structures proposed by structuralism — systems of signs, binary oppositions, the fixed relationship between signifier and signified — are not stable, and that meaning is produced by difference, context, and the play of language rather than grounded in transparent foundations.

Key ideas

Barthes's essay "The Death of the Author" (1967) displaced authority over a text's meaning from its creator to its readers. Derrida's Of Grammatology (1967) introduced deconstruction, analysing how texts undermine their own binary hierarchies and presuppose what they exclude. Foucault's genealogies treated knowledge as bound up with power, examining institutions such as prisons and clinics. Deleuze and Félix Guattari's Capitalism and Schizophrenia critiqued psychoanalysis and arborescent models of thought in favour of the rhizome.

Influence

Post-structuralism profoundly influenced literary theory, cultural studies, feminism, postcolonial studies, and — through Derrida's connections with architects such as Bernard Tschumi and Peter Eisenman — deconstructivist architecture. It has also been criticised for obscurity and relativism, charges prominent in debates such as the Sokal affair. Many of its figures resisted the label itself, and scholars debate whether it constitutes a unified movement at all.
