Modernism (architecture)

Modernist architecture emphasised function, new materials, and the rejection of ornament.

Modern architecture, also called modernist architecture, was an architectural movement and style that was prominent in the 20th century, between the earlier Art Deco and later postmodern movements. Based upon new and innovative technologies of construction (particularly steel, reinforced concrete, and glass), and on the idea that form should follow function, modernism embraced minimalism and rejected ornament and historical revivalism.

Origins and principles

The movement emerged in Europe and the United States in the first decades of the 20th century. Key figures included Le Corbusier, whose "Five Points of Architecture" (pilotis, free plan, free façade, ribbon windows, roof garden) were exemplified by the Villa Savoye (1931); Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, known for "less is more" and works such as the Barcelona Pavilion (1929) and the Seagram Building (1958); Walter Gropius, founder of the Bauhaus; and Frank Lloyd Wright in America. The 1932 MoMA exhibition codified the "International Style" of glass-and-steel boxes on pilotis with flat roofs.

Spread and criticism

After the Second World War modernism became the default style for corporate, governmental, and institutional building worldwide, and for mass housing. From the 1960s it faced mounting criticism for monotony, disregard of context, and the failures of some tower-block housing; Charles Jencks famously dated the "death" of modern architecture to the demolition of the Pruitt–Igoe housing project in St. Louis in 1972. Postmodernism, new urbanism, and later movements arose in reaction, although modernist principles continue to inform contemporary design and preservation campaigns.
