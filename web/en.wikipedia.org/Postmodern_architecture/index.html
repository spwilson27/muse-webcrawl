Postmodern architecture

Postmodern architecture is a style of building design that emerged in the late 1960s and flourished through the 1970s–1990s, reacting against the austerity, uniformity, and doctrinaire functionalism of modernist architecture and the International Style. It reintroduced ornament, historical reference, colour, symbolism, and wit — often mixing elements from different periods in an eclectic, sometimes ironic collage.

Origins

The movement's theoretical foundation was laid by Robert Venturi's Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture (1966), which attacked modernist simplicity ("Less is a bore," answering Mies van der Rohe's "Less is more"), and by Venturi, Denise Scott Brown, and Steven Izenour's Learning from Las Vegas (1972), which celebrated commercial vernacular design. Charles Jencks popularised the term and famously dated the "death of modern architecture" to the demolition of the Pruitt–Igoe housing project in St. Louis in 1972.

Characteristics and examples

  • Historical quotation: classical orders, pediments, and arches used freely and often playfully.
  • Ornament and colour: decoration restored as a legitimate carrier of meaning.
  • Contextualism: buildings respond to their surroundings rather than asserting universal form.
  • Double coding (Jencks): architecture that speaks simultaneously to professionals and to the general public.

Key buildings include Venturi's Vanna Venturi House (1964), Michael Graves's Portland Building (1982), Philip Johnson and John Burgee's AT&T (now Sony) Building in New York (1984) with its Chippendale top, James Stirling's Neue Staatsgalerie in Stuttgart (1984), and the work of Ricardo Bofill and Charles Moore (Piazza d'Italia, New Orleans, 1978). Postmodern ideas also shaped urban design, new urbanism, and — more controversially — the mass-produced commercial architecture of the late 20th century. By the 1990s the movement's influence waned in favour of deconstructivism, high-tech, and neomodern styles, though its critique of modernist orthodoxy remains widely accepted.
