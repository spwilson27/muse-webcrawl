The Colorado Plateau is a physiographic and desert region of the Intermontane Plateaus, roughly centred on the Four Corners region of the southwestern United States. It covers an area of about 337,000 km² in western Colorado, northwestern New Mexico, southern and eastern Utah, and northern Arizona. About 90% of the area is drained by the Colorado River and its main tributaries: the Green, San Juan, and Little Colorado rivers.

The plateau is notable for its largely undeformed, horizontal sedimentary rock layers, uplifted during the Laramide orogeny and later episodes, and then carved by rivers into spectacular canyons, mesas, buttes, and badlands. It contains the world's greatest concentration of exposed colourful sedimentary rock.

Geology

The region is underlain by Precambrian and Paleozoic basement capped by thick Mesozoic and Cenozoic strata. Uplift of roughly 1.5–3 km, mostly within the last 20 million years, allowed the Colorado River to incise the Grand Canyon and other deep gorges. Volcanic features, such as the San Francisco Peaks near Flagstaff, Arizona, punctuate the plateau's southern margin.

Protected areas and human history

The plateau hosts an exceptional concentration of U.S. national parks — including Grand Canyon, Zion, Bryce Canyon, Arches, Canyonlands, Capitol Reef, Mesa Verde, and Petrified Forest — and national monuments such as Grand Staircase–Escalante and Bears Ears. Ancestral Puebloan peoples built cliff dwellings and pueblos across the region; today the Navajo Nation, Hopi, Ute, and other tribal nations are centred on the plateau.

Climate and ecology

Most of the plateau is high desert, with pinyon–juniper woodland at mid-elevations, ponderosa pine and spruce–fir forest on high plateaus, and sagebrush and grassland in basins. The region holds significant deposits of uranium, coal, oil, and natural gas.
