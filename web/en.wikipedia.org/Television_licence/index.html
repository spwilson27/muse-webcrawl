Television licence

A television licence or broadcast receiving licence is a payment required in many countries to legally receive, or in some cases possess, a television broadcast. It is a form of hypothecated taxation used to fund public broadcasting, allowing public broadcasters to transmit programmes without, or with only supplemental, advertising. The first radio receiving licences were introduced in the early 20th century, and television licences followed with the spread of television after the Second World War.

United Kingdom

In the United Kingdom, a TV licence is required to watch or record live television broadcasts on any channel or service, and to use the BBC iPlayer catch-up service; it is not required for other on-demand services. The fee primarily funds the BBC. A separate radio licence was abolished in 1971. Historically, detection relied in part on TV detector vans. In recent years the licence fee has been frozen for periods by government, and in 2020 the free licence for most over-75s was ended, with exemptions retained for those receiving Pension Credit. The long-term future of the licence fee has been the subject of repeated government reviews, with the BBC's Royal Charter running to the end of 2027.

Other countries

Licence-based funding has been used across Europe and elsewhere, though several countries have abolished the separate fee in favour of general taxation or a household media levy:

  • Germany replaced its device-based fee with the Rundfunkbeitrag, a flat household levy, in 2013, funding ARD, ZDF and Deutschlandradio.
  • France abolished the contribution à l'audiovisuel public in 2022, funding public broadcasters from general taxation.
  • Japan funds NHK through receiving fees required of households with equipment capable of receiving its broadcasts.
  • Italy collects the canone RAI through electricity bills, a measure introduced in 2016 to reduce evasion.
  • Scandinavian countries such as Sweden, Norway and Finland replaced licence fees with public-service taxes collected through the income tax system.
  • Ireland retains a television licence, funding RTÉ and the Broadcasting Authority of Ireland.

Abolition and reform

The spread of streaming has weakened the link between television ownership and public-service viewing, prompting many states to shift from device-based licences to household levies or direct taxation. Some countries, including the Netherlands and much of Eastern Europe, abolished licence fees decades ago; others never adopted them, funding public media from general budgets.
