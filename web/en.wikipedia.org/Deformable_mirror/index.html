Deformable mirror

A deformable mirror (DM) is a mirror whose surface can be shaped in real time in order to control the wavefront of reflected light. Deformable mirrors are the central corrective element of adaptive optics systems, where they are used to compensate for optical aberrations — most famously the blurring of astronomical images caused by atmospheric turbulence, but also imperfections in optical instruments, the human eye, and high-power laser beams.

Principle of operation

A deformable mirror consists of a thin reflective facesheet supported by an array of actuators. By extending or retracting individual actuators, the surface can be bent into shapes that mirror-image the distortions measured by a wavefront sensor. A control computer updates the actuator commands hundreds or thousands of times per second, so the correction keeps pace with the rapidly changing atmosphere. The number of actuators ranges from a few dozen in simple systems to many thousands in the extreme adaptive optics instruments planned for extremely large telescopes.

Types

Several technologies are used. Segmented mirrors consist of individually piston- and tip/tilt-controlled rigid segments. Continuous facesheet mirrors bend a thin glass or silicon sheet with discrete piezoelectric, electrostrictive, or voice-coil actuators. Microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) deformable mirrors, fabricated with semiconductor techniques, offer thousands of actuators in a package only a few centimetres across and are widely used in laboratory and ophthalmic instruments. Bimorph mirrors use two bonded piezoelectric layers and are well suited to smooth, low-order corrections. Liquid and ferrofluid mirrors have also been investigated.

Applications

In astronomy, deformable mirrors enable ground-based telescopes such as the Keck, Gemini, and Very Large Telescope observatories to approach the diffraction limit in the near-infrared. In ophthalmology they are used in retinal imaging instruments and in vision research. Industrial and military applications include laser beam shaping, free-space optical communications, and microscopy, where adaptive optics corrects specimen-induced aberrations.
