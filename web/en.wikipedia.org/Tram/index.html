A tram (called a streetcar or trolley in North America) is a rail vehicle that travels on tramway tracks on public urban streets, sometimes with sections of track separated from other traffic. Trams are usually lighter and shorter than mainline and rapid transit trains, and most are electrically powered from overhead lines.

History

The first passenger horse trams ran in the early 19th century, including the Swansea and Mumbles Railway (1807). Cable cars and steam trams followed, but electric traction — pioneered at scale by Frank Sprague in Richmond, Virginia, in 1888 — made trams the dominant urban transit mode by 1900.

Decline and revival

Many systems, especially in North America, Britain, and France, were dismantled between the 1930s and 1960s as buses and private cars took over; controversy surrounds the role of automobile interests in the United States. From the 1970s, congestion, pollution, and energy concerns drove a worldwide tram renaissance, often in the form of light rail with low-floor vehicles.

Characteristics

Modern trams are frequently articulated and low-floor for accessibility, run on standard or metre gauge track, and can share streets or use reserved alignments. Melbourne operates the world's largest tram network; extensive historic networks survive across Europe, and cities such as Hong Kong and Istanbul run distinctive double-deck or heritage fleets.
