The El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is an irregular, periodic variation in winds and sea surface temperatures over the tropical eastern Pacific Ocean, affecting the climate of much of the tropics and subtropics. It is the most significant source of year-to-year climate variability on Earth. The warming phase of the sea temperature is known as El Niño and the cooling phase as La Niña; the Southern Oscillation is the accompanying atmospheric seesaw in surface pressure between the eastern and western tropical Pacific, first described by Sir Gilbert Walker in the 1920s.

Mechanism

Under neutral conditions, easterly trade winds pile warm surface water into the western Pacific warm pool while cold water upwells off South America, sustaining the zonal temperature gradient and the atmospheric Walker circulation. In an El Niño, the trades slacken, warm water sloshes eastward as downwelling Kelvin waves, upwelling is suppressed, convection and rainfall shift east toward the central and eastern Pacific, and the pressure gradient weakens — a positive feedback described by Jacob Bjerknes. La Niña represents an intensification of the normal state. The cycle is phase-locked to the annual cycle, with events typically peaking in November to January.

Global impacts

Through atmospheric teleconnections, ENSO alters rainfall, temperature, tropical cyclone activity and jet-stream patterns on every continent. El Niño tends to raise global mean temperatures temporarily, while La Niña lowers them. Forecasting centres monitor indices such as sea-surface temperature in the Niño 3.4 region and issue seasonal outlooks used in agriculture, water management and disaster preparedness worldwide.
