Charles Laughton


Charles Laughton (1899–1962), English-American actor and director.

Charles Laughton (1 July 1899 – 15 December 1962) was a British-American actor. He was trained in London at the Royal Academy of Dramatic Art and first appeared professionally on the stage in 1926. In 1927 he was cast in a play with his future wife, Elsa Lanchester. Laughton's film performances included Captain Bligh in Mutiny on the Bounty (1935) and Quasimodo in The Hunchback of Notre Dame (1939), and he won the Academy Award for Best Actor for The Private Life of Henry VIII (1933).

Career

After First World War service, Laughton worked in his family's hotel business before turning to acting. Success at the Old Vic and in the West End led to Broadway and then Hollywood. His 1930s roles spanned comedy, villainy and pathos: Dr. Moreau in Island of Lost Souls, the tyrannical father in The Barretts of Wimpole Street, the artist in Rembrandt (1936) and the title role in I, Claudius (unfinished). He became a United States citizen in 1950.

Directing

Laughton's only film as director, The Night of the Hunter (1955), a Southern Gothic thriller starring Robert Mitchum, was a commercial failure on release but is now consistently ranked among the greatest films ever made. He also directed stage productions, notably The Caine Mutiny Court-Martial and readings of Shaw's Don Juan in Hell.

Later work and death

His later screen roles included the wily senator in Advise & Consent, the Roman Gracchus in Spartacus (1960) and Sir Wilfrid Robarts in Witness for the Prosecution (1957), which earned him an Oscar nomination. Laughton died of renal cancer in Hollywood in 1962.
