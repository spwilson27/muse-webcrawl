Grammatical gender

In linguistics, a grammatical gender system is a specific form of a noun class system, where nouns are assigned to gender categories that are often not related to the real-world qualities of the entities denoted. More narrowly, languages with grammatical gender classify nouns into categories such as masculine, feminine, and neuter, and the gender of a noun determines the forms of other words related to it — articles, adjectives, pronouns, and sometimes verbs must agree with it.

Assignment

Gender assignment may be partly semantic (nouns denoting males are masculine, females feminine) and partly formal, based on the noun's sound or morphology. In German, for instance, der Löffel "the spoon" is masculine, die Gabel "the fork" feminine, and das Messer "the knife" neuter, showing that gender for inanimate objects is largely arbitrary. In French, le soleil (sun) is masculine and la lune (moon) feminine — the reverse of German die Sonne and der Mond.

Distribution

Most Indo-European languages have two or three genders; Proto-Indo-European is reconstructed with three (masculine, feminine, neuter), preserved in German, Russian, Latin, and Greek, while Romance languages merged the neuter into the masculine or feminine. Some languages have expanded the system into large noun-class systems, such as the Bantu languages with ten or more classes. English lost grammatical gender during the Middle English period, retaining only natural gender in pronouns (he/she/it).

Effects

Because gender forces agreement, it provides redundancy that can aid comprehension and reference tracking. Psycholinguistic studies have explored whether grammatical gender subtly influences how speakers conceptualise objects, though the strength of such effects is debated.
