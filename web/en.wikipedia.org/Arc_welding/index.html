Arc welding

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Arc welding is a welding process that is used to join metal to metal by using electricity to create enough heat to melt metal, and the melted metals, when cool, result in a fusion of the metals. It is a type of welding that uses a welding power supply to create an electric arc between a metal stick ("electrode") and the base material to melt the metals at the point of contact. Arc welding power supplies can deliver either direct (DC) or alternating (AC) current, and consumable or non-consumable electrodes are used.

Processes

  • Shielded metal arc welding (SMAW), or "stick" welding, uses a flux-coated consumable electrode; the flux decomposes to shield the weld pool from the atmosphere.
  • Gas metal arc welding (GMAW/MIG) feeds a continuous wire electrode and an external shielding gas.
  • Flux-cored arc welding (FCAW) uses a tubular wire filled with flux, with or without external gas.
  • Gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW/TIG) uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode and inert gas shielding, giving precise, high-quality welds.
  • Submerged arc welding (SAW) buries the arc under a blanket of granular flux and is used for heavy fabrication.
  • Plasma arc welding (PAW) constricts the arc through a nozzle for deep, narrow welds.

History

Arc welding developed in the late 19th century: Nikolay Benardos patented carbon arc welding in 1885–1887, and in 1888 Nikolay Slavyanov introduced the consumable metal electrode. C. L. Coffin developed metal arc welding in the United States, and Oscar Kjellberg in Sweden invented the coated electrode (1904–1907), founding ESAB. The process spread rapidly during the First and especially the Second World War, when welded ship construction — including the Liberty ships — replaced riveting on a massive scale.

Equipment and safety

A welding power supply (transformer, rectifier, or modern inverter) maintains the arc and controls current; an electrode holder, ground clamp, and cables complete the circuit. Hazards include electric shock, intense ultraviolet and infrared radiation causing "arc eye" and skin burns, metal fumes, and fire — related to but distinct from the accidental arc flash hazard of electrical equipment. Welders therefore wear helmets with dark filters, gloves, and flame-resistant clothing, and work areas are screened and ventilated.

Applications

Arc welding is fundamental to construction, shipbuilding, pipelines, railways, automotive manufacture, and repair work worldwide. Weld quality is governed by procedure and welder qualifications under codes such as AWS D1.1 and ASME Section IX.
