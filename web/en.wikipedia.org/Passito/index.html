Passito

Passito is an Italian style of wine made from grapes that have been partially dried (the process is called appassimento) to concentrate their sugars and flavours before fermentation. The resulting wines range from dry and powerful, like Amarone della Valpolicella, to intensely sweet dessert wines such as Recioto and Vin Santo. The name derives from appassito, "withered" or "dried".

Method

After harvest, healthy bunches are laid on mats of straw or bamboo (traditionally in lofts, in Italian fruttai) or hung from rafters, and allowed to lose water in well-ventilated rooms for weeks to several months. Weight losses of 30–40% are common, raising sugar concentration so that fermentation yields wines of high alcohol or pronounced residual sweetness. The technique descends from ancient Mediterranean practice: Roman authors described raisin wines, and dried-grape wines were traded across the ancient world.

Notable wines

In the Veneto, Corvina and Rondinella grapes dried for about 100 days produce Amarone (dry, ~15% alcohol) and Recioto della Valpolicella (sweet). Tuscany's Vin Santo is made from dried Trebbiano and Malvasia aged in small barrels called caratelli. On the island of Pantelleria, Zibibbo (Muscat of Alexandria) grapes yield the amber Passito di Pantelleria. Other examples include Sicily's Malvasia delle Lipari, Piedmont's Caluso Passito, and the Sciacchetrà of the Cinque Terre. The method has close analogues elsewhere, including German Strohwein and French vin de paille.
