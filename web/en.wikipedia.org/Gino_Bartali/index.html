Gino Bartali (18 July 1914 – 5 May 2000) was an Italian road racing cyclist, one of the most successful riders of the pre- and post-war era and, decades after his death, recognised for secretly saving hundreds of Jews during the Holocaust. A superb climber nicknamed Ginettaccio and "Gino the Pious" for his devout Catholicism, he won the Tour de France twice (1938 and 1948) and the Giro d'Italia three times (1936, 1937, 1946). His fierce rivalry with Fausto Coppi divided Italian cycling fans into bartaliani and coppiani.

Cycling career

Born in Ponte a Ema near Florence, Bartali turned professional in 1935 and quickly dominated Italian racing, winning the Giro in 1936 and 1937 and the Tour de France in 1938. The Second World War interrupted his prime years. Remarkably, in 1948 — a full decade after his first Tour victory — he won the Tour again with a celebrated comeback in the Alps, a triumph credited with helping to calm Italy after the attempted assassination of Communist leader Palmiro Togliatti. He also won Milan–San Remo four times and the Giro di Lombardia three times, and took mountain classifications in both Grand Tours.

Wartime rescue work

During the German occupation of Italy (1943–45), Bartali worked with a clandestine network organised by Cardinal Elia Dalla Costa and Rabbi Nathan Cassuto that hid Jews and supplied them with false identity papers. Bartali carried forged documents and photographs hidden inside the frame tubes of his bicycle on long "training" rides between Florence and Assisi, covering hundreds of kilometres; stopped at checkpoints, he insisted his bicycle not be touched because it was calibrated exactly for him. He also sheltered a Jewish family in an apartment he owned. An estimated 800 Jews were saved by the network. Bartali rarely spoke of this work, saying "good is something you do, not something you talk about." In 2013 Yad Vashem named him Righteous Among the Nations.

Legacy

Bartali died in Florence in 2000. His story became widely known only afterward, inspiring books and the documentary My Italian Secret (2014). A museum in his birthplace, the Museo del Ciclismo Gino Bartali, commemorates his career.
