Female ejaculation

Female ejaculation is the expulsion of fluid from the urethral (paraurethral) glands — principally Skene's glands — during or before orgasm. The fluid is typically small in volume and milky in appearance, and biochemical analyses have found it contains prostatic acid phosphatase and prostate-specific antigen, consistent with an origin in the glandular tissue sometimes called the "female prostate".

History of research

The phenomenon was described in ancient texts and by anatomists such as Regnier de Graaf in the 17th century. In the 20th century, Ernst Gräfenberg discussed urethral expulsions in connection with erotic stimulation of the urethral area. Modern sexology research, including studies by Beverly Whipple and John Perry in the 1980s, brought renewed attention to the topic.

Distinctions

Researchers distinguish female ejaculation proper — a small volume of thick, whitish fluid from Skene's glands — from "squirting" or gushing, the expulsion of a larger volume of clear fluid shown by ultrasound and biochemical studies (e.g., Salama et al., 2015) to originate largely from the bladder and consist chiefly of dilute urine, sometimes mixed with prostatic secretions. Reported prevalence of ejaculation experiences varies widely across surveys.
