On the Waterfront is a 1954 American crime drama film directed by Elia Kazan and written by Budd Schulberg, about union corruption and racketeering among longshoremen in Hoboken, New Jersey. It is regarded as one of the greatest American films.

Plot

Terry Malloy (Marlon Brando), an ex-boxer working as an errand boy for mobbed-up union boss Johnny Friendly, is implicated in the murder of a dockworker who planned to testify. Torn by guilt, urged on by the victim's sister Edie and the crusading Father Barry, Terry ultimately defies the code of silence — "D and D" (deaf and dumb) — and testifies, surviving a beating to lead the dockworkers back to work on his own terms. The film contains the famous taxicab scene with Terry's brother Charley:

"I coulda had class. I coulda been a contender. I coulda been somebody, instead of a bum, which is what I am."

Context and reception

The screenplay drew on Malcolm Johnson's Pulitzer-winning exposés of waterfront crime and on real hearings. The film is often read against Kazan's own controversial testimony before the House Un-American Activities Committee. It won eight Academy Awards, including Best Picture, Best Director, Best Actor for Brando, and Best Supporting Actress for Eva Marie Saint, and was selected for the National Film Registry. Leonard Bernstein composed the score, his only original film score.
