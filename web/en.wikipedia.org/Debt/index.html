Debt

Debt is an obligation that requires one party, the debtor, to pay money borrowed or withheld from another party, the creditor. Debt may be owed by sovereign states and territories, local governments, companies or individuals. Commercial debt is generally subject to contractual terms regarding the amount and timing of repayments of principal and interest. Loans, bonds, notes, and mortgages are all types of debt.

History

Debt predates coined money: Mesopotamian records document loans of grain and silver at interest from the third millennium BCE, and the Code of Hammurabi regulated interest rates and debt slavery. Periodic debt cancellations are attested in the Near East and echoed in the biblical Jubilee. In medieval Europe, religious prohibitions on usury shaped early banking through workarounds by Italian merchant banks. Sovereign borrowing grew with the Dutch and English financial revolutions of the 17th century, and consumer credit expanded massively in the 20th.

Types

  • Secured debt is backed by collateral — e.g. a mortgage on property — reducing the lender's risk and interest rate.
  • Unsecured debt, such as most credit-card balances, rests only on the borrower's creditworthiness.
  • Corporate debt includes bank loans and publicly traded bonds, rated by credit-rating agencies.
  • Sovereign debt is issued by governments; "external debt" denotes obligations owed to foreigners.
  • Household debt comprises mortgages, auto loans, student loans and credit cards.

Economics and risk

Debt finances investment and smooths consumption, and moderate leverage can raise returns on equity. Excessive debt, however, produces fragility: default, insolvency and bankruptcy law provide mechanisms to restructure or discharge obligations, while debt-deflation dynamics (described by Irving Fisher) can deepen recessions. Anthropologist David Graeber's Debt: The First 5,000 Years (2011) popularised debate about debt's moral framing and its role in social hierarchy.
