Psychodynamic therapy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Psychodynamic therapy is an approach to psychological treatment that emphasizes systematic study of the psychological forces underlying human behavior, feelings, and emotions, and how they might relate to early experience. Rooted in the theories of Sigmund Freud and psychoanalysis, psychodynamic therapy differs from classical psychoanalysis by being typically briefer, less intensive (often one or two sessions per week), and more focused on the present and on the therapeutic relationship, while retaining core ideas such as unconscious processes, internal conflicts, defense mechanisms, transference, and the influence of childhood relationships on adult personality.

Core principles

  • Unconscious processes: much mental life — feelings, memories, motives — operates outside awareness and shapes behavior.
  • Early experience: patterns formed in childhood relationships recur in adult life, including in the relationship with the therapist (transference).
  • Internal conflict and defenses: symptoms may arise from conflicts between wishes, fears, and moral prohibitions, managed by defenses such as repression, denial, and projection.
  • Insight and emotional experience: change occurs by bringing patterns into awareness within a safe relationship, enabling new ways of relating.

Forms and practice

Modern psychodynamic therapies include time-limited models such as short-term dynamic psychotherapy, mentalization-based treatment (developed for borderline personality disorder), transference-focused psychotherapy, and supportive-expressive therapy. Sessions typically involve free-flowing exploration rather than structured exercises; the therapist may interpret recurring themes and the here-and-now dynamics of the therapeutic relationship. Treatment length ranges from a few months (brief dynamic therapy) to several years.

Evidence

Meta-analyses have found psychodynamic therapy effective for depression, anxiety disorders, somatic symptom disorders, and personality disorders, with effect sizes comparable to other bona fide therapies and, in some studies, benefits that continue to grow after treatment ends. Critics have historically questioned the falsifiability of psychodynamic theory and the comparatively smaller number of large randomized controlled trials relative to cognitive-behavioral therapy, though the evidence base has expanded substantially since 2000.
