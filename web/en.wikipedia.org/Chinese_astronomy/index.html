Chinese astronomy

Astronomy in China has a long history stretching from the Neolithic period through the present day. Chinese astronomers maintained some of the most continuous and detailed observational records in the pre-modern world, recording eclipses, comets, sunspots, and "guest stars" (novae and supernovae) over more than two millennia, closely tied to the imperial calendar and the doctrine of the Mandate of Heaven.

Observational records

Oracle bone inscriptions from the Shang dynasty record lunar eclipses as early as the 13th century BCE. Chinese court astronomers catalogued the supernova of 1054 CE, whose remnant is the Crab Nebula, and recorded sunspots centuries before they were noted in Europe. Star catalogues divided the sky into the Three Enclosures and Twenty-Eight Mansions (xiu), an equatorial system distinct from the Babylonian-Greek zodiac.

Instruments and calendars

Astronomers such as Zhang Heng built armillary spheres and a seismoscope, while Su Song constructed a monumental water-driven astronomical clock tower in 11th-century Kaifeng. Because the emperor's legitimacy was linked to issuing an accurate calendar, calendrical reform was a major state enterprise; the Shoushi calendar of Guo Shoujing (1281) calculated the tropical year with remarkable precision.

Foreign influence and modern era

Indian, Islamic, and later Jesuit astronomers influenced Chinese practice; Jesuit missionaries such as Matteo Ricci and Johann Adam Schall von Bell reformed the calendar and directed the imperial observatory in the 17th century. Modern China operates major facilities including the FAST radio telescope and an active space program.
