Genetic diversity

Genetic diversity is the total number of genetic characteristics in the genetic makeup of a species. It is distinguished from genetic variability, which describes the tendency of genetic characteristics to vary. Genetic diversity serves as a way for populations to adapt to changing environments: with more variation, it is more likely that some individuals in a population will possess variations of alleles that are suited for the environment, and those individuals are more likely to survive to produce offspring bearing that allele.

Measuring diversity
Diverse landraces of maize illustrate within-species genetic diversity

Measures

Genetic diversity is commonly measured by heterozygosity (the fraction of individuals heterozygous at a locus, or the fraction of loci variable in a population), allelic richness, and nucleotide diversity — the average proportion of nucleotide sites differing between two randomly chosen sequences. Neutral genetic diversity arises chiefly from mutation and genetic drift, while selection, gene flow and recombination also shape it.

Importance

Survival and adaptation

Populations with low genetic diversity are at risk from environmental change and disease: a uniform population may have no individuals resistant to a new pathogen. The Irish potato famine was worsened by the near-clonal propagation of a single potato variety susceptible to late blight. Conservation programmes therefore aim to preserve diversity in endangered species, and crop breeders conserve landraces and wild relatives as reservoirs of useful alleles.

Domesticated species

Selective breeding has narrowed diversity in many domesticated species. Studies of maize, rice, dogs and cattle compare diversity in domesticates and their wild ancestors; domesticated populations commonly show reduced diversity due to domestication bottlenecks, though subsequent gene flow from wild relatives sometimes restores it.

Loss of diversity

Diversity is eroded by population bottlenecks, founder effects, inbreeding, habitat fragmentation and directional selection. The cheetah is a classic example of a species with strikingly low diversity, attributed to a bottleneck around the end of the last ice age. Maintaining effective population size and connectivity between habitat fragments are central goals of conservation genetics.
