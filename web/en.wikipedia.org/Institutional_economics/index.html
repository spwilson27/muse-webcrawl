FocusRole of institutions in shaping economic behaviour

Institutional economics focuses on understanding the role of the evolutionary process and the part institutions play in shaping economic behaviour. Its original focus lay in Thorstein Veblen's instinct-driven view of human development and John R. Commons's legal approach to economic activity. It emphasises a broader study of institutions and views markets as a result of the complex interaction of these institutions — laws, customs, norms, and organisations.

Old institutional economics

The American institutionalist tradition of the early twentieth century, led by Veblen, Commons, and Wesley C. Mitchell, criticised neoclassical abstraction and stressed empirical study, technology, and social habits. Veblen coined the phrase "conspicuous consumption" and analysed how social status drives economic behaviour.

New institutional economics

From the 1970s, the "new institutional economics" integrated institutions into mainstream analysis using tools such as transaction costs (Ronald Coase, Oliver Williamson), property rights, and principal–agent theory. Douglass North defined institutions as the "rules of the game" of a society and argued they are central to long-run economic performance. Coase, North, and Williamson all received Nobel Memorial Prizes.

Themes

Recurring themes include path dependence, the costs of transacting, credible commitment, and the comparison of governance structures. Institutional analysis is applied to development, economic history, law and economics, and organisational studies.
