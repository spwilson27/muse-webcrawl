Anode

Diagram of a galvanic cell, showing the anode at which oxidation occurs

An anode is an electrode of a polarised electrical device through which conventional current enters the device from an external circuit. Equivalently, the anode is the electrode at which oxidation occurs: it releases electrons to the external circuit. The electrode through which conventional current leaves the device is the cathode. The terms were coined by William Whewell at the request of Michael Faraday in 1834, from the Greek anodos, "way up".

Charge flow

Conventional current flows into a device through its anode and out through its cathode. Electrons flow in the opposite direction, out of the anode. Whether the anode is positively or negatively charged relative to the cathode depends on the mode of operation:

  • In a device that consumes power, such as an electrolytic cell or a recharging battery, the anode is positive.
  • In a device that supplies power, such as a discharging galvanic cell (battery), the anode is the negative terminal, because oxidation there releases electrons.

Examples

In a discharging lead-acid battery the lead electrode is the anode, where lead is oxidised to lead(II) sulfate. In electrolysis of water, oxygen is evolved at the anode. In vacuum tubes and semiconductor diodes, the anode (plate) collects electrons in normal forward operation. In cathodic protection of buried or submerged metal structures, a sacrificial anode of a more reactive metal (such as zinc or magnesium) corrodes preferentially, protecting the structure.

Anode in electrochemistry

In electrochemistry, the anode is where oxidation takes place and is the positive electrode in an electrolytic cell and the negative electrode in a galvanic cell. Anions in the electrolyte migrate toward the anode. A common mnemonic is "an ox" — anode oxidation.
