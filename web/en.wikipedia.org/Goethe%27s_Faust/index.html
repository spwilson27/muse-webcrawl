Goethe's Faust

Faust is a tragic play in two parts by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, usually known in English as Goethe's Faust to distinguish it from other versions of the Faust legend. It is widely regarded as the greatest work of German literature. Goethe worked on Faust for some sixty years: an early version (the Urfaust) dates from the 1770s, Faust, Part One was published in 1808, and Faust, Part Two appeared posthumously in 1832, the year of his death.

Part One

The ageing scholar Heinrich Faust, dissatisfied with the limits of human knowledge, is approached by Mephistopheles, the Devil's agent, who in a prologue in Heaven has wagered with God that he can corrupt Faust's soul. Faust signs a pact: Mephistopheles will serve him on Earth, but if Faust ever says to the moment "Stay, thou art so fair" (Verweile doch, du bist so schön), his soul is forfeit. Rejuvenated, Faust seduces the innocent Gretchen, with catastrophic results: her mother dies, her brother is killed by Faust, and Gretchen, mad with guilt, drowns her child and is condemned to death. In the final scene she refuses escape and is redeemed by a voice from above, while Faust is hurried away by Mephistopheles.

Part Two

The second part, far more allegorical and episodic, ranges across classical mythology, imperial courts, the creation of the artificial Homunculus, a union with Helen of Troy, and finally Faust's project of reclaiming land from the sea. Blind and a hundred years old, Faust imagines a free people on free land and, anticipating that vision, utters the fatal words and dies — yet angels carry off his soul, declaring that whoever strives unceasingly can be redeemed. Gretchen, now among the blessed, intercedes for him.

Influence

Faust has inspired countless adaptations, including operas by Gounod and Berlioz, Liszt's Faust Symphony, and Mahler's Eighth Symphony, whose finale sets the play's closing scene. Because of its scale, Part Two is read more often than staged complete, though marathon productions are mounted from time to time.
