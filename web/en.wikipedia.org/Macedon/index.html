Macedonia (Greek: Μακεδονία, Makedonía), also called Macedon, was an ancient kingdom on the periphery of Archaic and Classical Greece, later the dominant state of Hellenistic Greece. It emerged during the 7th century BC as the kingdom of the Argead dynasty, and rose to supremacy under Philip II (r. 359–336 BC), whose reforms created the army with which his son Alexander the Great conquered the Achaemenid Persian Empire.

History

According to tradition, the kingdom was founded by the Temenid (Argead) dynasty from Argos, with capitals first at Aegae (modern Vergina) and later at Pella. For much of its early history Macedon was a loosely organized highland kingdom, at times a vassal of Persia and later caught between Athens, Sparta, and Thebes. Philip II transformed it through diplomacy and military innovation—notably the sarissa-armed phalanx and combined-arms tactics—and by 338 BC, after the Battle of Chaeronea, he dominated Greece through the League of Corinth.

Alexander succeeded Philip in 336 BC and in a decade of campaigning overthrew Persia, taking Egypt, Mesopotamia, and the Iranian plateau and reaching the Indus Valley. After Alexander's death at Babylon in 323 BC his empire fragmented among the Diadochi; Macedon itself eventually fell to the Antigonid dynasty (from 276 BC), whose kings contended with the Greek leagues and, fatally, with Rome. Defeat in the Macedonian Wars—ending with the Battle of Pydna in 168 BC—ended the monarchy, and Macedonia became a Roman province in 146 BC.

Government and society

Macedon was a hereditary monarchy in which the king (basileus) commanded the army, conducted diplomacy, and presided over festivals and sacrifices; the Macedonian aristocracy, including the king's Companions (hetairoi), formed his council and elite cavalry. The tomb complexes at Vergina, including the tomb attributed to Philip II, and the palace at Pella attest to the kingdom's wealth and Hellenic court culture, even as southern Greeks long debated whether the Macedonians were fully Greek.

Language

The ancient Macedonian language is sparsely attested; it was either a dialect of Greek closely related to Northwest and Aeolic Greek, or a distinct Hellenic language. By the 4th century BC the court had adopted Attic Greek, the ancestor of the Koine that spread across Alexander's empire.
