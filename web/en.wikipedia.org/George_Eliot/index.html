Mary Ann Evans (1819–1880), known by her pen name George Eliot, was an English novelist, poet, journalist, and translator, regarded as one of the greatest novelists in the English language. Writing under a male name to ensure her work was taken seriously, she produced seven novels distinguished by psychological insight, realism, and moral depth.

Early life and career

Evans grew up in Warwickshire, whose landscape and provincial society furnish the settings of her fiction. Largely self-educated, she translated Strauss's Life of Jesus and Feuerbach's Essence of Christianity, and became assistant editor of the Westminster Review in London. From 1854 she lived with the philosopher and critic George Henry Lewes in a union they regarded as a marriage, though Lewes could not legally divorce.

Novels

Her first fiction, Scenes of Clerical Life (1857), was followed by Adam Bede (1859), an immediate success. The Mill on the Floss (1860) drew on her own youth; Silas Marner (1861) became a classic of the short novel. Her masterpiece, Middlemarch (1871–72), subtitled "A Study of Provincial Life," interweaves the fortunes of a whole community and was called by Virginia Woolf "one of the few English novels written for grown-up people." Her final novel, Daniel Deronda (1876), controversially addressed Jewish identity and proto-Zionism.

Legacy

Eliot died in 1880, shortly after marrying John Cross following Lewes's death. Her combination of philosophical seriousness, free indirect narration, and sympathetic moral realism profoundly influenced the modern novel.
