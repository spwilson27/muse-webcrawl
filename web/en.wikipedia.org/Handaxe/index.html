Handaxe

TypeStone tool (biface)
IndustriesAcheulean, Mousterian
PeriodLower Palaeolithic, from c. 1.7 million years ago
MaterialsFlint, quartzite, obsidian and other stone

A handaxe (or hand axe) is a prehistoric stone tool with two faces (a biface) worked to a roughly symmetrical, often teardrop or oval shape with a cutting edge around much of its perimeter. Handaxes are the characteristic tool of the Acheulean industry and were made and used by early humans, including Homo erectus and Neanderthals, for well over a million years.

Manufacture and use

Handaxes were knapped from stone — flint, quartzite, and other fine-grained rocks — using hammerstones and later soft hammers of bone or antler to thin and shape the biface. Their exact functions were probably varied: butchery, digging, woodworking, and cutting are all proposed, and experimental archaeology shows they are effective general-purpose tools. Some oversized or exceptionally symmetrical examples have prompted debate about display or symbolic roles.

Distribution

Handaxes appear in Africa from about 1.7 million years ago and spread across Europe and western and southern Asia. Their notable absence from much of East Asia east of the "Movius Line" long puzzled archaeologists, though bifaces have since been found in China and Korea.
