Connoisseurship

Connoisseurship is the practice and skill of judging the quality, authenticity and attribution of works of art (and, by extension, other crafted or collected objects) through close visual examination and accumulated expertise. A connoisseur is an expert in a particular field whose informed opinion is sought on questions of authorship, date and quality.

Method

The Italian physician and art historian Giovanni Morelli (1816–1891) proposed a quasi-scientific method of attribution based on minor, habitual details that artists render almost unconsciously — the shapes of ears, hands and fingernails — rather than on grand compositional features that imitators can copy. The Morellian method influenced later connoisseurs, including Bernard Berenson, whose attributions of Italian Renaissance paintings shaped collections and the art market.

Role and criticism

Connoisseurs' judgments underpin museum cataloguing, the valuation of works, and the detection of forgeries. The practice has been criticised for subjectivity and conflicts of interest — experts may be paid by owners or dealers — and attributions are sometimes overturned, as in the long-running reassessments by the Rembrandt Research Project, which reduced the number of paintings accepted as autograph Rembrandts. Modern practice increasingly combines connoisseurship with technical evidence: X-radiography, infrared reflectography, pigment analysis and dendrochronology.

Other fields

The term extends to wine, food, music, manuscripts, antiques and other domains where expert sensory judgment discriminates quality and authenticity.
