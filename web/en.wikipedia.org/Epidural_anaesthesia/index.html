Epidural administration

Epidural administration (from Ancient Greek ἐπί, "on, upon" + dura mater) is a method of medication administration in which a medicine is injected into the epidural space surrounding the spinal cord. The most familiar application is epidural anaesthesia, in which local anaesthetics (often combined with opioids) block pain transmission from a region of the body; it is widely used for pain relief in labour and childbirth and for major surgery.

Technique

With the patient sitting or lying on their side, a needle is advanced between the vertebrae, usually at the lumbar level, until its tip lies in the epidural space, identified by a loss of resistance to injection. A fine catheter is commonly threaded through the needle so that medication can be given continuously or in repeated doses. Unlike spinal anaesthesia, the dura is not pierced and the drug acts on the nerve roots as they traverse the epidural space, giving a slower onset and a segmental block that can be adjusted.

Uses and risks

Epidurals are used for labour analgesia, caesarean section, thoracic, abdominal and lower-limb surgery, and for chronic pain injections of corticosteroids. Risks include low blood pressure, headache (if the dura is accidentally punctured — "dural tap"), infection, bleeding into the epidural space (epidural haematoma), and, rarely, nerve injury.
