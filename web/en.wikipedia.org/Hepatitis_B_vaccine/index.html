Hepatitis B vaccine

The hepatitis B vaccine is a vaccine that prevents hepatitis B. The first dose is recommended within 24 hours of birth, with two or three further doses later; it is also recommended for adults at risk, including health care workers, people with diabetes, and household contacts of infected persons. The vaccine is 95% effective in preventing infection and its chronic consequences, including cirrhosis and liver cancer, and is regarded as the first "anti-cancer vaccine."

History and composition

The vaccine was developed from the work of Baruch Blumberg, who discovered the hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), and microbiologist Maurice Hilleman, whose plasma-derived vaccine was licensed in 1981. It was replaced in the mid-1980s by a recombinant vaccine in which HBsAg is produced in yeast cells — the first vaccine made by recombinant DNA technology. Current products (such as Engerix-B and Recombivax HB) contain purified surface antigen adsorbed onto an aluminium adjuvant; a two-dose formulation with a CpG adjuvant (Heplisav-B) is licensed for adults.

Use and impact

The World Health Organization recommends universal infant immunisation, and most countries include hepatitis B vaccine in childhood schedules, often within combination vaccines. Widespread vaccination has dramatically reduced chronic infection in children in countries such as Taiwan and China. Protection is measured by antibody to HBsAg (anti-HBs ≥ 10 mIU/mL) and is long-lasting in immunocompetent people.
