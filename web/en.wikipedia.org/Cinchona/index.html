Cinchona

Cinchona is a genus of flowering plants in the family Rubiaceae containing at least 23 species of trees and shrubs native to the tropical Andean forests of western South America. They are known in medicine as the source of quinine and other alkaloids obtained from their bark, long used to treat malaria.

Description

Cinchona species grow as large shrubs or small trees, reaching 5 to 15 metres in height, with evergreen foliage, opposite elliptical leaves, and clusters of small fragrant pink or red tubular flowers. The bark is bitter, containing the alkaloids quinine, quinidine, cinchonidine, and cinchonine.

History

Quechua peoples of the Andes used the bark against fevers. Jesuit missionaries brought "Peruvian bark" (or "Jesuit's bark") to Europe in the seventeenth century, where it became the first effective treatment for malaria. Carl Linnaeus named the genus in 1742, allegedly for the Countess of Chinchón, wife of a viceroy of Peru who was legendarily cured by the bark. In the nineteenth century, British and Dutch planters smuggled seeds out of South America and established plantations in India and Java, breaking the Andean monopoly.

Uses

Quinine extracted from cinchona bark remained the standard antimalarial until synthetic drugs were developed in the twentieth century. The bark's alkaloids also give tonic water its characteristic bitter flavor, and quinidine is used as an antiarrhythmic drug.
