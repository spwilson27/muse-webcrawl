The Char Dham (Sanskrit: चार धाम, "four abodes") are a set of four Hindu pilgrimage sites in India, located at the four cardinal points of the Indian subcontinent: Badrinath in the north, Dwarka in the west, Jagannath Puri in the east, and Rameswaram in the south. Tradition holds that the circuit was defined by the 8th-century philosopher Adi Shankaracharya, and that visiting the four abodes helps the pilgrim attain moksha (liberation).

The four shrines

  • Badrinath (Uttarakhand) — dedicated to Vishnu as Badrinarayan, seated in the Garhwal Himalayas on the banks of the Alaknanda River. It is the northernmost dham and is open only about six months a year because of snow.
  • Dwarka (Gujarat) — the Dwarkadhish Temple, dedicated to Krishna, traditionally identified with Krishna's legendary capital city.
  • Puri (Odisha) — the Jagannath Temple, dedicated to Jagannath, a form of Vishnu/Krishna, famous for the annual Rath Yatra chariot festival.
  • Rameswaram (Tamil Nadu) — the Ramanathaswamy Temple, dedicated to Shiva; though a Shaivite shrine, it is included in the circuit and is associated with Rama's journey to Lanka.

Order of pilgrimage

The yatra is traditionally undertaken clockwise, beginning at Badrinath in the north, proceeding to Dwarka in the west, then Puri in the east, and ending at Rameswaram in the south, so that the pilgrim's circuit symbolically respects the directions associated with the deities.

Chota Char Dham

A separate, smaller circuit in the Himalayan state of Uttarakhand — the Chota Char Dham ("small four abodes") — comprises Yamunotri, Gangotri, Kedarnath, and Badrinath. It should not be confused with the all-India Char Dham described here, though Badrinath belongs to both.

Significance

Shankaracharya is said to have established monastic seats (mathas) associated with the four dhams, binding the subcontinent into a single sacred geography. The Char Dham remains one of the most widely recognised pilgrimage circuits of Hinduism, attracting millions of devotees.
