Naval warfare of World War I

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Naval warfare in World War I was mainly characterized by blockade. The Allied Powers, with their larger fleets and positions commanding trade routes, imposed a blockade of the Central Powers, while Germany attempted a counter-blockade with U-boats and commerce raiders. The largest surface engagement, the Battle of Jutland (1916), ended inconclusively, and the German High Seas Fleet spent most of the war confined to port after it.

Background

The pre-war Anglo-German naval arms race centred on dreadnought battleships. Britain's Grand Fleet, based at Scapa Flow, aimed to keep the German High Seas Fleet bottled up in the North Sea, while Germany hoped attrition by mines and submarines could even the odds.

Major campaigns

In the North Sea, actions at Heligoland Bight (1914), Dogger Bank (1915) and Jutland (31 May – 1 June 1916) failed to change the strategic balance. In the Mediterranean, Allied forces fought Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire, and the Dardanelles campaign (1915) attempted to force the straits to Constantinople. German commerce raiders and the East Asia Squadron were hunted down in 1914, notably at the Battle of the Falkland Islands.

The U-boat war

Germany's unrestricted submarine warfare campaigns of 1915 and, decisively, from February 1917 sought to starve Britain of imports. Sinkings peaked in April 1917, but the adoption of convoys sharply reduced losses. The campaign also helped bring the United States into the war after incidents such as the sinking of Lusitania (1915) and the Zimmermann Telegram.

Outcome

The blockade contributed to severe shortages in Germany. In October 1918 the High Seas Fleet's planned final sortie triggered the Kiel mutiny, a spark of the German Revolution; the fleet was interned at Scapa Flow and scuttled there in June 1919.
