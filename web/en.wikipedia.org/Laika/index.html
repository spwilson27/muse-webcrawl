Laika (Russian: Лайка, IPA: [ˈlajkə]; c. 1954 – 3 November 1957) was a Soviet space dog who became the first animal to orbit the Earth. A stray mongrel from the streets of Moscow, she was selected as the occupant of the Soviet spacecraft Sputnik 2, which was launched into outer space on 3 November 1957.

Little was known about the impact of spaceflight on living creatures at the time of Laika's mission, and the technology to de-orbit had not yet been developed, so Laika's survival was never expected. Some scientists believed humans would be unable to survive the launch or the conditions of outer space, so engineers viewed flights by animals as a necessary precursor to human missions. Laika died within hours of launch from overheating, possibly caused by a failure of the central R-7 sustainer to separate from the payload.

Background

Sputnik 2 was launched only a month after the success of Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite. Under pressure to produce another triumph for the anniversary of the October Revolution, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev requested a flight with a dog on board. The spacecraft was built in less than four weeks, without enough time to design a re-entry system.

Selection and training

Laika was a stray dog found wandering the streets of Moscow. Soviet scientists chose to use Moscow strays because they assumed such animals already had experience enduring extreme cold and hunger. She was a mongrel, possibly part husky and part terrier, approximately three years old and weighing about 6 kilograms (13 lb).

Training involved confinement in progressively smaller cages for periods of up to twenty days, exposure to centrifuges simulating launch acceleration, and feeding with a nutritious gel that would serve as space food. Two other dogs, Albina and Mushka, were also trained; Albina had already flown twice on high-altitude test rockets, and Mushka was used to test instrumentation.

The flight

Sputnik 2 lifted off from the Baikonur Cosmodrome on 3 November 1957. Telemetry showed Laika's heart rate rose to three times its resting rate during launch, and it took far longer than expected to return to near normal. After reaching orbit, the nose cone was jettisoned successfully, but the Block A core stage did not separate as planned, preventing the thermal control system from operating correctly. Insulation tore loose, the cabin temperature rose to about 40 °C (104 °F), and Laika died within hours, most likely between five and seven hours into the flight.

Sputnik 2 continued to orbit with her remains aboard until it burned up in the Earth's atmosphere on 14 April 1958, after 162 days and roughly 2,570 orbits.

Legacy and ethics

Laika's flight proved that a living passenger could survive a launch into orbit and endure weightlessness, paving the way for human spaceflight. In 1961, Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space. The experiment nonetheless sparked debate over the ethics of animal testing; many in the Soviet Union itself questioned the decision to send a dog on a mission with no chance of recovery. In 1998, Oleg Gazenko, one of the scientists responsible for the mission, expressed regret: "The more time passes, the more I'm sorry about it. We shouldn't have done it."

A statue and plaque at the Star City cosmonaut training facility near Moscow honour fallen cosmonauts, and in 2008 a monument to Laika was unveiled near the military research facility in Moscow where she was prepared for her flight.
