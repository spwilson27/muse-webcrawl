Civil Rights Act of 1964

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 (Pub. L. 88–352, 78 Stat. 241, enacted July 2, 1964) is a landmark civil rights and labour law in the United States that outlaws discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, and national origin. It prohibits unequal application of voter registration requirements, racial segregation in schools and public accommodations, and employment discrimination. The act "remains one of the most significant legislative achievements in American history".

Background

Initially, powers given to enforce the act were weak, but these were supplemented during later years. Congress asserted its authority to legislate under several different parts of the United States Constitution, principally its power under the commerce clause. President John F. Kennedy proposed the legislation in June 1963 after televised violence against civil rights demonstrators in Birmingham, Alabama; after Kennedy's assassination, President Lyndon B. Johnson pressed Congress to pass it as a memorial to him.

Legislative passage

The bill passed the House of Representatives in February 1964 but was blocked in the Senate by a filibuster led by Southern senators lasting 60 working days — the longest in Senate history at the time. A bipartisan coalition led by Hubert Humphrey and Everett Dirksen secured the two-thirds vote for cloture on 10 June 1964, and the Senate passed the bill on 19 June. Johnson signed it into law on 2 July 1964.

Major features

  • Title I: bars unequal application of voter registration requirements.
  • Title II: outlaws discrimination in public accommodations — hotels, restaurants, theatres, and similar establishments engaged in interstate commerce.
  • Title IV: authorises the desegregation of public schools.
  • Title VI: prohibits discrimination under any programme receiving federal financial assistance.
  • Title VII: prohibits employment discrimination by employers with 15 or more employees and created the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC). Sex was added as a protected class during House debate.

Impact

The Act ended legal Jim Crow segregation in public life in the American South and laid the groundwork for the Voting Rights Act of 1965 and the Civil Rights Act of 1968. In Bostock v. Clayton County (2020), the Supreme Court held that Title VII's prohibition of sex discrimination covers discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity.
