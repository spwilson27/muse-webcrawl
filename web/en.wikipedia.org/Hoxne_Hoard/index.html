Hoxne Hoard

The Hoxne Hoard is the largest hoard of late Roman silver and gold discovered in Britain, and the largest collection of gold and silver coins of the fourth and fifth centuries found anywhere within the former Roman Empire. It was found by metal detectorist Eric Lawes in the village of Hoxne in Suffolk, England, in November 1992.

Discovery and excavation

Lawes, searching for a lost hammer at the request of a farmer, found silver spoons, gold jewellery, and coins; he reported the find promptly and archaeologists from Suffolk County Council excavated the remaining material under controlled conditions. The hoard was declared treasure trove and was acquired by the British Museum, with Lawes and the landowner each receiving a share of its valuation of about £1.75 million.

Contents

The hoard comprises 14,865 Roman gold, silver, and bronze coins, around 200 items of silver tableware, and gold jewellery, all apparently buried in an oak chest around AD 407–409, shortly after the end of Roman rule in Britain. Notable objects include a gold body-chain, silver pepper pots (piperatoria) — one in the form of an empress-like figure — silver tigress handles from a vessel, inscribed spoons, and bracelets, one bearing the name "Juliane" in a Latin inscription wishing her a good life.

Significance

Because it was excavated professionally with its context intact, the Hoxne Hoard is of exceptional importance for understanding late Roman Britain, coinage circulation, and the practice of wealth burial amid the instability of the early fifth century. It is displayed at the British Museum in London.
