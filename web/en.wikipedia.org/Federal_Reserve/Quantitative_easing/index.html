Quantitative easing

Quantitative easing (QE) is a monetary policy action in which a central bank purchases predetermined amounts of government bonds or other financial assets in order to stimulate economic activity. In the United States, the Federal Reserve used QE extensively after the 2007–2008 financial crisis and again during the COVID-19 pandemic.

How it works

When short-term interest rates are already near zero, a central bank can still ease policy by buying longer-term securities. These purchases push up the prices of the assets bought, lower their yields, and encourage investors to rebalance into riskier assets, thereby reducing borrowing costs across the economy. The purchases are settled by creating bank reserves, expanding the central bank's balance sheet.

The Federal Reserve's programs

  • QE1 (2008–2010): Announced in November 2008, the Fed bought mortgage-backed securities and agency debt, later adding Treasury securities, totaling about $1.75 trillion.
  • QE2 (2010–2011): A further $600 billion of longer-term Treasury securities.
  • Operation Twist (2011–2012): The Fed sold short-term and bought long-term Treasuries without expanding the balance sheet.
  • QE3 (2012–2014): Open-ended purchases of mortgage-backed securities and Treasuries, ended in October 2014.
  • Pandemic QE (2020–2022): Purchases begun in March 2020 roughly doubled the balance sheet to nearly $9 trillion before tapering and subsequent quantitative tightening.

Effects and criticism

Studies generally find that QE lowered long-term interest rates and supported recovery, though estimates of its effect on output and inflation vary. Critics argue that QE inflates asset prices and inequality, distorts markets, encourages excessive risk-taking, and blurs the line between monetary and fiscal policy.
