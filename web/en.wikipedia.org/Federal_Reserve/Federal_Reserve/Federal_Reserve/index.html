Federal Reserve

The Federal Reserve System (often shortened to the Federal Reserve, or simply the Fed) is the central banking system of the United States. It was created on December 23, 1913, with the enactment of the Federal Reserve Act, largely in response to a series of financial panics, particularly the Panic of 1907.

Structure

The Federal Reserve System comprises several components: the Board of Governors, appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate; the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), which sets monetary policy; twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks located in cities including New York, Chicago, and San Francisco; and thousands of member banks. The Chair of the Board is the system's most prominent official.

Functions and responsibilities

The Federal Reserve performs five general functions: conducting the nation's monetary policy to promote maximum employment and stable prices; promoting the stability of the financial system; supervising and regulating banks and other financial institutions; fostering the safety and efficiency of the payment and settlement system; and promoting consumer protection and community development.

Its primary monetary policy tool is influencing the federal funds rate, the overnight rate at which banks lend reserves to one another. In response to the 2007–2008 financial crisis and later crises, the Fed also employed large-scale asset purchases known as quantitative easing.

History

Before the Fed, the United States had experimented with the First and Second Banks of the United States, both of whose charters were allowed to lapse amid political controversy. Recurring banking panics in the laissez-faire era led to the secret 1910 meeting on Jekyll Island, Georgia, where bankers and Senator Nelson Aldrich drafted the plan that became the Federal Reserve Act under President Woodrow Wilson. The system's powers expanded after the Great Depression, the 1951 Treasury–Fed Accord restored its policy independence, and the 1977 Humphrey–Hawkins Act formalized its dual mandate of price stability and maximum employment.

Criticism

The Federal Reserve has been criticized from many directions: for causing or worsening boom-and-bust cycles, for bailing out large financial institutions, for secrecy in its deliberations, and for policies said to favor creditors or debtors. Audits of its financial statements are published, and its officials regularly testify before Congress.
