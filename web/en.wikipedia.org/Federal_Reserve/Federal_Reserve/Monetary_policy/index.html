Monetary policy of the Federal Reserve

The monetary policy of the Federal Reserve is the set of actions by which the United States central bank influences the availability and cost of money and credit to achieve its statutory goals of maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates. Decisions are made principally by the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC).

The dual mandate

The Federal Reserve Act, as amended in 1977, directs the Fed to promote effectively the goals of maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates — commonly summarized as the dual mandate. Since 2012 the FOMC has interpreted price stability as inflation of 2 percent per year in the price index for personal consumption expenditures over the longer run.

Tools

  • Federal funds rate: The FOMC sets a target range for the overnight interbank rate, implemented through administered rates including interest on reserve balances.
  • Open market operations: Purchases and sales of Treasury and agency securities by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York adjust the supply of reserves and the Fed's balance sheet.
  • Quantitative easing: In periods when the funds rate is at its effective lower bound, large-scale asset purchases aim to lower longer-term interest rates.
  • Forward guidance: Public communication about the likely future path of policy influences financial conditions.
  • Lender of last resort: The discount window and emergency lending facilities supply liquidity during financial stress.

Transmission and criticism

Changes in policy affect borrowing costs, asset prices, the exchange rate, and ultimately spending, employment, and inflation, with lags commonly estimated at one to two years. Critics debate whether Fed policy was too loose before the 2008 financial crisis and the 2021–2022 inflation surge, or too tight during recoveries; rules-based approaches such as the Taylor rule are often proposed as benchmarks.
