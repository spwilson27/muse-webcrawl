Inflation targeting

The Federal Reserve aims for PCE price inflation of 2 percent per year over the longer run.

Inflation targeting is a monetary policy framework in which a central bank publicly announces a numerical target for the inflation rate and steers policy — chiefly through interest rates — to hit that target over the medium term. The Federal Reserve adopted a formal inflation target in January 2012, when the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) issued its first Statement on Longer-Run Goals and Monetary Policy Strategy, declaring that inflation of 2 percent, measured by the annual change in the price index for personal consumption expenditures (PCE), is most consistent with the Fed's statutory mandate of stable prices and maximum employment.

Why 2 percent

A small positive target gives monetary policy room to cut real interest rates in a downturn without hitting the zero lower bound, provides a buffer against deflation, and accommodates measurement bias in price indexes. Under Chairman Ben Bernanke, long an academic advocate of inflation targeting, the Fed moved gradually from informal comfort zones toward the explicit goal. In August 2020 the Fed revised its framework to "flexible average inflation targeting", seeking inflation that averages 2 percent over time and allowing periods moderately above 2 percent after periods below it; a further framework review concluded in 2025 restored flexible inflation targeting language.

Practice and criticism

Inflation targeting central banks set the policy rate — in the Fed's case the federal funds rate — according to forecasts of inflation, because policy affects prices only with long lags. The framework is credited with anchoring inflation expectations in the decades after 1990. Critics argue it can be too rigid in the face of supply shocks, contributed to overly loose policy after the pandemic when the Fed treated the 2021 inflation surge as "transitory", and neglects asset prices and financial stability. Other major central banks — the Bank of England, the European Central Bank, and the Reserve Bank of New Zealand, the pioneer of the framework in 1990 — use similar targets, usually of 2 percent.
