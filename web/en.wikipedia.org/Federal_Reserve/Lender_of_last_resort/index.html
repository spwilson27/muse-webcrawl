Lender of last resort

A bank run: the classic emergency in which a central bank must lend freely to solvent but illiquid institutions.

A lender of last resort is an institution — normally a country's central bank — that provides emergency liquidity to financial institutions that cannot obtain funding from the market, in order to prevent the institution's failure from spreading through the financial system. For the United States, this function belongs to the Federal Reserve, and providing it was one of the principal reasons the Federal Reserve System was created in 1913, after the Panic of 1907 showed that no private arrangement could reliably stop nationwide bank runs.

Bagehot's rules

The classical doctrine was stated by Walter Bagehot in Lombard Street (1873), drawing on the earlier work of Henry Thornton: in a panic, the central bank should lend early and freely, to solvent institutions, against good collateral, at a penalty rate. The penalty rate was meant to discourage routine use and limit moral hazard — the risk that banks, expecting rescue, take excessive risks.

The Federal Reserve in practice

The Fed lends routinely through its discount window and, in crises, has created emergency facilities under Section 13(3) of the Federal Reserve Act. During the 2008 financial crisis it lent to investment banks and money market funds and financed the rescues of Bear Stearns and AIG; in March 2020 it opened facilities supporting corporate bonds, municipalities, and small-business loans during the pandemic crash; and in March 2023, after the failures of Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank, it created the Bank Term Funding Program, lending against Treasury securities at par. The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 constrained 13(3) lending to broad-based programmes rather than rescues of individual firms.

Controversy

Lender-of-last-resort operations are controversial because they socialise losses, may reward reckless institutions, and blur the line between illiquidity and insolvency in real time. The Federal Reserve's failures to prevent mass bank failures in 1930–1933 — analysed famously by Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz — are regarded as a central cause of the Great Depression's depth, and the episode remains the standard cautionary example in central banking.
