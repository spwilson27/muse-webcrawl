Central bank

The Federal Reserve, headquartered in Washington, D.C., is the central bank of the United States.

A central bank, reserve bank, or monetary authority is an institution that manages the currency and monetary policy of a country or monetary union and oversees its commercial banking system. In the United States, this role is performed by the Federal Reserve System, created by the Federal Reserve Act of 1913. Unlike an ordinary commercial bank, a central bank can issue the nation's currency, act as a lender of last resort to the banking system, and set key interest rates to influence the whole economy.

The Federal Reserve as central bank

The Federal Reserve was established after a series of financial panics — most severely the Panic of 1907 — convinced Congress that the United States needed a central institution able to supply an elastic currency and liquidity in a crisis. Its structure deliberately balances public and private and central and regional control: a presidentially appointed Board of Governors in Washington, a Federal Open Market Committee that sets monetary policy, and twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks (in Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Cleveland, Richmond, Atlanta, Chicago, St. Louis, Minneapolis, Kansas City, Dallas, and San Francisco) owned by member banks.

Functions

As a central bank, the Federal Reserve conducts monetary policy to pursue Congress's dual mandate of maximum employment and stable prices; supervises and regulates banks to ensure the safety of the financial system; maintains the stability of the financial system and contains systemic risk; and provides financial services to the government and banks, including operating payment systems such as Fedwire and the check-clearing network. It issues Federal Reserve notes — the familiar dollar bills — and holds the government's account at the Treasury.

Central banks generally

Most countries have a central bank; among the most important are the European Central Bank, the Bank of England (founded 1694), the Bank of Japan, and the People's Bank of China. Modern central banks are typically granted operational independence from day-to-day politics, on the theory that elected governments face a temptation to stimulate the economy before elections at the cost of later inflation; research associates central bank independence with lower average inflation.
