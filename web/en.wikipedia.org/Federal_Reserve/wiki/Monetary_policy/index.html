Monetary policy

Monetary policy is the process by which a central bank or monetary authority controls the supply of money and the cost of credit in an economy, generally to promote price stability and full employment. It is conventionally distinguished from fiscal policy, which concerns government taxation and spending. Modern monetary policy is normally implemented by setting a short-term policy interest rate, which influences borrowing costs throughout the economy, and is conducted under a framework such as inflation targeting, adopted first by New Zealand in 1990 and now used by dozens of countries.

Central banks conduct monetary policy.

Instruments

The standard instruments are open market operations (buying and selling government securities to adjust bank reserves), the policy or discount rate at which banks borrow from the central bank, reserve requirements, and communication — "forward guidance" — about the expected path of policy. Since the financial crisis of 2008, central banks have supplemented these with unconventional tools: quantitative easing (large-scale asset purchases), negative interest rates, and targeted lending schemes.

Transmission and lags

Changes in the policy rate affect the economy through interest-rate, credit, exchange-rate, asset-price and expectations channels. Milton Friedman emphasised that the effects arrive with "long and variable lags," often a year or more, which complicates calibration: tightening too late risks entrenched inflation, while easing too late risks recession. Under the "dual mandate" in the United States, the Federal Reserve weighs both price stability and maximum employment; the European Central Bank has a hierarchical mandate giving primacy to price stability.

History

Monetary policy in its modern sense emerged with the development of central banking in the 19th century. The gold standard constrained discretion before 1914; the interwar period saw competitive devaluations; the post-war Bretton Woods system fixed exchange rates and subordinated monetary policy to external balance. After its collapse in the early 1970s, fiat-money regimes combined with the Great Inflation of the 1970s prompted a turn toward central bank independence and, from the 1990s, explicit inflation targets — typically 2 percent — that remain the dominant framework, tested by the inflation surge of 2021–23.
