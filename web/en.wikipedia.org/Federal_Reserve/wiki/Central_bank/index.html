Central bank

A central bank, reserve bank, national bank or monetary authority is an institution that manages the currency and monetary policy of a country or monetary union. In contrast to a commercial bank, a central bank possesses a monopoly on increasing the monetary base and usually also supervises the commercial banking system. Well-known central banks include the United States Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, the Bank of England and the Bank of Japan.

Functions

The principal functions of a central bank are to conduct monetary policy — typically by setting short-term interest rates and, in many modern frameworks, targeting a low and stable rate of inflation — to issue banknotes and serve as banker to the government, to hold the country's foreign exchange and gold reserves, and to act as lender of last resort to the banking system during financial crises, a doctrine articulated by Walter Bagehot in Lombard Street (1873). Many central banks are also responsible for banking supervision and for the stability of the payment system, though the allocation of prudential supervision varies by country.

History

The Swedish Riksbank, founded in 1668, is the world's oldest central bank, followed by the Bank of England in 1694, which was chartered to raise funds for war against France and gradually assumed the full range of central banking functions during the 19th century. The United States created the Federal Reserve System in 1913 after a series of banking panics. Most central banks acquired operational independence during the late 20th century, on the theory that delegating monetary policy to technocrats insulated from electoral pressure produces lower inflation.

Independence and accountability

Central bank independence ranges from full statutory independence, as with the European Central Bank, whose mandate is fixed by treaty, to de facto government direction. The tension between independence and democratic accountability remains a live subject of debate, particularly since 2008, when central banks adopted unconventional policies such as quantitative easing and negative interest rates, and in response to the inflation surge of the early 2020s.
