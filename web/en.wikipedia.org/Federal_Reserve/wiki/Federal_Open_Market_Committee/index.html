Federal Open Market Committee

The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) is the monetary policy-making body of the United States Federal Reserve System. It sets the target range for the federal funds rate and directs open market operations — the buying and selling of securities by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York — through which the Federal Reserve influences money and credit conditions in pursuit of maximum employment and stable prices. This article describes the committee within the institutional framework of the Federal Reserve.

Role within the Federal Reserve

Within the Federal Reserve System, the Board of Governors in Washington exercises general oversight, the twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks conduct operations and supervision, and the FOMC coordinates national monetary policy. Open market operations became the System's principal policy instrument because they affect the reserves of the whole banking system; the committee's modern structure, with a majority for Board appointees, dates from the Banking Act of 1935.

Composition and voting

Of the twelve Reserve Bank presidents, the president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York votes at every meeting by tradition reflecting that bank's role in executing operations; the remaining four voting seats rotate annually among groups of banks. All nineteen participants — governors and presidents — take part in deliberations and submit quarterly economic projections, including individual expectations for the federal funds rate published anonymously as the "dot plot."

Transparency

Until the 1990s the FOMC did not announce its decisions; under reforms beginning in 1994 it began issuing post-meeting statements, and it now holds press conferences by the Chair after every meeting, publishes minutes after three weeks, and releases verbatim transcripts with a five-year lag.
