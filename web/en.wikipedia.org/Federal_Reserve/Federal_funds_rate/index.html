Federal funds rate

The effective federal funds rate over time, showing the Volcker-era peak near 20% around 1981 and the long near-zero period after 2008.

The federal funds rate is the interest rate at which depository institutions (banks and credit unions) lend reserve balances to one another overnight, on an uncollateralised basis. Within the Federal Reserve System, the rate is the central instrument of United States monetary policy: the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) sets a target range for it, and the Federal Reserve uses its tools — interest on reserve balances, overnight reverse repurchase agreements, and open market operations — to keep the actual, or "effective", rate within that range.

Why it matters

Changes in the federal funds rate ripple through the entire economy. They influence the prime rate, credit card and auto loan rates, adjustable-rate mortgages, savings yields, government borrowing costs, corporate investment decisions, and exchange rates. Raising the rate is intended to restrain inflation by cooling demand; lowering it is intended to support employment and growth by making credit cheaper.

History

An active federal funds market developed in the 1920s. Under Chairman Paul Volcker, the rate was driven to a monthly average peak of about 19–20% in 1981 to break the Great Inflation. After the 2008 financial crisis, the FOMC cut the target to a range of 0–0.25%, where it remained until 2015, and returned it to near zero during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020. In 2022–2023 the committee raised the target rapidly, to 5.25–5.50%, in response to the highest inflation in forty years.

Target range

Since December 2008 the FOMC has announced its target as a range rather than a single number. The effective federal funds rate, calculated by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York as a volume-weighted median of overnight transactions, is published daily. Financial markets watch the FOMC's decisions intensely, and futures markets price in the probability of future changes, a signal summarised by tools such as the CME FedWatch.
