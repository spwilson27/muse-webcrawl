Federal Reserve Act

The Federal Reserve Act is an Act of Congress that created the Federal Reserve System, the central banking system of the United States. It was signed into law by President Woodrow Wilson on December 23, 1913.

Background

The Act was a response to a long history of banking panics, most recently the Panic of 1907, in which the private banker J. P. Morgan had personally organized the rescue. The National Monetary Commission, created after the panic, recommended a central banking system; its Aldrich Plan was politically contentious because of Wall Street influence. Representative Carter Glass and Senator Robert Latham Owen sponsored the compromise bill that became law, combining a publicly controlled central board with twelve privately owned regional reserve banks.

Provisions

The Act authorized the creation of up to twelve Federal Reserve Banks, established the Federal Reserve Board in Washington, and created Federal Reserve Notes as a uniform national currency backed by commercial paper and gold. National banks were required to join the system and subscribe to stock in their regional reserve bank. The stated purpose was to furnish an elastic currency, provide means of rediscounting commercial paper, and establish more effective supervision of banking.

Amendments

The Act has been amended many times. The Banking Act of 1935 restructured the system, strengthening the Board and creating the modern Federal Open Market Committee. The Employment Act era, the 1977 Federal Reserve Reform Act (which clarified the monetary policy goals), and the Humphrey–Hawkins Act of 1978 further elaborated the Fed's mandate. The Dodd–Frank Act of 2010, passed after the financial crisis, altered emergency lending authority and supervision.
