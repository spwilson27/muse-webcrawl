Federal Open Market Committee

Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)
Formed: 1933 (Banking Act); restructured 1935
Voting members: 12
Meetings: 8 per year
Body of: Federal Reserve System
The FOMC meets eight times a year in Washington, D.C., to set the target for the federal funds rate.

The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) is the committee within the Federal Reserve System that makes key decisions about interest rates and the growth of the United States money supply — in short, U.S. monetary policy. It directs open market operations, the buying and selling of U.S. Treasury and agency securities, which are the principal tool by which the Federal Reserve influences the federal funds rate, the interest rate at which banks lend reserves to one another overnight.

Membership

The committee consists of twelve voting members: the seven members of the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, the president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, and four of the eleven other Reserve Bank presidents, who serve one-year terms on a rotating basis. The chair of the Board of Governors traditionally also chairs the FOMC. All Reserve Bank presidents attend meetings and contribute to discussion, but only the designated members vote.

Meetings and decisions

The FOMC meets eight times a year in Washington, D.C. Members review economic and financial conditions and vote on the stance of policy, principally by setting a target range for the federal funds rate. After each meeting the committee issues a closely watched public statement; minutes follow three weeks later, and full transcripts are published after five years. Four times a year members also publish their projections for growth, unemployment, inflation, and the expected path of interest rates — the "Summary of Economic Projections", whose median rate forecasts are known as the "dot plot".

How policy works

By announcing a target for the federal funds rate and conducting open market operations through the New York Fed's trading desk, the FOMC raises or lowers short-term interest rates throughout the economy, influencing mortgage rates, business borrowing, asset prices, the dollar, and ultimately employment and inflation. Since 2008 it has also used large-scale asset purchases ("quantitative easing") and forward guidance about the future path of policy as additional tools.
