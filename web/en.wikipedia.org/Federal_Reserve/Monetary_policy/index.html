Monetary policy

Monetary policy is the policy adopted by the monetary authority of a nation to affect monetary and other financial conditions to accomplish broader objectives like high employment and price stability. In the United States, monetary policy is conducted by the Federal Reserve System, principally through the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), which since the Federal Reserve Reform Act of 1977 has operated under a congressional mandate to promote effectively the goals of maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates — commonly called the dual mandate.

Tools

The principal traditional tool is the target for the federal funds rate, the overnight rate at which banks lend reserves to each other; the FOMC sets a target range and implements it through open market operations conducted by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, interest paid on reserve balances, and the overnight reverse repurchase agreement facility. Other tools have included the discount rate, reserve requirements (set to zero in 2020), and large-scale asset purchases (quantitative easing), in which the central bank buys longer-term securities to lower long-term interest rates.

Strategy

Since 2012 the FOMC has published a statement of its longer-run goals, including a 2 percent target for inflation in the price index for personal consumption expenditures. In 2020 the Committee adopted flexible average inflation targeting, aiming for inflation that averages 2 percent over time. Policy decisions are communicated through post-meeting statements, minutes, press conferences by the Chair, and quarterly economic projections.

Transmission and debates

Changes in the policy rate influence borrowing costs throughout the economy, affecting consumption, investment, asset prices, the exchange rate and ultimately employment and inflation, but with long and variable lags. Debates over policy concern the appropriate trade-offs between inflation and employment, the risk of asset bubbles, the distributional effects of quantitative easing, and the limits of monetary policy at the effective lower bound on interest rates.
