Federal Reserve

The Federal Reserve System (often shortened to the Federal Reserve, or simply "the Fed") is the central banking system of the United States. It was created on December 23, 1913, with the enactment of the Federal Reserve Act, largely in response to a series of financial panics — in particular the Panic of 1907 — which had exposed the dangers of an inelastic currency and the absence of a lender of last resort. Over the years the Fed's roles have expanded, and today it conducts the nation's monetary policy, supervises and regulates banks, maintains the stability of the financial system, and provides financial services to depository institutions and the federal government.

Structure

The system combines public and private elements. The Board of Governors in Washington, D.C. is a federal agency whose seven members are appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for staggered 14-year terms; its chair serves a four-year renewable term. Twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks — in Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Cleveland, Richmond, Atlanta, Chicago, St. Louis, Minneapolis, Kansas City, Dallas and San Francisco — operate as separately chartered corporations serving their districts; the Federal Reserve Bank of New York is the largest and most important, implementing policy and housing the system's open-market operations. Member banks hold stock in their regional Reserve Bank, though it confers none of the usual rights of ownership.

Monetary policy

Monetary policy is set by the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), comprising the seven governors and five Reserve Bank presidents voting in rotation (with New York always voting). Congress has given the Fed a "dual mandate": maximum employment and stable prices, interpreted since 2012 as inflation of 2 per cent in the personal consumption expenditures price index. Its principal tools are the target federal funds rate, interest on reserve balances, open market operations in Treasury and agency securities, the discount window, and — since the financial crisis of 2007–08 — large-scale asset purchases ("quantitative easing").

History in brief

The Fed was criticised for its role in the Great Depression, then reformed by the Banking Acts of 1933 and 1935, which created the modern FOMC and federal deposit insurance alongside it. The Treasury–Fed Accord of 1951 restored its independence in setting interest rates. Under Paul Volcker (1979–1987) the Fed crushed double-digit inflation with very high interest rates; under Alan Greenspan it navigated the stock-market crash of 1987 and the long 1990s expansion. Ben Bernanke led it through the 2008 crisis and its aftermath of near-zero rates and quantitative easing; Janet Yellen, the first woman chair, oversaw normalisation; Jerome Powell, chair from 2018, confronted the pandemic shock of 2020 and the inflation surge of 2021–23.
