Election monitoring

Election monitoring is the observation of an election or referendum by independent third parties, typically from another country or from a non-governmental organisation (NGO), primarily to assess the conduct of the election process on the basis of national legislation and international election standards.

Observers monitor counting to assess whether an election is free and fair.

History

Modern international election monitoring grew after the Second World War and expanded dramatically after the end of the Cold War, when newly democratising states invited foreign observers to legitimise their transitions. The first systematic observation is often traced to the 1857 plebiscite in Moldavia and Wallachia, supervised by a commission of European powers.

Organisations

Major monitoring organisations include the OSCE's Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights (ODIHR), the European Union, the Commonwealth, the Organization of American States, the African Union, and non-governmental groups such as the Carter Center and the National Democratic Institute. Long-term observers assess the legal framework, voter registration, campaigning, and media coverage, while short-term observers watch voting, counting, and tabulation on election day.

Methods and criticism

Missions issue preliminary statements shortly after the vote and detailed final reports with recommendations. In 2005 dozens of organisations endorsed the UN Declaration of Principles for International Election Observation. Monitoring has been criticised where governments invite only friendly observers, where missions' cautious language blunts findings, or where "zombie" observation groups produce favourable reports to legitimise flawed elections.
