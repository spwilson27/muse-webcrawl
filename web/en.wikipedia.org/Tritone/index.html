Tritone

In music theory, the tritone is a musical interval composed of three adjacent whole tones (six semitones). For instance, the interval from F up to B above it is a tritone, as F–G, G–A and A–B are three whole tones. In twelve-tone equal temperament the tritone divides the octave exactly in half, spanning six semitones, and is enharmonically both an augmented fourth and a diminished fifth.

History and reputation

The tritone has long been regarded as a dissonant and unstable interval. It is often popularly claimed that the medieval church banned it as the diabolus in musica ("the devil in music"), though historians regard a formal ban as a myth; the phrase is first documented in the early eighteenth century, and theorists such as Guido d'Arezzo and later writers warned singers about the interval's difficulty rather than prohibiting it. In tonal harmony the tritone within the dominant seventh chord is a key driver of resolution to the tonic.

Uses

The tritone is central to dominant-function harmony, the tritone substitution in jazz, and expressive gestures in many genres: famous openings built on it include Bernstein's "Maria" from West Side Story, the theme of The Simpsons, and the riff of Black Sabbath's "Black Sabbath". In just intonation, several ratios (such as 45:32 for the augmented fourth) approximate the interval.

Properties

Because it bisects the octave symmetrically, inversion of a tritone yields another tritone, a property exploited in atonal and serial music. It is the only interval that remains unchanged, enharmonically, under inversion.
