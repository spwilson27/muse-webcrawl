Stop consonant

In phonetics, a stop consonant, also known as a plosive or oral stop, is a consonant in which the vocal tract is blocked so that all airflow ceases. The occlusion may be made with the tongue blade ([t], [d]), tongue body ([k], [ɡ]), lips ([p], [b]), or glottis ([ʔ]). It contrasts with nasal stops, which are also stops but at which air escapes through the nose; continuants (fricatives and approximants), which partially obstruct but do not fully cut off airflow; and affricates, which begin as stops but are released as fricatives.

Articulation

A stop typically has three phases: the approach (or closure phase), in which the articulators move to block the airflow; the hold (or occlusion), during which air pressure builds up behind the closure; and the release, in which the closure is opened and the built-up air escapes, often with a small burst of noise. When the release is accompanied by a significant delay before voicing of a following vowel begins, the stop is described as aspirated, as in the [pʰ] of English pin.

Common stops

All languages in the world have stops, and most have at least the voiceless stops [p], [t] and [k]. The most common voiced stops are [b], [d] and [ɡ]. A few languages lack one of these: Arabic has no [p] (except in loanwords), and Hawaiian lacks [t] (having [k] instead). Stops are classified in the International Phonetic Alphabet by place of articulation — bilabial, dental, alveolar, retroflex, palatal, velar, uvular and glottal — and by voicing.

Terminology

The term plosive is often used for oral stops; some phoneticians reserve "stop" for the broader class including nasals, since nasals also involve a complete oral closure. This distinction matters in phonology, where nasals and oral stops may pattern together as one natural class.
