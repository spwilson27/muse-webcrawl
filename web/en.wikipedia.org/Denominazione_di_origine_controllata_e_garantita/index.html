Denominazione di origine controllata e garantita

Denominazione di origine controllata e garantita (abbreviated DOCG; Italian for "controlled and guaranteed designation of origin") is the highest classification for Italian wines. The classification, introduced in 1980, requires that a wine satisfy all the regulations of its DOC zone, plus additional, stricter controls: production is limited to a smaller defined area, yields are lower, and every wine must pass chemical analysis and a government tasting panel.

DOCG bottles carry a numbered government seal over the capsule

History

The DOCG category was created to distinguish Italy's most prestigious wines and to restore confidence after quality scandals in the 1970s. The first wines elevated were Barolo and Barbaresco from Piedmont and Brunello di Montalcino and Vino Nobile di Montepulciano from Tuscany, all in 1980; Chianti followed in 1984.

Requirements

DOCG regulations specify the exact production zone (often narrower than the corresponding DOC), permitted grape varieties and blends, maximum yields per hectare, minimum ageing — frequently including time in barrel and bottle — and bottling within the zone. Quality is guaranteed by the numbered state seal (fascetta) applied to each bottle, allowing every bottle to be traced.

List and extent

There are more than 70 DOCG wines, concentrated in Piedmont and Tuscany but spread across most Italian regions, including sparkling wines such as Franciacorta and Prosecco Superiore (Conegliano Valdobbiadene), and sweet wines such as Recioto di Soave. The category also covers a small number of non-wine products by analogy in common usage, though legally DOCG applies to wine.
