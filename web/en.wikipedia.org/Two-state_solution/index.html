The two-state solution is a proposed approach to resolving the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, envisioning an independent State of Palestine existing alongside the State of Israel — two states for two peoples. For decades it has been the internationally preferred framework for a negotiated settlement.

History

The concept dates to the 1937 Peel Commission, which first proposed partition, and was adopted in the 1947 UN Partition Plan for Palestine (Resolution 181), accepted by Jewish leaders and rejected by Arab leaders. The idea returned as a serious diplomatic formula after the 1967 war, when UN Security Council Resolution 242 set "land for peace" principles. The 1993 Oslo Accords created the Palestinian Authority and a staged path toward Palestinian self-rule, and the 2002 Arab Peace Initiative offered Arab recognition of Israel in exchange for a Palestinian state.

Core disputes

The final-status issues the two sides must agree on are borders (typically discussed around the 1967 lines with mutually agreed land swaps), Israeli settlements in the West Bank, the status of Jerusalem (East Jerusalem is claimed as the Palestinian capital while Israel claims the undivided city), Palestinian refugees and their descendants (the "right of return"), and security arrangements, including demilitarization and control of borders.

Status

Support for the two-state solution has declined sharply in polls of both Israelis and Palestinians since the 2000s, amid settlement expansion, successive Gaza wars, and political fragmentation on both sides. Alternative proposals — the one-state solution, a confederation model, and unilateral plans — continue to be debated.
