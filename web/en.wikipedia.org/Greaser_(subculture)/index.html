Greaser (subculture)

The greasers were an American youth subculture of working-class teenagers and young adults that emerged in the 1950s, particularly in the northeastern and southern United States. The name derives from the greased-back hairstyles — pompadours, ducktails and quiffs slicked with pomade or petroleum jelly — favoured by members, and was also associated with work as mechanics. Greaser style centred on T-shirts, cuffed jeans, leather or denim jackets, engineer boots, and an enthusiasm for hot rods, motorcycles and rock and roll music.

Origins and character

Greaser culture grew out of post-war working-class and motorcycle-club scenes and was amplified by popular culture: Marlon Brando in The Wild One (1953) and James Dean in Rebel Without a Cause (1955) supplied its screen archetypes. The subculture drew in Italian-American, Hispanic and other working-class youth and was often contrasted, in schools and in fiction, with middle-class "preppies" or "socs" — the opposition dramatised in S. E. Hinton's 1967 novel The Outsiders.

In popular culture and later revivals

Nostalgia for the greaser era fuelled the 1970s revival embodied by the musical and film Grease (1978) and by Henry Winkler's "Fonzie" in Happy Days, while American Graffiti (1973) depicted the related hot-rod scene. Rockabilly revivals and Japanese and European rocker scenes have kept greaser fashion — pompadours, cuffed denim, leather jackets — alive in niche form to the present day.
