Magnetoreception

Magnetoreception is a sense that allows an organism to detect the Earth's magnetic field in order to perceive direction, altitude or location. It is present in many animals, including migratory birds, sea turtles, salmon, lobsters, honeybees, and some bacteria, and it may underlie their remarkable long-distance navigation abilities.

Mechanisms

Two main mechanisms are proposed. The radical-pair mechanism involves light-sensitive proteins called cryptochromes in the retina; magnetic fields subtly alter the chemistry of radical pairs, potentially giving birds a visual impression of field direction. The magnetite-based mechanism involves crystals of the iron mineral magnetite, found in the beaks of some birds and in other tissues, which could act as tiny compass needles coupled to the nervous system. Magnetotactic bacteria use chains of magnetite crystals (magnetosomes) to align with the field.

Evidence

Behavioural experiments show that many species respond to changes in magnetic field intensity and inclination. European robins, for example, orient using the field's inclination rather than its polarity. Whether humans possess a functional magnetic sense remains controversial, though cryptochromes exist in the human retina.
