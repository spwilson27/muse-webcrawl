Physiocracy

Physiocracy (from Greek φύσις physis, "nature", and κράτος kratos, "rule") is an economic theory developed by a group of 18th-century Age of Enlightenment French economists who believed that the wealth of nations derived solely from the value of "land agriculture" or "land development" and that agricultural products should be highly priced. Their theories originated in France and were most popular during the second half of the 18th century.

Key ideas

The school's founder, François Quesnay, presented the economy's circulation of wealth in his Tableau économique (1758), an early model of inter-sectoral flows. Physiocrats held that only agriculture produces a produit net (net surplus), that manufacturing and trade are "sterile", and that society should be governed by a natural order (ordre naturel) with free trade (laissez-faire) and a single tax on land (impôt unique).

Influence

Followers included Victor de Riqueti, Marquis de Mirabeau, and Anne Robert Jacques Turgot, who attempted reforms as French finance minister. Physiocracy influenced Adam Smith, though he rejected the claim that manufacturing was unproductive, and it anticipated aspects of modern national accounting.
