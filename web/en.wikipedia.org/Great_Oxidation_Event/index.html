Great Oxidation Event

Great Oxidation Event
WhenAbout 2.4–2.0 billion years ago (Paleoproterozoic)
CauseOxygenic photosynthesis by cyanobacteria
EffectsRise of atmospheric O₂; Huronian glaciation; mass extinction of anaerobic life; banded iron formations

The Great Oxidation Event (GOE), also called the Great Oxygenation Event or Oxygen Catastrophe, was a period early in Earth's history when the atmosphere and shallow oceans first experienced a sustained rise in the amount of oxygen, roughly 2.4 to 2.0 billion years ago during the Paleoproterozoic era.

Cause

Cyanobacteria had evolved oxygenic photosynthesis well before the GOE, but the oxygen they released was initially absorbed by chemical sinks — dissolved iron in the oceans and reduced minerals and gases — precipitating the banded iron formations. Once these sinks were saturated, free oxygen began to accumulate in the atmosphere, as recorded by the disappearance of mass-independent fractionation of sulfur isotopes around 2.4 billion years ago.

Consequences

Oxygen was toxic to most anaerobic life, making the GOE arguably the greatest mass extinction in Earth's history. Oxidation of atmospheric methane, a potent greenhouse gas, likely triggered the Huronian glaciation, possibly a "Snowball Earth". In the long term, oxygen enabled aerobic respiration, much larger energy yields, the formation of the protective ozone layer, and ultimately the evolution of complex eukaryotic life.
