Velocity of money

The velocity of money (or velocity of circulation) is a measurement of the rate at which money is exchanged in an economy. It is the number of times that money changes hands, or is used to purchase goods and services, during a given period. It is calculated as V = (P × Y) / M, where P×Y is nominal GDP and M is the money supply.

Interpretation

Velocity links the money supply to nominal output through the equation of exchange, MV = PY, central to the quantity theory of money and monetarism. High velocity suggests a buoyant economy in which money circulates rapidly; falling velocity — as in depressions, when households and banks hoard cash — can blunt monetary stimulus. U.S. M2 velocity peaked around the late 1990s and fell sharply after the 2008 financial crisis and again in 2020.

Determinants

Interest rates, payment technology, financial innovation, expectations of inflation, and the demand for money holdings all affect velocity, which is usually measured residually rather than observed directly — a fact that has drawn criticism of its usefulness as a stable behavioural parameter.
