Large-scale structure of the Universe

The large-scale structure of the Universe refers to the patterns of galaxies and matter on scales much larger than individual galaxies or galaxy groups—typically scales of hundreds of millions of light-years. On these scales the Universe exhibits a "cosmic web" of filaments, sheets (walls), clusters, and enormous nearly empty voids, with superclusters lying at the intersections of filaments.

Structures

Redshift surveys such as the CfA2 survey, the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS), and the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey revealed structures like the Great Wall (CfA2), the Sloan Great Wall—one of the largest known structures, about 1.4 billion light-years long—and giant voids such as the Boötes void. The Local Group belongs to the Laniakea Supercluster, identified in 2014, which spans some 500 million light-years. Studies suggest an "end of greatness" scale around 300 million light-years, above which the Universe becomes approximately homogeneous and isotropic, consistent with the cosmological principle.

Formation

In the standard ΛCDM model, structure grew by gravitational amplification of tiny primordial density fluctuations—imprinted in the cosmic microwave background—with cold dark matter collapsing first into halos into which ordinary matter fell. Baryon acoustic oscillations, fossil sound waves from the early Universe, provide a standard ruler (≈150 Mpc) used to measure cosmic expansion history and dark energy.
