Trochoid

Curtate, common (cycloid), and prolate trochoids

In geometry, a trochoid (from Greek τροχός trokhós 'wheel') is a roulette curve formed by a circle rolling along a straight line. It is the curve traced out by a point fixed to a circle (where the point may be on, inside, or outside the circle) as it rolls along a straight line. If the point is on the circle, the trochoid is called a cycloid; if inside, it is curtate; if outside, it is prolate (forming loops).

Equations

If a circle of radius r rolls along the x-axis, and the tracing point is at distance d from the centre, the curve is given parametrically by

x = rθ − d sin θ,   y = r − d cos θ

where θ is the rolling angle. When d = r the equations reduce to those of the cycloid; when d < r the curve is a smooth wave (curtate); when d > r it loops back on itself (prolate).

Applications

Trochoidal curves appear in engineering and physics. The path of a point on a paddle wheel, the profile of certain rotary-engine housings (related to the epitrochoid traced when rolling around another circle), and the waveform of deep-water ocean waves (Gerstner's trochoidal wave) are all trochoidal. Trochoidal milling is a machining strategy in which the cutter follows a trochoid-like path. The classic Spirograph toy draws related roulette curves called hypotrochoids and epitrochoids.

The cycloid — the special case traced by a point on the rim — has famous properties: it is the brachistochrone curve (the curve of fastest descent) and the tautochrone curve (on which a sliding bead reaches the bottom in the same time regardless of starting point).
