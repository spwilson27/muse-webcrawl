Thomas precession

Thomas precession, named after Llewellyn Thomas who described it in 1926, is a relativistic correction in special relativity that applies to the spin of an elementary particle or the rotation of a macroscopic gyroscope and relates the angular velocity of the spin to the angular velocity of the particle's orbit. It arises because Lorentz boosts in different directions do not commute: the composition of two non-collinear boosts is equivalent to a boost combined with a rotation, known as a Wigner rotation.

Explanation

For a particle moving along a curved trajectory, successive infinitesimal Lorentz transformations between its instantaneous rest frames produce not only the expected boost but also a small rotation of the particle's spatial axes. A spin carried along with the particle therefore precesses relative to an inertial frame even in the absence of any torque. For an object with velocity v and acceleration a, the Thomas precession angular frequency is, to lowest order in v/c,

ωT ≈ (1/2c²) a × v,

with the exact relativistic expression containing the Lorentz factor γ as ωT = (γ²/(γ+1)) (a × v)/c².

Applications

Thomas precession is essential in atomic physics: it accounts for a factor of one-half in the spin–orbit interaction of an electron in an atom. An electron orbiting a nucleus experiences, in its rest frame, a magnetic field due to the nucleus's motion, which would naively give twice the observed fine-structure splitting; the Thomas precession of the electron's spin corrects this, yielding the well-known "Thomas half" and agreement with observation. The effect also enters the dynamics of gyroscopes in orbit and the Bargmann–Michel–Telegdi equation describing spin motion in electromagnetic fields.
