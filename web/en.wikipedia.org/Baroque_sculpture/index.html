Baroque sculpture

Baroque sculpture is the sculpture associated with the Baroque style of the period between the early seventeenth century and the mid-eighteenth century. It was characterised by dynamic movement, emotional intensity, and the illusion of figures breaking free of their architectural settings — in deliberate contrast to the poised, self-contained balance of Renaissance sculpture. Baroque sculptors sought to capture a dramatic instant: swirling draperies, straining bodies, and faces caught in moments of ecstasy, terror, or transformation.

Italy and Bernini

The dominant figure was Gian Lorenzo Bernini (1598–1680), whose works for Roman patrons defined the style: the Apollo and Daphne and David for Cardinal Scipione Borghese, the Ecstasy of Saint Teresa (1647–1652) in the Cornaro Chapel — in which marble seems to dissolve into mystical light — and the vast Fountain of the Four Rivers in Piazza Navona. Working under the patronage of the popes, Baroque sculpture served the Catholic Counter-Reformation, aiming to move viewers to devotion through sensory splendour. Other leading Italian sculptors included Alessandro Algardi and the stucco virtuoso Giacomo Serpotta in Sicily.

France, Spain, and beyond

In France the classical strain of Baroque predominated at the court of Louis XIV, where sculptors such as François Girardon and Antoine Coysevox decorated Versailles with more restrained, antique-inspired grandeur. Spain developed a distinctive tradition of polychromed wood sculpture of intense realism, used in religious processions, by artists such as Gregorio Fernández and Pedro de Mena. Related styles spread through Flanders, Germany, Austria, and colonial Latin America, where local materials and craftspeople transformed European models.

Technique

Baroque sculptors exploited daring undercutting, mixed materials (coloured marbles, gilded bronze, stucco), concealed light sources, and compositions that spill into the viewer's space, reinforcing the period's theatre-like unity of architecture, painting, and sculpture — the bel composto.
