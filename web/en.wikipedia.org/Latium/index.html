Latium

Latium is the region of central western Italy in which the city of Rome was founded and grew to be the capital city of the Roman Empire. Its inhabitants, the Latins, spoke Latin, the language from which the region's name and the Romance languages derive. The modern Italian region of Lazio corresponds roughly, though not exactly, to ancient Latium.

Geography

Ancient Latium in its earliest sense (Latium vetus) was a small, fertile triangle of volcanic plain bounded by the lower Tiber River to the northwest, the Apennines to the east, and the Alban Hills and the Pomptine marshes to the south. It included towns such as Alba Longa, Tusculum, Tibur (Tivoli), Praeneste (Palestrina), Lanuvium, and Ardea. Roman expansion later extended the name to Latium adiectum, reaching southeast along the coast toward Campania as far as the Garigliano River and including Antium, Terracina, and Formiae.

History

Latium was settled by Italic peoples in the early Iron Age; the Latial culture of the region is the archaeological setting for the traditional founding of Rome in 753 BC. The Latin cities formed the Latin League, a religious and defensive association centred on the sanctuary of Jupiter Latiaris on the Alban Mount. After conflicts including the Latin War (340–338 BC), Rome dissolved the League and absorbed the Latins, granting many of them Roman citizenship. Under Augustus, Latium together with Campania formed the first region (Regio I) of Roman Italy.
