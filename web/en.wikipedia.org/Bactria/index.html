Bactria (Bactrian: Bakhlo; Greek: Βακτριανή) was an ancient region of Central Asia, located between the Hindu Kush mountain range to the south and the Oxus river (modern Amu Darya) to the north, corresponding roughly to present-day northern Afghanistan together with parts of southern Tajikistan and Uzbekistan. Its capital was Bactra, near modern Balkh. Fertile and well irrigated, Bactria stood at a crossroads of trade routes linking Persia, India and China, and it played a major role in the history of Zoroastrianism, Hellenism in the East, and Buddhism's spread along the Silk Road.

Early history

In the Bronze Age, the region formed part of the Bactria–Margiana Archaeological Complex (BMAC, c. 2300–1700 BCE), characterised by fortified oasis settlements. Bactria later became associated in Iranian tradition with the prophet Zoroaster, whom some traditions place in the region.

Achaemenid and Macedonian periods

Bactria was conquered by Cyrus the Great in the 6th century BCE and became one of the wealthiest satrapies of the Achaemenid Empire, supplying troops including its famed cavalry. Alexander the Great campaigned there in 329–327 BCE against the resistance of Spitamenes, married the Bactrian noblewoman Roxana, and founded Greek cities such as Alexandria Eschate and Ai-Khanoum.

Greco-Bactrian and Indo-Greek kingdoms

Under the Seleucids Bactria remained a rich province; around 256 BCE its satrap Diodotus I declared independence, founding the Greco-Bactrian Kingdom. Kings such as Euthydemus I and Demetrius I expanded Greek rule into northern India, giving rise to the Indo-Greek kingdoms, whose bilingual coinage and fusion of Greek and Indian art (Gandhara art) left a lasting legacy. The most famous Indo-Greek king, Menander I (Milinda), is remembered in the Buddhist text Milindapanha.

Later history

Nomadic invasions by the Xiongnu-driven Yuezhi in the 2nd century BCE ended Greek rule; the Yuezhi's Kushan Empire (1st–3rd centuries CE) made Bactria — by then often called Tokharistan — a centre of Mahayana Buddhism and Silk Road commerce. The region was subsequently ruled by Sasanians, Hephthalites, Turks and, from the 8th century, the Arab caliphates.
