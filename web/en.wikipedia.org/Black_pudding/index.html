Black pudding

Slices of black pudding in a cooked breakfast.

Black pudding is a distinct type of blood sausage originating in the United Kingdom and Ireland. It is made from pork or occasionally beef blood, with pork fat or beef suet, and a cereal, usually oatmeal, oat groats, or barley groats. The high proportion of cereal, along with the use of certain herbs such as pennyroyal, distinguishes it from blood sausages eaten elsewhere in the world.

History and regional varieties

Blood sausages are among the oldest forms of sausage, mentioned as early as Homer's Odyssey. Black pudding is a traditional component of the full breakfast in England, Scotland, Ireland, and parts of Canada. Regional varieties exist across the British Isles: Stornoway black pudding from the Isle of Lewis holds Protected Geographical Indication status and is regarded as one of the finest; Lancashire, and particularly the town of Bury, is famous for its black puddings, traditionally boiled and eaten with mustard or as a snack. Irish varieties include drisheen from Cork. The town of Ramsbottom hosts a World Black Pudding Throwing Championship.

Preparation and nutrition

The mixture is stuffed into casings and simmered; slices are usually grilled or fried before serving. Black pudding is rich in iron, zinc, protein, and potassium, though also high in fat and salt; it gained popular attention in 2016 when some press reports labelled it a "superfood."

Similar sausages include French boudin noir, Spanish morcilla, and white pudding, a related British and Irish sausage made without blood.
