Architrave

An architrave (from Italian architrave, "chief beam", derived from Greek ἀρχι- and Latin trabs beam) is the lintel or beam, typically made of wood or stone, that rests on the capitals of columns. It is an architectural element in Classical architecture.

Overview

In classical architecture the architrave is the lowest part of the entablature, resting directly on the capitals of the columns. The architrave forms the basis of how the classical orders in architecture are defined. The term can also apply to all sides, including the vertical members, of a frame with mouldings around a door or window. The word is sometimes used to mean the inner side of the moulding above a door, especially when talking of the plastic arts, and in Judaism the term is used for the beam placed over the forehead of a doorpost.

In classical architecture

In an entablature in classical architecture, it is the lowest part, below the frieze and cornice. The word derives from the Greek and Latin words arche and trabs combined together to mean "main beam". The proportions take a different shape in different styles. In the Tuscan order, it only carries a plain face, crowned with a fillet, and is 7 modules in height.

Indian architecture

In śilpaśāstra, the ancient Indian science of sculpture, the architrave is commonly called bārah. It is present in a Hindu temple to support the weight of the structure above it.

Non-classical uses

The term architrave is also used in carpentry and joinery for the moulded frame around a doorway, window, or other rectangular opening, where it performs a decorative function covering the joint between the wall and the frame. It is also used to mean the lintel that sits over the top of an opening.
