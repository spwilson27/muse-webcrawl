Goodfellas (stylised as GoodFellas) is a 1990 American biographical crime film directed by Martin Scorsese, written by Scorsese and Nicholas Pileggi, and produced by Irwin Winkler. It is a film adaptation of the 1985 non-fiction book Wiseguy by Pileggi. Starring Ray Liotta, Robert De Niro, Joe Pesci, Lorraine Bracco and Paul Sorvino, the film narrates the rise and fall of mob associate Henry Hill and his friends and family from 1955 to 1980.

Scorsese initially titled the film Wise Guy and postponed making it; he and Pileggi later changed the title to Goodfellas. To prepare for their roles in the film, De Niro, Pesci and Liotta often spoke with Pileggi, who shared research material left over from writing the book. According to Pesci, improvisation and ad-libbing came out of rehearsals wherein Scorsese gave the actors freedom to do whatever they wanted. The director made transcripts of these sessions, took the lines he liked best and put them into a revised script, from which the cast worked during principal photography.

Plot

Growing up in Brooklyn, teenager Henry Hill idolises the Lucchese crime family gangsters across the street and goes to work for local boss Paulie Cicero, rising from errand boy to trusted associate — though, being of Irish-Sicilian descent, he can never be a "made man". With the volatile Tommy DeVito and the charismatic Jimmy Conway, Henry enjoys the money, status, and impunity of mob life, participates in scores including the 1978 Lufthansa heist at JFK Airport — then the largest cash robbery in American history — and marries Karen, who is drawn into the lifestyle. The murders following the heist, Henry's descent into cocaine addiction and dealing, and an increasingly paranoid final day culminate in his arrest in 1980; facing prison and believing Jimmy plans to kill him, Henry enters the witness protection programme and testifies against his former associates, ending as "an average nobody" in suburbia.

Reception and legacy

Goodfellas premiered at the Venice Film Festival, where Scorsese won the Silver Lion for Best Director, and grossed $47 million against a $25 million budget. It was nominated for six Academy Awards, with Pesci winning Best Supporting Actor for his role as the psychopathic Tommy. Widely regarded as one of the greatest films ever made, particularly in the crime genre, it was selected for preservation in the National Film Registry by the Library of Congress in 2000 as "culturally, historically, and aesthetically significant". Its kinetic style — freeze frames, long Steadicam shots such as the Copacabana entrance, rapid editing, dual voiceover narration, and a soundtrack of period pop songs — has been hugely influential on film and television, including The Sopranos, whose cast includes several Goodfellas actors.
