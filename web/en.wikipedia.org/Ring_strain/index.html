Ring strain

In organic chemistry, ring strain is a type of instability that exists when bonds in a molecule form angles that are abnormal. Strain is most commonly discussed for small or uncommon rings such as cyclopropanes, cyclobutanes and cyclobutanes, whose internal angles are substantially smaller than the ideal tetrahedral angle of approximately 109°.

Causes

Adolf von Baeyer proposed in 1885 that strain arises from deviation of bond angles from the tetrahedral ideal (Baeyer strain). Ring strain also includes torsional strain from eclipsing interactions and transannular (Prelog) strain in medium rings. Cyclopropane, with 60° bond angles, has a total strain energy of about 115 kJ/mol; cyclohexane, able to adopt the chair conformation, is essentially strain-free.

Consequences

High ring strain makes small rings unusually reactive: cyclopropane and cyclobutane derivatives readily undergo ring-opening reactions, and strained systems such as epoxides are important synthetic intermediates. Strain-release is exploited in areas from polymer chemistry (ring-opening polymerisation) to materials such as propellanes.
