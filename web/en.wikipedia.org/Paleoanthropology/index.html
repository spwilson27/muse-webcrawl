Paleoanthropology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Paleoanthropology or palaeoanthropology is a subfield of anthropology and palaeontology concerned with the study of human evolution. It reconstructs the biology, behaviour, and evolutionary history of hominins — humans and their fossil relatives — from skeletal remains, archaeological traces such as stone tools, geological context, and, increasingly, ancient DNA.

Scope and methods

Palaeoanthropologists excavate and analyse fossil remains, dating them with methods including radiometric dating of volcanic layers, palaeomagnetism, and electron spin resonance. Comparative anatomy, biomechanics, and CT imaging of fossils are used to infer locomotion, diet, brain size, growth, and life history. Genetic studies of ancient and modern DNA complement the fossil record, revealing population divergences and interbreeding between lineages such as Neanderthals, Denisovans, and early modern humans.

History of the field

The discipline grew from nineteenth-century discoveries of Neanderthal remains and the acceptance of human antiquity. Landmark finds reshaped it repeatedly: the Taung Child (Australopithecus africanus, 1924), which placed human origins in Africa; the extensive record from Olduvai Gorge and Laetoli in East Africa; "Lucy" (Australopithecus afarensis, 1974); the near-complete Turkana Boy (Homo ergaster); and the tiny island species Homo floresiensis (2003) and Homo naledi (2015).

Major questions

Key debates include the origins and adaptive significance of bipedalism, the timing and number of dispersals out of Africa, the roles of climate variability in driving evolutionary change, encephalisation and the emergence of language and symbolic behaviour, and the causes of the extinction of archaic human populations.
