Swiss Alps

The Swiss Alps are the portion of the Alps mountain range lying within Switzerland, covering about 65% of the country's surface area and defining its landscape, climate and identity. They form part of the great Alpine arc created by the collision of the African and Eurasian plates, and drain into four of Europe's great river systems: the Rhine, Rhône, Po (via Ticino) and Danube (via the Inn).

Geography

The range is traditionally divided by the Rhône and Rhine valleys and the Furka and St. Gotthard passes into the Western and Eastern Alps, and into regions including the Bernese Alps, Pennine Alps, Glarus Alps, Lepontine Alps and Rhaetian Alps. The highest point is the Dufourspitze of Monte Rosa at 4,634 metres; the most famous peak is the Matterhorn (4,478 m), first climbed in 1865 by Edward Whymper's party in an ascent that ended with four climbers' deaths. The Alps contain Europe's largest glaciers west of the Caucasus, including the Aletsch Glacier, a UNESCO World Heritage site — though Swiss glaciers are retreating rapidly with climate warming.

Transport, economy and culture

Historic passes — the Great St Bernard, Simplon, Gotthard and San Bernardino — have linked northern and southern Europe since Roman times; today they are crossed by celebrated railways and tunnels, including the Gotthard Base Tunnel (opened 2016), the world's longest railway tunnel. The mountains support hydroelectric power, alpine farming and transhumance, and a tourism industry built on mountaineering since the golden age of alpinism in the 19th century, and on skiing at resorts such as Zermatt, St. Moritz, Davos and Verbier. The Alps are also a global symbol of Switzerland, central to its culture from Heidi to the Matterhorn imagery on Swiss products.
