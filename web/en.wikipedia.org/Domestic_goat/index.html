Domestic goat

Scientific nameCapra aegagrus hircus
AncestorBezoar ibex (Capra aegagrus)
Global populationover 1 billion

The domestic goat (Capra aegagrus hircus) is a domesticated subspecies of the wild goat of Southwest Asia and Eastern Europe. One of the earliest animals domesticated by humans — around 10,000 years ago in the Zagros Mountains region — the goat is kept worldwide for its milk, meat, fibre, and skin, and as a pack and companion animal.

Biology and behaviour

Goats are ruminant browsers with a four-chambered stomach, horizontal slit-shaped pupils, and a famously curious, exploratory temperament. Both sexes may bear horns and beards; males (bucks or billies) typically outweigh females (does or nannies). Goats are sure-footed climbers and can thrive on sparse forage that cattle cannot use, a hardiness that also makes feral goats destructive to island ecosystems.

Uses

Goat milk accounts for a major share of world milk consumption outside the West and is made into cheeses such as feta and chèvre. Goat meat (chevon, or cabrito) is the most widely eaten red meat globally. Fibre breeds produce mohair (Angora goats) and cashmere; pygmy and Nigerian Dwarf goats are kept as pets, and goats are used for targeted grazing to clear brush and reduce wildfire fuel.

Culture

Goats feature prominently in mythology and religion — from the Norse Thor's chariot goats and the Greek god Pan to the scapegoat of Leviticus — and in idioms and folklore across cultures.
