Patient autonomy is the principle in medical ethics that patients have the right to make informed decisions about their own medical care, free from coercion. Derived from the Greek autos ("self") and nomos ("rule"), autonomy in the framework of Beauchamp and Childress's Principles of Biomedical Ethics is one of the "four principles" of bioethics, alongside beneficence, non-maleficence and justice. It marks the shift away from medical paternalism — captured in the older legal maxim that "every human being of adult years and sound mind has a right to determine what shall be done with his own body" (Justice Cardozo, Schloendorff v. Society of New York Hospital, 1914).

Requirements and instruments

Respecting autonomy presupposes that the patient has decision-making capacity: the ability to understand relevant information, appreciate its significance for their situation, reason about options, and communicate a choice. Its principal legal and ethical instrument is informed consent, which requires disclosure of the nature, benefits, risks and alternatives of a proposed intervention. Autonomy is also projected into future incapacity through advance directives, living wills and powers of attorney for health care, and is expressed in the right to refuse even life-sustaining treatment.

Limits and tensions

Autonomy is not unlimited. It can conflict with beneficence when patients refuse beneficial treatment, with public health when refusal endangers others (for example in infectious disease), and with justice in allocating scarce resources. Questions arise for patients lacking capacity — children, some people with dementia or severe mental illness — where surrogate decision-making and best-interests standards apply. Critics note that autonomy as practised is individualistic, and that in many cultures decisions are made relationally, within families; "relational autonomy" attempts to reconcile self-determination with such contexts. Debates over assisted dying, conscientious objection and genetic information all turn in part on the scope of patient autonomy.
