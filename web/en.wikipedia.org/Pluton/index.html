Pluton

In geology, a pluton is a body of intrusive igneous rock (called a plutonic rock) that is crystallized from magma slowly cooling below the surface of the Earth. Plutons represent the "plumbing system" of volcanoes that solidified at depth and may later be exposed by erosion. Most plutons are thought to be the magma chambers that fed volcanoes.

Classification

Plutons are classified by size, shape, and relation to the surrounding country rock. A batholith is a large pluton with more than 100 km² exposed at the surface; a smaller body is a stock. Tabular plutons concordant with layering are called sills or, when bowl- or dome-shaped, laccoliths and lopoliths; discordant tabular bodies are dikes. Plutons may be composed of granite, granodiorite, diorite, gabbro, and other intrusive rocks, typically with coarse-grained (phaneritic) textures reflecting slow cooling.

Emplacement

How magma makes room for itself in the crust—the "pluton emplacement problem"—has been debated for a century. Proposed mechanisms include stoping (detaching and sinking blocks of country rock), diapirism (buoyant rise of magma bodies), assimilation, roof uplift, and incremental assembly: many plutons are now understood to have been built sheet by sheet over hundreds of thousands to millions of years rather than emplaced as single huge molten chambers, as shown by high-precision uranium–lead dating of zircons.

Exposure and significance

Deep erosion of mountain belts exposes plutons, as in the Sierra Nevada Batholith of California, the Coast Plutonic Complex of British Columbia, and the Cornubian batholith of southwest England. Plutons are important economically for associated deposits of tin, tungsten, copper, gold, and other metals concentrated by late-stage magmatic fluids, and scientifically for what they reveal about crustal growth and magma evolution.

Plutonism

The term derives from Pluto, Roman god of the underworld. Historically, "Plutonism" was the 18th–19th-century geological doctrine—associated with James Hutton—that rocks formed through heat and igneous processes, opposed to Neptunism, which attributed rocks to precipitation from a primeval ocean.
