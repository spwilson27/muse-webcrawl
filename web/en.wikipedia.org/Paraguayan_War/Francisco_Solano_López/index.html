Francisco Solano López Carrillo (24 July 1827 – 1 March 1870) was the second president of Paraguay, ruling from 1862 until his death in 1870. He is widely regarded as the central figure of the Paraguayan War (1864–1870), the bloodiest interstate conflict in South American history, in which Paraguay fought against the Triple Alliance of Brazil, Argentina, and Uruguay.

Early life and rise

López was the eldest son of president Carlos Antonio López, who had modernized Paraguay and kept it relatively prosperous and isolated. The younger López was educated partly in Europe, served as a diplomat, and became commander of the Paraguayan army. On his father's death in 1862 he succeeded to the presidency, inheriting one of the most militarized states in South America.

The Paraguayan War

Determined to make Paraguay a regional power and to defend its access to the sea, López intervened in the Uruguayan civil war, seized a Brazilian steamer, invaded the Brazilian province of Mato Grosso in December 1864, and then invaded the Argentine province of Corrientes in 1865. Brazil, Argentina, and Uruguay formed the Triple Alliance against him. Despite disastrous defeats such as the Battle of Tuyutí, López refused all peace terms and fought on until he was killed at the Battle of Cerro Corá on 1 March 1870, reportedly declaring "I die with my fatherland."

Legacy

The war devastated Paraguay, which lost a large share of its population and territory. López remains a controversial figure: condemned by many historians as a reckless dictator, he is nonetheless revered in Paraguay as a national hero who resisted annihilation.
