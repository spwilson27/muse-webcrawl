Byzantine literature

Byzantine literature is the Greek literature of the Middle Ages, written in the Byzantine Empire from the 4th century until the fall of Constantinople in 1453. It continues the traditions of ancient Greek literature — epic, historiography, rhetoric, philosophy and theology — while absorbing Christian and, especially in its later vernacular works, folk influences.

Language and forms

Byzantine writing is marked by diglossia: learned authors wrote in an archaizing literary Greek modelled on classical Attic, while a vernacular Greek closer to the spoken language appears in chronicles, saints' lives, and the vernacular romances and epics of the later period. The great bulk of surviving Byzantine literature is religious: hymnography (such as the kontakia of Romanos the Melodist), hagiography, sermons and theological treatises.

Secular writing

Historiography was the empire's most prestigious secular genre, from Procopius in the 6th century through the chronicle tradition to Anna Komnene's Alexiad and the historians of the Palaiologan period. Encyclopedism flourished under Constantine VII, whose compilations preserved much ancient material. The vernacular epic Digenes Akritas, celebrating a frontier hero of the Arab wars, is often called the national epic of medieval Greece.

Legacy

Byzantine scribes copied and preserved the overwhelming majority of surviving classical Greek literature. After 1453, émigré scholars carried manuscripts and learning to Italy, contributing directly to the Renaissance revival of Greek studies in the West.
