Great Flood (China)

The Yellow River, whose floods stand behind the Great Flood legend.

The Great Flood of Chinese mythology, also known as the Gun–Yu flood myth, is a foundational legend describing a devastating deluge that lasted for generations and was finally controlled through the efforts of the hero Yu the Great. The myth is set during the reign of the legendary Emperor Yao and forms an important part of early Chinese accounts of the origins of civilization, agriculture, and the Xia dynasty.

The myth

According to the traditional account, a catastrophic flood inundated the land during Yao's reign. Yao appointed Gun to control the waters; Gun spent nine years building dams and dikes, even stealing xirang, a magical self-expanding soil, from the gods in his attempt. His efforts failed, and he was executed at Feather Mountain. His son Yu took up the task and pursued the opposite strategy: instead of damming the waters, he spent thirteen years dredging channels and guiding the floodwaters to the sea, passing his own home three times without entering. Yu's success led to his selection as successor to Emperor Shun and, traditionally, to his founding of the Xia dynasty.

Historical background

Scholars have long debated whether the myth preserves a memory of real flooding on the Yellow River plain. In 2016, researchers reported geological evidence of a massive outburst flood from a landslide-dammed lake at Jishi Gorge on the Yellow River, dated to about 1920 BCE, which they proposed as a possible historical basis for the legend and for the chronology of the Xia dynasty. The interpretation remains debated among historians and archaeologists.
