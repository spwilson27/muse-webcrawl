Phylogenetics

Phylogenetics is the study of the evolutionary history and relationships among or within groups of organisms. These relationships are determined by phylogenetic inference methods that focus on observed heritable traits, such as DNA sequences, protein amino acid sequences, or morphology. The result of such an analysis is a phylogenetic tree—a diagram showing a hypothesis of the order in which evolutionary divergences are thought to have occurred.

Methods

Classical methods rely on morphological characters and were formalized by Willi Hennig's cladistics, which groups taxa by shared derived characters (synapomorphies). Molecular phylogenetics uses sequence data and statistical methods such as maximum parsimony, maximum likelihood, and Bayesian inference, together with models of molecular evolution. The concept of the molecular clock allows divergence times to be estimated from genetic distances.

Applications

Phylogenetics underpins modern biological classification, the reconstruction of the tree of life, and the study of how traits evolve. It is used in epidemiology to track the spread of pathogens, in conservation biology to prioritize evolutionarily distinct lineages, and in forensics and medicine.

History

The idea of a "tree of life" goes back to Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859). In the 20th century, Hennig's Phylogenetic Systematics (1950, English 1966) and the advent of protein and DNA sequencing transformed the field. Today, whole-genome data and computational methods allow phylogenies of thousands of taxa.
