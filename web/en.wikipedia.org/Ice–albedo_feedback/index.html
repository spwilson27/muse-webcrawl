Ice–albedo feedback

Ice–albedo feedback is a positive feedback climate process in which a change in the area of ice caps, glaciers and sea ice alters the albedo (reflectivity) of a planet's surface. Bright ice reflects much incoming solar radiation back to space; when ice melts, it is replaced by darker ocean or land, which absorbs more sunlight, warming the surface further and causing yet more ice to melt.

Role in climate change

The feedback is a major contributor to polar amplification — the faster warming of the Arctic compared with the global average. Declining Arctic sea ice since the late 1970s has measurably reduced Earth's northern albedo. The same feedback, run in reverse, helped drive past glaciations: as ice sheets grew, more sunlight was reflected, cooling the planet further. In extreme form, it is central to Snowball Earth hypotheses.

Modelling

Ice–albedo feedback was introduced into early energy-balance climate models by Mikhail Budyko and William Sellers in 1969, demonstrating that the climate system could have multiple stable states. Modern models include the process along with surface-melt pond and vegetation effects.
