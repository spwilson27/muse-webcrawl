Kyushu

Kyushu, the southernmost of Japan's four main islands.

Kyushu (Japanese: 九州, "Nine Provinces") is the third-largest and southernmost of Japan's four main islands, with an area of about 36,782 square kilometres and a population of roughly 14 million including the associated Ryukyu chain administration. It is separated from Honshu by the Kanmon Straits and is composed of seven prefectures: Fukuoka, Saga, Nagasaki, Kumamoto, Oita, Miyazaki, and Kagoshima.

Geography

Kyushu is mountainous and volcanic: Mount Aso, one of the world's largest active calderas, lies in Kumamoto Prefecture, and Sakurajima near Kagoshima erupts frequently. The island has a subtropical climate in its south, abundant hot springs (notably Beppu and Yufuin), and the long Ariake Sea coastline. The Chikugo River is its longest river.

History

Kyushu was a cradle of early Japanese civilization and the gateway for continental culture from Korea and China. The Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281 were repelled on its northern shores with the aid of typhoons — the original kamikaze. Nagasaki, its great port, was Japan's sole window to the outside world during the Edo-period isolation, hosting Dutch and Chinese traders at Dejima; it was also the second city destroyed by an atomic bomb, on 9 August 1945. Satsuma domain (Kagoshima) played a leading role in the Meiji Restoration.

Economy

Northern Kyushu around Fukuoka and Kitakyushu is industrial — steel, automobiles, and semiconductors have earned it the nickname "Silicon Island" — while the south is known for agriculture, shochu, and tourism.
