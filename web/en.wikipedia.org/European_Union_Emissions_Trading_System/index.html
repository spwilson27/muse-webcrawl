European Union Emissions Trading System

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The European Union Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) is a carbon emission trading scheme (or cap-and-trade scheme) that began in 2005 and is intended to help the European Union meet its greenhouse-gas reduction goals under the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement. It is the world's second-largest emissions trading system after China's, and the first major one, covering around 40% of the EU's greenhouse gas emissions.

How it works

The system sets a cap on total emissions from installations in the energy sector, energy-intensive industries (such as steel, cement, chemicals, and refining), and aviation within Europe. Companies receive or buy emission allowances (EUAs), each permitting one tonne of CO2-equivalent, and must surrender allowances equal to their verified emissions each year. Allowances can be traded, creating a carbon price. The cap declines annually — by a linear reduction factor raised to 4.3% under the "Fit for 55" reforms — so that covered emissions fall over time.

Phases

The EU ETS has operated in phases: a pilot phase (2005–2007) marked by over-allocation and a price collapse; a second phase (2008–2012) aligned with the Kyoto commitment period; a third phase (2013–2020) introducing EU-wide cap setting and more auctioning; and a fourth phase (2021–2030). A Market Stability Reserve began operating in 2019 to absorb the surplus of allowances and support prices, which rose to around €80–100 per tonne in 2022–2023.

Expansion

Under legislation agreed in 2023, a separate system (ETS2) will cover fuels used in buildings, road transport, and small industry from 2027, while maritime transport emissions are being phased into the main EU ETS. Revenues from auctioning fund the EU Innovation Fund and Modernisation Fund and member-state climate spending.
