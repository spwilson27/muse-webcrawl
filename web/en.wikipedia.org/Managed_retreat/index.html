Managed retreat

Managed retreat is the deliberate, coordinated movement of people, infrastructure and other assets away from areas exposed to natural hazards, such as coastlines threatened by sea-level rise and erosion or land subject to repeated flooding. In coastal engineering the related term managed realignment describes the deliberate breaching or relocation of flood defences to allow the shoreline to move inland, often creating intertidal habitat such as salt marsh.

Approaches

Managed retreat is one of three broad responses to coastal hazards, alongside holding the line with hard defences and accommodating the hazard. Instruments include government buyout programmes for flood-prone homes (used extensively after Hurricane Sandy in the United States), rolling easements, land-use planning and zoning, and relocation of whole communities — for example the resettlement of the Isle de Jean Charles community in Louisiana and of Fijian villages threatened by rising seas.

Debates

Retreat can be cost-effective and ecologically beneficial but raises difficult questions of compensation, property rights, cultural attachment to place, and equity, since retreat may be easiest for wealthy owners and most disruptive for low-income and Indigenous communities. Scholars increasingly frame retreat as a long-term adaptation strategy requiring community participation rather than a purely technical measure.
