An electric current is a flow of charged particles, such as electrons or ions, moving through an electrical conductor or space. It is defined as the net rate of flow of electric charge through a surface. The moving particles are called charge carriers, which may be electrons, ions, or holes, depending on the conductor. In electric circuits, the charge carriers are often electrons moving through a wire.

Definition and the ampere

Current is measured in amperes: one ampere is one coulomb of charge passing a point per second. Since the 2019 redefinition of SI base units, the ampere is fixed by the elementary charge, exactly 1.602176634×10−19 coulombs. Current is measured with an ammeter connected in series with the circuit.

Conventional current

By a convention dating to Benjamin Franklin, current direction is defined as the direction positive charge would flow — opposite to the actual drift of electrons in metals. This "conventional current" is used in circuit analysis regardless of the true carriers. André-Marie Ampère, after whom the unit is named, established key laws linking current and magnetism in the 1820s.

Direct and alternating current

In direct current (DC), charge flows steadily in one direction, as from batteries and rectified supplies. In alternating current (AC), the flow reverses periodically — 50 or 60 times per second in mains electricity — which permits efficient transformation of voltages and long-distance transmission. Ohm's law relates current to voltage and resistance: I = V/R.

Effects

Currents produce heating (Joule heating), magnetic fields (the basis of electromagnets and motors), and chemical changes (electrolysis). Currents through the human body as low as tens of milliamperes can be lethal, which is why protective devices such as fuses, circuit breakers, and residual-current devices are standard.
