Grapheme–color synesthesia

Grapheme–color synesthesia (or coloured grapheme synesthesia) is a form of synesthesia in which an individual's perception of numerals and letters is associated with the experience of colors. Like all forms of synesthesia, grapheme–color synesthesia is involuntary, consistent and memorable.

Characteristics

Synesthetes report that each letter of the alphabet and each digit has a specific, consistent color — for example, the letter A might always be experienced as red, 5 as green, and so on. The associations are idiosyncratic: no two grapheme–color synesthetes share exactly the same palette, although certain tendencies (such as A being red) are more common than chance would predict.

Consistency testing

The hallmark diagnostic is consistency over time. When asked to pick the color of each grapheme from a large palette, synesthetes choose nearly identical colors even when retested months or years later without warning, whereas control subjects asked to memorize associations perform far worse.

Research

Studies using Stroop-like tasks, visual search and neuroimaging suggest that the phenomenon involves cross-activation between the grapheme-recognition region and the color area V4 of the visual cortex, which lie adjacent to one another in the fusiform gyrus. Heritability studies indicate a genetic component.
