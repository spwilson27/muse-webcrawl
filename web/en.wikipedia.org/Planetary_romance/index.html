Early planetary romances appeared in pulp magazines and emphasised adventure on exotic worlds.

Planetary romance is a subgenre of science fiction in which the bulk of the action consists of adventures on one or more exotic alien planets, characterised by distinctive physical and cultural backgrounds. The genre emphasises the planet itself — its landscapes, inhabitants and societies — as an object of wonder, and usually owes more to the adventure romance than to rigorous science.

Characteristics

Planetary romances typically transport a protagonist, often an Earth person, to another world where they encounter strange creatures, decadent civilisations, swordplay and romance. Scientific plausibility is secondary to atmosphere and adventure; many works mix advanced technology with feudal societies, a blend often labelled science fantasy. The closely related "sword and planet" subgenre foregrounds swashbuckling combat and a lone hero.

History

Precursors include early voyages to the Moon, but the form crystallised with Edgar Rice Burroughs's Barsoom series, beginning with A Princess of Mars (serialised 1912), in which John Carter is transported to a dying Mars of warring city-states. Leigh Brackett, with stories such as "The Sword of Rhiannon", became known as the queen of space opera and planetary romance in the pulp era. Later examples brought greater literary and anthropological depth, notably Ursula K. Le Guin's Rocannon's World and The Left Hand of Darkness, Jack Vance's Planet of Adventure series, and Gene Wolfe's The Book of the New Sun, set on a far-future Earth treated as an alien world. The 2009 film Avatar, set on Pandora, is a prominent modern example.

Distinction from space opera

Where space opera ranges across star systems and empires, planetary romance stays local, exploring a single world in depth. Many works blend the two modes.
