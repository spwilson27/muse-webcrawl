Phenotypic plasticity

FieldEvolutionary biology, developmental biology, ecology
Key conceptReaction norm
Term introducedEarly 20th century (Woltereck's "Reaktionsnorm", 1909)
Related conceptsAcclimation, polyphenism, developmental noise, epigenetics

Phenotypic plasticity is the ability of an organism with a given genotype to produce different phenotypes in response to different environmental conditions. It is a fundamental property of development: the phenotype is a product of the genotype, the environment, and their interaction, so a single genome can generate a range of morphologies, physiologies, and behaviours.

Reaction norms

The set of phenotypes a genotype can produce across an environmental gradient is called its reaction norm. Plotting phenotype against environment for several genotypes reveals whether plasticity itself varies genetically — the basis for its evolution. If reaction norms cross, the ranking of genotypes changes between environments, an important consideration in plant and animal breeding.

Examples

Classic examples include predator-induced defences in Daphnia (which grow helmets and spines when they detect predator chemicals), seasonal wing patterns in butterflies, temperature-dependent sex determination in reptiles, sun and shade leaves on a single plant, acclimation of physiology to altitude or temperature, and the honeybee caste system, in which diet determines whether a female larva becomes a worker or a queen. Discrete alternative phenotypes produced by an environmental switch are called polyphenisms.

Adaptive significance

Plasticity can be adaptive, allowing organisms to cope with variable environments within a lifetime — potentially much faster than genetic change — or non-adaptive, a passive consequence of environmental stress. Plasticity may facilitate evolution by exposing populations to new environments in which selection can then act (the "plasticity-first" or Baldwin-effect hypothesis), and it is of growing interest for predicting responses to climate change. Plastic responses typically involve changes in gene expression, often mediated by hormonal and epigenetic mechanisms.
