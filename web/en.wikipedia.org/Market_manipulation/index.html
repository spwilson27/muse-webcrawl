In economics and finance, market manipulation is a type of market abuse where there is a deliberate attempt to interfere with the free and fair operation of the market; the most blatant of cases involve creating false or misleading appearances with respect to the price of, or market for, a product, security or commodity. Market manipulation is prohibited in most countries, in particular it is prohibited in the United States under Section 9(a)(2) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, in the European Union under Article 12 of the Market Abuse Regulation, and in Australia under section 1041A of the Corporations Act 2001.

Techniques

Pump and dump schemes artificially inflate the price of an owned stock through false recommendations, after which the manipulator sells at the elevated price. Wash trading creates the illusion of volume by trading with oneself or colluding accounts. Spoofing involves placing large orders with no intent to execute them, to mislead other traders about supply or demand. Cornering the market means acquiring enough of an asset to control its price, as in the Hunt brothers' attempt to corner silver in 1980. Other methods include bear raids, marking the close, and spreading false rumors ("short and distort").

Enforcement and notable cases

Regulators pursue manipulation through civil enforcement and criminal prosecution. Notable cases include the manipulation of LIBOR benchmark interest rates by major banks in the 2000s, which led to billions of dollars in fines, and more recent actions against spoofing in futures markets and pump-and-dump schemes in cryptocurrency and microcap stocks.
