Saudi Vision 2030 (Arabic: رؤية السعودية 2030) is a strategic framework to reduce Saudi Arabia's dependence on oil, diversify its economy, and develop public service sectors such as health, education, infrastructure, recreation, and tourism. Announced on 25 April 2016 by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, the programme is organized around three themes: a vibrant society, a thriving economy, and an ambitious nation.

Goals and programmes

Headline targets originally included raising non-oil government revenue from SAR 163 billion to SAR 1 trillion, increasing the private sector's contribution to GDP from 40 to 65 percent, growing the Public Investment Fund into one of the world's largest sovereign wealth funds, localizing defence and renewable-energy industries, and raising household spending on culture and entertainment. Delivery runs through Vision Realization Programs (VRPs) covering housing, quality of life, financial sector development, privatization, and the National Transformation Program. Social reforms under the Vision have included lifting the ban on women driving (2018), reopening cinemas, expanding tourist visas, and curbing the powers of the religious police.

Giga-projects

The plan's flagship developments include Neom, a planned futuristic region in the northwest featuring The Line, a proposed 170-kilometre linear city (subsequently scaled back in scope and timelines); the Red Sea Global tourism destinations; Qiddiya entertainment city near Riyadh; Diriyah Gate heritage development; and the King Salman Park and Green Riyadh urban greening schemes. Saudi Arabia has also pursued global sports investments — including LIV Golf, football signings, and hosting rights for Expo 2030 and the 2034 FIFA World Cup — as soft-power and diversification instruments.

Criticism

Commentators have questioned the feasibility of some giga-project timelines and costs, the sustainability of funding amid volatile oil prices, and the contrast between liberalizing social reforms and the kingdom's human rights record and restrictions on political dissent. Supporters point to rapid growth in non-oil GDP, tourism, female labour-force participation (which surpassed the 30 percent target ahead of schedule), and sovereign-fund investment returns.
