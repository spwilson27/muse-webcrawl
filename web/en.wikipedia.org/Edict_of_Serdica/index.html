Edict of Serdica

The Edict of Serdica, also known as the Edict of Toleration by Galerius, was issued in 311 in Serdica (now Sofia, Bulgaria) by the Roman emperor Galerius. The edict officially ended the Diocletianic Persecution of Christianity in the Eastern Roman Empire.

Background

Galerius had been one of the most zealous persecutors of Christians under the Tetrarchy established by Diocletian. Beginning in 303, a series of edicts had ordered the destruction of churches, the burning of scriptures, and the arrest of clergy. Persecution was enforced most harshly in the East, where Galerius ruled.

By 311 Galerius was gravely ill, suffering from a painful disease (possibly bowel cancer or gangrene). Convinced, according to the church historians Lactantius and Eusebius, that his illness might be a punishment from the Christian God, he issued an edict of toleration from his deathbed. He died only days later, on 5 May 311.

Content

The edict granted Christians the right to exist legally and to practise their religion, provided they prayed for the well-being of the emperor and the state. It permitted them to rebuild their places of worship. The text, preserved by Lactantius in De Mortibus Persecutorum and by Eusebius in his Ecclesiastical History, framed toleration as an act of imperial clemency intended to restore public order.

Significance

The Edict of Serdica was the Empire's first official recognition of Christianity as a lawful religion. It was surpassed two years later by the so-called Edict of Milan (313), issued by Constantine and Licinius, which granted full religious tolerance and restored confiscated Christian property.
