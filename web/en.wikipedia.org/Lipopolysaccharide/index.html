Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is a large glycolipid that forms the major component of the outer leaflet of the outer membrane of most Gram-negative bacteria, including Escherichia coli. LPS is also known as endotoxin because it is a toxin located inside the bacterial cell wall that is released when the bacterium disintegrates; it is a potent activator of the immune system and a major cause of the fever and shock seen in Gram-negative sepsis.

Structure

LPS consists of three regions. Lipid A is a phosphorylated glucosamine disaccharide carrying multiple fatty-acid chains; it anchors the molecule in the membrane and carries most of the endotoxic activity. Attached to it is the core oligosaccharide, containing the unusual sugars KDO and heptose. The outermost O-antigen is a long polysaccharide chain of repeating units whose composition varies between strains and serves as the basis of serotyping (for example, E. coli O157).

Biological activity

In animals, LPS is recognised by the innate immune receptor complex TLR4–MD-2 together with CD14 and LPS-binding protein. Minute quantities trigger macrophages to release inflammatory cytokines such as tumour necrosis factor. Large amounts entering the bloodstream — during sepsis or bacterial lysis — can cause endotoxic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and death. Because LPS is heat-stable, it is a persistent contaminant of pharmaceuticals and laboratory reagents; its presence is measured with the Limulus amoebocyte lysate (LAL) test, and it is removed by depyrogenation.

Function for the bacterium

For the bacterium, the LPS layer forms a selective permeability barrier that excludes bile salts, detergents, and many antibiotics, and the O-antigen shields the cell from complement attack and phagocytosis.
