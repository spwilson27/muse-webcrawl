The United States Department of Labor (DOL) is one of the executive departments of the US federal government, responsible for federal labour standards, occupational safety and health, wage and hour standards, unemployment insurance, re-employment services and labour statistics. Its statutory mission is "to foster, promote, and develop the welfare of the wage earners, job seekers, and retirees of the United States."

History

Labor functions began in the Bureau of Labor (1884) within the Department of the Interior, then moved to the combined Department of Commerce and Labor in 1903. On 4 March 1913, his last day in office, President William Howard Taft signed the act splitting the departments and creating a separate Department of Labor; William B. Wilson was its first secretary. Frances Perkins, secretary from 1933 to 1945 — the longest-serving secretary and the first woman in the US Cabinet — oversaw the department's New Deal expansion, including the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938.

Agencies and laws administered

Major components include the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA, workplace safety, under the 1970 OSH Act), the Wage and Hour Division (federal minimum wage and overtime under the Fair Labor Standards Act), the Bureau of Labor Statistics (employment and inflation data, including the monthly jobs report and CPI), the Employment and Training Administration (unemployment insurance and workforce programmes), the Employee Benefits Security Administration (pension and health plans under ERISA), the Mine Safety and Health Administration, and the Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs.

Statutes enforced by the department affect most private and public employment in the United States, covering more than 150 million workers and 10 million employers.
