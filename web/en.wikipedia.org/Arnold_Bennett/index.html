Arnold Bennett

Enoch Arnold Bennett (27 May 1867 – 27 March 1931) was an English novelist, playwright and journalist, best known for his realistic novels set in the Staffordshire Potteries, which he fictionalized as the "Five Towns". Among the most popular and commercially successful British writers of his day, he was also a prolific author of self-help books, including the bestseller How to Live on 24 Hours a Day (1908).

Life

Bennett was born in Hanley, one of the six towns of Stoke-on-Trent, the son of a solicitor. He worked in his father's office, then moved to London as a solicitor's clerk before turning to journalism, becoming editor of the magazine Woman. From 1903 he lived for a decade in Paris, absorbing French literature — Balzac, Flaubert and Maupassant were lasting influences — before returning to England a famous and wealthy author. He served in wartime propaganda work and died of typhoid fever in 1931, contracted, legend has it, from drinking tap water in Paris to prove it safe.

Works

His major novels include Anna of the Five Towns (1902), The Old Wives' Tale (1908) — widely regarded as his masterpiece, tracing the lives of two sisters from the Potteries to Paris — the Clayhanger trilogy (1910–1916), Hilda Lessways (1911) and Riceyman Steps (1923), which won the James Tait Black Memorial Prize. Modernist writers such as Virginia Woolf famously criticized Bennett's material realism in her essay "Mr Bennett and Mrs Brown" (1924), a polemic that shaped his later reputation even as his best novels continued to be reprinted.
