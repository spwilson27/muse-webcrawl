Cleisthenes

Cleisthenes (Greek: Κλεισθένης; c. 570 – c. 508 BC) was an Athenian lawgiver and aristocrat credited with reforming the constitution of ancient Athens and setting it on a democratic footing in 508 BC. A member of the Alcmaeonid family, he is often called the "father of Athenian democracy".

Background

Athens in the late 6th century BC was dominated by rival aristocratic factions. After the tyranny of the Peisistratids ended in 510 BC, Cleisthenes competed for power with Isagoras, who was backed by the Spartan king Cleomenes I. When Isagoras expelled Cleisthenes and hundreds of households, the Athenian people rose up, besieged the Spartans on the Acropolis and recalled Cleisthenes, who then carried through his reforms.

Reforms

Cleisthenes reorganised the citizen body into ten new tribes (phylai), each composed of demes drawn from three different regions — the city, the coast and the inland — so as to break the regional power bases of the old aristocratic families. The demes became the basic units of local self-government, and citizens were identified by their deme name. He expanded the council (boule) to 500 members, fifty from each tribe chosen by lot, to prepare business for the assembly. His system, which Herodotus and Aristotle describe, rested on the principle of isonomia — equality before the law. The institution of ostracism is also traditionally attributed to his reforms.

Legacy

Cleisthenes' reorganisation gave Athens the institutional framework within which its classical democracy developed over the following two centuries.
