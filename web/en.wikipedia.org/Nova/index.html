Nova

A nova (plural novae or novas) is a transient astronomical event that causes the sudden appearance of a bright, apparently "new" star—hence the name, from the Latin for "new"—that slowly fades over weeks or months. Unlike a supernova, a nova does not destroy its host star; the star survives and the event can recur.

Mechanism

Novae occur in close binary systems in which a white dwarf accretes hydrogen-rich material from a companion star. The transferred gas accumulates on the white dwarf's surface in a degenerate layer. As temperature and pressure at the base of the layer rise, hydrogen ignites in an uncontrolled thermonuclear runaway (via the CNO cycle), blowing off the accreted envelope at speeds of hundreds to thousands of kilometres per second. The system brightens by 7 to 16 magnitudes, ejecting typically 10−4 solar masses of material.

Classification

Classical novae are one-off observed outbursts, though all are thought to recur on timescales of centuries to millennia. Recurrent novae, such as T Coronae Borealis and RS Ophiuchi, erupt repeatedly on timescales of decades. Dwarf novae are a separate class of cataclysmic variable in which brightening is caused by instability in the accretion disc rather than surface fusion, and are far less luminous.

Observations

Several novae per year are discovered in the Milky Way, and many more are observed in the Andromeda Galaxy. Historically, bright novae such as Nova Aquilae 1918 were conspicuous naked-eye events. Novae serve as standardisable candles in some distance measurements, and their ejecta enrich the interstellar medium with isotopes such as lithium-7.
