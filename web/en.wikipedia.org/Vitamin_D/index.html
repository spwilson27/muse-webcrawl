Vitamin D

Cholecalciferol, vitamin D3

Main forms: Ergocalciferol (D2), cholecalciferol (D3)

Sources: Sunlight on skin, fatty fish, fortified foods, supplements

Deficiency: Rickets, osteomalacia

Vitamin D is a group of fat-soluble secosteroids responsible for increasing intestinal absorption of calcium, magnesium, and phosphate, and for many other biological effects. The most important compounds are vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) and vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol). Humans synthesize cholecalciferol in the skin when exposed to ultraviolet B radiation from sunlight, and can also obtain vitamin D from food and supplements.

Metabolism

Vitamin D from skin or diet is converted in the liver to calcifediol (25-hydroxyvitamin D), the main circulating form measured in blood tests, and then in the kidneys to calcitriol (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D), the active hormone that binds the vitamin D receptor and regulates calcium homeostasis and gene expression.

Health effects

Severe deficiency causes rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults, diseases of defective bone mineralization that were historically treated with cod liver oil and sunlight. Vitamin D status has been studied in relation to immune function, cancer, cardiovascular disease, and falls and fractures, though evidence for benefits of supplementation in already-replete people is mixed. Excessive intake can cause hypercalcemia — vitamin D toxicity is almost always from supplements, not sun exposure.

Recommendations

Reference intakes vary by country; the U.S. National Academy of Medicine recommends 600–800 IU per day for most adults. Deficiency is common at high latitudes in winter and among people with limited sun exposure or darker skin.
