Gastrostomy

A gastrostomy is a surgical or endoscopic procedure that creates an opening (stoma) through the abdominal wall into the stomach, usually to provide a route for long-term enteral feeding when a person cannot eat or swallow safely. The tube placed through the opening is often called a G-tube.

Indications

Gastrostomy feeding is used in conditions causing prolonged dysphagia or inadequate oral intake: stroke, motor neurone disease, head and neck cancers, cerebral palsy, dementia in selected cases, and congenital abnormalities. It may also be used for gastric decompression or administration of medication.

Techniques

The most common method is percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG), introduced by Gauderer and Ponsky in 1980: an endoscope transilluminates the stomach wall, a needle and guidewire are passed through the abdominal wall, and the tube is pulled or pushed into place. Alternatives include radiologically inserted gastrostomy (RIG) and open or laparoscopic surgical gastrostomy.

Care and complications

Complications include peristomal infection, tube blockage or dislodgement, leakage, bleeding, and, rarely, perforation or buried bumper syndrome. With proper care, gastrostomy tubes can remain in place for months to years and are replaced as needed. Feeds, fluids, and medications are given through the tube according to a dietetic plan.
