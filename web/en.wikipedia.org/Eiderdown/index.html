Eiderdown

Eiderdown is the soft down feather of the common eider duck (Somateria mollissima), harvested from the nests of wild birds and used as a filling for duvets, pillows, and winter clothing. It is among the warmest and lightest natural insulating materials known, and among the most expensive: a single duvet may require the cleaned down of dozens of nests. The word is also used generically, especially in British English, for a quilted bed covering — "an eiderdown".

Properties

Eiderdown clusters have tiny hooks that cling to one another, forming a coherent mass that traps large amounts of air while remaining compressible and extremely light. Unlike the down of farmed geese and ducks, eiderdown is collected from wild birds' nests, and it has a natural cohesiveness that lets it recover its loft after compression. Its rarity and the labour-intensive harvest make it a luxury product; genuine eiderdown duvets sell for many thousands of pounds.

Harvesting

Down is gathered in northern coastal regions, especially Iceland — the source of the great majority of the world's supply — as well as Norway, Canada, and Russia. Female eiders pluck down from their breasts to line their nests. Harvesters, often farming families who protect the nesting colonies from predators, collect a portion of the down during incubation or after the ducklings leave, replacing it with hay so the eggs or chicks are not harmed. Because the ducks return to the same protected colonies year after year, the system is sometimes described as a centuries-old partnership between farmers and wild birds. Icelandic eider farming has been practised for roughly a thousand years, and the birds are fully protected by law there.

Processing and trade

Raw down is dried, baked to destroy bacteria, and cleaned by machine and by hand to remove feathers and debris — a slow process yielding only a fraction of the collected material as finished down. A kilogram of cleaned eiderdown requires the down of some 60–80 nests. Annual world production is only a few tonnes, and Iceland accounts for most of the legally traded supply.
