Fodder

Fodder refers to food given to domesticated animals, including livestock raised in an agricultural setting — particularly ruminants such as cattle and sheep, and other grazing animals. The term derives from the Old English fōdor, related to food and feed. In a wider sense it can include similar plants cut and carried to animals, as opposed to forage, which the animals obtain by grazing in the field.

Types of fodder

Fodder includes hay (dried grasses and legumes), straw, silage (fermented green forage), compressed and pelleted feeds, sprouted grains and legumes, oils and mixed rations. Root crops such as fodder beet and swedes are grown specifically as high-energy winter feed for cattle and sheep in northern Europe and New Zealand. Historically, leaves and branches of certain trees ("tree fodder") and by-products of milling and brewing were also important.

Production and trade

Fodder crops are grown on arable land or as part of grassland systems, and their yields and nutritional content — energy, protein, fibre and minerals — are central to livestock economics. Droughts that cut fodder harvests can cause regional "fodder crises", forcing farmers to buy imported feed or reduce herds. In the modern feed industry, fodder is commonly formulated as a total mixed ration matched to the animal's stage of production.
