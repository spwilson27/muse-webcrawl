Fermi's interaction

In particle physics, Fermi's interaction (also the Fermi theory of beta decay or the Fermi four-fermion interaction) is an explanation of beta decay proposed by Enrico Fermi in 1933, published in 1934. The theory posits four fermions directly interacting with one another at a single vertex: for example, a neutron converts directly into a proton, an electron, and an electron antineutrino. Fermi's work was the first successful quantitative theory of the weak interaction.

The theory

Fermi introduced a coupling constant, now denoted GF (the Fermi constant), characterizing the strength of the contact interaction between the four fermion fields. Modelling the decay on the electromagnetic interaction but with massive particles created and destroyed at a point, Fermi derived the shapes of beta-decay spectra and lifetime systematics that matched experiment well, including the systematics later codified in the Kurie plot. The theory treated the weak force as a point-like interaction of zero range.

Subsequent development

After parity violation was discovered in 1956–57, the interaction was refined to the V − A (vector minus axial vector) form by Sudarshan and Marshak and by Feynman and Gell-Mann. Although extraordinarily successful at low energies, the four-fermion theory is not renormalizable and fails at high energies; it was superseded by electroweak theory, in which the weak interaction is mediated by the W and Z bosons. Fermi's interaction survives as the low-energy effective field theory limit of the Standard Model, and the Fermi constant remains a basic measured parameter of weak physics, with a value of about 1.166 × 10−5 GeV−2.
