Atom

An atom is the smallest unit of ordinary matter that forms a chemical element. Every solid, liquid, gas, and plasma is composed of neutral or ionised atoms. Atoms are extremely small, typically around 100 picometres (0.1 nanometres) across. Every atom consists of a nucleus, which contains protons and neutrons, and one or more electrons bound to the nucleus. The number of protons determines the chemical element; atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons are called isotopes.

Structure

More than 99.97% of an atom's mass is concentrated in its nucleus, which is about 10,000 times smaller than the atom itself. Protons carry a positive electric charge, neutrons are neutral, and electrons carry a negative charge equal in magnitude to the proton's. In a neutral atom the numbers of protons and electrons are equal. Electrons occupy orbitals described by quantum mechanics; their arrangement in shells determines the atom's chemical behaviour and bonding. When atoms gain or lose electrons they become ions.

History

The concept of indivisible particles dates to ancient Greek philosophers such as Democritus, whose word atomos means "uncuttable". Modern atomic theory began with John Dalton (1803–1808), who proposed that elements consist of atoms of characteristic mass. J. J. Thomson discovered the electron in 1897; Ernest Rutherford's gold-foil experiment (1909–1911) revealed the nucleus; Niels Bohr's 1913 model introduced quantised electron orbits; and the quantum-mechanical model of the 1920s replaced orbits with probability distributions. The neutron was discovered by James Chadwick in 1932.

Properties and behaviour

Atoms combine to form molecules and chemical compounds through ionic and covalent bonds. Their spectra of absorbed and emitted light are unique fingerprints used throughout science. At very high energies atoms can be split (fission) or fused (fusion), releasing nuclear energy — processes unrelated to ordinary chemistry.
