Astrophysics

Astrophysics is a branch of astronomy and physics that deals with the physics of the universe, including the physical properties (luminosity, density, temperature, and chemical composition) of celestial objects such as stars, galaxies, and the interstellar medium, as well as their interactions. In modern practice the terms "astronomy" and "astrophysics" are often used interchangeably: astrophysics applies the theories and methods of physics to understand the structure, dynamics, and evolution of astronomical objects and phenomena.

History

Astrophysics emerged in the mid-19th century when spectroscopy, developed by Joseph von Fraunhofer, Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen, made it possible to determine the chemical composition of the Sun and stars from their light. Photography and photometry at observatories such as Harvard produced the stellar classification schemes (the Harvard classification, and later the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram) that underpin stellar physics. In the 20th century, astrophysics explained stellar energy through nuclear fusion, predicted the expansion of the universe, and — following discoveries such as quasars, pulsars, and the cosmic microwave background — extended observation beyond visible light to radio, X-ray, gamma-ray, and gravitational-wave signals.

Observational and theoretical astrophysics

Observational astrophysicists gather data across the electromagnetic spectrum using ground-based and space telescopes, deducing physical conditions from brightness, spectra, polarisation, and variability. Theoretical astrophysicists build analytical models and computer simulations of processes such as star formation, supernova explosions, accretion onto black holes, galaxy mergers, and the large-scale evolution of the universe. The two branches continually feed one another: observations constrain theory, and theory predicts new phenomena to seek.

Major topics

Important topics include stellar structure and evolution, nucleosynthesis, supernovae, neutron stars and black holes, exoplanets, galaxy formation, dark matter, dark energy, and physical cosmology (the Big Bang model). Multi-messenger astronomy — combining electromagnetic radiation, neutrinos, cosmic rays and gravitational waves — has become a defining frontier of the field since the first gravitational-wave detection in 2015.
