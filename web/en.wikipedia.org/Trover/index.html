Trover

Trover was a common law action for the recovery of damages for the wrongful conversion of personal property.

In common law, trover was a legal action (a form of action) by which a plaintiff recovered damages against a person who had found the plaintiff's goods and wrongfully converted them to their own use. Over time the action expanded beyond its original scope — actual finding — to become the standard remedy for any wrongful interference with another's personal property, a tort now generally known simply as conversion.

History

The action originated in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries as a development of trespass on the case. The plaintiff's declaration alleged, by way of fiction, that he had casually lost the goods and that the defendant had found them (trover being from the French trouver, "to find") and converted them to his own use. Neither the loss nor the finding could be denied by the defendant under the rules of pleading, so the real issue — the wrongful conversion — was tried. By the seventeenth century trover lay for any act of conversion, whether or not any finding had occurred, and it displaced the older action of detinue in most disputes over chattels.

Elements and damages

To succeed, the plaintiff had to show a right to immediate possession of the goods and an act by the defendant inconsistent with that right, such as selling, destroying, refusing to return, or wrongfully using them. The measure of damages was ordinarily the full value of the goods at the time of conversion, plus interest; the judgment was for money damages rather than the return of the specific goods, although payment of full value in effect operated as a forced sale of the goods to the defendant.

In England the old forms of action were abolished in the nineteenth century, but the substance of trover survives everywhere in the tort of conversion. The Torts (Interference with Goods) Act 1977 now governs wrongful interference with goods in England and Wales, while trover/conversion remains a living tort in the United States and other common-law jurisdictions.
