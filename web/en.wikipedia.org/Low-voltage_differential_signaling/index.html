Low-voltage differential signaling

Low-voltage differential signaling (LVDS), also known as TIA/EIA-644, is a technical standard that specifies electrical characteristics of a differential, serial signaling standard. LVDS operates at low power and can run at very high speeds using inexpensive twisted-pair copper cables. It was introduced in 1994 and has become widely used in computers and displays for high-speed data transmission.

Operation

An LVDS driver converts a single-ended signal into a differential pair: it steers a roughly constant current of about 3.5 mA through one wire or the other of a twisted pair, producing a voltage swing of about 350 mV across a 100-ohm termination resistor at the receiver. The receiver senses only the polarity of the differential voltage, so the link is tolerant of ground shifts and common-mode noise, generates little electromagnetic interference because the two currents cancel, and consumes little power.

Applications

Because it is an electrical-layer standard only, LVDS is used underneath many protocols and links. Notable applications include Flat Panel Display Link (FPD-Link) connecting graphics processors to LCD panels, Camera Link for machine-vision cameras, SCI (Scalable Coherent Interface), and numerous backplane and clock-distribution systems. Data rates of hundreds of megabits per second per pair are typical, with rates above 1 Gbit/s achievable over short distances.
