Purine metabolism

Purine metabolism refers to the metabolic pathways by which purines — principally adenine and guanine — are synthesized and broken down in living organisms. Purines are essential components of the nucleic acids DNA and RNA, of the energy currency ATP, and of signalling molecules such as cyclic AMP. Many organisms can synthesize purines de novo from simple precursors, salvage them from degraded nucleotides, or obtain them from the diet.

De novo synthesis

In de novo synthesis, the purine ring is built atom by atom on a ribose phosphate scaffold. The pathway begins with phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate (PRPP) and proceeds through ten steps to form inosine monophosphate (IMP), the common precursor of AMP and GMP. Atoms of the purine ring are contributed by glycine, aspartate, glutamine, carbon dioxide, and formate (carried by tetrahydrofolate). The pathway is tightly regulated by feedback inhibition of its first committed step by the end products IMP, AMP, and GMP.

Salvage

Salvage pathways recycle free purine bases and nucleosides released by nucleic acid turnover. The enzymes hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT) and adenine phosphoribosyltransferase (APRT) convert bases back into nucleotides using PRPP. Salvage is energetically cheaper than de novo synthesis and is particularly important in tissues such as the brain.

Catabolism

Purine degradation converts AMP and GMP through inosine and hypoxanthine to xanthine, and then to uric acid via xanthine oxidase. In humans and other great apes, uric acid is the final product and is excreted in urine; most other mammals possess the enzyme uricase, which further oxidizes uric acid to the more soluble allantoin.

Disorders

Excess uric acid production or underexcretion causes hyperuricemia, which can lead to gout and kidney stones. Complete deficiency of HGPRT causes Lesch–Nyhan syndrome, a severe X-linked disorder with hyperuricemia and neurological symptoms. Deficiency of adenosine deaminase impairs purine catabolism in lymphocytes and causes a form of severe combined immunodeficiency. The xanthine oxidase inhibitor allopurinol is a mainstay of gout treatment.
