Malaysia Airlines Flight 370

Malaysia Airlines Flight 370 (MH370/MAS370) was an international passenger flight operated by Malaysia Airlines that disappeared on 8 March 2014 while flying from Kuala Lumpur International Airport in Malaysia to its planned destination, Beijing Capital International Airport in China. The crew of the Boeing 777-200ER, registered 9M-MRO, last communicated with air traffic control around 38 minutes after takeoff when the flight was over the South China Sea. The aircraft was lost from ATC radar screens minutes later but was tracked by Malaysian military radar for another hour, deviating westwards from its planned flight path, crossing the Malay Peninsula and Andaman Sea.

Timeline

Flight 370 took off at 00:42 MYT and reached its cruising altitude at 01:01. The final voice communication, "Good night. Malaysian three seven zero," came at 01:19, shortly before the transponder ceased transmitting as the aircraft crossed into Vietnamese airspace. Military radar showed the aircraft turning back, flying across the Malay Peninsula and around Penang before heading northwest over the Andaman Sea. Satellite "handshakes" with an Inmarsat satellite continued until 08:19 MYT, indicating the aircraft flew for hours along either a northern or a southern corridor; analysis of the signals placed it on the southern track over the Indian Ocean.

Search

The multinational search for Flight 370 became the most expensive in aviation history. A surface search of the South China Sea and later the Indian Ocean found nothing, and a deep-sea sonar search of 120,000 km² of seabed off Western Australia, led by Australia, ended in January 2017 without locating the main wreckage. In 2018 the Malaysian government contracted the US company Ocean Infinity to search on a "no find, no fee" basis; that search also ended without success.

Debris

Several pieces of marine debris confirmed or believed to be from the aircraft washed ashore on Indian Ocean coasts, most notably a flaperon found on Réunion Island in July 2015, and further items in Mozambique, Madagascar, Mauritius and Tanzania. Analysis of the flaperon's drift suggested it had not been deployed at impact, an inference bearing on theories of the flight's final moments.

Theories and legacy

The official Malaysian safety investigation report of 2018 was unable to determine the cause, noting evidence of manual turns and deliberate disabling of communications but no conclusive attribution. Theories ranging from mechanical failure and hypoxia to deliberate pilot action remain debated. The disappearance drove major changes in aircraft tracking standards, including ICAO rules requiring routine position reporting every 15 minutes and distress tracking, so that a large airliner could never again vanish untracked.
