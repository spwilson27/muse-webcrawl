Cell potency

LevelsTotipotency, pluripotency, multipotency, oligopotency, unipotency
DomainDevelopmental biology, stem cell biology
ExamplesZygote (totipotent); embryonic stem cells (pluripotent)

Cell potency is a cell's ability to differentiate into other cell types. The more cell types a cell can differentiate into, the greater its potency. Potency is also described as the gene activation potential within a cell, which, like a continuum, begins with totipotency to designate a cell with the most differentiation potential, and descends through pluripotency, multipotency, oligopotency, and finally unipotency.


The hierarchy of cell potency

Totipotency and pluripotency

A totipotent cell, such as the zygote and the first blastomeres, can give rise to all embryonic and extra-embryonic tissues — an entire organism. Pluripotent cells, including embryonic stem cells of the inner cell mass, can form all cell types of the three germ layers but not the placenta. In 2006 Shinya Yamanaka showed that adult cells could be reprogrammed to pluripotency (induced pluripotent stem cells) by a small set of transcription factors, a discovery awarded the Nobel Prize in 2012.

Multipotency and below

Multipotent stem cells produce a limited family of related cell types: haematopoietic stem cells, for example, generate all blood cells, and mesenchymal stem cells form bone, cartilage, and fat. Oligopotent cells can form a few types (such as lymphoid progenitors), and unipotent cells — such as muscle satellite cells — produce only one type while retaining self-renewal.

Importance

The potency hierarchy underlies regenerative medicine: controlling differentiation allows researchers to grow replacement tissues, model disease in culture, and test drugs, while the progressive restriction of potency is a central phenomenon of embryonic development.
