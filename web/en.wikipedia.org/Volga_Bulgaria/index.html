Volga Bulgaria (also known as Volga–Kama Bulgaria) was a state of the Oghuric Bulgars that existed between the late 7th century and the 1240s in the region where the Volga and Kama rivers meet, in what is today the Russian republics of Tatarstan and Chuvashia. It was one of the earliest states in the region and the first in Eastern Europe to adopt Islam as its official religion, in 922.

History

The Volga Bulgars were a branch of the Bulgar tribes who migrated northward from the Pontic steppe, likely under pressure from the Khazars. By the 8th century they had established control over the middle Volga basin. Their first capital was Bolgar, situated on the Volga's left bank; the later centre was Bilär, one of the largest cities of medieval Eastern Europe.

In 922 the ruler Almış accepted Islam, following an embassy from the Abbasid caliph al-Muqtadir to Baghdad's envoy Ibn Fadlan, whose travel account is a key source for the region's peoples. Islamisation linked Volga Bulgaria to the wider Muslim world and its trade networks.

The state grew wealthy from the fur trade and from commerce along the Volga route between Northern Europe, Rus', and the Islamic world. It clashed repeatedly with the principalities of Kievan Rus' and later Vladimir-Suzdal. The state was destroyed by the Mongol invasion of Batu Khan in 1236, though Bolgar was later rebuilt under the Golden Horde.

Economy and society

Volga Bulgaria combined sedentary agriculture — unusual for a steppe-derived polity — with stockbreeding, craft production (leatherwork, jewellery, pottery), and transit trade. Its merchants dealt in furs, slaves, wax, honey, and amber, exchanging them for silver dirhams, textiles, and spices from the south.

Legacy

The Volga Bulgars are regarded as ancestors of the modern Chuvash, whose language is the only surviving member of the Oghuric branch, and are also claimed in the heritage of the Volga Tatars. The ruins of Bolgar are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
