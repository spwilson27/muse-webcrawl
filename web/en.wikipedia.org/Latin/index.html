Latin (Latin: lingua latīna, IPA: [ˈlɪŋɡʷa laˈtiːna]) is a classical language belonging to the Italic branch of the Indo-European languages. Latin was originally spoken by the Latins in Latium (now known as Lazio), the lower Tiber area around Rome, Italy. Through the expansion of the Roman Republic, it became the dominant language in the Italian Peninsula and subsequently throughout the Roman Empire. By the late Roman era, spoken Latin had evolved into the Romance languages, including Italian, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Romanian, and Catalan.

Latin is considered a dead language in the sense that it no longer has native speakers, but it is still used in many contexts. It was the language of international communication, science, and academia in Europe until well into the early modern period, and its vocabulary is still used in modern science, law, and theology. Latin remains the official language of the Holy See and the Roman Rite of the Catholic Church.

History

Old Latin

The earliest form of Latin, Old Latin (also called Early Latin), was spoken from roughly the 7th century BC. Surviving inscriptions, such as the Praeneste fibula and the Lapis Niger, attest to its archaic features, including case endings and vocabulary that later disappeared. Old Latin was the language of the Roman Kingdom and the early Republic.

Classical Latin

Classical Latin, the literary standard of the late Republic and early Empire, was polished by authors such as Cicero, Caesar, Virgil, Horace, Ovid, and Livy during the "Golden Age" of Latin literature (roughly 70 BC – AD 14), followed by the "Silver Age" of authors such as Tacitus, Juvenal, and Pliny the Younger. It was a highly inflected language with a relatively fixed syntax taught through grammar and rhetoric.

Vulgar Latin

Vulgar Latin was the colloquial speech of ordinary people, differing from the literary standard in vocabulary, pronunciation, and grammar. Over centuries it gradually differentiated by region, giving rise to the Romance languages. Evidence for Vulgar Latin comes from graffiti, inscriptions, and corrective lists such as the Appendix Probi.

Medieval and Renaissance Latin

During the Middle Ages, Latin remained the language of the Church, scholarship, and administration across Europe, absorbing new vocabulary for contemporary concepts. Renaissance humanists sought to restore classical purity, while Neo-Latin continued to be used by scholars such as Erasmus, Newton (whose Principia Mathematica was written in Latin), and Linnaeus.

Grammar

Latin is a synthetic, fusional language with a rich system of inflection. Nouns, pronouns, and adjectives are declined for case (nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, ablative, vocative, and vestigial locative), number (singular and plural), and gender (masculine, feminine, neuter). Verbs are conjugated for person, number, tense (present, imperfect, future, perfect, pluperfect, future perfect), mood (indicative, subjunctive, imperative, infinitive), and voice (active and passive). Word order is relatively free, though subject–object–verb order is common in prose.

Alphabet and pronunciation

The Latin alphabet derives from the Etruscan alphabet, itself adapted from Greek. Classical Latin used 23 letters; the letters J, U, and W were later developments. Pronunciation is reconstructed from descriptions by ancient grammarians, rhymes, puns, and loanword evidence; a separate "ecclesiastical" pronunciation developed in Church use, influenced by Italian.

Legacy

Latin profoundly influenced the vocabularies of the Romance languages and, through Norman French and scholarly borrowing, English: an estimated 60 percent of English words derive from Latin directly or indirectly. Latin roots, prefixes, and suffixes remain productive in scientific and technical nomenclature, including the binomial names of organisms established by Linnaeus.
