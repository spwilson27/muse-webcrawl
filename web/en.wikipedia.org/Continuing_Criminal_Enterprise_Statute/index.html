Continuing Criminal Enterprise Statute

The Continuing Criminal Enterprise Statute (CCE Statute), codified at 21 U.S.C. § 848, is a United States federal law aimed at the leaders of large-scale drug trafficking organizations. Enacted as part of the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, it is sometimes called the "kingpin statute" because it targets the organizers and supervisors of major narcotics enterprises rather than low-level participants. A related provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3559, is sometimes informally called the federal "super kingpin" law.

Elements

To convict under the statute, the government must prove that the defendant (1) committed a felony violation of the federal drug laws; (2) as part of a continuing series of violations; (3) undertaken in concert with five or more other persons; (4) with respect to whom the defendant occupied a position of organizer, supervisor, or manager; and (5) from which the defendant obtained substantial income or resources.

Penalties

A conviction carries a mandatory minimum sentence of 20 years' imprisonment and a maximum of life imprisonment, along with substantial fines and forfeiture of profits and property obtained from the enterprise. Enhanced penalties apply where the enterprise involves very large quantities of drugs, and the statute provides the death penalty in certain cases where the enterprise's activities resulted in death.

Notable prosecutions

The statute has been used against leaders of major trafficking organizations, including the heads of drug cartels and large domestic distribution networks. It complements the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO), which addresses a broader range of criminal enterprises.
