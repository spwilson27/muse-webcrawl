Plant wax

Plant waxes are organic compounds produced by plants, typically as coatings on leaves, stems, and fruits. They consist mainly of long-chain aliphatic compounds — hydrocarbons, wax esters, fatty acids, aldehydes, and primary alcohols — and form the epicuticular wax layer of the plant cuticle.

Function

Epicuticular wax limits uncontrolled water loss, sheds water and dirt from the surface (the "lotus effect"), reflects ultraviolet light, and provides a barrier against insects and pathogens. The crystalline structure of the wax often gives foliage a white or bluish bloom, as on grapes, plums, and many succulents.

Commercial waxes

Several plant waxes are harvested commercially. Carnauba wax, from the leaves of the Brazilian palm Copernicia prunifera, is among the hardest natural waxes and is used in polishes, cosmetics, and food coatings. Candelilla wax comes from the stems of a Mexican desert shrub, and Japan wax is obtained from the berries of sumac species. Historically, bayberry wax was used for scented candles in colonial America.
