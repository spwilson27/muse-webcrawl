The June Rebellion, also known as the Paris Uprising of 1832 (French: Insurrection républicaine à Paris en juin 1832), was an anti-monarchist insurrection of Parisian republicans against the July Monarchy of King Louis Philippe, fought from 5 to 6 June 1832. The rebellion originated in an attempt by republicans to reverse the gains of the absolutist supporters of the legitimate Bourbon dynasty during the July Revolution of 1830.

Background

The July Monarchy disappointed many who had fought in 1830: republicans wanted universal suffrage and social reform, while economic distress, a cholera epidemic, and high unemployment fuelled unrest in the capital. The death of the popular liberal general Jean Maximilien Lamarque from cholera on 1 June 1832 provided the spark; his funeral was scheduled for 5 June.

The uprising

During Lamarque's funeral procession on 5 June, crowds clashed with troops and the red flag was raised. Insurgents, many of them students, workers, and members of republican secret societies such as the Society of the Rights of Man, built barricades in the eastern districts of Paris, notably around the Cloître Saint-Merry. By the evening, rebels controlled some central neighbourhoods.

Suppression

The government responded with overwhelming force: National Guards and regular troops, supported by artillery, systematically retook the barricades on 6 June. The last resistance, at the cloister of Saint-Merry, was overwhelmed that afternoon. Roughly 800 people were killed or wounded on both sides. A state of siege was declared, and leading republicans were arrested, though several were acquitted by juries.

In literature

The rebellion was immortalised by Victor Hugo in his 1862 novel Les Misérables, in which the uprising forms the backdrop to the story of the barricade of the rue de la Chanvrerie and the deaths of Gavroche and the students led by Enjolras. Hugo, who witnessed part of the fighting, set his fictional barricade in the same streets where the real insurgents had fought.
