Curry–Howard correspondence

The Curry–Howard correspondence (also called the Curry–Howard isomorphism, or proofs-as-programs) is a direct relationship between computer programs and mathematical proofs: a proposition in a formal logic corresponds to a type in a programming language, and a proof of the proposition corresponds to a program of that type. Checking a proof corresponds to type checking, and simplifying a proof (cut elimination) corresponds to evaluating a program.

History

The correspondence rests on observations by Haskell Curry (1934, 1958), who noticed that the types of the basic combinators match axioms of intuitionistic logic, and by William Alvin Howard, whose 1969 manuscript (published 1980) made the correspondence explicit for intuitionistic natural deduction and the simply typed lambda calculus. It was later extended to many logics and calculi.

The correspondence

Under the isomorphism, logical implication corresponds to the function type, conjunction to the product (pair) type, disjunction to the sum (tagged union) type, the true proposition to the unit type, and falsehood to the empty type. Universal and existential quantification correspond to dependent function (Π) and dependent pair (Σ) types, respectively. Extensions identify classical logic with continuations and control operators, and modal and linear logics with corresponding type systems.

Significance

The correspondence underlies proof assistants and dependent type theories such as Coq, Agda, and Lean, in which writing a program of a sufficiently expressive type constitutes a machine-checked proof. It unifies logic and computation — captured in the slogan "propositions as types, proofs as programs, simplification of proofs as evaluation of programs" — and continues to structure research in programming-language theory, including connections with category theory and homotopy type theory.
