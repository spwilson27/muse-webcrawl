Foundationalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Foundationalism concerns philosophical theories of knowledge resting upon justified belief, or some secure foundation of certainty such as a conclusion inferred from a basis of sound, undisputable knowledge. Its main purpose is to identify a secure foundation for knowledge in order to answer the regress problem: if every justified belief requires support from another belief, justification appears to require an infinite chain.

Basic and non-basic beliefs

Foundationalists hold that some beliefs — basic beliefs — are justified independently of support from other beliefs, and that these beliefs provide support for all other justified beliefs, often called superstructure beliefs. Classical foundationalism, associated with Descartes, held that basic beliefs must be infallible and indubitable, such as the belief in one's own existence expressed in the cogito argument.

Modest foundationalism

Modern or modest foundationalism relaxes the classical requirements: basic beliefs need not be certain, but may be justified by perceptual experience, memory, or introspection, and may later be defeated by contrary evidence. This makes foundationalism compatible with fallibilism, the view that no belief is immune to revision.

Criticism and alternatives

Critics argue that allegedly basic beliefs are not truly independent of other beliefs, and that perception itself is shaped by background theory. The principal alternative is coherentism, according to which beliefs are justified by their coherence with the rest of a belief system rather than by resting on a foundation. Neurath's image of sailors rebuilding their ship at sea is often used to illustrate the coherentist alternative.
