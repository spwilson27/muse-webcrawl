Wolfgang Pauli

Wolfgang Ernst Pauli (25 April 1900 – 15 December 1958) was an Austrian-Swiss theoretical physicist and one of the pioneers of quantum physics. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1945 for the discovery of the Pauli exclusion principle.

Career

A prodigy mentored by Max Born and Niels Bohr, Pauli earned his doctorate in Munich in 1921 with a thesis on the hydrogen molecule ion. He worked in Göttingen, Copenhagen and Hamburg before taking a chair at ETH Zurich in 1928, a post he held for most of his career, apart from wartime years at Princeton's Institute for Advanced Study.

Contributions

In 1925 Pauli formulated the exclusion principle: no two identical fermions (such as electrons) in an atom can occupy the same quantum state simultaneously — a rule that explains the structure of the periodic table and the stability of matter. He introduced the Pauli matrices for electron spin, and in 1930 he proposed the existence of a tiny neutral particle, later named the neutrino, to explain the apparent non-conservation of energy in beta decay; the particle was detected in 1956. Pauli was famed for his devastating criticism, encapsulated in his quip that a theory was "not even wrong," and for the joking "Pauli effect" — the tendency of experimental equipment to fail in his presence.
