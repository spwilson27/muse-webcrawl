Christian democracy

Christian democracy is a political ideology that emerged in nineteenth-century Europe under the influence of Catholic social teaching and, in some places, neo-Calvinist thought. It combines conservative commitments to traditional values and gradual change with support for a welfare state, workers' rights, and a regulated market economy. Christian democratic parties became the dominant centre-right force in much of continental Western Europe after the Second World War.

Origins

The ideology draws on papal encyclicals such as Rerum novarum (1891), in which Pope Leo XIII defended private property and trade unions while rejecting both laissez-faire capitalism and socialism. The principles of subsidiarity — decision-making at the most local competent level — and solidarity, along with the idea of the inviolable dignity of the human person, form its philosophical core. Catholic parties such as the German Centre Party and Belgium's Catholic Party were forerunners of the modern movement.

Post-war ascendancy

After 1945, Christian democratic parties led governments in Germany (Konrad Adenauer), Italy (Alcide De Gasperi), France, the Benelux countries, Austria and elsewhere. They were central to European integration: Adenauer, De Gasperi and Robert Schuman, sometimes called the founding fathers of the European Union, were all Christian democrats. In Latin America, parties such as Chile's Christian Democratic Party also became major forces.

Positions

Christian democrats typically support a social market economy, strong welfare provision, family-oriented policies, and historically conservative positions on abortion and euthanasia, though stances vary by country and have liberalised in many parties over time. In European politics they are grouped in the European People's Party, the largest party family in the European Parliament.
