Antonin Gregory Scalia (11 March 1936 – 13 February 2016) was an American jurist who served as an associate justice of the Supreme Court of the United States from 1986 until his death in 2016. He was described as the intellectual anchor of the Court's conservative wing and was a leading proponent of originalism in constitutional interpretation and textualism in statutory interpretation.

Early life and education

Scalia was born in Trenton, New Jersey, the only child of Italian immigrants. He grew up in Queens, New York, attended Xavier High School in Manhattan, and graduated valedictorian and summa cum laude from Georgetown University in 1957. He then studied at Harvard Law School, where he was an editor of the Harvard Law Review, graduating magna cum laude in 1960.

Legal career

After a clerkship and several years in private practice in Cleveland, Scalia taught law at the University of Virginia and the University of Chicago. He held a series of posts in the Nixon and Ford administrations, including Assistant Attorney General for the Office of Legal Counsel. In 1982 President Ronald Reagan appointed him to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit.

Supreme Court

In 1986 Reagan nominated Scalia to the Supreme Court to fill the seat of William Rehnquist, who had been elevated to Chief Justice. The Senate confirmed him 98–0, making him the first Italian-American justice. Over nearly thirty years on the bench, Scalia became known for his vigorous, often witty dissents, his advocacy of interpreting the Constitution according to its original public meaning, and his insistence that statutes be read according to their text rather than legislative history or perceived purpose.

Notable opinions

Scalia wrote the majority opinion in District of Columbia v. Heller (2008), holding that the Second Amendment protects an individual right to keep and bear arms. Other significant majority opinions include Crawford v. Washington (2004) on the Confrontation Clause and Printz v. United States (1997) on federalism. His dissents in cases such as Lawrence v. Texas (2003) and Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) were widely quoted for their rhetorical force.

Death and legacy

Scalia died in his sleep on 13 February 2016 while on a hunting trip at a ranch near Marfa, Texas. His death during a presidential election year set off a prolonged political dispute over his successor; the seat ultimately went to Neil Gorsuch in 2017. Scalia's jurisprudence profoundly shaped modern American legal debate, and originalism — once a minority position — became a dominant approach in part through his advocacy.
