Metal carbonyl

General formulaMx(CO)y
LigandCarbon monoxide
First isolated1890 (Ni(CO)4)

Metal carbonyls are coordination complexes of transition metals with carbon monoxide ligands. Metal carbonyls are useful in organic synthesis and as catalysts or catalyst precursors in homogeneous catalysis, such as hydroformylation and the Reppe chemistry. In the organometallic chemistry classification, metal carbonyls are organometallic compounds, even though they were studied long before that field had a name.

History

The first metal carbonyl, tetracarbonylnickel, Ni(CO)4, was discovered by Ludwig Mond, Carl Langer, and Friedrich Quincke in 1890. Iron pentacarbonyl, Fe(CO)5, was reported soon afterwards. Walter Hieber, working in the 1920s and 1930s, developed much of the systematic chemistry of binary carbonyls and their anions.

Structure and bonding

Carbon monoxide binds to metals through synergic bonding: the carbon lone pair donates into an empty metal orbital (sigma donation), while filled metal d-orbitals donate back into the empty pi-antibonding orbitals of CO (pi back-bonding). This back-bonding strengthens the metal–carbon bond and weakens the C–O bond, lowering the CO stretching frequency in the infrared spectrum, a diagnostic feature of these compounds. Most binary carbonyls obey the 18-electron rule.

Examples

Well-known examples include Ni(CO)4, Fe(CO)5, Cr(CO)6, Mo(CO)6, W(CO)6, and the polynuclear species Fe2(CO)9, Co2(CO)8, and Mn2(CO)10. Many metal carbonyls are volatile and highly toxic, since they release carbon monoxide and can carry the metal into the body; nickel tetracarbonyl was historically used in the Mond process for refining nickel.

Applications

Metal carbonyls serve as precursors to catalysts, reagents in carbonylation reactions, and sources of "naked" metal atoms or fragments in synthesis. Rhodium and cobalt carbonyl clusters underpin industrial hydroformylation, while iron carbonyls feature in the Pauson–Khand reaction and related synthetic methods.
