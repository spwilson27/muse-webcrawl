Basque (Basque: euskara) is a language spoken by the Basques in the Basque Country, a region straddling the western Pyrenees in northeastern Spain and southwestern France. It is one of the world's few language isolates — it has no demonstrable relatives among living languages — and is believed to descend from the language spoken in the region before the arrival of Indo-European languages, related to ancient Aquitanian, whose inscriptions survive from Roman times.

History and status

Basque survived Roman, Visigothic, and Frankish rule in its mountain homeland but lost ground to Spanish and French from the 19th century, and its public use was suppressed in Spain under Franco. Since the 1960s a revival, driven by ikastolak (Basque-medium schools) and official recognition after 1978, has increased the number of speakers, especially among the young. A unified standard, euskara batua, was promulgated by the Royal Academy of the Basque Language (Euskaltzaindia) in 1968 alongside the traditional dialects.

Features

Basque is an ergative–absolutive language with polypersonal verbal agreement: the auxiliary verb agrees with subject, direct object, and indirect object. Nouns take case suffixes from a rich inventory, word order is typically subject–object–verb, and the language uses a vigesimal (base-20) counting system. Roughly a quarter of its vocabulary is borrowed, mainly from Latin, Spanish, and French, but its core grammar and lexicon are non-Indo-European.

Dialects

Traditional dialects include Biscayan, Gipuzkoan, Upper Navarrese, Navarro-Lapurdian, and Souletin (Zuberoan); the last surviving eastern varieties have declined sharply. Standard Basque is based largely on the central dialects.
