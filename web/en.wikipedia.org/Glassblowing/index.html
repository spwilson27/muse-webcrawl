Glassblowing

Glassblowing is a glassforming technique that involves inflating molten glass into a bubble (or parison) with the aid of a blowpipe. A person who blows glass is called a glassblower, glassmith, or gaffer. The technique, invented in the Roman world in the 1st century BC, revolutionised glassmaking by making glass vessels cheap and widely available, and it remains the basis of both artisanal and studio glass production.

Process

The glassblower gathers a blob of molten glass (about 1,100 °C) from a furnace on the end of a blowpipe, rolls it on a marver to shape it and form a skin, then blows a small bubble into it. The gather is repeatedly reheated in a furnace known as the "glory hole", shaped with tools, blocks, and wet newspaper pads, and may be transferred to a solid rod called a punty for finishing. Once complete, the piece is detached and cooled slowly in an annealing oven (lehr) to prevent cracking from thermal stress.

History

Glassblowing emerged around the mid-1st century BC, probably in the Syro-Palestinian region of the Roman Empire, and rapidly spread throughout the Roman world. Venice — especially the island of Murano, to which furnaces were confined from 1291 — became the leading European centre, famous for cristallo and elaborate decorative techniques. The studio glass movement of the 1960s, pioneered by Harvey Littleton and Dominick Labino, made glassblowing accessible to individual artists outside factories.

Related techniques

Lampworking (flameworking) shapes glass softened in the flame of a torch, and is used for beads and scientific glassware. Mold-blowing, developed in antiquity, inflates glass inside a patterned mold. Modern industrial glass containers are made by automated blow-and-blow and press-and-blow machines descended from these hand techniques.
