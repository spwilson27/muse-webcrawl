Sexual Offences Act 2003

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Sexual Offences Act 2003 (c. 42) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom governing sexual offences in England and Wales. It received royal assent on 20 November 2003 and came into force on 1 May 2004, replacing the Sexual Offences Act 1956 with a substantially restructured and modernised code. Corresponding provisions for Northern Ireland were later made by the Sexual Offences (Northern Ireland) Order 2008; Scotland has its own Sexual Offences (Scotland) Act 2009.

Background

The Act followed the Home Office review Setting the Boundaries (2000) and the White Paper Protecting the Public (2002). The review concluded that the old law was archaic, incoherent, and discriminatory, and recommended a clearer definition of consent and stronger protection for children and vulnerable adults.

Main provisions

Part 1 of the Act defines the principal offences. Section 1 restates the offence of rape as intentional penile penetration of another person without consent and without reasonable belief in consent; section 74 defines consent as agreement by choice, with the freedom and capacity to make that choice, and sections 75 and 76 set out evidential and conclusive presumptions. The Act also creates offences of assault by penetration (section 2), sexual assault (section 3), and causing sexual activity without consent (section 4).

The Act consolidated and strengthened offences against children, including offences involving children under 13 (for whom consent is legally impossible), abuse of positions of trust, and familial child sex offences. It also created offences of sexual exploitation, trafficking for sexual purposes, voyeurism, and exposure.

Notification requirements

Part 2 retains and extends the system popularly called the "sex offenders register": offenders convicted of specified offences must notify the police of their name, address, and other prescribed details, and of changes and foreign travel. The Supreme Court held in R (F) v Secretary of State for Justice (2010) that indefinite notification without any possibility of review was incompatible with Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights, prompting the introduction of a review mechanism after fifteen years.
