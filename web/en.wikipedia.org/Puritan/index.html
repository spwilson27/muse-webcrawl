Puritans

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A Puritan couple in New England, 17th century

The Puritans were English Protestants in the 16th and 17th centuries who sought to rid the Church of England of perceived Roman Catholic practices, maintaining that the English Reformation had not gone far enough. Puritans believed the Church of England was too similar to the Roman Catholic Church and argued it should eliminate ceremonies and practices not rooted in the Bible.

Puritans became noted for a spirit of moral and religious earnestness that informed their whole way of life, and they sought through church reform to make their lifestyle the pattern for the whole nation. Their efforts to transform the nation led to civil war in England and to the founding of colonies in America as working models of the Puritan way of life.

Beliefs

Puritan theology rested on Calvinist foundations: the sovereignty of God, the authority of Scripture, and the necessity of grace through faith. Central doctrines included predestination, total depravity, and the covenant theology that framed God's relationship with humanity as a series of covenants. Puritans emphasised personal conversion, experienced as the work of the Holy Spirit, and a disciplined life of prayer, Bible study and self-examination.

Worship was to be simple and centred on the preaching of the Word. Puritans opposed set forms of prayer, vestments, and the observance of saints' days, and many objected to bishops, favouring Presbyterian or congregational forms of church government.

History

The movement arose during the reign of Elizabeth I among clergy and laity dissatisfied with the Elizabethan Religious Settlement. Under James I and Charles I, Puritans faced increasing pressure; thousands emigrated to New England in the Great Migration of the 1630s, founding the Massachusetts Bay Colony and other settlements.

During the English Civil War, Puritan influence reached its height: the Westminster Assembly produced doctrinal standards still used by Presbyterian churches, and under Oliver Cromwell the Commonwealth attempted a godly reformation of English society. The Restoration of 1660 ended Puritan political ascendancy; the Act of Uniformity 1662 ejected some two thousand Puritan ministers from the Church of England, and Puritanism merged into English Dissent.

Influence

Puritan ideas shaped the religious and cultural development of New England and, through it, the United States; Puritan emphases on education, covenant and self-government influenced American institutions, including Harvard College, founded in 1636. In England, the Puritan heritage fed into evangelical movements within and beyond the established church.
