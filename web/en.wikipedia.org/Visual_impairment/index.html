Visual impairment

Visual impairment, also known as vision impairment or vision loss, is a decreased ability to see to a degree that causes problems not fixable by usual means such as glasses or contact lenses. The term includes both partial sight and blindness. Globally, at least 2.2 billion people have a near or distance vision impairment according to the World Health Organization, and in at least 1 billion of these cases the impairment could have been prevented or remains unaddressed — chiefly because of uncorrected refractive errors and untreated cataracts.

Definitions

Definitions vary by purpose. The WHO classifies distance vision impairment by visual acuity in the better eye: mild (worse than 6/12), moderate (worse than 6/18), severe (worse than 6/60), and blindness (worse than 3/60). Legal definitions, such as "legal blindness" in the United States (acuity of 20/200 or worse in the better eye with correction, or a visual field of 20 degrees or less), determine eligibility for benefits and services.

Causes

The commonest causes worldwide are uncorrected refractive errors and cataracts, followed by glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, corneal opacities and trachoma. In children, congenital conditions, retinopathy of prematurity and vitamin A deficiency are important in different regions. Many causes are preventable or treatable: cataract surgery is one of the most cost-effective operations in all of medicine.

Living with visual impairment

People with visual impairment use a range of strategies and technologies: corrective lenses and low-vision devices, orientation and mobility training with a white cane or guide dog, braille and audio formats, and digital accessibility tools such as screen readers and magnification software. Visual impairment can affect education, employment and mental health, and it is associated with increased risk of falls and social isolation, particularly in older people.
