Swahili Coast

The Swahili Coast is the coastal region of Southeast Africa inhabited by the Swahili people, stretching from southern Somalia through Kenya and Tanzania to northern Mozambique, together with offshore islands such as Zanzibar, Pemba, Mafia, Lamu, and the Comoros. For over a millennium it was home to a network of wealthy trading city-states linking Africa to the wider Indian Ocean world.

History

From around the 8th century CE, coastal settlements grew rich on trade in gold, ivory, and other goods from the African interior, exchanged for textiles, beads, and porcelain from Arabia, Persia, India, and China. Cities such as Kilwa, Mombasa, Malindi, Lamu, and Mogadishu were ruled by Muslim sultans and built in a distinctive coral-stone architecture with carved doors and mosques. Islam and intermarriage with Arab and Persian traders shaped Swahili culture and the Swahili language, a Bantu language written for centuries in Arabic script.

The arrival of the Portuguese after Vasco da Gama's voyage of 1498 disrupted the city-states' dominance; from the late 17th century the coast came increasingly under the influence of the Sultanate of Oman, whose rulers moved their court to Zanzibar in the 19th century and built an economy on cloves and the slave trade. In the colonial era the coast was divided between German, British, and Portuguese possessions.

Culture

The Swahili Coast remains distinctive for its language, cuisine, music (such as taarab), and stone towns; the old town of Lamu and Stone Town of Zanzibar are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.
