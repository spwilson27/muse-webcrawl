Liar paradox

The liar paradox is a family of paradoxes arising from self-referential statements about truth. In its simplest form, the sentence "This statement is false" (or "I am lying") appears to be both true and false: if it is true, then what it says holds, so it is false; but if it is false, then it is true. The paradox has been a central problem in logic and the philosophy of language since antiquity.

History

The paradox is attributed to Eubulides of Miletus in the 4th century BCE. A related version was attached to Epimenides, a Cretan who supposedly said "All Cretans are liars" — quoted in the New Testament's Epistle to Titus. Medieval logicians studied it intensively as one of the insolubilia. In the 20th century, Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems were developed by replacing "false" with "unprovable", turning the liar's self-reference into a rigorous mathematical result.

Proposed solutions

  • Tarski's hierarchy: Alfred Tarski argued that a language cannot contain its own truth predicate without contradiction; truth for a language must be defined in a metalanguage, dissolving self-reference across levels.
  • Paracomplete logics: Saul Kripke's theory of truth allows the liar sentence to be neither true nor false — a truth-value gap.
  • Paraconsistent logics and dialetheism: Graham Priest and others accept that the liar is both true and false, using logics that tolerate true contradictions without exploding.
  • Contextualist approaches: argue the sentence's truth value shifts with context, so no single evaluation yields contradiction.

Related paradoxes

The liar belongs to a family including Russell's paradox, the barber paradox, Curry's paradox and Grelling's paradox. Strengthened versions, such as "This statement is not true", resist gap-based solutions and are called revenge paradoxes.
