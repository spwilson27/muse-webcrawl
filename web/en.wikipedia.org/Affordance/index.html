Affordance

An affordance is the possibility of an action on an object or environment. The term was coined by psychologist James J. Gibson in his 1966 book The Senses Considered as Perceptual Systems and developed in The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception (1979). Examples include buttons for pushing, handles for pulling, chairs for sitting, and mugs for drinking from. Affordances are relational: they depend on both the object's properties and the actor's capabilities.

Design

Donald Norman popularized the concept in The Psychology of Everyday Things (1988), emphasizing "perceived affordances"—design features that suggest how an object should be used. In human–computer interaction, affordances guide users toward correct actions, and Norman later introduced "signifiers" for the cues that communicate them.

Criticism and development

Scholars debate whether affordances are properties of objects, environments, or relationships, and how they are learned. The concept is influential in ecological psychology, industrial design, HCI, and robotics.
