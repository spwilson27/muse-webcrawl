Surname

A surname, also known as a family name or last name, is the portion of a person's personal name that indicates their family, tribe or community. In most Western cultures the surname follows the given name, while in many East Asian cultures (Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese) and in Hungarian it precedes the given name.

Origins

Hereditary surnames developed independently in many societies as populations grew and distinguishing individuals by a single given name became impractical. In England, surnames became widespread between the 11th and 16th centuries. They commonly derived from patronymics (Johnson, "son of John"), occupations (Smith, Baker, Miller), places (Hill, Wood, London), and nicknames or physical characteristics (Short, Armstrong, Russell, "red-haired"). Chinese family names are among the oldest, some dating back several thousand years; a small set — Wang, Li, Zhang — is shared by hundreds of millions of people.

Customs and law

Practices vary widely: Spanish naming customs use two surnames, drawn from both parents; Icelandic names are patronymic (or matronymic) and not, strictly, family names at all; in many Arab and Slavic traditions names encode a chain of ancestry. Married name change is customary in some cultures and legally constrained in others, such as Quebec. Some jurisdictions regulate the surnames that may be given to children. In 2011 Iceland and several other countries maintained lists of approved names; Denmark historically required surnames to be chosen from an approved list.

Study

The academic study of names is onomastics. Surname distributions are used by genealogists, historians and geneticists — for example, in studying the inheritance of the Y chromosome, which in patrilineal systems is transmitted alongside the surname.
