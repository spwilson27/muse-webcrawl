John Bright

[Image placeholder: portrait of John Bright]

John Bright (16 November 1811 – 27 March 1889) was a British Radical and Liberal statesman, one of the greatest orators of his generation and a promoter of free trade policies. A Quaker from Rochdale, he came to national prominence with Richard Cobden in the Anti-Corn Law League campaign of the 1840s.

Bright entered Parliament in 1843 as MP for Durham and, with Cobden, led the agitation that secured repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846. He opposed British involvement in the Crimean War — his famous "Angel of Death" speech lamented that it "has spread its wings over the country" — and lost his seat over his unpopular stance, later representing Manchester and then Birmingham.

Reform and later career

Bright campaigned for parliamentary reform and a wider franchise, helping build the momentum behind the Reform Act of 1867; he is credited with coining the phrase "the mother of parliaments" about England. He opposed capital punishment and church establishments, supported the Union during the American Civil War, and sympathised with Irish grievances while resisting Home Rule. He served in Gladstone's governments as President of the Board of Trade and later as Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster, resigning in 1882 over the British intervention in Egypt.

Legacy

Bright's plain, powerful oratory made him the pre-eminent popular speaker of Victorian England. His name is associated with the Manchester School of liberalism — free trade, peace, and retrenchment — and he remains an iconic figure in the history of the Liberal Party.
