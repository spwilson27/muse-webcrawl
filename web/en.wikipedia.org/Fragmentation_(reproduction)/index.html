Fragmentation (reproduction)

Some animals, such as starfish, can reproduce by fragmentation followed by regeneration.

Fragmentation in multicellular or colonial organisms is a form of asexual reproduction or cloning in which an organism is split into fragments. Each of these fragments develops into mature, fully grown individuals that are clones of the original organism.

Mechanism

Fragmentation may be deliberate (a reproductive strategy) or accidental, caused by predators, waves or human activity. After splitting, each fragment must regenerate the tissues it lacks: a fragment survives only if it contains enough of the right cell types and structures to rebuild a complete body. The process relies on mitotic cell division and, in animals, on developmental flexibility such as the stem cells (neoblasts) found in planarian flatworms.

Examples

Many sponges, cnidarians (including corals), bryozoans and tunicates reproduce by fragmentation, as do annelid worms and echinoderms — some starfish can regrow an entire animal from a single arm plus part of the central disc. Among plants, mosses and liverworts fragment readily, and many algae, fungi and lichens propagate through fragments or specialised structures such as soredia. Coral fragmentation is used in reef restoration, where broken coral pieces are grown out and replanted.

Advantages and disadvantages

Fragmentation allows rapid colonisation of a habitat without mates, seeds or fertilisation, but because offspring are genetically identical to the parent, populations produced this way have little genetic diversity and may be vulnerable to disease or environmental change.
