Property tax

A property tax (also called a millage rate or levies) is an ad valorem tax on the value of a property, usually levied by local governments on real estate. The tax is typically calculated by applying a rate to the assessed value of the property, and the property owner is obliged to pay it regardless of income derived from the property. Property taxes are a principal source of revenue for municipalities, counties, school districts, and other local authorities.

Assessment

Taxing authorities periodically assess the value of taxable property. Assessments may be based on fair market value, and the tax bill is computed by multiplying the assessed value by the local tax rate, often expressed in mills (one mill = one dollar per thousand dollars of value). Many jurisdictions offer exemptions, such as homestead exemptions for a primary residence, or relief for elderly, disabled, or veteran property owners.

By country

In the United States, property taxes fund the majority of local public school budgets and vary widely between states and localities. In the United Kingdom, domestic property was historically taxed through rates, replaced by the Community Charge and then Council Tax in 1993, while business premises pay non-domestic (business) rates. Many European countries levy recurring taxes on immovable property, though often at lower levels than in North America.

Criticism and reform

Critics argue that property taxes can burden owners whose incomes do not rise with property values, and assessment disputes are common. Reform measures have included assessment caps and rate limits, most famously California's Proposition 13 (1978), which sharply limited property tax increases. Economists often note that the land component of the property tax is relatively efficient, an idea developed in proposals for a land value tax.
