A cruise missile is a guided missile used against terrestrial or naval targets that remains in the atmosphere and flies the major portion of its flight path at an approximately constant speed. Cruise missiles are designed to deliver a large warhead over long distances with high precision. Modern cruise missiles are capable of travelling at high subsonic, supersonic, or hypersonic speeds, are self-navigating, and are able to fly on a non-ballistic, extremely low-altitude trajectory.

History

The concept dates to the First World War, when experimental "aerial torpedoes" such as the Kettering Bug were developed. Germany's V-1 flying bomb of the Second World War was the first cruise missile used in combat. During the Cold War both the United States and the Soviet Union developed nuclear-armed cruise missiles, and the technology matured with terrain-following radar, small turbofan engines and satellite navigation.

Design and guidance

Cruise missiles typically use a small jet engine and deployable wings, flying at low altitude to stay beneath radar coverage. Guidance systems combine inertial navigation and GPS with terrain-contour matching (TERCOM), digital scene matching, and in some cases a terminal seeker for the final approach to the target. Warheads may be conventional unitary charges, submunitions, or nuclear.

Categories

Cruise missiles are broadly classed by speed (subsonic, supersonic, hypersonic), by range, and by launch platform: air-launched (ALCM), sea- or submarine-launched (SLCM), and ground-launched (GLCM). Well-known examples include the American Tomahawk and AGM-86, the Russian Kalibr family, and the Anglo-French Storm Shadow/SCALP.
