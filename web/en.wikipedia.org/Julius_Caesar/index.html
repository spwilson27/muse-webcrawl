Gaius Julius Caesar (100 BC – 15 March 44 BC) was a Roman general and statesman whose conquests and political career fatally weakened the Roman Republic and paved the way for the Roman Empire. A member of the First Triumvirate with Pompey and Crassus, he conquered Gaul (58–50 BC), invaded Britain, and defeated his rivals in a civil war, becoming dictator of Rome before his assassination on the Ides of March.

Early career

Born into the patrician Julii family, Caesar survived Sulla's proscriptions, served in the military in Asia and Cilicia, and was famously captured by pirates, whom he later had crucified after his ransom. Rising through the cursus honorum — quaestor, aedile, praetor — he used lavish games and alliances to build popularity, and in 63 BC was elected Pontifex Maximus.

Gaul and civil war

As proconsul of Gaul, Caesar subdued the Gallic tribes over nine years, culminating in the siege of Alesia (52 BC) against Vercingetorix; his account, Commentarii de Bello Gallico, remains a classic of Latin prose. When the Senate ordered him to surrender his command, he crossed the Rubicon River in January 49 BC — "the die is cast" — sparking civil war. He defeated Pompey at Pharsalus (48 BC), campaigned in Egypt (where he allied with Cleopatra), Pontus ("veni, vidi, vici"), Africa and Spain.

Dictatorship and assassination

As dictator, Caesar reformed the calendar (the Julian calendar), extended citizenship, founded colonies and reorganized taxation. In February 44 BC he was named dictator perpetuo. Fearing he aimed at kingship, a group of senators led by Brutus and Cassius stabbed him to death on 15 March 44 BC. His death triggered further civil wars ending with his adoptive heir Octavian's victory and the founding of the Empire.
