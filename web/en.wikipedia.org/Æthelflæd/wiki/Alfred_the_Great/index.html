Alfred the Great (c. 847–849 – 26 October 899) was King of the West Saxons from 871 to 886 and King of the Anglo-Saxons from 886 until his death. He is the only English monarch to be given the epithet "the Great". Alfred successfully defended Wessex against the Viking Great Heathen Army, defeating them decisively at the Battle of Edington in 878, and went on to lay the foundations of a unified English kingdom.

War with the Danes

After his elder brother Æthelred's death, Alfred succeeded while the Danes occupied much of England. Driven into the Somerset marshes at Athelney in 878 — the setting of the later legend of the burnt cakes — he regrouped, won at Edington, and imposed the baptism of the Danish king Guthrum and a treaty fixing the Danelaw boundary. He then reorganised his kingdom's defences with a network of fortified burhs, a restructured fyrd, and a small navy.

Reforms and learning

Alfred promoted literacy and personally sponsored translations from Latin into Old English, including Gregory's Pastoral Care, Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy, and Orosius's history. His law code combined Mosaic, Christian, and earlier English law. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle was begun under his patronage.

Legacy

His children Edward the Elder and Æthelflæd, Lady of the Mercians, extended his work, and his grandson Æthelstan became the first king of all England. Alfred was buried in Winchester, first in the Old Minster and later at Hyde Abbey.
