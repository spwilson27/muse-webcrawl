Celtic mythology

Celtic mythology is the body of myths belonging to the Celtic peoples. Like other Iron Age Europeans, Celtic peoples followed a polytheistic religion, having many gods and goddesses. The mythologies of continental Celtic peoples, such as the Gauls and the Celtiberians, did not survive Roman conquest and Christianisation in their native forms, whereas the mythology of Celtic peoples in Ireland and Wales was recorded in medieval manuscripts written by Christian scribes.

Sources

Irish mythology survives in cycles of tales: the Mythological Cycle, the Ulster Cycle (centred on Cú Chulainn and the Táin Bó Cúailnge), the Fenian Cycle (Finn mac Cumhaill and the Fianna), and the Cycles of the Kings. Welsh mythology survives chiefly in the Four Branches of the Mabinogi, the Book of Taliesin and Arthurian tradition.

Deities and themes

Common figures include the Dagda, Lugh, Morrígan, Brigid and Danu in Ireland, and Rhiannon, Arawn and Lleu Llaw Gyffes in Wales. Romano-Celtic religion on the continent honoured deities such as Taranis, Esus, Teutates, Epona and Cernunnos, known mainly from inscriptions, iconography and classical authors. Recurring themes include the Otherworld, magical cauldrons, heroic cattle raids, shape-shifting and sacred kingship.

Legacy

Celtic mythology influenced later European literature through the Arthurian romances and was mined extensively by the Celtic Revival and modern fantasy writers.
