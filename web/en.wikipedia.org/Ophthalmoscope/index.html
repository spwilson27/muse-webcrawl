Ophthalmoscope

An ophthalmoscope, also known as a funduscope, is a handheld instrument used to examine the interior of the eye (the fundus), including the retina, optic disc, macula, and blood vessels. The examination performed with it, ophthalmoscopy or fundoscopy, is a routine part of physical and eye examinations and allows clinicians to detect eye diseases and signs of systemic conditions such as hypertension and diabetes.

Types

The direct ophthalmoscope provides an upright, magnified view (about 15x) of a small area of the fundus. The indirect ophthalmoscope, worn on the head and used with a condensing lens, gives a wider, binocular, inverted view with lower magnification and is favoured by ophthalmologists. Slit-lamp biomicroscopy and modern fundus cameras extend the same principle.

History

The ophthalmoscope was invented in 1851 by Hermann von Helmholtz, who realized that light shone into the eye along the examiner's line of sight would illuminate the retina. His "Augenspiegel" revolutionized ophthalmology by making the living retina visible for the first time.
