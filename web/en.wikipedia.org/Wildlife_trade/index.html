Wildlife trade

Wildlife trade refers to the commerce of products derived from non-domesticated animals or plants, usually extracted from their natural environment or raised under controlled conditions. It involves live animals and plants as well as parts and derivatives — meat, fur, ivory, horn, timber, and ingredients for traditional medicine — and is worth many billions of dollars annually.

Regulation

International trade is regulated by the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES), signed in 1973 and in force since 1975, which lists tens of thousands of species in appendices according to the level of protection they require. National laws and enforcement networks supplement the treaty, and organizations such as TRAFFIC monitor trade flows.

Illegal trade

The illegal wildlife trade is a major driver of species decline. Poaching of African elephants for ivory and rhinoceroses for horn, the demand for pangolin scales and meat, and trafficking in exotic pets and rare timber have pushed numerous species toward extinction. Illegal trade is also linked to organized crime and raises concerns about zoonotic disease transmission, since crowded wildlife markets can facilitate the spillover of pathogens to humans. Proponents of legal, regulated trade argue that sustainable use can fund conservation and benefit local communities, a position that remains debated.
