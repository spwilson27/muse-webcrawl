Panic of 1907

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Panic of 1907 – also known as the 1907 Bankers' Panic or Knickerbocker Crisis – was a financial crisis that took place in the United States over a three-week period starting in mid-October 1907, when the New York Stock Exchange fell almost 50% from its peak the previous year. The panic occurred during a period of economic recession, amid numerous runs on banks and trust companies.

Causes and course

The crisis was triggered by a failed attempt in October 1907 to corner the market on the stock of the United Copper Company by Augustus Heinze and his brother. When the corner collapsed, banks that had lent to the scheme suffered losses, and suspicion fell on institutions associated with Heinze, including the Knickerbocker Trust Company, then New York's third-largest trust. A run on the Knickerbocker forced it to suspend operations on 22 October 1907, and panic spread to other trusts and banks.

With no central bank to act as lender of last resort, the financier J. P. Morgan organised a rescue: he pledged his own money, persuaded other bankers to do the same, locked trust presidents in his library until they agreed to a bailout plan, and arranged liquidity to keep solvent institutions afloat. The federal government also deposited funds in New York banks. Confidence gradually returned by late November.

Aftermath

The panic produced a sharp recession in 1907–08 and exposed the fragility of the American banking system, including the inadequacy of reserves and the lack of an elastic currency. It spurred monetary reform: Congress passed the Aldrich–Vreeland Act in 1908, creating the National Monetary Commission, whose work led to the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 and the establishment of the Federal Reserve System, the United States' central bank.
