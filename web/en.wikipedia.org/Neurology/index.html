Neurology (from Greek: νεῦρον (neûron), "string, nerve" and the suffix -logia, "study of") is the branch of medicine dealing with the diagnosis and treatment of all categories of conditions and disease involving the nervous system, which comprises the brain, the spinal cord and the peripheral nerves. Neurological practice relies heavily on the field of neuroscience, the scientific study of the nervous system.

Scope

A neurologist is a physician specialising in neurology and trained to investigate, diagnose and treat neurological disorders. Neurologists diagnose and treat numerous conditions, among them stroke and other cerebrovascular diseases; epilepsy; headache disorders including migraine; dementias such as Alzheimer's disease; movement disorders such as Parkinson's disease; demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis; neuromuscular diseases; infections of the nervous system; and disorders of sleep, pain and consciousness. Surgical treatment is the province of the related speciality of neurosurgery.

Examination and diagnosis

The neurological examination tests mental status, cranial nerves, motor and sensory function, reflexes, coordination and gait, and remains central to localising lesions — often summarised in the maxim "where is the lesion?" — before investigations such as neuroimaging, electroencephalography (EEG), nerve conduction studies and electromyography, lumbar puncture and genetic testing.

History

Modern neurology emerged in the 19th century with clinicians such as Jean-Martin Charcot at the Salpêtrière in Paris, John Hughlings Jackson, and William Gowers, and was transformed in the 20th century by EEG, CT and MRI, thrombolysis for stroke, and molecular genetics.
