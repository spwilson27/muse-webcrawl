Valuation (finance)

Valuation is the analytical process of determining the current (or projected) worth of an asset or a company. In finance, valuation is used to estimate the fair value of securities, businesses, real estate and other assets for purposes such as investment analysis, mergers and acquisitions, capital budgeting, financial reporting and taxation.

Approaches

The three broad approaches to valuation are the income approach, the market approach and the asset-based approach. In the income approach, value derives from the present value of expected future cash flows, discounted at a rate reflecting risk — the basis of discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis. The market approach infers value from the prices of comparable assets or companies, using multiples such as price-to-earnings. The asset-based approach sums the values of a company's individual assets less its liabilities.

Valuation of securities

For publicly traded shares, absolute valuation models such as the dividend discount model and DCF analysis estimate intrinsic value independently of market prices, while relative valuation compares multiples across peer companies. Bonds are valued by discounting their coupon and principal payments at the market yield. Options and other derivatives employ models such as Black–Scholes. Valuation conclusions are sensitive to assumptions about growth, discount rates (often the weighted average cost of capital) and terminal value, so analysts commonly present ranges and sensitivity analyses.

Contexts

Independent valuations are required in many settings: fairness opinions in takeovers, purchase price allocation after acquisitions, impairment testing of goodwill, employee share option schemes, and disputes. In venture capital, the term "valuation" — pre-money and post-money — denotes the negotiated worth of a start-up at a funding round, and a private company valued above US$1 billion is colloquially called a unicorn.
