Metabolism

A simplified map of the major metabolic pathways in a cell.

Metabolism (from Greek: μεταβολή metabolē, "change") is the set of life-sustaining chemical reactions in organisms. The three main functions of metabolism are: the conversion of food into energy to run cellular processes; the conversion of food/fuel into building blocks for proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and some carbohydrates; and the elimination of metabolic wastes.

These enzyme-catalysed reactions allow organisms to grow and reproduce, maintain their structures, and respond to their environments. The word metabolism can also refer to the sum of all chemical reactions that occur in living organisms, including digestion and the transportation of substances between cells.

Catabolism and anabolism

Metabolic reactions are traditionally divided into two categories. Catabolism is the set of reactions that break down large molecules into smaller units, releasing energy — for example, the breakdown of glucose in cellular respiration. Anabolism comprises the energy-consuming reactions that build complex molecules from simpler ones, such as protein synthesis from amino acids. Energy released by catabolism, largely captured as adenosine triphosphate (ATP), powers anabolic work.

Key pathways

  • Glycolysis: anaerobic breakdown of glucose to pyruvate in the cytoplasm, netting ATP and NADH.
  • Citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle): oxidation of acetyl-CoA in the mitochondrial matrix, producing electron carriers.
  • Oxidative phosphorylation: ATP synthesis driven by the electron transport chain and chemiosmosis.
  • Photosynthesis: in plants and some bacteria, fixation of carbon dioxide using light energy.
  • Gluconeogenesis, glycogenesis, lipogenesis, beta oxidation, and amino-acid metabolism integrate carbohydrate, fat, and protein pools.

Regulation and history

Metabolism is regulated by hormones such as insulin and glucagon, by allosteric control of enzymes, and by feedback inhibition. The scientific study of metabolism began with early experiments on digestion and fermentation; Eduard Buchner's discovery of cell-free fermentation (1897), and the mapping of pathways by Hans Krebs, Fritz Lipmann and others in the 20th century, founded modern biochemistry. Basal metabolic rate, measurable by calorimetry, quantifies resting energy expenditure in animals.
