News media

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The news media or news industry are forms of mass media that focus on delivering news to the general public. These include news sources in print, broadcast, and digital formats: newspapers and news magazines, television and radio news programmes, and online journalism, including news websites, news aggregators, and social media distribution of reporting.

History

News publishing began with handwritten newsletters and early printed newspapers in the 17th century, expanded with the penny press of the 1830s, and was transformed successively by the telegraph, wire services such as the Associated Press, radio broadcasting in the 1920s, television in the mid-20th century, 24-hour cable news from 1980, and finally the internet and mobile phones, which made news continuous and interactive.

Roles and criticism

In democratic theory, news media serve as watchdogs over government and business, agenda-setters for public debate, and a shared source of factual information; journalism's professional norms emphasise verification, independence, and accountability. The business models of news media have been severely disrupted by the migration of advertising to online platforms, leading to widespread closures of local newspapers in many countries. News media are also criticised for sensationalism, political bias, concentration of ownership, and the pressures of the 24-hour news cycle, and public trust in news media has declined in many surveys. The term "fake news" rose to prominence in the 2010s in debates about misinformation online.
