Septic shock is the most severe form of sepsis, a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Under the Sepsis-3 definitions (2016), septic shock is a subset of sepsis in which the underlying circulatory and cellular/metabolic abnormalities are profound enough to substantially increase mortality: clinically, a patient requires vasopressor therapy to maintain a mean arterial pressure of at least 65 mmHg and has a serum lactate above 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation.

Pathophysiology

Microbial products such as bacterial endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide) trigger an overwhelming inflammatory response with massive release of cytokines and nitric oxide. The results are profound peripheral vasodilation, increased capillary permeability with leakage of plasma into tissues, maldistribution of blood flow, myocardial depression, and activation of coagulation that can progress to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Tissues are starved of oxygen both by hypotension and by microcirculatory failure, producing lactic acidosis and sequential failure of the lungs (ARDS), kidneys, liver, brain, and other organs — multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS).

Diagnosis and treatment

Septic shock is a medical emergency. Management follows the Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines: rapid measurement of lactate and blood cultures, broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics ideally within one hour, aggressive crystalloid fluid resuscitation, and vasopressors — norepinephrine first-line, with vasopressin often added — titrated to maintain arterial pressure. The source of infection (for example an abscess, obstructed urinary tract, or perforated bowel) must be found and controlled, by drainage or surgery if necessary. Supportive intensive care includes mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy, blood glucose control, and corticosteroids in refractory cases.

Epidemiology

Sepsis affects tens of millions of people worldwide each year; the World Health Organization has estimated some 48.9 million cases and 11 million sepsis-related deaths in 2017 alone, about one in five of all deaths globally. Septic shock carries a hospital mortality commonly above 40 percent, rising steeply with each hour's delay in antibiotic treatment and with the number of organs failing.
