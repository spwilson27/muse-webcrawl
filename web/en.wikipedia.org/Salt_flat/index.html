Salt flat

A salt flat is a flat area of ground covered by salt and other minerals, usually found in deserts. It forms where a body of salty water has evaporated, leaving behind its dissolved minerals; the resulting crust is bright white and stretches as a level plain. The term includes features also called salt pans, salars (in South America), and playas in the broadest usage.

Formation

Salt flats occur in closed (endorheic) drainage basins where inflow carries dissolved salts but there is no outlet to the sea. As water evaporates in the hot, arid climate, salts — chiefly halite (common salt), gypsum, and other evaporites — concentrate and precipitate on the surface, often cracking into polygonal patterns as the crust dries and expands. Seasonal flooding may dissolve and re-deposit the crust, producing surfaces of extraordinary flatness; satellite altimeters use large salt flats as calibration references.

Notable examples

The largest is the Salar de Uyuni in Bolivia, about 10,582 km² (4,086 sq mi), also a major source of lithium. Others include the Bonneville Salt Flats in Utah — famous for land-speed record runs because of their hard, level surface — Badwater Basin in Death Valley, Etosha Pan in Namibia, the Rann of Kutch in India, and the salt lakes of central Australia.

Uses and hazards

Besides mineral extraction (salt, lithium brines, potash, borates) and speed records, salt flats can be dangerous: the crust may be thin over soft mud that can trap vehicles, and the glare and total lack of landmarks make navigation disorienting. See also Playa and Evaporite.
