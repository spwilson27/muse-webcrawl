Amber

Amber is fossilised tree resin that has been appreciated for its colour and natural beauty since Neolithic times. It is used for jewellery and ornaments, as an ingredient in perfume, and as a healing agent in folk medicine. Most amber is 30–90 million years old; the oldest, from the Carboniferous, is over 300 million years old.

Amber
Material: Fossilised tree resin
Colour: Golden yellow to orange, brown, red
Famous deposits: Baltic coast, Dominican Republic, Myanmar

Formation

Resin exuded by trees — in the Baltic case, conifers — hardens and is buried in sediments, polymerising over millions of years. Younger, incompletely fossilised resin is called copal. Baltic amber (succinite) accounts for most of world supply; the Amber Room of the Catherine Palace was paneled with it.

Inclusions

Amber often traps insects, spiders, plant fragments, and rarely small vertebrates, preserved in minute detail. Burmese amber (~99 Ma) has yielded feathered dinosaur tails, frogs, and lizards, transforming palaeontology. The Greek word for amber, ēlektron, gave rise to "electricity" because amber rubbed with fur attracts light objects via static charge.

Trade and use

Prehistoric Amber Road routes carried Baltic amber to the Mediterranean. Amber is worked by carving and polishing, imitated by plastics and copal, and tested by its warmth to the touch, low density (it floats in saturated salt water), and pine-like smell when heated.
