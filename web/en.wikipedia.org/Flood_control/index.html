Flood control

A storm surge barrier protecting a low-lying estuary.

Flood control (or flood mitigation, flood protection, or flood defence) includes all methods used to reduce or prevent the detrimental effects of flood waters. Flood relief methods are used to reduce the effects of flood waters or high water levels and include structural measures such as dams and levees as well as non-structural measures such as land-use planning, forecasting, and emergency response.

Structural methods

  • Dams and reservoirs — store floodwater and release it at controlled rates; examples include the Hoover Dam and the Three Gorges Dam.
  • Levees, dikes and flood walls — raised embankments or walls running alongside rivers and coasts that confine water to its channel.
  • Storm surge barriers — movable gates across estuaries, such as the Thames Barrier and the Dutch Delta Works, closed when dangerous surges threaten.
  • Channel improvements — dredging, straightening and lining of rivers to increase conveyance, and diversion channels or floodways that carry excess water around settlements.
  • Coastal defences — seawalls, breakwaters, revetments and beach nourishment against coastal flooding and erosion.

Non-structural methods

Modern flood risk management increasingly favours non-structural approaches: floodplain zoning and building codes that keep development out of high-risk areas; flood forecasting and early-warning systems; flood insurance; wetland and floodplain restoration to store water naturally; sustainable urban drainage systems (SuDS); and "building with nature" designs that work with sediment and water processes rather than against them. The Netherlands' "Room for the River" programme exemplifies the shift from raising dikes to giving rivers more space.

Limitations

Structural defences can create a false sense of security known as the "levee effect", encouraging dense development behind defences that fails catastrophically when design limits are exceeded. Hard defences may also transfer flood risk downstream, damage ecosystems, and require expensive maintenance. Climate change — with heavier rainfall and rising seas — is placing increasing strain on defences designed for historical conditions.

History

Flood control works are ancient: earthen embankments were built along the Nile, the Indus, and China's Yellow River millennia ago. Large-scale state flood management grew with the Mississippi River Commission and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers after the Great Mississippi Flood of 1927, and with the Netherlands' response to the North Sea flood of 1953, which produced the Delta Works.
