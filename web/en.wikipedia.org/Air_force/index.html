Air force

An air force is the branch of a nation's armed forces principally responsible for aerial warfare. Air forces typically maintain aircraft such as fighters, bombers, transports, helicopters and unmanned aerial vehicles, and conduct operations to gain air superiority, strike ground and naval targets, provide airlift, and perform reconnaissance and surveillance.

History

Military aviation began within armies and navies in the years before the First World War, with aircraft used for reconnaissance, artillery spotting, bombing and air combat. The world's first independent air force was Britain's Royal Air Force, formed on 1 April 1918 by merging the Royal Flying Corps and Royal Naval Air Service. Between the wars, theorists such as Giulio Douhet and Billy Mitchell argued that airpower could be decisive through strategic bombing. The Second World War confirmed airpower's central role. Many nations established independent air forces after 1945, including the United States Air Force in 1947.

Roles

  • Air superiority: defeating enemy air forces and air defences
  • Strike: strategic bombing and interdiction of enemy forces and infrastructure
  • Close air support of ground forces
  • Airlift: transport of troops and cargo; also aerial refuelling
  • ISR: intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance
  • Other roles: combat search and rescue, special operations support, nuclear deterrence, and in some countries ballistic missile forces

Organisation

Air forces are commonly organised into commands, numbered air forces or air divisions, wings, groups and squadrons, a squadron of roughly 12–24 aircraft being the basic operational unit. Not all military aviation belongs to air forces: many countries also have naval aviation and army aviation branches.

The largest air forces by aircraft inventory include those of the United States (which operates the two largest fleets, through its Air Force and Navy), Russia, China and India.
