Locarno Treaties

The Locarno Treaties were seven agreements negotiated at Locarno, Switzerland, from 5 to 16 October 1925 and formally signed in London on 1 December 1925. In the central agreement, Germany, France and Belgium mutually guaranteed the inviolability of their common borders — the frontier established by the Treaty of Versailles, including the demilitarised Rhineland — with Britain and Italy acting as guarantors.

Contents

The conference was the fruit of German foreign minister Gustav Stresemann's policy of reconciliation with the West. Germany concluded arbitration treaties with France, Belgium, Poland and Czechoslovakia, while France signed mutual-assistance treaties with Poland and Czechoslovakia intended to protect them against German attack. Notably, Germany refused to guarantee its eastern borders with Poland and Czechoslovakia, accepting only arbitration — a deliberate ambiguity that historians have long debated.

Spirit of Locarno

The treaties ushered in the "Spirit of Locarno", a period of optimism about permanent peace in Europe. Germany joined the League of Nations in 1926, and Stresemann, Austen Chamberlain and Aristide Briand received Nobel Peace Prizes for their roles. The agreements also paved the way for the evacuation of Allied troops from parts of the Rhineland.

Collapse

Hitler denounced the treaties when German troops remilitarised the Rhineland on 7 March 1936, citing the Franco-Soviet pact as incompatible with Locarno. The guarantor powers did not respond militarily, and the Locarno system collapsed.
