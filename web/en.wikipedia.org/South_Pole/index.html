The South Pole, also known as the Geographic South Pole or Terrestrial South Pole, is the point in the Southern Hemisphere where the Earth's axis of rotation meets its surface. It is located on the continent of Antarctica, on a featureless, windswept plateau at an elevation of 2,835 metres (9,301 ft) above sea level, beneath about 2,700 metres of ice. Every direction from the South Pole is north.

Exploration

James Clark Ross located the south magnetic pole region in 1841, but the geographic pole was not reached until the Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration. Roald Amundsen's Norwegian expedition arrived on 14 December 1911, five weeks before Robert Falcon Scott's British party, all of whom perished on the return journey. Richard E. Byrd claimed a flight over the pole in 1929. A permanent station was built for the International Geophysical Year: the Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station, occupied continuously since 1957.

Climate

The South Pole has a desert climate with almost no precipitation and average temperatures ranging from about −28 °C in summer to −60 °C in winter. The sun rises and sets only once a year, giving six months of daylight and six of darkness.

Research

The station hosts major astronomy and astrophysics projects exploiting the clear, dry air and stable atmosphere, including the South Pole Telescope and the IceCube Neutrino Observatory, which uses a cubic kilometre of Antarctic ice to detect neutrinos.
