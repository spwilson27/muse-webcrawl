The Northwest Passage is the sea route between the Atlantic and Pacific oceans through the waterways of the Canadian Arctic Archipelago. Sought for centuries as a trading shortcut from Europe to Asia, it was finally navigated by Roald Amundsen in 1903–1906. As Arctic sea ice declines with climate change, the passage is becoming seasonally navigable, reviving both its commercial promise and the legal dispute over its status.

Search and exploration

From Martin Frobisher (1576) and John Davis to Henry Hudson, explorers probed the Arctic coast without success, often at terrible cost. After the Napoleonic Wars the Royal Navy mounted systematic searches; the Franklin expedition of 1845 — HMS Erebus and Terror, 129 men — vanished entirely, and the rescue expeditions that followed mapped the archipelago and found the route. Robert McClure's party completed a transit (partly by sledge) in 1850–1854. Amundsen's small sloop Gjøa made the first complete single-vessel transit in 1903–1906, wintering at Gjoa Haven and learning survival skills from the Netsilik Inuit.

Modern use

The icebreaker-tanker SS Manhattan transited in 1969 to test oil shipments from Alaska, prompting Canada to assert sovereignty; the US icebreaker Polar Sea transit of 1985 deepened the dispute, which remains unresolved: Canada claims the passage as internal waters enclosed by straight baselines, the United States and others regard it as an international strait. The first commercial transit came in 2013 (the bulk carrier Nordic Orion carrying coal), and cruise ships such as Crystal Serenity (2016) have since made the voyage, though ice, remoteness and sparse infrastructure still limit traffic. The wrecks of Franklin's ships were located in 2014 and 2016 with Inuit knowledge playing a key role.
