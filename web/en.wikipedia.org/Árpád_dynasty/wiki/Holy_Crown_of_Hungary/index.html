Holy Crown of Hungary

The Holy Crown of Hungary, with its characteristically tilted cross.

The Holy Crown of Hungary (Hungarian: Szent Korona), also known as the Crown of Saint Stephen, was the coronation crown used by the Kingdom of Hungary for most of its existence; kings were crowned with it from the 12th century onward. It is traditionally identified as the crown associated with Saint Stephen I, the first king of Hungary, though scholars hold that the surviving crown was assembled from parts of different dates.

Description

The crown consists of two main parts: a lower band of Byzantine origin (the corona graeca), set with enamel plaques depicting Christ and Byzantine emperors, and a Latin upper structure (the corona latina) of intersecting arches with enamel images of the apostles. It is surmounted by a cross that is famously bent, probably damaged in the 17th century. Tradition held that no king was legitimate until crowned with the Holy Crown, and the "doctrine of the Holy Crown" developed into a theory of Hungarian statehood in which sovereignty resided in the crown itself.

History

The crown survived fires, thefts and burials during Hungary's turbulent medieval and early modern history. At the end of the Second World War it was taken west and eventually held by the United States at Fort Knox; it was returned to Hungary in 1978 in a ceremony led by Secretary of State Cyrus Vance. Since 2000 it has been displayed in the central hall of the Hungarian Parliament Building, guarded by the Crown Guard.
