From the Hungarian conquest to the Holy Crown

The Hungarian conquest of the Carpathian Basin (honfoglalás, "taking of the homeland"), completed around 900 AD under Grand Prince Árpád and his successors, created the Magyar principality from which the Kingdom of Hungary emerged. A century later, the coronation of Stephen I — traditionally at Christmas 1000 or in 1001, with a crown sent, according to legend, by Pope Sylvester II — founded the Christian kingdom whose chief symbol became the Holy Crown of Hungary.

Continuity in later doctrine

Medieval and early-modern Hungarian law gradually developed the doctrine of the Holy Crown: sovereignty was vested in the crown as a corporate body comprising king and nobility, and the crown's lands could not be alienated. In this view the crown preserved the legal continuity of the state founded on the Árpád-era conquest and Stephen's coronation through Ottoman occupation, Habsburg rule and the dual monarchy.

The crown itself

The surviving crown is a composite object: its lower band (the "Greek crown") is a Byzantine work of the 1070s, while the upper part (the "Latin crown") is later, probably twelfth-century. Its bent cross, famously askew, became a national emblem.
