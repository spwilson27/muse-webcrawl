The Holy Crown of Hungary (Hungarian: Szent Korona), also known as the Crown of Saint Stephen, was the coronation crown used by the Kingdom of Hungary for most of its existence; kings have been crowned with it since the twelfth century. The crown symbolises Hungary's continuity from the state founded after the Hungarian conquest of the Carpathian Basin and the coronation of Stephen I, the kingdom's first Christian king, around 1000 AD.

Structure

The crown consists of two main parts: the lower Corona Graeca, an open Byzantine band of enamel plaques sent from Constantinople in the 1070s, and the upper Corona Latina, formed of gold straps bearing enamel images of the apostles. A cross on top leans to one side, likely damaged in the seventeenth century, and the tilt has itself become iconic.

The doctrine of the Holy Crown

From the later Middle Ages, Hungarian jurists — classically Stephen Werbőczy's Tripartitum (1517) — taught that sovereignty resided in the Holy Crown, uniting monarch and political nation. The doctrine underpinned Hungarian claims to constitutional distinctness within the Habsburg monarchy and, after 1867, Austria-Hungary. The crown was returned by the United States in 1978 and since 2000 has been kept in the Parliament Building.
