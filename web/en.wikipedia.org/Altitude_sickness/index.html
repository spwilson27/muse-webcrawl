Altitude sickness

CauseLow oxygen at high altitude

Descent and oxygen are the definitive treatments for serious altitude illness.

Altitude sickness, also known as acute mountain sickness (AMS), is a condition caused by exposure to low partial pressure of oxygen at high altitude, typically above 2,500 metres (8,000 ft), when ascent is faster than the body's acclimatisation. It ranges from mild headache and nausea to the life-threatening high-altitude cerebral oedema (HACE) and high-altitude pulmonary oedema (HAPE).

Symptoms and forms

AMS symptoms — headache with nausea, dizziness, fatigue and disturbed sleep — usually begin 6–12 hours after arrival at altitude and resolve in a day or two if ascent stops. HACE is a progression to brain swelling causing ataxia and confusion; HAPE is fluid accumulation in the lungs causing breathlessness at rest and a cough. Both can be rapidly fatal.

Cause

At altitude the lower air pressure reduces the oxygen available per breath, producing hypoxaemia. The body's compensatory hyperventilation and fluid shifts, together with hypoxic cerebral vasodilation, are thought to produce the symptoms; individual susceptibility varies and is partly genetic, while fitness does not protect.

Prevention and treatment

Gradual ascent with rest days is the main prevention; acetazolamide and, in some settings, dexamethasone are used prophylactically. The definitive treatment for worsening illness is descent; supplemental oxygen, portable hyperbaric bags, dexamethasone for HACE and nifedipine for HAPE are used when descent is delayed.
