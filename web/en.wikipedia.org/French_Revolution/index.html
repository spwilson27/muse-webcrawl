French Revolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

French Revolution
Part of the Atlantic Revolutions
Date: 5 May 1789 – 9 November 1799
Location: France
Result: Abolition of the Ancien Régime and feudal privileges; Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen; rise of Napoleon Bonaparte

The French Revolution was a period of radical political and societal change in France that began with the Estates General of 1789 and ended with the formation of the French Consulate in November 1799. Many of its ideas are considered fundamental principles of liberal democracy, while its values and institutions remain central to modern French political discourse.

Causes

France faced a fiscal crisis after costly wars, including support for the American Revolution, an inequitable tax system burdening the Third Estate, food shortages, and Enlightenment ideas challenging absolute monarchy under Louis XVI. See Enlightenment and Louis XVI.

Revolutionary course

The Estates General convened in May 1789; the Third Estate formed the National Assembly and swore the Tennis Court Oath. On 14 July 1789 Parisians stormed the Bastille. In August the Assembly abolished feudal privileges and adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. The monarchy was abolished in 1792, Louis XVI was executed in January 1793, and Maximilien Robespierre and the Committee of Public Safety presided over the Reign of Terror (1793–94).

Thermidor and Directory

Robespierre was overthrown in Thermidor (July 1794). A more conservative Directory governed from 1795 until Napoleon Bonaparte's coup of 18 Brumaire in 1799.

Legacy

The Revolution spread ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity, reformed law — later codified in the Napoleonic Code — and inspired revolutions across Europe and Latin America, while its violence remains debated.
