Civil Rights Movement

Date: 1954–1968 (main phase)
Location: United States, especially the South
Key figures: Martin Luther King Jr., Rosa Parks, Malcolm X, John Lewis
Result: Civil Rights Act of 1964; Voting Rights Act of 1965; Civil Rights Act of 1968

The civil rights movement was a social and political movement in the United States, principally during the 1950s and 1960s, that sought to abolish legalized racial segregation and discrimination against African Americans and to secure their constitutional rights, including the right to vote.

Origins and milestones

The movement built on decades of organizing by the NAACP and others. The Supreme Court's decision in Brown v. Board of Education (1954) declared school segregation unconstitutional. The Montgomery bus boycott (1955–56), sparked by Rosa Parks's arrest, brought Martin Luther King Jr. to prominence. Sit-ins, Freedom Rides, and the Birmingham campaign used nonviolent direct action to expose segregation, while the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom in August 1963 drew some 250,000 people and featured King's "I Have a Dream" speech.

Legislation

Federal action followed sustained activism: the Civil Rights Act of 1964 outlawed segregation in public accommodations and employment discrimination; the Voting Rights Act of 1965 struck down barriers to Black voting in the South; and the Civil Rights Act of 1968 addressed housing discrimination, passed days after King's assassination in Memphis.

Diversity and legacy

The movement encompassed many organizations and strategies, from the Southern Christian Leadership Conference and the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee to the Nation of Islam and, later, the Black Power movement. Its tactics and moral framing influenced subsequent movements for women's rights, disability rights, and other causes, and it remains a defining episode in American history.
