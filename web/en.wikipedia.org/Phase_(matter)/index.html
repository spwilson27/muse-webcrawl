Phase (matter)

Other phasesBose–Einstein condensate, supercritical fluid, liquid crystal, quark–gluon plasma, superfluid
Key conceptPhase transition
RepresentationPhase diagram

In the physical sciences, a phase is a region of space (a thermodynamic system) throughout which all physical properties of a material are essentially uniform. Familiar examples are the distinct states of matter: solid, liquid, gas, and plasma. Within a system, different phases are separated by phase boundaries across which properties change discontinuously (in first-order transitions).

Definition

A phase is characterised by uniform physical and chemical properties. A system may contain several phases of the same substance — for example, a glass of ice water contains solid ice and liquid water as two phases — as well as phases of different composition, as in oil and water mixtures, which are two distinct liquid phases. The number of phases, components, and degrees of freedom is governed by the Gibbs phase rule, F = C − P + 2.

Types of phases

Beyond the classical states, many more exotic phases are known: polymorphs of solids (e.g. diamond and graphite; the many phases of ice), liquid crystals, superfluids, superconductors (distinguished by electrical properties), magnetic phases (ferromagnetic, antiferromagnetic), Bose–Einstein condensates, supercritical fluids, and the quark–gluon plasma believed to exist in the early universe.

Phase transitions

Changes between phases — melting, freezing, vaporisation, condensation, sublimation, deposition, and ionisation — occur when temperature, pressure, or another variable crosses a phase boundary, as mapped on a phase diagram. At the triple point all three classical phases coexist; beyond the critical point the liquid–gas distinction disappears.
