Mississippi Delta

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Mississippi Delta, also known as the Yazoo–Mississippi Delta, is the distinctive northwestern section of the U.S. state of Mississippi that lies between the Mississippi and Yazoo rivers. It is an alluvial plain of exceptionally fertile soil, roughly 200 miles (320 km) long and up to 87 miles (140 km) wide, bounded by Memphis, Tennessee to the north and Vicksburg, Mississippi to the south.

Despite its name, the region is not the river delta of the Mississippi River at the Gulf of Mexico — that is the Mississippi River Delta in Louisiana — but an inland floodplain built by millennia of river flooding.

History and culture

The Delta was largely wilderness and swamp until the late nineteenth century, when railroads and levee building opened it to intensive cotton cultivation. Large plantations worked first by enslaved people and later by sharecroppers made the Delta one of the world's great cotton-producing regions and one of the poorest regions of the United States, with a majority African-American population.

The Delta is internationally known as the birthplace of Delta blues. Musicians such as Charley Patton, Robert Johnson, Son House, Muddy Waters and B.B. King emerged from its plantation communities, and their music profoundly shaped American popular music, including rock and roll. The Great Migration carried many Delta residents — and their music — to Chicago and other northern cities.

Geography and economy

The land is remarkably flat, built from rich silt deposited by flooding. Levees and drainage projects in the twentieth century reduced flooding and expanded mechanised farming of cotton, soybeans, rice and corn. Catfish farming became a significant industry in the late twentieth century. Mechanisation sharply reduced farm employment, contributing to long-term population decline in many Delta counties.
