Soft science fiction

Soft science fiction, or soft SF, is a category of science fiction with two possible definitions: it explores the "soft" sciences — psychology, political science, anthropology, economics and sociology — or it is science fiction that is not scientifically rigorous, depending more on speculative extrapolation of human behaviour and society than on hard technology.

The term emerged in contrast to hard science fiction, which emphasises scientific accuracy and technical detail. Soft science fiction works are characterised more by speculation on future societies, political systems, human relationships and character development than by detailed depictions of technology or physics.

Definition and scope

Two overlapping usages exist. In the first, "soft" refers to the subject matter: stories concerned with the social sciences, sometimes called social science fiction. In the second, "soft" describes the treatment: the science is treated loosely, and plausibility of machinery or physics is secondary to theme, mood or allegory. Many works satisfy both senses.

Soft SF often employs devices such as faster-than-light travel, telepathy or time travel without detailed justification, using them as narrative tools to examine the human condition.

Examples

Authors frequently associated with soft science fiction include Ursula K. Le Guin, whose novels The Left Hand of Darkness and The Dispossessed explore anthropology, gender and political theory; Ray Bradbury, whose The Martian Chronicles and Fahrenheit 451 are more concerned with society and censorship than with engineering; and Frank Herbert, whose Dune saga foregrounds ecology, religion and politics. The New Wave movement of the 1960s and 1970s is often identified with a turn toward softer, more literary concerns.
