North-West Rebellion

The North-West Rebellion (French: Rébellion du Nord-Ouest), also known as the North-West Resistance, was a resistance by the Métis people under Louis Riel and an associated uprising by First Nations Cree and Assiniboine against the Canadian government in 1885. The conflict took place in the District of Saskatchewan of the North-West Territories, in what is now the Canadian province of Saskatchewan and, to a lesser extent, Alberta.

Causes

The Métis of the Saskatchewan valley feared for their land rights, their culture and their livelihoods as settlers arrived from the east and the buffalo herds on which many Plains peoples depended disappeared. Their petitions to Ottawa went largely unanswered. In June 1884 the Métis brought Louis Riel back from exile in Montana to lead their cause; in March 1885, after negotiations failed, Riel proclaimed a provisional government at Batoche.

Course of the conflict

Fighting began at Duck Lake on 26 March 1885, where a party of North-West Mounted Police and volunteers was defeated. Cree forces under Poundmaker and Big Bear became involved, with victories at Frog Lake and Cut Knife, and an inconclusive action at Fish Creek. General Frederick Middleton advanced on Batoche with a force transported rapidly west by the new Canadian Pacific Railway. The Battle of Batoche (9–12 May 1885) ended with the fall of the Métis capital and Riel's surrender on 15 May.

Aftermath

Poundmaker and Big Bear surrendered in the following weeks. Eight Indigenous men were hanged at Battleford in the largest mass execution in Canadian history, and Louis Riel was tried for high treason and hanged at Regina on 16 November 1885, an event that deepened the political divide between English and French Canada. The rebellion was the first occasion on which the Canadian militia fought as an independent force, and it prompted the completion and financing of the transcontinental railway.
