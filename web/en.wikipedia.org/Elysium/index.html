Elysium, also called the Elysian Fields (Greek: Ἠλύσιον πεδίον, Ēlýsion pedíon), is the blessed afterlife of ancient Greek and later Roman religion — a paradisiacal realm reserved for the heroic, the virtuous and those beloved by the gods, where the dead live in perpetual happiness without labour, sorrow or disease.

In Greek literature

In Homer's Odyssey, the Elysian Plain lies at the western edge of the earth, where the blessed enjoy a gentle climate with soft breezes and an easy afterlife granted by the gods; only figures such as Menelaus are admitted. In later poetry, notably Pindar and Virgil, Elysium becomes a more developed region of the underworld itself: in the Aeneid, Aeneas descends into the underworld and finds the Elysian Fields as the final resting place of the blessed, with green meadows, myrtle groves and inhabitants singing in choirs. Virgil's account strongly influenced Roman and Christian conceptions of heaven.

Inhabitants and geography

The blessed dead are said to drink from Lethe to forget their earthly lives before rebirth, and in some traditions only those initiated into the Eleusinian Mysteries are admitted. Elysium is ruled in some accounts by Cronus, and its entrance is variously placed beyond the Pillars of Hercules. It stands in contrast to Tartarus, the abyss of punishment for the wicked, and to the grey, shadowy afterlife of ordinary souls described in Homer.

In later culture

The word "Elysian" became a poetic synonym for anything blissful or paradisiacal, most famously in the Champs-Élysées ("Elysian Fields") of Paris. The concept has inspired countless works of art, music — including Schubert's "Elysium" and Strauss's Also sprach Zarathustra — and modern popular culture.
