Asymptotic expansion


Successive partial sums of an asymptotic expansion approach the function, then diverge.

In mathematics, an asymptotic expansion, or asymptotic series, is a formal series of functions whose partial sums approximate a given function increasingly well for each fixed number of terms as the argument approaches a limit point — even though the series as a whole usually diverges.

Definition

A series Σ anφn(z) is an asymptotic expansion of a function f if, for every N, the difference between f and the sum of the first N terms is of the order of the last term retained as z approaches the limit. Poincaré formalised the concept in 1886. Because the series diverges, each function has at most one expansion in a given scale, but one expansion may serve for many functions differing by exponentially small amounts (the Stokes phenomenon).

Examples

  • Stirling's series for the gamma function
  • Asymptotic expansions of Bessel and Airy functions
  • Integration-by-parts expansions of the exponential integral
  • The asymptotic series of the error function

Asymptotic expansions are indispensable in applied mathematics and physics for approximating special functions and solutions of differential equations in limiting regimes.
