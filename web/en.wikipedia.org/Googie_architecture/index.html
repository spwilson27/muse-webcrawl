Googie architecture is a type of futurist architecture influenced by car culture, jets, the Atomic Age and the Space Age. It originated in Southern California in the late 1940s and continued approximately into the mid-1960s. Googie-style buildings — motels, coffee houses, diners, gas stations, bowling alleys, and car washes — were designed to catch the attention of motorists speeding past, using bold, upswept roofs, geometric shapes, and extensive neon signs and glass.

Origins of the name

The term comes from Googie's, a now-demolished coffee shop at Sunset Boulevard and Crescent Heights in Los Angeles, designed in 1949 by architect John Lautner. Yale professor Douglas Haskell coined "Googie" in a 1952 article, initially as a pejorative label; the style was long dismissed by the architectural establishment as vulgar and excessive, and many examples were demolished before preservationists began campaigning for them.

Characteristics

Typical Googie features include cantilevered, upswept or butterfly roofs; starburst, boomerang, and amoeba shapes; atomic motifs; kidney-shaped pools and signs; large panes of glass; and extravagant neon lettering. The style expressed postwar optimism about technology, space travel, and the automobile. Notable examples include the Theme Building at Los Angeles International Airport (1961), Seattle's Space Needle (1962, in a related idiom), the original McDonald's restaurant designs with their golden arches, and countless roadside signs along highways such as Route 66 and in Las Vegas.

Legacy

Googie strongly influenced later commercial design and pop culture imagery of the 1950s — famously echoed in The Jetsons — and surviving examples, such as Norms restaurants and Pann's in Los Angeles, are now frequently protected as historic landmarks.
