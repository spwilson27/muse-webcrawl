Georg Ferdinand Ludwig Philipp Cantor (3 March 1845 – 6 January 1918) was a German mathematician who created set theory, which has become a fundamental theory in mathematics. He established the importance of one-to-one correspondence between the members of two sets, defined infinite and well-ordered sets, and proved that the real numbers are more numerous than the natural numbers.

Life

Cantor was born in Saint Petersburg to a Danish merchant family and moved to Germany at age eleven. He studied at the universities of Zürich, Berlin, and Göttingen, receiving his doctorate from Berlin in 1867. He spent his career at the University of Halle, where he became a full professor in 1879. His later years were marked by recurring bouts of depression and periods in sanatoria; he died in Halle in 1918.

Work

In papers of the 1870s, Cantor proved that the set of real numbers is uncountable and that the algebraic numbers are countable. His celebrated diagonal argument (1891) showed that for any set there is a strictly larger set — its power set — leading to the hierarchy of infinite cardinal numbers, denoted by the Hebrew letter aleph (ℵ). He introduced the continuum hypothesis, the conjecture that there is no cardinal number between that of the integers and that of the reals; it was later shown to be independent of the standard axioms of set theory.

Reception

Cantor's work was fiercely opposed by contemporaries such as Leopold Kronecker, who called him a "corrupter of youth", while David Hilbert famously declared: "No one shall expel us from the paradise that Cantor has created."
