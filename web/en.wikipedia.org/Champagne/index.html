Champagne is a sparkling wine originating from the Champagne region of France. The term is legally protected: under EU law and the laws of most countries, "champagne" is reserved exclusively for sparkling wine from this region made in accordance with its appellation rules. The wine is produced by inducing secondary fermentation in the bottle — the méthode champenoise — creating its characteristic carbon dioxide bubbles.

Production

The primary grapes used are the black Pinot noir and Pinot Meunier and the white Chardonnay. Most champagnes are blends of wines from multiple vintages; the blend is bottled with yeast and a small amount of sugar (liqueur de tirage), fermented a second time in the sealed bottle, aged on its lees for a minimum of 15 months for non-vintage wines, then riddled, disgorged, and dosed before final corking. Styles range from ultra-brut to sweet doux, and include blanc de blancs (white grapes only) and blanc de noirs (black grapes only).

History

Wines from Champagne have been made since the Roman era; the still red wines of the region were prized through the Middle Ages. The sparkling style rose to prominence in the late 17th and 18th centuries in association with houses such as Veuve Clicquot and Moët & Chandon. Popular tradition credits the monk Dom Pérignon with inventing champagne — he in fact worked to prevent unwanted refermentation — though his innovations in blending and corks were real. The region, its houses, and its hillside vineyards and chalk cellars were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2015.
