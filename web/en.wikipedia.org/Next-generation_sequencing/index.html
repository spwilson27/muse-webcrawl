Next-generation sequencing

Next-generation sequencing (NGS), also called high-throughput or massively parallel sequencing, refers to a family of DNA sequencing technologies that sequence millions of fragments simultaneously. They reduced the cost of sequencing a human genome from hundreds of millions of dollars in 2001 to under a thousand dollars within two decades.

Methods

Most NGS platforms fragment DNA, attach adapters, amplify the fragments and read bases in parallel. Illumina's sequencing-by-synthesis uses reversible terminator nucleotides imaged in clusters on a flow cell. Other approaches include Ion Torrent semiconductor detection of hydrogen ions, Pacific Biosciences single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing of long reads, and Oxford Nanopore sequencing, which threads DNA through protein pores and measures current changes.

Applications

NGS underpins whole-genome and exome sequencing, transcriptomics (RNA-seq), metagenomics, ancient DNA studies, cancer diagnostics and pathogen surveillance, including the rapid sequencing of SARS-CoV-2 genomes during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Context

NGS succeeded first-generation Sanger sequencing and is in turn grouped with long-read "third-generation" methods. Analysis of the huge datasets it produces relies on bioinformatics pipelines for alignment, variant calling and assembly.
