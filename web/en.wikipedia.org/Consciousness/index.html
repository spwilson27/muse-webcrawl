Consciousness

Consciousness, at its simplest, is awareness of internal and external existence. It encompasses perception, sensation, subjective experience and self-awareness, and remains one of the central problems of philosophy of mind and cognitive science: how and why physical processes in the brain give rise to experience — the so-called "hard problem", in David Chalmers's formulation.

Philosophy

René Descartes treated consciousness as indubitable — the thinking self of the cogito — yet separate from the body, a dualism that has shaped debate ever since. Modern positions range from physicalism, which identifies experience with brain processes, to functionalism, illusionism and panpsychism. Thomas Nagel's question of what it is like to be a particular organism remains a touchstone of the discussion.

Science

Neuroscience seeks the neural correlates of consciousness and has produced candidate theories including global workspace theory and integrated information theory. Clinically, levels of consciousness are assessed in disorders such as coma, the vegetative state and anaesthesia, and phenomena such as sleep, dreaming and altered states provide natural experiments for studying how experience is generated and lost.
