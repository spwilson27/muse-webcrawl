Oriented strand board

Oriented strand board (OSB) is a type of engineered wood similar to particle board, formed by adding adhesives and then compressing layers of wood strands (flakes) in specific orientations. It was invented by Armin Elmendorf in California in 1963. OSB may have a rough and variegated surface with the individual strips of around 2.5 cm × 15 cm (1 in × 6 in), lying unevenly across each other, and is produced in a variety of types and thicknesses.

Manufacture and properties

Logs are debarked and sliced into strands, which are dried, blended with synthetic resin adhesives and wax, and formed into mats in which the outer layers' strands run parallel to the panel's length and core strands run perpendicular—like the cross-lamination of plywood. The mats are pressed under heat and high pressure into dense, rigid panels. Common resins include phenol formaldehyde and polymeric MDI. OSB grades (OSB/0 to OSB/4 in European standards) range from non-load-bearing boards to heavy-duty structural panels.

Uses

OSB is widely used for wall, floor, and roof sheathing in residential construction, where in North America it has largely displaced plywood in many applications because of its lower cost and efficient use of fast-growing trees such as aspen poplar and southern yellow pine. It is also used for I-joist webs, packaging, and furniture frames. Its main weakness is edge swelling when exposed to prolonged moisture, so it is graded and protected for dry or humid service conditions accordingly.
