Machine tool

A machine tool is a machine for handling or machining metal or other rigid materials, usually by cutting, boring, grinding, shearing, or other forms of deformation. Machine tools employ some sort of tool that does the cutting or shaping, and all have a means of constraining the workpiece and providing guided movement of the machine's parts. They are the foundation of modern manufacturing: nearly every manufactured product depends, directly or through its tooling, on machine tools.

Principal types

The lathe rotates the workpiece against a cutting tool and produces turned parts such as shafts and threads; it is sometimes called the "mother of machine tools" because it was the first type capable of reproducing itself. Milling machines move a rotating multi-point cutter across a fixed workpiece to produce flat and contoured surfaces. Drilling machines and boring machines make and enlarge holes, while grinding machines use abrasive wheels to achieve fine finishes and tight tolerances. Other classes include planers, shapers, broaches, saws, and electrical discharge machines (EDM), which erode material with sparks.

History

Bow-driven lathes existed in ancient Egypt, and water-powered trip hammers and boring mills appeared in the medieval period. Precision machine tools are inseparable from the Industrial Revolution: John Wilkinson's boring machine (1774) produced cylinders accurate enough for James Watt's steam engines; Henry Maudslay's screw-cutting lathe (c. 1800) standardised screw threads; and Joseph Whitworth's measuring machines pushed accuracy toward the millionth of an inch. In the United States, the "American system" of interchangeable parts, developed in armouries such as Springfield, relied on specialised milling machines and gauges.

Numerical control

From the 1950s, numerical control (NC), and later computer numerical control (CNC), automated machine tools using programmed instructions. Modern machining centres combine milling, drilling, and tapping in one enclosure with automatic tool changers, while CNC lathes and multi-axis machines produce complex aerospace and medical components in a single setup. Contemporary developments include additive–subtractive hybrid machines, high-speed machining, and adaptive control using in-process sensing.

Accuracy and structure

Machine tools are built for stiffness and thermal stability, since deflection and expansion directly limit accuracy. Hand-scraped guideways, hydrostatic bearings, and polymer-concrete beds are used in high-end machines. Precision is verified with laser interferometers and ball-bar tests, and accuracy grades range from hundredths of a millimetre in general work to sub-micrometre in jig boring and ultraprecision machining.
