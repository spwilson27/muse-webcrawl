Jus soli

Jus soli (Latin: "right of the soil")
Meaning: Citizenship by place of birth
Contrasts with: Jus sanguinis ("right of blood")

Jus soli (Latin for "right of the soil") is the principle by which a person's citizenship is determined by their place of birth. Under an unrestricted jus soli regime, any child born within a country's territory is automatically a citizen of that country, regardless of the citizenship of the parents. It is commonly called birthright citizenship.

Distribution

Jus soli is most characteristic of the Americas, where it was adopted widely in the 19th century partly to encourage immigration and nation-building. The United States guarantees it through the Citizenship Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment (1868), affirmed in United States v. Wong Kim Ark (1898); Canada likewise grants near-unrestricted birthright citizenship. Some thirty countries, most in the Western Hemisphere, currently apply unrestricted jus soli.

Restricted versions

Many other states apply modified forms: citizenship at birth only if a parent is a citizen or lawful permanent resident, or upon fulfilling residence requirements. The United Kingdom (from 1983), Australia (from 1986), and Ireland (from 2005) all moved from unrestricted to conditional jus soli. Most European and Asian countries rely primarily on jus sanguinis — descent from a citizen parent — often combined with limited jus soli elements to prevent statelessness.

Debate

Birthright citizenship is debated in terms of immigration policy, social cohesion, and constitutional interpretation; critics associate it with incentives for unauthorized immigration, while defenders stress its role in assimilation and in avoiding hereditary alienage.
