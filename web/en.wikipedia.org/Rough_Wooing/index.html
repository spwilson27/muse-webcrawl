Rough Wooing

The Rough Wooing (1543–1550) was a war between England and Scotland in which King Henry VIII of England sought to compel the Scots to agree to the marriage of the infant Mary, Queen of Scots, to his son Edward, and thereby to unite the two crowns and break Scotland's alliance with France, the Auld Alliance. The phrase "rough wooing" was coined by the 19th-century historian Walter Scott, adapting a quip attributed to the Earl of Huntly that he did not like "the manner of the wooing."

Background

Following the Scottish defeat at the Battle of Solway Moss in November 1542, James V of Scotland died, leaving his six-day-old daughter Mary as queen. The Treaty of Greenwich (1543) between England and Scotland's governor, the Earl of Arran, provided for Mary's marriage to Prince Edward. However, the Scottish Parliament repudiated the treaty in December 1543, preferring the French alliance, and Henry VIII responded with war.

The campaigns

In May 1544 the Earl of Hertford led an English army that landed near Leith and burned Edinburgh, followed by destructive raids across the Scottish Borders and Lothians in 1545. Hertford's orders were to put the country "to fire and sword." In February 1545 the Scots won a notable victory at the Battle of Ancrum Moor. War resumed after Henry's death in 1547 under the Duke of Somerset, Lord Protector of Edward VI: the English won a crushing victory at the Battle of Pinkie Cleugh in September 1547, the last full-scale pitched battle between the two kingdoms, and occupied garrisons in southern Scotland.

Outcome

The campaign backfired. In 1548 the Scots agreed by the Treaty of Haddington to marry Mary to the French Dauphin Francis instead, and the five-year-old queen was sent to France, where she remained for thirteen years. French troops arrived to support Scotland, and the war wound down by 1550. England gained nothing durable; the marriage policy failed, and Anglo-Scottish union eventually came peacefully in 1603 by inheritance.
