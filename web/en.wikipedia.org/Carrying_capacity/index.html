Carrying capacity

The S-shaped logistic curve: population growth slows as it approaches the carrying capacity K

The carrying capacity of an environment is the maximum population size of a biological species that can be sustained by that specific environment, given the food, habitat, water, and other resources available. In population ecology, carrying capacity is conventionally denoted K and underlies the logistic model of population growth developed by Pierre François Verhulst in 1838.

The logistic model

In the logistic equation, growth is exponential when the population is small but slows as the population approaches K, producing an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve that levels off at the carrying capacity. Populations may temporarily overshoot K when there are time lags in resource effects, leading to oscillations or "die-off" until balance is restored. Carrying capacity is not fixed: it varies with seasons, climate, technology and the presence of competitors, predators and disease.

Ecology and wildlife management

The concept is central to ecology, conservation biology and wildlife management, where K (or related density limits) informs decisions about hunting quotas, habitat protection and reintroduction. A classic case study is the reindeer herd introduced to St. Matthew Island in Alaska in 1944, which grew from 29 animals to about 6,000, destroyed its lichen food base, and crashed to 42 by 1966 — an overshoot driven by the collapse of the island's effective carrying capacity.

Human carrying capacity

Applying the idea to human beings — an idea going back to Malthus's 1798 essay — is more contentious, because technology, trade and changing consumption continually shift the relevant constraints. Published estimates of Earth's human carrying capacity vary enormously, from under one billion to well over one hundred billion, depending on assumed living standards and resource use. Related frameworks include ecological footprint accounting and the I = PAT identity (impact = population × affluence × technology).
