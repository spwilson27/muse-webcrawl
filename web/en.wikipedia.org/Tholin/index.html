Tholins are a wide variety of organic compounds formed by solar ultraviolet or cosmic ray irradiation of simple carbon-containing compounds such as methane or ethane. The term was coined by Carl Sagan and Bishun Khare from the Greek word for "muddy," describing the brownish, tar-like residues produced in experiments simulating the atmospheres of the outer Solar System.

Formation and occurrence

In laboratory experiments, tholins form when nitrogen and methane mixtures are subjected to electrical discharge or ultraviolet light. On Saturn's moon Titan, atmospheric tholins produce its orange haze and fall to the surface as dunes of organic sand. Tholins are also thought to give Pluto, Triton, and many Kuiper belt objects their reddish colouration.

Astrobiological significance

Tholins are rich in prebiotic chemistry: when hydrolysed they can yield amino acids and other building blocks of life. Although unstable in oxygen-rich environments like modern Earth's surface, they may have been abundant on the early Earth and are a major focus of research into the chemistry of the outer Solar System.
