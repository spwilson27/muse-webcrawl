The German revolutions of 1848–1849 were a series of liberal and nationalist uprisings across the states of the German Confederation, part of the wider Revolutions of 1848 in Europe. Protesters demanded constitutional government, freedom of the press and assembly, and the creation of a unified German national state.

Course of the revolution

News of the February 1848 revolution in Paris ignited protests in March — the "March demands" — in Baden, Austria, Prussia and elsewhere. In Berlin, barricade fighting on 18–19 March forced King Frederick William IV of Prussia to make concessions. In May 1848 the first freely elected all-German parliament, the Frankfurt Parliament, convened in St Paul's Church in Frankfurt to draft a constitution for a united Germany.

Failure

The parliament deliberated while princely governments regained strength. It adopted a constitution in March 1849 and offered the imperial crown to Frederick William IV, who refused it as a crown offered "from the gutter". Prussian and other government troops suppressed the remaining revolutionary campaigns, notably in Baden and the Palatinate, by the summer of 1849, and the rump parliament was dispersed in Stuttgart.

Legacy

Although defeated, the revolutions established enduring ideals of German liberal democracy and civil rights; the colours black-red-gold of the revolutionary flags later became Germany's national colours. Many defeated revolutionaries, the "Forty-Eighters", emigrated to the United States and became influential in American public life.
