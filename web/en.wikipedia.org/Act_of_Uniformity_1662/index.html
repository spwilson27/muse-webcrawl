Act of Uniformity 1662

The Act of Uniformity 1662 (14 Cha. 2 c. 4) is an Act of the Parliament of England. It required all Church of England ministers to give "unfeigned assent and consent" to everything in the 1662 Book of Common Prayer and to be ordained by a bishop if they had not already been so ordained. The Act formed part of the Clarendon Code, the series of laws by which the restored monarchy under Charles II sought to re-establish Anglican uniformity after the Civil War and Commonwealth.

Provisions

The Act mandated use of the revised Book of Common Prayer in every parish, required episcopal ordination, and demanded a declaration against the Solemn League and Covenant and against taking up arms against the king. Ministers had until St Bartholomew's Day, 24 August 1662, to comply or lose their livings.

The Great Ejection

About 2,000 clergy — roughly a fifth of the ministry — refused and were ejected from their parishes in what became known as the Great Ejection. Many continued to preach in conventicles and formed the nucleus of organized English Nonconformity: Presbyterians, Independents (Congregationalists), Baptists and Quakers who worshipped outside the established church despite persecution under the Conventicle and Five Mile Acts.

Legacy

Religious dissent remained illegal in various forms until the Toleration Act 1689 granted freedom of worship to most Protestant dissenters. The 1662 Prayer Book imposed by the Act remained the official liturgy of the Church of England, and the Act itself was gradually amended in the 19th and 20th centuries; it is regarded as a defining moment in the history of English Protestantism and the origin of the modern denominational landscape.
