Cover crop

A cover crop is a plant grown primarily for the benefit of the soil rather than for the crop yield. Cover crops are planted to manage soil erosion, soil fertility, soil quality, water, weeds, pests, diseases, and biodiversity in agroecosystems. They are often grown during off-season periods when fields would otherwise lie fallow, and are typically terminated before or as the main cash crop is planted.

Benefits

Living roots and surface residue protect soil from wind and water erosion and improve infiltration. Leguminous cover crops such as clover, vetch, and alfalfa fix atmospheric nitrogen in association with rhizobium bacteria, reducing fertiliser needs; deep-rooted species like tillage radish relieve compaction and scavenge leftover nutrients, preventing nitrate leaching. Cover crops also suppress weeds by competition and allelopathy, add organic matter, and provide habitat for beneficial insects.

Common species and management

Widely used species include cereal rye, oats, annual ryegrass, crimson clover, hairy vetch, buckwheat, and brassicas, often sown in mixtures. Management choices include the planting date and method (drilling, broadcasting, or interseeding) and the termination method — winter kill, mowing, rolling with a roller-crimper, or herbicide — which in no-till systems leaves a mulch through which the cash crop is planted.
