The Double-Cross System or XX System was a British counter-espionage and deception operation of the Second World War. Operated by MI5, it systematically captured German spies sent to Britain and "turned" them, so that instead of reporting to the Abwehr they transmitted controlled misinformation prepared by their British handlers. The system's name derives from the Twenty Committee, the body that oversaw it, whose Roman numeral XX resembled a double cross.

Operation

By early 1941 British intelligence, aided by Ultra decrypts, could verify that essentially every German agent in Britain was under control. Captured agents were given the choice of working for Britain or facing execution; most cooperated, motivated variously by money, ideology, or self-preservation. Their reports were carefully composed mixtures of checkable truth ("chickenfeed") and deception, approved by the Twenty Committee under J. C. Masterman, with day-to-day running by officers including "Tar" Robertson and case officers for individual agents.

The system's most celebrated agent was Juan Pujol García, codenamed Garbo, who built a wholly fictitious network of sub-agents and fed Berlin voluminous plausible reports. Agents such as Roman Czerniawski (Brutus), Nathalie Sergueiew (Treasure), and Dušan Popov (Tricycle) added credibility.

Deception successes

Double Cross underpinned the great strategic deceptions of the war. In Operation Mincemeat and, above all, Operation Fortitude, turned agents convinced German intelligence that the 1944 invasion of France would fall at the Pas-de-Calais (and in Norway), rather than Normandy, helping pin down the German Fifteenth Army during the crucial weeks after D-Day. German faith in their agents remained so complete that several were awarded the Iron Cross.

Masterman's official history, The Double-Cross System in the War of 1939 to 1945, written in 1945, was published in 1972 and remains the classic account of the system.
