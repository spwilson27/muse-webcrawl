The Apollo Guidance Computer (AGC) was a digital computer produced for the Apollo program, installed on board each Apollo Command Module (CM) and Apollo Lunar Module (LM). The AGC provided computation and electronic interfaces for guidance, navigation and control of the spacecraft. It was one of the first computers based on silicon integrated circuits and a landmark in the development of embedded real-time computing.

Design

The AGC was designed under the direction of Charles Stark Draper at the MIT Instrumentation Laboratory, with hardware manufactured by Raytheon. Its logic was built entirely from NOR gates in flat-pack integrated circuits: the later Block II version contained about 2,800 dual-NOR chips. The machine had a 16-bit word length, a 2.048 MHz clock divided down to an effective instruction rate of roughly 85,000 instructions per second, 4 kilowords of erasable magnetic-core RAM and up to 72 KB of fixed core rope memory, in which software was literally woven by hand as wires threaded through or around magnetic cores.

Interface

Astronauts communicated with the AGC through the DSKY (display and keyboard, pronounced "diss-key"), which featured a keypad, a set of indicator lights, and numeric displays driven by electroluminescent seven-segment-style digits. Commands were entered as "verbs" and "nouns" — for example, Verb 16 Noun 62 displayed velocity and altitude — an interface remembered for the famous "1202" program alarms during the Apollo 11 landing, which the computer handled by shedding low-priority tasks and continuing guidance without interruption.

Software

The flight software, written in AGC assembly language and an interpreted language for guidance equations, was developed by a team led by Margaret Hamilton at MIT — one of the earliest uses of the term "software engineering." Programs included Colossus for the Command Module and Luminary for the Lunar Module. The system's executive prioritised tasks and could restart gracefully after faults, features crucial to the success of the lunar landings.

Legacy

Two AGCs flew on each lunar mission — one in the Command Module and one in the Lunar Module. The computer's demand for integrated circuits made NASA one of the largest early consumers of the technology, accelerating its reliability and falling cost. Surviving AGCs and their source code, publicly released and later restored to running order by enthusiasts, remain icons of early digital computing.
