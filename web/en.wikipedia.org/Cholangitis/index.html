Cholangitis is inflammation of the bile duct system. The term most often refers to acute (ascending) cholangitis, a bacterial infection of the biliary tree usually occurring when a gallstone or other obstruction blocks bile flow; it is a medical emergency that can progress to sepsis. Other, distinct forms include primary sclerosing cholangitis and primary biliary cholangitis, which are chronic autoimmune diseases of the bile ducts.

Signs and symptoms

The classic description, Charcot's triad, combines fever (often with rigors), right upper quadrant abdominal pain, and jaundice. In severe disease, hypotension and confusion may be added, forming Reynolds' pentad. Blood tests typically show raised bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase with signs of infection; imaging by ultrasound, CT or MRCP looks for duct dilatation and the obstructing cause.

Causes and management

Obstruction most commonly results from choledocholithiasis (stones in the common bile duct), and less often from strictures, tumours of the pancreas or bile duct, or parasites. Management follows sepsis protocols: resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and — critically — relief of the obstruction, usually by endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with stone extraction or stenting, graded in urgency by severity criteria such as the Tokyo Guidelines.

Other forms

Primary sclerosing cholangitis is a chronic fibrosing inflammation of the intra- and extrahepatic ducts, strongly associated with ulcerative colitis and carrying increased risk of cholangiocarcinoma; primary biliary cholangitis (formerly "primary biliary cirrhosis") destroys small intrahepatic ducts and mainly affects middle-aged women.
