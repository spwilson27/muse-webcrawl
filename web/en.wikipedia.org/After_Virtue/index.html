After Virtue is a book of moral philosophy by Alasdair MacIntyre, first published in 1981. It is one of the most influential works of 20th-century ethics and is credited with reviving interest in virtue ethics and communitarian critiques of liberal individualism.

Argument

MacIntyre argues that contemporary moral discourse is in a state of grave disorder: modern societies retain fragments of an older, coherent moral tradition — chiefly the Aristotelian tradition of the virtues — but have lost the conceptual scheme that made those fragments intelligible. The result is "emotivism", a culture in which moral judgments are treated as mere expressions of preference, and interminable moral disagreement becomes inevitable.

He traces this breakdown through the Enlightenment project of justifying morality independently of teleology, and proposes a return to an Aristotelian account in which virtues are understood within social practices, narrative unities of human lives, and living moral traditions. The book closes with the famous claim that we are waiting "not for a Godot, but for another — doubtless very different — St. Benedict".

Influence

After Virtue helped launch the modern revival of virtue ethics and remains a central reference for communitarian political philosophy.
