Laser

A laser is a device that emits light through a process of optical amplification based on the stimulated emission of electromagnetic radiation. The word laser originated as an acronym for "light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation". Laser light is characterised by high spatial and temporal coherence, allowing it to be focused to a tight spot and to remain narrow over long distances.

Principles

A laser consists of a gain medium inside an optical resonator, usually a pair of mirrors, and a means of supplying energy to the medium ("pumping"). Atoms or molecules in the gain medium are excited to create a population inversion; photons of the right energy then stimulate the emission of further identical photons, and the resonator feeds the light back through the medium to amplify it. Part of the light escapes through a partially transmitting mirror as the output beam.

The theoretical basis was laid by Albert Einstein's 1917 analysis of stimulated emission. Charles H. Townes and colleagues built the maser (the microwave precursor) in 1953, and in 1960 Theodore H. Maiman operated the first laser, using a ruby crystal, at Hughes Research Laboratories.

Types and applications

Lasers are classified by their gain medium: gas lasers (such as helium–neon and CO2), solid-state lasers (such as Nd:YAG), semiconductor diode lasers, dye lasers, and fibre lasers. Their applications are extremely wide: optical disc drives and barcode scanners, fibre-optic communications, cutting and welding in industry, surgery and dentistry, holography, spectroscopy, rangefinding and LIDAR, printing, and scientific research from fusion experiments to gravitational-wave detectors. Laser pointers and high-power beams pose eye hazards, and lasers are regulated in safety classes from Class 1 (safe) to Class 4 (hazardous).
