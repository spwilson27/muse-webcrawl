The Third Battle of Panipat took place on 14 January 1761 between the Maratha Empire and the invading army of the Durrani Empire, commanded by Ahmad Shah Durrani (also known as Ahmad Shah Abdali). Fought at Panipat, about 97 km (60 mi) north of Delhi, it was one of the largest and most fiercely contested battles of the 18th century, and one of the decisive battles in Indian history.

Background

After the death of Aurangzeb (1707), the Mughal Empire fragmented while the Maratha Confederacy expanded across India, reaching the Punjab by 1758 and expelling the Durrani governor from Lahore. Ahmad Shah, who had invaded India repeatedly and sacked Delhi in 1757, returned in 1759 to avenge these gains, allying with the Rohilla chief Najib-ud-Daulah and Shuja-ud-Daula of Awadh. The Marathas dispatched a huge expedition north under Sadashivrao Bhau, with the Peshwa's son Vishwasrao, accompanied by an enormous train of non-combatants and pilgrims.

The campaign and the battle

The Marathas captured Delhi in August 1760 and plundered Kunjpura, but their heavy artillery and baggage slowed them, and Durrani cut their supply lines. Both armies encamped at Panipat for over two months, with the Maratha camp — containing perhaps 150,000–200,000 non-combatants — gradually starving as forage ran out. On 14 January 1761 the Marathas marched out and attacked. Ibrahim Khan Gardi's artillery and infantry initially broke the Rohilla wing, and Vishwasrao's cavalry pressed the Durrani center hard; but Ahmad Shah fed in fresh reserves, including camel-mounted swivel guns (zamburaks) and his guard, while the Maratha left wing faltered. Vishwasrao was shot dead, Sadashivrao Bhau disappeared in the mêlée (his body was later found), and by afternoon the Maratha army collapsed into rout. The pursuit and the slaughter of fugitives and camp followers continued into the next day; tens of thousands were killed, and many captives were massacred or enslaved.

Aftermath

The disaster stunned the Maratha Confederacy: Peshwa Balaji Baji Rao died within months, and a generation of Maratha leadership was lost. Yet the Durrani victory was hollow — Ahmad Shah's army, itself badly mauled, mutinied over pay and he withdrew to Afghanistan, never again able to exploit the result. Within a decade the Marathas revived under Peshwa Madhavrao I, reoccupying Delhi in 1771, though their confederacy never recovered its former unity. The power vacuum in northern India is often said to have eased the expansion of the British East India Company, victors at Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764).
