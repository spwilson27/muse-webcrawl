Generalized Stokes theorem

In vector calculus and differential geometry, the generalized Stokes theorem (also called the Stokes–Cartan theorem) is a statement about the integration of differential forms on manifolds, which both simplifies and generalizes several theorems from vector calculus. It says that the integral of the exterior derivative of a differential form over a manifold equals the integral of the form itself over the manifold's boundary:

∫∂Ω ω = ∫Ω dω

where Ω is an oriented smooth manifold of dimension n with boundary ∂Ω, and ω is a smooth (n − 1)-form with compact support on Ω.

Classical special cases

The single formula contains the great integral theorems of vector calculus as special cases. With Ω a region of the plane it gives Green's theorem; with Ω a volume it gives the divergence theorem of Gauss; with Ω a surface in three-dimensional space it gives the classical Stokes' theorem relating a surface integral of the curl to a line integral around the boundary curve; and with Ω an interval of the real line it reduces to the fundamental theorem of calculus, the value of a function at the endpoints being the "integral" over the boundary.

History

The theorem was developed in stages during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. George Gabriel Stokes stated the surface version in a famous examination question set at Cambridge in 1854, William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) having communicated it to him in a letter of 1850; related results were given by Ostrogradsky, Gauss, and Green. The fully general statement in the language of differential forms is due to Élie Cartan in the 1920s–1940s.

Significance

Beyond unifying calculus, the generalized Stokes theorem underlies much of modern geometry and physics: it connects integration with the boundary operator of homology in de Rham cohomology, and conservation laws and field equations in physics are routinely expressed through it.
