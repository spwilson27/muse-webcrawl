Reformation

The Reformation, also known as the Protestant Reformation, was a major movement within Western Christianity in 16th-century Europe that posed a religious and political challenge to the Catholic Church, in particular to papal authority, arising from what were perceived to be errors, abuses and discrepancies by the Catholic Church. It is conventionally dated to 1517, when Martin Luther published his Ninety-five Theses.

Causes

Precursors such as John Wycliffe and Jan Hus had criticised the Church centuries earlier. By 1500, corruption, the sale of indulgences (notably Johann Tetzel's campaign to fund St Peter's Basilica), Renaissance humanism, the printing press and growing state power all set the stage for reform.

Luther and the German Reformation

Luther, an Augustinian friar and professor at Wittenberg, attacked indulgences in his Ninety-five Theses (1517). His theology of justification by faith alone (sola fide) and Scripture alone (sola scriptura) led to his excommunication (1521) and condemnation at the Diet of Worms, but protection by Frederick the Wise allowed the movement to spread. The Augsburg Confession (1530) systematised Lutheran doctrine; the Peace of Augsburg (1555) let princes choose their territory's religion.

Reformed and Radical movements

In Switzerland, Zwingli and then Calvin in Geneva developed the Reformed tradition, which spread to Scotland under John Knox, France (Huguenots) and the Netherlands. The Radical Reformation produced Anabaptists, who advocated believers' baptism and separation from state churches, and suffered persecution from Catholics and mainstream Protestants alike.

English Reformation

In England, Henry VIII's desire for an annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon led to the break with Rome and the Acts of Supremacy (1534). Under Edward VI the Church of England became more Protestant; Mary I attempted a Catholic restoration; Elizabeth I established a lasting settlement in 1559.

Counter-Reformation and wars of religion

The Catholic Church responded with the Council of Trent (1545–1563), new religious orders such as the Jesuits, and internal reforms. Religious division fuelled a century of conflict, including the French Wars of Religion and the Thirty Years' War, ended by the Peace of Westphalia (1648).

Legacy

The Reformation permanently divided Western Christianity, accelerated vernacular Bible translation and literacy, reshaped education and politics, and is often counted among the foundational events of the modern world.
