Carvel (boat building)

Carvel built or carvel planking is a method of boat building in which hull planks are laid edge to edge and fastened to a robust frame, producing a smooth hull surface. It contrasts with clinker-built construction, in which planks overlap one another.

Technique

In carvel construction, a skeleton of frames is erected first, and planks are bent and fastened to it so that their edges lie flush; the seams are then caulked with cotton or oakum and payed with pitch or seam compound to make them watertight. Because the planks are supported by heavy frames, carvel hulls can be built much larger than clinker hulls, enabling the great ships of the age of sail.

History

The method developed in the Mediterranean in the medieval period, superseding the shell-first mortise-and-tenon planking of antiquity, and spread to northern Europe by the 15th century; the carrack and later the galleon were carvel-built. Modern variants include strip-planking, cold-moulding, and plywood construction, all producing smooth-skinned hulls descended from the carvel principle.
