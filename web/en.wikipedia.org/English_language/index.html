English is a West Germanic language in the Indo-European language family, which originated in early medieval England and is today the most spoken second language in the world and the primary international language of business, science, diplomacy, aviation, and the internet. English is the most widely learned second language and is either the official language or one of the official languages in 59 sovereign states, as well as a working language of the United Nations and the European Union.

History

English developed from the dialects of Angles, Saxons, and Jutes who settled in Britain in the fifth and sixth centuries. Old English, the language of Beowulf, was heavily inflected and drew vocabulary from Old Norse during the Viking Age. The Norman Conquest of 1066 brought thousands of French words and began the transition to Middle English, the language of Chaucer. Between about 1400 and 1700 the Great Vowel Shift reshaped English pronunciation, and printing, the Renaissance, and the King James Bible helped fix Early Modern English — the language of Shakespeare — from which present-day English descends.

Geographic distribution

Because of the British Empire and the later global influence of the United States, English spread to every continent. The largest populations of native speakers are in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand (the "Anglosphere"), while very large second-language populations exist in India, Nigeria, Pakistan, the Philippines, and elsewhere. Linguists group world Englishes into the "inner circle" of native-speaker countries, the "outer circle" where English has official roles, and the "expanding circle" of foreign-language learners.

Grammar and vocabulary

Modern English is largely analytic: it relies on word order and auxiliary verbs rather than case endings, and has lost grammatical gender. Its vocabulary is unusually large and layered — Germanic core words overlaid with immense borrowing from Latin, French, Greek, and hundreds of other languages. Spelling, standardised before the Great Vowel Shift completed, is famously irregular.

Varieties

Major standards include British, American, Canadian, Australian, and Indian English, differing in spelling, vocabulary, and pronunciation. English-based creoles and pidgins are spoken worldwide, and the language continues to evolve through slang, technology, and global media.
