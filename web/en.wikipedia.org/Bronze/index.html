Bronze

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

CompositionCopper with ~12% tin (typically)
PropertiesHard, corrosion-resistant, low friction, sonorous
Historical eraBronze Age (c. 3300–1200 BC in the Near East)

Bronze is an alloy consisting primarily of copper, commonly with about 12% tin and often with other elements such as aluminium, manganese, nickel, phosphorus, or zinc. Harder and more durable than copper or stone, it was the first alloy to be worked on a large scale, giving its name to the Bronze Age.

History

Bronze working appeared in the Near East by the 4th millennium BC and spread across Eurasia. Because tin is geographically scarce, bronze production drove long-distance trade networks linking, for example, Cornish and Central Asian tin sources to bronze workshops in the Mediterranean. Bronze was used for tools, weapons, armour, coinage, mirrors, and monumental statuary before being largely displaced by iron for tools and weapons after about 1200 BC in the Near East and later elsewhere.

Properties and types

Bronze resists corrosion from seawater, has low metal-on-metal friction, and rings clearly when struck — properties that keep it in use for ship propellers and fittings, bearings and bushings, springs, bells, and cymbals. Varieties include phosphor bronze (with phosphorus, for springs and guitar strings), aluminium bronze (golden-coloured and very corrosion-resistant), silicon bronze, and bell bronze (about 23% tin). In sculpture, bronze remains the prestige material, cast by the lost-wax process and finished with chemical patinas ranging from brown to green.

Uses today

Modern bronzes serve in marine hardware, electrical connectors, medals ("bronze" is the third-place medal at the Olympic Games), coinage, and architecture. Most "bronze" coins and some modern statuary are in fact brass or other copper alloys, a distinction of composition rather than appearance.
