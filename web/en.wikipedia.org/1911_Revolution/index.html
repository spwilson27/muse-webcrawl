The 1911 Revolution, also known as the Xinhai Revolution (after the Chinese sexagenary year in which it began), was a revolution in China that overthrew the Qing dynasty, the country's last imperial dynasty, and led to the establishment of the Republic of China. The revolution began with the Wuchang Uprising on 10 October 1911 and ended with the abdication of the six-year-old Xuantong Emperor, Puyi, on 12 February 1912.

Background

By the early twentieth century the Qing dynasty had been weakened by foreign encroachment, defeat in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95), the suppression of the Boxer Rebellion (1900) and the reform movements that followed. Revolutionary societies, most prominently Sun Yat-sen's Tongmenghui (founded in Tokyo in 1905), agitated for a republic based on Sun's Three Principles of the People: nationalism, democracy and people's livelihood. A series of failed uprisings in southern China preceded the successful revolt of 1911. Discontent over the Qing court's railway nationalisation policy, which sparked the Railway Protection Movement in Sichuan, diverted imperial troops westward and left the garrison at Wuchang understrength.

The Wuchang Uprising

On the evening of 9 October 1911 a bomb being built by revolutionaries in the Russian concession at Hankou exploded accidentally, exposing the plot. Facing arrest, soldiers of the New Army in Wuchang mutinied the following night, 10 October, seized the arsenal and captured the city. Li Yuanhong, a reluctant New Army brigade commander, was pressed into service as military governor. Within weeks, province after province declared independence from the Qing.

Course of the revolution

The Qing court recalled the powerful Beiyang Army commander Yuan Shikai, who recaptured Hankou and Hanyang but delayed a final assault on Wuchang while negotiating from a position of strength. Delegates from the independent provinces met at Nanjing and elected Sun Yat-sen, newly returned from exile, as provisional president of the Republic of China; he took office on 1 January 1912. A north–south compromise followed: Yuan agreed to secure the emperor's abdication in exchange for the presidency. On 12 February 1912 the Empress Dowager Longyu issued the abdication edict on Puyi's behalf, ending more than two thousand years of imperial rule in China.

Aftermath and legacy

Sun resigned and Yuan Shikai became president in March 1912, but the republic soon fragmented into warlordism after Yuan's death in 1916. The 1911 Revolution is commemorated as National Day (Double Ten Day) in Taiwan on 10 October, and is honoured in mainland China as a landmark in the country's modern transformation.
