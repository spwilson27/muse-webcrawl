Lech Wałęsa

Born: September 29, 1943, Popowo, Poland
Office: President of Poland, 1990–1995
Known for: Co-founding Solidarity; Nobel Peace Prize 1983

Lech Wałęsa (born September 29, 1943) is a Polish statesman, dissident, and Nobel Peace Prize laureate. A shipyard electrician by trade, he co-founded and led Solidarity (Solidarność), the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, whose strikes and negotiations with the Polish communist government in 1980–81 and again in 1989 helped bring about the peaceful end of communist rule in Poland and inspired change across Eastern Europe. He served as President of Poland from 1990 to 1995.

Early life and activism

Wałęsa was born in Popowo, in German-occupied Poland, and trained as an electrician. From 1967 he worked at the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk, where he became a labour activist after the government violently suppressed worker protests in 1970. Fired in 1976 for his organising activities, he became a leading figure in the underground Free Trade Unions of the Coast.

Solidarity

In August 1980, Wałęsa climbed the fence of the Gdańsk shipyard to join an occupation strike, emerging as its leader. The resulting Gdańsk Agreement legalised independent unions, and Solidarity grew to some 10 million members. When the government imposed martial law in December 1981, Wałęsa was interned for eleven months and the union was driven underground. He received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1983, which his wife Danuta accepted on his behalf, as he feared he would not be allowed to return to Poland.

Presidency and later life

Renewed strikes in 1988 led to the Round Table Talks of 1989 and semi-free elections that swept Solidarity to power. Wałęsa won the 1990 presidential election, becoming Poland's first democratically elected president in the post-war era. His term navigated the shock-therapy economic transition but was marked by political instability and conflicts with parliament; he lost re-election to Aleksander Kwaśniewski in 1995. He later founded the Lech Wałęsa Institute and remained an elder statesman, though allegations concerning his alleged contacts with the communist security service in the 1970s have been debated by historians.
