Fundamental solution

In mathematics, a fundamental solution for a linear partial differential operator L is a function (more precisely, a distribution) F such that L F = δ, where δ is the Dirac delta distribution. The concept generalises the notion of a Green's function.

Motivation

Once a fundamental solution is known, the solution of the inhomogeneous equation L u = f can be written as the convolution u = F ∗ f, provided the convolution is well defined. Intuitively, F represents the response of the system to an idealised point source, and arbitrary sources are built up by superposing point sources.

Examples

For the Laplacian in three dimensions, a fundamental solution is F(x) = −1/(4π|x|); in two dimensions it is (1/2π) log |x|. For the heat operator, the fundamental solution is the heat kernel, a Gaussian concentrated in time. For the one-dimensional operator d/dx, a fundamental solution is the Heaviside step function.

Existence

The Malgrange–Ehrenpreis theorem states that every linear partial differential operator with constant coefficients has a fundamental solution. Fundamental solutions need not be unique: any two differ by a solution of the corresponding homogeneous equation.

History

The idea originates in the work of George Green on electrostatics in 1828 and was recast rigorously in the 20th century within Laurent Schwartz's theory of distributions.
