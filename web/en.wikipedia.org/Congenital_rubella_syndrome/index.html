Congenital rubella syndrome

Congenital rubella syndrome (CRS) is a condition that occurs in a developing baby in the womb whose mother is infected with the rubella virus. The risk and severity are greatest when infection occurs in the first trimester of pregnancy. The classic triad of CRS consists of sensorineural deafness, eye abnormalities (especially congenital cataracts), and congenital heart disease (especially patent ductus arteriosus and pulmonary artery stenosis).

History

The syndrome was first described in 1941 by the Australian ophthalmologist Norman Gregg, who linked an epidemic of congenital cataracts to a rubella outbreak. The devastating rubella pandemic of 1962–1965, which caused tens of thousands of cases of CRS in the United States, spurred the development and licensing of rubella vaccines in 1969.

Prevention and epidemiology

CRS is preventable by vaccinating girls and women of childbearing age with the rubella-containing vaccine, usually given as MMR. Widespread immunisation has eliminated endemic rubella and CRS from the Americas, but the World Health Organization estimates that around 100,000 babies are still born with CRS each year worldwide, mostly in regions with low vaccine coverage. Other possible manifestations include low birth weight, developmental delay, microcephaly, and "blueberry muffin" skin lesions.
