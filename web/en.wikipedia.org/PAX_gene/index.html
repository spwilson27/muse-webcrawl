PAX gene

The PAX genes are a family of genes encoding paired box transcription factors — tissue-specific proteins that play crucial roles in embryonic development, particularly in the formation of the nervous system, sense organs, and other structures. They are characterised by the presence of a paired domain, a conserved DNA-binding region.

Family members

In mammals there are nine PAX genes, PAX1 through PAX9, grouped into subfamilies according to sequence similarity and the presence of additional domains such as a homeodomain or an octapeptide motif. PAX6, the best known, is a master control gene for eye development across the animal kingdom; PAX2, PAX5 and PAX8 are involved in kidney, B-cell and thyroid development respectively.

Function and disease

PAX proteins act as transcription factors that switch other genes on or off at precise times and places during development. Mutations in human PAX genes cause congenital disorders: PAX6 mutations lead to aniridia, PAX3 mutations to Waardenburg syndrome, PAX2 mutations to renal-coloboma syndrome, and PAX9 mutations to tooth agenesis. Altered PAX expression is also implicated in several cancers.
