The secretary-general of the United Nations is the chief administrative officer of the United Nations, heading the UN Secretariat, and serves as the organization's de facto leader and spokesman. Described by the UN Charter's framers as the world's top diplomat, the secretary-general is appointed by the General Assembly on the recommendation of the Security Council — meaning any of the five permanent members can veto a candidate — for a five-year term, conventionally renewable once and rotated among world regions.

Role and powers

Under Chapter XV of the UN Charter, the secretary-general performs functions entrusted by the principal organs, prepares the budget and annual report, and may bring to the Security Council's attention any matter that in their opinion threatens international peace and security (Article 99). Beyond these administrative duties, the office carries moral authority used for mediation and "good offices" diplomacy between states.

History

Norwegian foreign minister Trygve Lie became the first secretary-general in 1946. He was succeeded by Dag Hammarskjöld of Sweden (1953–1961), who expanded peacekeeping and died in a plane crash in the Congo, and U Thant of Burma (1961–1971). Later holders included Kurt Waldheim, Javier Pérez de Cuéllar, Boutros Boutros-Ghali — the only one denied a second term, by a United States veto — Kofi Annan, and Ban Ki-moon. António Guterres of Portugal took office on 1 January 2017 and was reappointed for a second term beginning in 2022. Despite repeated campaigns, no woman has yet held the post.

Selection

Reforms adopted in 2015–2016 made the process more transparent, with public candidate lists and General Assembly hearings, though the decisive straw polls remain in the Security Council.
