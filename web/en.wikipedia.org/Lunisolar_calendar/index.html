Lunisolar calendar

Lunar months reconciled with the solar year by intercalation

Examples: Hebrew, Chinese, Hindu, Tibetan, Babylonian calendars

A lunisolar calendar is a calendar in many cultures whose date indicates both the Moon phase and the time of the solar year. If the solar year is defined as a tropical year, then a lunisolar calendar will give an indication of the season; if it is taken as a sidereal year, the calendar will predict the constellation near which the full moon may occur.

Intercalation

A lunar year of twelve months totals about 354 days, roughly 11 days shorter than the solar year. To keep months aligned with the seasons, lunisolar calendars periodically insert an extra month, a process called intercalation or embolism. The Babylonian calendar pioneered a regular cycle, and the Metonic cycle — 19 years containing 235 lunar months, with leap months in 7 of the years — underlies the Hebrew calendar and the traditional Chinese calendar.

Examples

The Chinese calendar adds a leap month roughly every three years, determined by the position of the Sun along the ecliptic. The Hebrew calendar similarly inserts an extra month of Adar in 7 years of each 19-year cycle. Hindu calendars, in both their solar-regional and lunisolar forms, add an adhika maas (extra month) about every 32–33 months. The ancient Greek, Celtic Coligny, Babylonian, Tibetan, and traditional Korean and Japanese calendars are or were lunisolar.

Contrast with other calendars

Purely lunar calendars, such as the Islamic calendar, allow months to drift through all seasons over a 33-year cycle. Purely solar calendars, such as the Gregorian and Julian calendars, ignore the Moon entirely. Lunisolar calendars occupy the middle ground, tracking both cycles at the price of irregular year lengths of 353–385 days.
