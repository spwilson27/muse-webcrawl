Bayon

The Bayon (Khmer: ប្រាសាទបាយ័ន) is a richly decorated Khmer temple at the centre of Angkor Thom in Cambodia. Built in the late 12th or early 13th century as the state temple of the Mahayana Buddhist King Jayavarman VII, it stands at the exact centre of the king's capital. The Bayon is famous for its 54 towers bearing serene, smiling stone faces — traditionally counted at 216 in total — widely believed to represent the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, and possibly modelled on the king himself. Its outer gallery bas-reliefs depict vivid scenes of everyday life, markets, naval battles, and the wars of Jayavarman VII's reign.

History

Jayavarman VII (reigned 1181–c. 1218) rebuilt the Khmer capital after the Cham sack of Angkor in 1177, enclosing it within the walls of Angkor Thom and placing the Bayon at its centre, aligned with the cardinal directions. Subsequent kings altered the temple: Jayavarman VIII, a Shivaite, converted or destroyed many Buddhist images, and under the later Theravada tradition a great Buddha image from the central well was removed — it was recovered in the 20th century and is now displayed in a nearby pavilion.

Architecture

The Bayon is a "temple mountain" in three levels, though its cluttered, additions-over-time plan gives it a more intimate, labyrinthine feel than Angkor Wat. Its most distinctive features are the face towers of the upper terrace and two concentric galleries of narrative bas-reliefs. The outer gallery shows historical and daily-life scenes — cockfights, fish markets, a naval battle on the Tonlé Sap — while the inner gallery carries more mythological themes. Conservation has been led since the 1990s notably by Japanese (JSA) and APSARA teams.
