Euler–Mascheroni constant

The Euler–Mascheroni constant (also called Euler's constant), denoted by the lowercase Greek letter gamma (γ), is a mathematical constant defined as the limiting difference between the harmonic series and the natural logarithm: γ ≈ 0.5772156649. It was introduced by Leonhard Euler in 1734–35 and studied by Lorenzo Mascheroni; whether it is irrational remains an open problem.

Definition and properties

Formally, γ = limn→∞ (Hn − ln n), where Hn is the nth harmonic number. It also equals an integral of −ln(ln(1/x)) and appears in many formulas. Unlike π or e, γ has no known closed form and its arithmetic nature is unknown — it is not even known whether it is irrational, though continued-fraction results show any rational value would need an enormous denominator.

Appearances

The constant occurs in number theory (Mertens theorems, the distribution of primes), analysis (the gamma and digamma functions, the exponential integral), and probability (the Gumbel distribution, coupon-collector problems). Euler first computed it to several digits; modern computations reach hundreds of billions of digits.

Generalisations

The Stieltjes constants generalise γ in the Laurent expansion of the Riemann zeta function. See also Harmonic series, Leonhard Euler and Gamma function.
