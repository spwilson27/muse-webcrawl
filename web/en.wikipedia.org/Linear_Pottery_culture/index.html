The Linear Pottery culture (German: Linearbandkeramik, abbreviated LBK) is a major archaeological horizon of the European Neolithic, flourishing from about 5500 to 4500 BC. It is named for the characteristic pottery vessels decorated with incised linear bands filled with punctate or notched ornament. The LBK represents the first widespread farming culture of temperate Central Europe and one of the principal routes by which agriculture spread from the Near East into the European interior.

Origins and spread

The culture emerged around 5500 BC in the middle Danube region of present-day Hungary, developing out of the earlier Starčevo–Körös complex of the northern Balkans. Within a few centuries LBK settlements had spread along the loess soils of the Danube, Rhine, Elbe, Oder, and Vistula valleys, reaching the Paris Basin in the west and Ukraine in the east — one of the most rapid expansions of any Neolithic culture. Ancient DNA studies indicate that LBK populations were largely descended from early farmers originating in Anatolia, admixing to varying degrees with local Mesolithic hunter-gatherers.

Settlements and economy

LBK villages consisted of clusters of large timber longhouses, often 20–40 metres long, divided into sections that may have housed extended families, stored produce, and stalled livestock. Settlements were sited on fertile loess near water. Farmers cultivated emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, peas, lentils, and flax, and kept cattle, sheep, goats, and pigs. Spondylus shell ornaments, traded over hundreds of kilometres from the Aegean and Adriatic, attest to long-distance exchange networks. Fine stone adzes, sometimes of jadeitite from the western Alps, circulated as prestige goods.

Burial and ritual

Burials were typically crouched inhumations in cemeteries beside settlements, accompanied by pottery, stone tools, and shell ornaments. Exceptional deposits, such as the mass grave at Talheim in Germany, preserve evidence of violent conflict late in the culture's history, as do the ditched enclosures at Herxheim, where the processed remains of hundreds of individuals were deposited.

Legacy

After about 4500 BC the LBK fragmented into regional successor cultures including the Rössen, Lengyel, and Stroke-ornamented ware groups. Its expansion permanently transformed Europe, establishing the farming way of life across the continent's temperate heartland and contributing substantially to the ancestry of later European populations.
