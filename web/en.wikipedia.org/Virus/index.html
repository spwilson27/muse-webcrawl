Virus

A virus is a submicroscopic infectious agent that replicates only inside the living cells of an organism. Viruses infect all life forms, from animals and plants to microorganisms, including bacteria and archaea. Since Dmitri Ivanovsky's 1892 article describing a non-bacterial pathogen infecting tobacco plants, and Martinus Beijerinck's coining of the term in 1898, more than 11,000 species of virus have been described in detail, out of millions of types in the environment.

Structure

A complete virus particle, a virion, consists of genetic material (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protective protein coat called a capsid, and sometimes an outer lipid envelope derived from the host cell membrane. Viruses display helical or icosahedral symmetry, as well as more complex structures, and are far smaller than bacteria — typically 20–300 nanometres. They have no metabolism of their own and lack ribosomes, so they must hijack host-cell machinery to produce new virions.

Life cycle

Viral replication typically proceeds through attachment to a host cell, penetration, uncoating, replication of the genome and synthesis of viral proteins, assembly of new virions, and release — often destroying the host cell (the lytic cycle). Some viruses can persist in a dormant lysogenic state, integrating their genome into the host's DNA. Viruses spread by many routes: through the air, bodily fluids, insect vectors, and contaminated surfaces.

Disease and classification

In humans, viruses cause diseases including the common cold, influenza, rabies, measles, HIV/AIDS, and COVID-19. Vaccines provide effective prevention against many viral diseases, while antiviral drugs treat some infections; antibiotics are ineffective against viruses. The Baltimore classification groups viruses into seven classes according to their genome type and mode of messenger RNA synthesis. Whether viruses count as "alive" remains a matter of definition, since they lack cells and independent metabolism.
