Detroit bankruptcy

Detroit bankruptcy
Filed: 18 July 2013
Court: US Bankruptcy Court, Eastern District of Michigan
Debts: approx. $18–20 billion
Judge: Steven W. Rhodes
Exited bankruptcy: 10 December 2014

The city of Detroit, Michigan, filed for Chapter 9 bankruptcy on 18 July 2013. It was the largest municipal bankruptcy filing in United States history by both debt — estimated at $18–20 billion — and population, Detroit having about 685,000 residents at the time.

Background

Detroit's finances had deteriorated over decades of population loss — from a peak of 1.85 million in 1950 — deindustrialisation, a shrinking tax base and rising pension and retiree health-care liabilities, compounded by borrowing to cover operating deficits. In March 2013 Michigan governor Rick Snyder appointed Kevyn Orr as emergency manager under state law, giving him sweeping powers over city finances. After failed negotiations with creditors, Orr filed the bankruptcy petition.

Proceedings

Judge Steven W. Rhodes ruled in December 2013 that Detroit was eligible for Chapter 9 protection and that municipal pensions — despite a state constitutional provision protecting them — were contractual obligations that could be impaired in bankruptcy, a landmark holding. Intense mediation followed, producing the "grand bargain": foundations, the State of Michigan and the Detroit Institute of Arts raised roughly $816 million to shore up pensions and protect the museum's city-owned collection from sale.

Outcome

The court confirmed the city's plan of adjustment in November 2014, and Detroit exited bankruptcy on 10 December 2014. The plan cut about $7 billion in obligations; general retirees took a 4.5 per cent pension cut and lost cost-of-living adjustments, while police and fire retirees fared somewhat better. The city committed funds to blight removal and improved services, and its finances came under the oversight of a state review commission.
