Rear-engine, rear-wheel-drive layout

In the RR layout the engine sits behind the rear axle line and drives the rear wheels.

The rear-engine, rear-wheel-drive layout (RR) is an automobile layout in which the engine is located at the rear of the vehicle, behind the rear axle, and drives the rear wheels. It is one of the oldest automobile configurations and differs from the rear mid-engine layout, in which the engine sits ahead of the rear axle but behind the passenger compartment.

Characteristics

Placing the engine over or behind the driven wheels gives excellent traction when accelerating, since weight transfer under power further loads the rear tyres. The absence of a driveshaft tunnel and of drive hardware at the front permits a flat floor and a compact drivetrain. Against this, the concentration of mass at the rear produces a handling balance prone to oversteer: if the rear tyres lose grip in a corner, the heavy tail tends to swing outward, a characteristic that demands skill from the driver and careful suspension tuning from the manufacturer.

Cooling is another challenge, since the engine sits away from the airflow at the front of the car. Many classic RR cars, including the Volkswagen Beetle and early Porsche 911s, used air-cooled engines to avoid long coolant runs; later designs route coolant pipes through the body.

History

The layout was pioneered in the 1930s by cars such as the Tatra designs of Hans Ledwinka and the Mercedes-Benz 130H, and was popularised after the Second World War by the Volkswagen Beetle, the Renault 4CV and Fiat 500, and the Chevrolet Corvair. The Corvair's handling was criticised in Ralph Nader's Unsafe at Any Speed (1965), which damaged the reputation of the layout in the United States.

The most famous surviving RR car is the Porsche 911, which has retained a rear-mounted engine since 1964, its engineers progressively taming the layout's traits with wider rear tyres, revised suspension geometry and electronic stability control. The layout also remains standard for most city buses and coaches, where it frees the front for a low entrance and places the engine away from passengers.

Examples

Notable RR vehicles include the Volkswagen Beetle and Type 2, the Porsche 911, the Škoda 1000 MB, the Hillman Imp, the NSU Prinz and the Smart Fortwo (whose engine sits just ahead of the rear axle in some classifications). Modern electric vehicles have blurred these categories, since motors can be packaged at either axle without a mechanical connection.
