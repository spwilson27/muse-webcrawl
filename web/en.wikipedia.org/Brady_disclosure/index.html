Brady disclosure

Brady disclosure is the obligation, under United States constitutional law, of prosecutors to disclose exculpatory evidence to the criminal defence. It derives from the Supreme Court's decision in Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), which held that the prosecution's suppression of evidence favourable to an accused violates due process where the evidence is material to guilt or punishment.

Scope of the rule

Brady material includes evidence that exculpates the defendant and evidence that impeaches the credibility of prosecution witnesses. Giglio v. United States (1972) extended the rule to impeachment material, and United States v. Bagley (1985) set the standard of materiality: evidence is material if there is a reasonable probability that, had it been disclosed, the result of the proceeding would have been different. Under Kyles v. Whitley (1995), prosecutors are responsible for favourable evidence known to police and others acting on the government's behalf.

Brady violations

Failure to disclose — a Brady violation — can result in convictions being vacated, but violations are difficult to prove and sanctions against prosecutors are rare. Prosecutors' offices maintain "Brady lists" (also called Giglio lists) of officers whose credibility issues must be disclosed; wrongful convictions attributed partly to withheld evidence include numerous cases documented by the Innocence Project, and the issue has driven reforms such as open-file discovery statutes in several states.
