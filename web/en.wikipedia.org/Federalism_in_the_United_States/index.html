Federalism in the United States


The fifty states of the Union

In the United States, federalism is the constitutional division of power between the federal government and the governments of the individual states. Unlike unitary states, the U.S. Constitution grants the national government only enumerated powers and reserves the remainder to the states or the people (Tenth Amendment), while the Supremacy Clause makes valid federal law "the supreme Law of the Land." The system evolved substantially from the founders' design, through the Civil War and Reconstruction, the New Deal, and into modern cooperative federalism.

Constitutional foundations

Article I, Section 8 enumerates Congress's powers, including taxation, regulation of interstate and foreign commerce, coining money, and declaring war; the Necessary and Proper Clause extends them to laws needed to execute those powers. The states retain general police powers over health, safety, and welfare, and are restricted by provisions such as the prohibition on coining money or entering treaties. The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, applies key provisions of the Bill of Rights against the states through the doctrine of incorporation.

Historical development

Scholars describe successive eras: dual federalism (c. 1789–1901), in which national and state spheres were treated as largely separate; cooperative federalism (c. 1901–1960), marked by joint programs and federal grants-in-aid; creative federalism of the 1960s Great Society; and the new federalism since the 1970s, which has sought to return power to the states through block grants and devolution. Key judicial landmarks include McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), Gibbons v. Ogden (1824), the New Deal-era commerce decisions, and United States v. Lopez (1995).

Fiscal federalism

Federal grants account for a large share of state budgets, funding programs such as Medicaid, highway construction, and education. Categorical grants carry detailed conditions, whereas block grants give states broader discretion. Conditions attached to federal funds — upheld within limits in South Dakota v. Dole (1987) — are a principal mechanism by which national policy influences state governments.
