Delay (audio effect)

Delay is an audio signal processing technique that records an input signal to a storage medium and then plays it back after a period of time. When the delayed playback is mixed with the live audio, it creates an echo-like effect whereby the original audio is heard followed by the delayed audio. The delayed signal may be played back multiple times, or fed back into the recording, to create the sound of a repeating, decaying echo.

History

Early delay effects were achieved with tape loops, such as the Echoplex and the Roland Space Echo, in which a loop of magnetic tape passed over record and playback heads; the tape speed and head spacing determined the delay time. In the 1970s, analog bucket-brigade device (BBD) chips made compact delay pedals possible, and digital delay units appeared in the early 1980s, offering longer and cleaner repeats.

Types

Slapback echo is a single, very short delay (roughly 60 to 120 milliseconds) associated with 1950s rock and roll and rockabilly recordings. Doubling uses a slightly longer delay to thicken a sound. Longer delays synchronized to a song's tempo are common in rock and electronic music; the dotted-eighth-note delay pattern is famously associated with the guitar style of U2's The Edge. Ping-pong delay alternates repeats between left and right stereo channels.

Controls

Typical delay units offer controls for delay time, feedback (the number of repeats), and mix level. Many digital units also include modulation of the delay time, filtering of the repeats to emulate tape degradation, and tap-tempo input to set the delay time in real time.
