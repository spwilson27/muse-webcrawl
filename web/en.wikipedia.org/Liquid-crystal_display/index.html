Liquid-crystal display

A liquid-crystal display (LCD) is a flat-panel display that uses the light-modulating properties of liquid crystals combined with polarizers. LCDs are used in televisions, monitors, phones, instruments and many other products.

How it works

Liquid crystals do not emit light directly. A backlight shines through layers of polarizing filters and a liquid-crystal layer whose molecules twist in response to electric fields, controlling how much light passes through each pixel. Colour filters produce red, green and blue subpixels.

Types

Common panel types include twisted nematic (TN), in-plane switching (IPS) and vertical alignment (VA), trading off viewing angle, contrast and response time. Active-matrix LCDs use a thin-film transistor (TFT) at each pixel.

History

Liquid crystals were described in 1888; practical displays emerged in the 1960s–70s, first in calculators and watches, before colour TFT panels enabled laptops and flat televisions in the 1990s and 2000s.

Advantages and drawbacks

LCDs are thin, light and energy-efficient, but have limited contrast compared with emissive displays such as OLED because the backlight cannot be fully blocked.
