Japanese counter word

Japanese counter words, also called counters or josūshi (助数詞), are a category of words used in the Japanese language together with numerals to count things, actions, and events. Like the numeral classifiers of Chinese, Korean, and many other languages of Asia, Japanese grammar generally requires a counter appropriate to the kind of object being counted: one does not simply say "three books" but "three [flat-object-counter] of book."

Counters are among the most distinctive features of Japanese counting.

Usage

A counter typically follows the numeral and precedes or follows the noun depending on construction: san-mai no kami ("three sheets of paper"), or the noun may precede with a particle: kami san-mai. The choice of counter depends on the shape, animacy, size, or category of the object. Small animals take -hiki (匹), long thin objects such as bottles and pencils take -hon (本), flat thin objects take -mai (枚), people take -nin (人), and machines and vehicles take -dai (台).

Some counters combine with native Japanese numerals (the wago series): one day is futsuka, one item hitotsu, with the generic counter -tsu (つ) used for objects lacking a specific counter, up to nine plus "how many." Most other counters combine with the Sino-Japanese numerals ichi, ni, san…, often with sound changes (rendaku and gemination): thus ippon, nihon, sanbon for one, two, three long objects.

Sound changes

When numeral and counter combine, predictable phonological alternations occur. Numbers ending in certain sounds trigger gemination (a doubled consonant) in the counter's initial: ichi + hon → ippon, roku + hon → roppon. Counters beginning with h- shift to p- after gemination and to b- after san and nan ("what/how many"): sanbon. The counter for people is irregular for one and two: hitori and futari use native Japanese numerals, while three and above use Sino-Japanese sannin, yonnin (note yon-, not shi-).

Origins and comparison

Many Japanese counters derive from Chinese classifiers borrowed along with Sino-Japanese vocabulary, while others are native formations. Similar systems exist in Korean (sugōsa), the Ryukyuan languages, Ainu, and historically in many languages of mainland and insular Southeast Asia. Estimates of the number of counters in Japanese range from around 70 in everyday use to several hundred including rare and specialised ones. Mastery of the common counters is a standard part of Japanese language education.
