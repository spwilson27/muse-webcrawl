Urea, also known as carbamide, is an organic compound with chemical formula CO(NH2)2. This amide has two amino groups (–NH2) joined by a carbonyl group (–C(=O)–). It is thus the simplest amide of carbonic acid.

Urea serves an important role in the cellular metabolism of nitrogen-containing compounds by animals and is the main nitrogen-containing substance in the urine of mammals. Urea is Neo-Latin, from French urée, from Ancient Greek οὖρον (oûron) "urine". It is a colorless, odorless solid, highly soluble in water, and practically non-toxic. Dissolved in water, it is neither acidic nor alkaline.

History

Urea was first isolated from urine in 1773 by the French chemist Hilaire Rouelle. In 1828 Friedrich Wöhler synthesized urea from inorganic starting materials — ammonium cyanate — in what is regarded as a landmark in the history of chemistry: the first synthesis of an organic compound from inorganic precursors, undermining the doctrine of vitalism.

Physiology

In most land animals, ammonia produced by amino-acid catabolism is converted in the liver to urea through the urea cycle (ornithine cycle), the first metabolic cycle ever described (Hans Krebs and Kurt Henseleit, 1932). Urea is then excreted in urine; an average adult human excretes about 30 grams per day. Blood urea nitrogen is a standard clinical measure of kidney function.

Production and uses

Industrial urea is produced from ammonia and carbon dioxide (the Bosch–Meiser process, patented 1922), via ammonium carbamate. World production exceeds 180 million tonnes per year. More than 90% is used as a nitrogen fertiliser, the highest nitrogen content (46%) of any solid nitrogen fertiliser in common use. Urea is also a feedstock for urea-formaldehyde resins, melamine, adhesives and plastics; an ingredient in skin creams (as a keratolytic and humectant); a protein supplement in ruminant feed; and, as diesel exhaust fluid (AdBlue/DEF), a reductant in selective catalytic reduction of NOx emissions.
