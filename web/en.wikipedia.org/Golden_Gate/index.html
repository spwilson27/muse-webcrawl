The Golden Gate is a strait on the west coast of North America that connects San Francisco Bay to the Pacific Ocean. It is spanned by the Golden Gate Bridge, one of the most internationally recognised symbols of San Francisco and California.

Geography

The strait is approximately three miles (4.8 km) long by one mile (1.6 km) wide, with powerful tidal currents and frequent summer fog. It was carved during the Ice Ages as the Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers cut through the Coast Ranges on their way to the sea.

History

Although Spanish explorers sailed the coast from the 1540s, the entrance to the bay was long missed because of fog. The first recorded European passage through the strait was by Juan Manuel de Ayala aboard the San Carlos in 1775. John C. Frémont named the strait Chrysopylae — Golden Gate — in 1846, likening it to the Golden Horn of Byzantium. Two years later the California gold rush transformed the strait into one of the world's busiest maritime gateways.

The bridge

Construction of the Golden Gate Bridge began in 1933 under chief engineer Joseph B. Strauss; the suspension bridge opened to pedestrians in May 1937. Its towers rise 746 feet (227 m) and its main span of 4,200 feet (1,280 m) was the longest in the world until 1964.
