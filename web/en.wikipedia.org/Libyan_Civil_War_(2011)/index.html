Libyan Civil War (2011)

The Libyan Civil War of 2011, also known as the First Libyan Civil War, was an armed conflict in the North African country of Libya between forces loyal to Colonel Muammar Gaddafi, who had ruled since 1969, and rebel groups seeking to oust his government. It formed part of the wider Arab Spring wave of protests.

Course of the war

Protests erupted in Benghazi in February 2011 after the arrest of a human rights lawyer, and escalated into a rebellion centred on eastern Libya. Rebels formed the National Transitional Council (NTC) as an interim government. After Gaddafi's forces threatened to retake Benghazi, the United Nations Security Council adopted Resolution 1973 on 17 March 2011, authorising a no-fly zone and "all necessary measures" to protect civilians. NATO-led air and missile strikes followed under Operation Unified Protector.

Rebel forces, supported by NATO airpower, advanced westward through the summer. Tripoli fell in late August 2011, and Gaddafi fled. He was captured and killed in his hometown of Sirte on 20 October 2011. The NTC declared Libya liberated on 23 October, formally ending the war.

Aftermath

An estimated 15,000–30,000 people were killed during the conflict. Libya held elections for a General National Congress in July 2012, but rival militias and weak state institutions undermined stability, and a second civil war erupted in 2014. The intervention remains debated: supporters credit it with preventing a massacre in Benghazi, while critics cite the chaos that followed and question its execution.
