Relative risk

Relative risk compares incidence between exposed and unexposed groups.

The relative risk (RR) or risk ratio is the ratio of the probability of an outcome in an exposed group to the probability of the outcome in an unexposed group. It is a central measure of association in epidemiology and in randomised controlled trials.

Calculation

From a standard 2×2 table with a exposed cases, b exposed non-cases, c unexposed cases and d unexposed non-cases, the relative risk is RR = [a/(a+b)] / [c/(c+d)]. An RR of 1 means exposure is not associated with the outcome; RR > 1 indicates increased risk among the exposed; RR < 1 indicates a protective effect. Statistical inference is usually performed on the logarithm of RR, which is approximately normally distributed; 95% confidence intervals are standard in reporting.

Interpretation

Relative risk must be read alongside absolute risk: a doubling of risk (RR = 2) matters little if the outcome is extremely rare, but greatly if it is common. Media reporting that cites only relative risks can exaggerate the practical importance of findings. Relative risk differs from the odds ratio, which it approximates when the outcome is rare, and from the hazard ratio used in survival analysis. In vaccine studies, vaccine efficacy is often expressed as 1 − RR.
