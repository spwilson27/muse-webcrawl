Occupation of Poland (1939–1945)

The occupation of Poland by Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union during World War II (1939–1945) began with the German-Soviet invasion of Poland in September 1939, and it was formally concluded in May 1945 by the defeat of Germany by the Allies. Poland suffered the highest relative population losses of any country in the war.

Division of the country

Under the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, Poland was divided between the two occupiers. Germany annexed western territories directly into the Reich and created the General Government in the central regions, while the Soviet Union annexed the eastern territories, deporting hundreds of thousands of Polish citizens to Siberia and Kazakhstan. After Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, all of Poland fell under German control.

Terror and genocide

The occupation was exceptionally brutal. Nazi policy aimed at the destruction of Polish culture and leadership and the extermination of Poland's roughly three million Jewish citizens, nearly all of whom were murdered in the Holocaust, most of the German extermination camps being built on occupied Polish soil. In total, about six million Polish citizens, roughly a fifth of the population, died, around half of them Polish Jews.

Resistance

The Polish government-in-exile maintained an underground state with an army, the Home Army, courts and education. Major uprisings included the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of 1943 and the Warsaw Uprising of 1944, after which the Germans systematically destroyed much of the capital.

Aftermath

In 1945 Poland's borders were shifted westward: the eastern territories remained in the Soviet Union while Poland received formerly German lands in the west, accompanied by mass expulsions and population transfers.
