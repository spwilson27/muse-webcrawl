The Bathysphere (from Greek bathys, "deep", and sphaira, "sphere") was a unique spherical deep-sea submersible that was lowered into the ocean on a cable. It was designed by the American engineer Otis Barton and used by the naturalist William Beebe for a series of pioneering dives off Bermuda in the early 1930s, during which the two men became the first to descend deep enough to observe deep-sea animals in their native environment.

Design

The Bathysphere was a hollow cast-steel sphere just under 1.5 metres in diameter, with walls about 3.8 centimetres thick and three small windows of fused quartz. It was unpowered and had no propulsion: it hung from a steel cable from the mother ship Ready, with a second cable carrying telephone lines and electrical power for a searchlight. Inside, oxygen was supplied from cylinders and carbon dioxide was scrubbed by trays of soda lime and calcium chloride. The two occupants communicated with the surface by telephone, and Beebe dictated his observations to staff writer Gloria Hollister, whose accounts made the dives famous.

Dives

The first dives took place in 1930 near Nonsuch Island, Bermuda. On 15 August 1934 Beebe and Barton reached a depth of 923 metres (3,028 feet), a record that stood for fifteen years. Beebe described bioluminescent fishes, siphonophores and other creatures never before seen alive, and his popular book Half Mile Down (1934) brought deep-sea exploration to a wide audience. The dives were supported by the New York Zoological Society and were broadcast live on radio.

Legacy

Barton later descended to 1,372 metres in his improved Benthoscope in 1949. The Bathysphere itself is preserved at the New York Aquarium at Coney Island. Its dives paved the way for self-propelled deep submersibles such as the bathyscaphe Trieste and Alvin.
