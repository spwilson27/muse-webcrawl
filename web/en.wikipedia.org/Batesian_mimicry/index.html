Batesian mimicry


A hoverfly (mimic) resembling a wasp (model)

Batesian mimicry is a form of mimicry where a harmless species has evolved to imitate the warning signals of a harmful species directed at a predator of them both. It is named after the English naturalist Henry Walter Bates, who worked on butterflies in the rainforests of Brazil and published his account in 1862, providing some of the first empirical support for natural selection.

In a Batesian system there are three roles: the model, an unprofitable or defended species (such as a wasp or a poisonous butterfly); the mimic, a harmless species (such as a hoverfly) that resembles the model; and the dupe, typically a predator that learns to avoid the model's warning coloration and consequently also avoids the mimic. The mimic gains protection without paying the cost of producing toxins or a sting.

Frequency dependence

Batesian mimicry is negatively frequency-dependent: it works best when mimics are rare relative to models, so that predators mostly encounter genuinely defended prey and maintain their avoidance. If mimics become too common, predators learn that the signal is often a bluff, and protection for both mimic and model breaks down. Classic examples include the viceroy butterfly mimicking the monarch, hoverflies mimicking bees and wasps, and harmless milk snakes resembling venomous coral snakes.

Contrast with Müllerian mimicry

Batesian mimicry contrasts with Müllerian mimicry, in which two or more genuinely defended species converge on a shared warning signal, mutually reinforcing predator learning.
