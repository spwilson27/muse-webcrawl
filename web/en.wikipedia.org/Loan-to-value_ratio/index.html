Loan-to-value ratio

The loan-to-value ratio (LTV) is a financial term used by lenders to express the ratio of a loan to the value of an asset purchased. It is commonly used in mortgage lending to describe the size of a loan relative to the value of the property securing it.

Definition

LTV is calculated as the loan amount divided by the appraised value (or purchase price, if lower) of the property, expressed as a percentage. For example, a borrower taking a $160,000 loan to buy a $200,000 home has an LTV of 80%.

Risk assessment

Lenders regard higher LTV ratios as riskier, because less equity cushions the lender against falls in property values and against the costs of foreclosure. Mortgages with LTV above 80% in the United States typically require private mortgage insurance. Combined loan-to-value (CLTV) adds secondary financing such as a home equity line to the calculation.

Regulation

Many jurisdictions cap LTV for certain loan products or require additional capital against high-LTV lending. During housing booms, loans at 100% LTV or higher — where the loan exceeds the property value, producing negative equity if prices fall — have been associated with higher default rates.
