Lake Agassiz

Maximum extent of Lake Agassiz around 9,000 years ago

Lake Agassiz was a vast proglacial lake that existed in central North America during the last ice age, ponded against the southern margin of the Laurentide Ice Sheet roughly between 13,000 and 8,400 years ago. At its maximum it covered more than 440,000 km² — an area larger than all the modern Great Lakes combined — across what is now Manitoba, Ontario, Saskatchewan, Minnesota and North Dakota. It was named by geologist Warren Upham for the Swiss-American naturalist Louis Agassiz.

History and drainage

As the ice sheet retreated northward, meltwater collected in the basin now occupied by the Red River valley and the Canadian plains lakes. Over its life the lake drained through a succession of outlets: southward via the River Warren through the Minnesota River to the Mississippi; eastward through outlets toward Lake Superior and the St Lawrence; northwestward at times toward the Mackenzie system; and finally northward to Hudson Bay. Strandlines and beach ridges of its various phases — named Herman, Norcross, Tintah and Campbell among others — are still visible across the remarkably flat Red River Valley.

The final drainage, about 8,470 years ago, when the ice dam separating Agassiz from the Ojibway lake collapsed, released an enormous pulse of fresh water into Hudson Bay and the Labrador Sea. This "8.2-kiloyear event" is believed to have disrupted North Atlantic deep-water formation and caused an abrupt, centuries-long cooling across the Northern Hemisphere, recorded in Greenland ice cores.

Remnants

Modern remnants of the lake include Lake Winnipeg, Lake Manitoba, Lake Winnipegosis, Cedar Lake and Minnesota's Lake of the Woods and Upper and Lower Red Lakes. Its deep, fine lakebed sediments form the fertile farmland of the Red River Valley.
