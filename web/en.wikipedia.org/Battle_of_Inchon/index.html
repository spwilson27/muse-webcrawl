The Battle of Inchon was an amphibious invasion and battle of the Korean War that resulted in a decisive victory for the United Nations Command and a strategic reversal of fortune for the North Korean People's Army. Code-named Operation Chromite, the landings took place on 15 September 1950 at the port of Inchon (present-day Incheon), far behind North Korean lines.

Background

By September 1950, UN and South Korean forces had been driven back to the Pusan Perimeter in the south-east of the peninsula. General Douglas MacArthur, the UN commander, championed a risky landing at Inchon, the port serving Seoul, despite its extreme tides, narrow channels, and sea walls. The plan involved more than 75,000 troops and 261 naval vessels.

The landing

After preparatory bombardment and the seizure of Wolmi-do island in the harbour, the US 1st Marine Division landed on Red and Blue beaches on either side of the morning and evening high tides. Resistance in the port was light, and Inchon was secured within days at a cost of a few hundred UN casualties. The X Corps then advanced inland, and Seoul was recaptured by the end of September after hard street fighting.

Aftermath

The landing cut North Korean supply lines and, combined with the breakout from the Pusan Perimeter, caused the collapse of the North Korean army in the south. Most of South Korea was liberated within weeks — though the UN advance toward the Yalu River soon brought Chinese intervention and a new phase of the war.
