Cognitive map

A cognitive map is a mental representation that serves an individual to acquire, code, store, recall and decode information about the relative locations and attributes of phenomena in their everyday spatial environment. The term was introduced by psychologist Edward Tolman in 1948, based on experiments showing that rats learned the layout of mazes rather than merely chains of stimulus–response habits.

Research

Human cognitive maps are studied through sketch maps, distance and direction estimates, and wayfinding tasks; they are typically distorted — for example, distances are compressed or rotated toward regular axes. Kevin Lynch's The Image of the City (1960) analysed urban cognitive maps in terms of paths, edges, districts, nodes and landmarks. Neuroscientific support came with the discovery of place cells in the hippocampus by John O'Keefe and grid cells in the entorhinal cortex by May-Britt and Edvard Moser, work recognised by the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Broader uses

The term is also used metaphorically for structured representations of beliefs and concepts — for example, in the "cognitive mapping" of political decision-makers' causal beliefs, and in concept mapping in education.
