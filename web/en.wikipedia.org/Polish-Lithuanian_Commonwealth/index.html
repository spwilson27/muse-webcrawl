The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth — formally the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania — was a bi-confederational state founded by the Union of Lublin in 1569. At its height in the early 17th century it covered almost 1 million square kilometres and held a multi-ethnic population of about 11 million, making it one of the largest and most populous states in Europe.

Political system

The Commonwealth was characterised by an unusual political system sometimes called the Noble's Democracy or Golden Liberty. The king was elected for life by the nobility (szlachta), who formed the Sejm parliament and enjoyed wide legal rights and privileges. The notorious liberum veto, allowing a single deputy to dissolve a Sejm session, contributed to increasing paralysis in the 18th century.

History

The Commonwealth flourished in the late 16th and early 17th centuries, withstanding wars against Sweden, the Ottoman Empire and the Tsardom of Russia. Its fortunes declined after the mid-17th-century Deluge — the Swedish invasion — and a series of Cossack uprisings beginning with the Khmelnytsky Uprising of 1648. In the 18th century the state fell under the growing influence of Russia and was progressively dismantled in the three Partitions of Poland (1772, 1793, 1795), ceasing to exist after the Third Partition.

Culture and society

The Commonwealth was notable for its relative religious tolerance, guaranteed by the Warsaw Confederation of 1573, and for a large Jewish population that enjoyed communal autonomy. Its lands today form parts of Poland, Lithuania, Latvia, Belarus, Ukraine, Moldova, Romania, Slovakia and Russia.
