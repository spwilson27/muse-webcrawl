Tetration

In mathematics, tetration (or hyper-4) is an operation based on iterated, or repeated, exponentiation. There is no standard notation for tetration, though Knuth's up-arrow notation ↑↑ and the left-exponent notation are common. Under the definition as repeated exponentiation, na means a raised to itself n times, forming a tower of exponents evaluated from the top down.

Introduction

Just as addition is repeated counting, multiplication is repeated addition, and exponentiation is repeated multiplication, tetration is repeated exponentiation. The four primary arithmetic operations thus form a hierarchy of hyperoperations: succession, addition, multiplication, exponentiation, and then tetration. Because exponentiation is not associative, a power tower must be read from the top down (right-associative): a^(a^(a^a)) rather than ((a^a)^a)^a.

Properties

Tetration grows extraordinarily fast: even 4 tetrated to 4 produces a tower whose number of digits is itself astronomically large. Tetration is neither commutative nor associative. Extending tetration to non-integer or complex heights is an open problem; various approaches, such as regular iteration and Kneser's construction for real analytic tetration of the exponential function, provide solutions under certain conditions, but no extension is universally accepted as canonical.

Terminology and history

The word tetration, from tetra- (four) and iteration, was coined by English mathematician Reuben Louis Goodstein in 1947 in connection with his system of hyperoperations. Power towers had been studied earlier by Euler and others in connection with infinite exponential towers, which converge only for bases in a certain interval between e−e and e1/e.
