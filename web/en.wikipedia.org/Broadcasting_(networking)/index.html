Broadcasting (networking)

In computer networking, broadcasting is the transmission of a packet to all possible destinations in a network at once — a one-to-all form of communication. It contrasts with unicast (one-to-one), multicast (one-to-many, restricted to a group) and anycast (one-to-any of a group).

Broadcast domains

A broadcast is normally confined to a single broadcast domain: a segment of a network, such as a LAN or VLAN, within which a device can reach all others by broadcast at the data link layer. In Ethernet, a frame addressed to the all-ones MAC address FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF is delivered to every interface on the segment. Routers do not ordinarily forward broadcasts between networks, which limits their spread; excessive broadcast traffic (a "broadcast storm") can cripple a network, so switches and VLANs are used to segment broadcast domains.

IP broadcasting

In IPv4, the special address 255.255.255.255 is the limited broadcast address, and a subnet may also have a "directed" broadcast address (the highest address in the subnet, with all host bits set to 1). Broadcasting is used by protocols such as ARP (to resolve IP addresses to MAC addresses) and DHCP (for a client to discover a server before it has an address). IPv6 has no broadcast addresses; their functions are performed by multicast groups, notably the all-nodes group ff02::1.
