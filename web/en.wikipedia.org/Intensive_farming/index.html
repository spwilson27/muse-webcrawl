Intensive farming

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Intensive agriculture, also known as intensive farming (as opposed to extensive farming), conventional farming, or industrial agriculture, is a type of agriculture, both of crop plants and of animals, characterized by high inputs of capital, labour, and chemicals relative to land area, and by high outputs per unit of land. Most commercial agriculture is intensive in one or more ways.

Crop production

Intensive crop farming relies on mechanization, synthetic fertilizers produced by the Haber–Bosch process, pesticides and herbicides, irrigation, and high-yielding varieties developed during the Green Revolution. Monocultures of cereals such as wheat, maize, and rice, and plantations of fruit and vegetables, achieve yields many times those of traditional methods, feeding urban populations from a smaller land footprint.

Livestock production

Intensive animal farming — often called factory farming — confines large numbers of animals indoors or in feedlots at high stocking densities. Poultry, pigs, dairy cattle, and beef cattle are raised with formulated feeds, veterinary medicines including antibiotics, and controlled environments to maximize growth rates and output of meat, milk, and eggs.

Advantages and criticisms

Advocates note that intensification produces abundant, cheap food and can spare land for nature compared with low-yield systems. Critics point to environmental costs: greenhouse-gas emissions, nutrient runoff causing eutrophication, soil degradation, pesticide impacts on biodiversity, and water consumption; animal-welfare concerns over confinement; and public-health issues such as antimicrobial resistance and zoonotic disease risk. These debates shape policies on farm subsidies and regulation in the EU, United States, and elsewhere.

Alternatives

Organic farming, agroecology, permaculture, and sustainable intensification seek to maintain yields while reducing external inputs and environmental harm.
