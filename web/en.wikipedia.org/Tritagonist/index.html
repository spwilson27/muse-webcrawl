Tritagonist

In ancient Greek theatre, the tritagonist was the third actor in the company, after the protagonist and deuteragonist.

In literature, the tritagonist (from Ancient Greek τριταγωνιστής, tritagōnistḗs, meaning "third actor") is the third most important character in a narrative, after the protagonist and the deuteragonist. The term originates in the theatre of ancient Greece, where plays were performed by companies of three actors who shared all the speaking roles between them.

In Greek theatre

The early Greek tragedians gradually expanded the number of actors in a play. Aeschylus is credited with introducing the second actor (the deuteragonist), and Sophocles with the third actor (the tritagonist). The three actors divided the roles, with the protagonist taking the leading part, the deuteragonist the second, and the tritagonist the remaining — often lesser or more functional — roles, though in practice the tritagonist could play significant figures, including antagonists and messengers. Euripides and later dramatists worked within this three-actor convention even as the number of characters grew, with actors changing masks and costumes between scenes.

In modern narrative

In modern usage, "tritagonist" denotes the third-ranked character in a story — a member of the central trio in many works of fiction. Examples often cited include Hermione Granger in the Harry Potter series (after Harry and Ron), and in ensemble storytelling the third lead of a television ensemble. Discussions of the tritagonist typically concern how the character supports or contrasts with the protagonist and deuteragonist, completing a trio of perspectives on the story's central conflict.
