Radio astronomy

Radio astronomy
Subfield of astronomy
Studies celestial objects at radio frequencies (roughly 3 kHz – 900 GHz)

Radio astronomy is a subfield of astronomy that studies celestial objects at radio frequencies. The first detection of radio waves from an astronomical object was made in 1933, when Karl Jansky at Bell Telephone Laboratories reported radiation coming from the Milky Way. Subsequent observations have identified a number of different types of radio emission, from stars and galaxies to the cosmic microwave background.

History

Grote Reber built the first parabolic radio telescope in 1937 and produced the first radio maps of the sky. After the Second World War, radar technology spurred rapid progress: the 21-cm hydrogen line was detected in 1951, and observatories such as Jodrell Bank and Parkes made landmark discoveries, including quasars (1963) and pulsars (1967, by Jocelyn Bell Burnell and Antony Hewish). Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson's 1965 detection of the cosmic microwave background earned a Nobel Prize.

Techniques

Because radio wavelengths are long, single dishes must be enormous or — more commonly — arrays of dishes are combined by interferometry to synthesize a large aperture, achieving arcsecond or finer resolution. Very-long-baseline interferometry links telescopes on different continents. Radio telescopes observe day and night and through clouds, but require radio-quiet sites shielded from human transmissions.

Sources studied

Radio observations reveal synchrotron emission from active galactic nuclei and supernova remnants, thermal emission from ionized gas, molecular spectral lines in cold clouds, solar activity, and the planets.
