Zodiac
DefinitionBelt of the sky around the ecliptic divided into twelve signs of 30° each
OriginBabylonian astronomy, by the 5th century BC
SystemsTropical (Western) and sidereal (Hindu) zodiacs

The zodiac is a belt-shaped region of the sky that extends approximately 8° north and south of the ecliptic, the apparent path of the Sun across the celestial sphere. The paths of the Moon and the visible planets also lie within the zodiac. In Western astrology, and formerly astronomy, the zodiac is divided into twelve signs, each occupying 30° of celestial longitude and roughly corresponding to the constellations through which the ecliptic passes. The term derives from the Greek zōidiakos kuklos, "circle of little animals", most of the signs being represented by animals.

History

The division of the ecliptic into twelve signs of equal length was developed by Babylonian astronomers by the fifth century BC, building on earlier star catalogues and the twelve-month lunisolar calendar. Hellenistic astronomy and astrology adopted and systematised the scheme — Ptolemy's Tetrabiblos codified it for the Western tradition — and it passed into Indian astrology during the Indo-Greek period and into Persian and Arabic astronomy in late antiquity and the Middle Ages.

Tropical and sidereal zodiacs

The Western (tropical) zodiac fixes the signs to the seasons, beginning Aries at the March equinox. Because of the precession of the equinoxes, the signs have drifted almost 24° from the constellations of the same names since antiquity. Hindu astrology uses a sidereal zodiac anchored to the stars, applying a correction (ayanamsa) of about 24°, so the two systems' signs no longer coincide. A thirteenth constellation, Ophiuchus, also crosses the ecliptic but has never been a zodiacal sign in the twelve-sign scheme.

Astronomical usage

In modern astronomy the zodiac survives chiefly in the system of ecliptic coordinates and in the names of the zodiacal constellations; the "zodiacal light" — sunlight scattered by interplanetary dust along the ecliptic — also takes its name from the belt. Chinese astronomy developed an independent system, including the lunar mansions and the twelve Earthly Branches, sometimes loosely called the Chinese zodiac, whose twelve animal years form a calendrical rather than ecliptic scheme.
