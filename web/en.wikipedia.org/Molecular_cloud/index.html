A molecular cloud, sometimes called a stellar nursery, is a type of interstellar cloud whose density and temperature permit the formation of molecules, principally molecular hydrogen (H₂). Molecular clouds are the coldest and densest parts of the interstellar medium and are the birthplaces of stars: within their densest cores, gravity overcomes internal pressure and the gas collapses to form protostars.

Properties

Molecular clouds consist mostly of hydrogen in molecular form, with about 28% helium and a few percent heavier elements and dust grains. Typical densities range from hundreds to thousands of molecules per cubic centimetre in giant clouds, rising to 10⁵–10⁶ cm⁻³ in dense cores. Temperatures are only 10–20 kelvin, because the dust shields the interior from starlight while molecules such as carbon monoxide radiate heat away. Because H₂ itself is nearly invisible at these temperatures, astronomers trace molecular clouds mainly through CO emission and through the dark silhouettes they cast against background stars — the dark nebulae catalogued by E. E. Barnard.

Types

  • Giant molecular clouds (GMCs): vast complexes 15–600 light-years across with masses of 10⁴–10⁶ solar masses; concentrated in galactic spiral arms. The Orion Molecular Cloud, about 1,300 light-years away, is the nearest region of massive star formation.
  • Small molecular clouds and Bok globules: isolated, compact dark clouds of a few light-years, often containing one or a few collapsing cores.
  • High-latitude diffuse molecular clouds: wispy, translucent clouds far from the galactic plane, including the infrared cirrus.

Star formation

Clouds are supported against gravity by turbulence and magnetic fields, but where a core becomes dense enough — exceeding the Jeans mass — it collapses. Collapse can proceed undisturbed or be triggered by supernova shock waves, stellar winds, or galactic collisions. Only a few percent of a cloud's mass typically converts into stars before feedback from the newborn stars disperses the remainder; cloud lifetimes are estimated at 10–30 million years. The chemistry within clouds is rich: more than 200 molecular species have been detected, including water, methanol, and complex organic molecules that may feed the formation of planetary systems.
