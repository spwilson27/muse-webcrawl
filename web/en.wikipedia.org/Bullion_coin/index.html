Bullion coin

A bullion coin is a coin struck from precious metal — usually gold, silver, platinum or palladium — and kept as a store of value or investment rather than for use in everyday commerce. Although bullion coins are legal tender in their country of issue, their face value is typically far below the market value of the metal they contain, and they trade at a modest premium over the spot price of the metal.

History and major issues

The modern bullion coin era began with South Africa's Krugerrand in 1967, the first coin marketed directly to private investors as a vehicle for owning gold. It was followed by Canada's Gold Maple Leaf (1979), distinguished by its very high purity, the American Gold Eagle (1986), the British Britannia, the Australian Kangaroo/Nugget, the Chinese Gold Panda and the Austrian Vienna Philharmonic — the latter also issued in euro denominations. Silver versions of these series typically contain one troy ounce of metal; gold coins are also struck in fractional sizes.

Investing

Bullion coins are valued for their recognisability, liquidity and the guarantee of weight and purity implied by a sovereign mint. Mints also issue proof and collector versions at higher premiums, while "junk silver" — pre-decimal and pre-1965 circulating coins — trades mainly for metal content. Prices follow the underlying bullion markets, plus premiums that vary with demand. See also: Gold coin, Bullion, Krugerrand.
