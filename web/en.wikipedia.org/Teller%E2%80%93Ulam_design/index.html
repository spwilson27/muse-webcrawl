Teller–Ulam design
TypeThermonuclear weapon design
InventorsEdward Teller, Stanisław Ulam
Date1951

The Teller–Ulam design is the technical concept behind most modern thermonuclear weapons (hydrogen bombs). It is named for its contributors, physicists Edward Teller and Stanisław Ulam, who developed it in 1951 at Los Alamos National Laboratory.

Concept

The design uses a two-stage arrangement: a fission primary stage produces intense X-ray radiation, which is contained within the weapon casing and used to compress and ignite a physically separate secondary stage containing fusion fuel such as lithium deuteride. This process, called radiation implosion, allows thermonuclear weapons to be made arbitrarily powerful in principle and compact enough for delivery by missile.

History

The design was first tested in the Ivy Mike shot of 1952, which yielded 10.4 megatons. Variants of the concept form the basis of the nuclear arsenals of all five recognized nuclear-weapon states.
