Tristan chord

The Tristan chord is a chord of the notes F, B, D♯ and G♯ made famous by its use in the opening of Richard Wagner's opera Tristan und Isolde (premiered 1865). It consists of an augmented fourth, an augmented sixth and an augmented ninth above a bass note, and is often described structurally as a half-diminished seventh chord.

Function and analysis

The chord has provoked more analysis than perhaps any other in Western music. Traditional functional harmony struggles to classify it: it has been read as an altered subdominant, a French augmented-sixth chord, or an appoggiatura-laden dominant preparation. In the opera's opening phrase it resolves, unusually, to a dominant seventh — a resolution postponed throughout the opera, mirroring the lovers' unfulfilled desire and contributing to the work's intense chromaticism.

Significance

The Tristan chord became a symbol of the dissolution of traditional tonality. By withholding conventional resolution, Wagner's score pointed toward the atonality of the early 20th century; composers and theorists from Debussy and Mahler to Schoenberg cited the chord as a turning point — sometimes called the beginning of modern music. The chord has also been quoted by later composers, including Britten and even in popular music.
