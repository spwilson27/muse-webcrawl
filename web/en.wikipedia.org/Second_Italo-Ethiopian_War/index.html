The Second Italo-Ethiopian War, also called the Second Italo-Abyssinian War, was a war of aggression fought between the Kingdom of Italy and the Ethiopian Empire from October 1935 to February 1937. It is seen as an early example of the expansionist policy of the Axis powers and of the ineffectiveness of the League of Nations before the outbreak of World War II.

Background

Fascist Italy under Benito Mussolini sought to expand its colonial empire in Africa and to avenge Italy's humiliating defeat at the Battle of Adwa in 1896 during the First Italo-Ethiopian War. The Walwal incident of December 1934, a clash at an oasis on the border between Ethiopia and Italian Somaliland, provided the pretext for invasion. Ethiopia appealed to the League of Nations, but the League's sanctions excluded vital materials such as oil and proved ineffective.

Course of the war

Italy invaded on 3 October 1935 from Eritrea in the north and Italian Somaliland in the south. Despite numerical and technological inferiority, Ethiopian forces resisted stoutly, but Italian commanders Emilio De Bono and then Pietro Badoglio used overwhelming air power, tanks, and — in violation of the Geneva Protocol — mustard gas against soldiers and civilians. The Ethiopian counteroffensive of December 1935 ("Christmas Offensive") temporarily checked the northern advance but was broken by early 1936. Addis Ababa fell on 5 May 1936; Emperor Haile Selassie fled into exile, and on 9 May Mussolini proclaimed the annexation of Ethiopia and the creation of Italian East Africa, with King Victor Emmanuel III taking the title Emperor of Ethiopia.

Aftermath

Guerrilla resistance (the arbegnoch, or Patriots) continued throughout the occupation, which was marked by brutal reprisals such as the Yekatit 12 massacre in Addis Ababa in February 1937. The League's failure to stop the aggression discredited collective security and pushed Italy toward alliance with Nazi Germany. Ethiopia was liberated by British and Commonwealth forces together with Ethiopian patriots in 1941, and Haile Selassie returned to his throne. Estimates of Ethiopian deaths range into the hundreds of thousands; the war is remembered in Ethiopia as a defining national struggle.
