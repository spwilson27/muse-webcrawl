Norwegian folk music

Key instrumentHardanger fiddle (hardingfele)
Vocal formsStev, ballads (kjempeviser), lullabies, work songs
Dance formsSpringar, gangar, halling, pols
Other instrumentsLangeleik, bukkehorn (goat horn), seljefløyte (willow flute), accordion
InstitutionsNational folk music competitions (Landskappleiken, since 1888)

Norwegian folk music comprises the traditional music of Norway, rooted in rural communities and preserved, unusually in Europe, in strong regional traditions. Its best-known emblem is the Hardanger fiddle (hardingfele), a richly decorated violin with sympathetic strings, whose music drove the dance traditions — springar, gangar, halling and pols — of valleys such as Telemark, Setesdal, Hallingdal and Valdres.

Vocal traditions

Vocal folk music includes the stev, short four-line improvised or traditional stanzas exchanged antiphonally; medieval heroic ballads (kjempeviser) such as "Draumkvedet"; hymns in distinctive local variants; and functional singing — lullabies, herding calls (lokk) sung across mountain pastures, and wedding music. The Sami joik of the north is a separate, ancient tradition.

History and revival

In the nineteenth century, national romantic collectors such as Ludvig Mathias Lindeman transcribed folk melodies, which composers — above all Edvard Grieg — wove into art music; the virtuoso fiddler Ole Bull carried Norwegian fiddle music to international fame. Pietistic suspicion of fiddling suppressed the tradition in some districts, but the national folk music competition Landskappleiken has been held since 1888, and the twentieth century brought institutional support, archives, and a continuing scene that ranges from strict traditionalism to folk-jazz and folk-metal fusions.
