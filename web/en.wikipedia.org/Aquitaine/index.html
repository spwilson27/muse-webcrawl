Aquitaine (French: Aquitaine [akitɛn]; Occitan: Aquitània) is a historical region of southwestern France and was an administrative region of the country until 2016, when it was merged with Limousin and Poitou-Charentes to form the new region of Nouvelle-Aquitaine. Its capital was Bordeaux, one of France's great port cities and the centre of a world-famous wine region.

Geography

Aquitaine occupied the southwestern corner of France, bordered by the Atlantic Ocean to the west, the Pyrenees and Spain to the south, and the Massif Central to the northeast. The former region comprised five departments: Dordogne, Gironde, Landes, Lot-et-Garonne and Pyrénées-Atlantiques. Its long, straight Atlantic coast is backed by the Landes forest, one of Europe's largest planted pine forests, and by the Dune of Pilat, the tallest sand dune in Europe. The Garonne and Dordogne rivers water its wine-growing plains, while its south includes part of the French Basque Country.

History

Roman Gallia Aquitania took its name from the Aquitani, an Iberian-related people between the Pyrenees and the Garonne. After Rome's fall the region passed between Visigoths, Franks and the independent dukes of Aquitaine. Eleanor of Aquitaine, heiress of Duke William X, married first Louis VII of France and then, in 1152, Henry II of England, bringing Aquitaine into the Angevin Empire; it remained a contested Anglo-French possession until the French victory at Castillon in 1453 ended the Hundred Years' War. The wines of Bordeaux, the pilgrimage routes to Santiago de Compostela through the region, and the prehistoric cave art of the Dordogne (including Lascaux) are hallmarks of its history.

Economy and culture

Aquitaine is famous for Bordeaux wine, foie gras, Armagnac (from neighbouring Gascony), oysters from Arcachon Bay, surfing resorts such as Hossegor and Biarritz, and an aerospace and tourism economy centred on Bordeaux and the coast. The region's languages historically include Occitan (Gascon) and Basque in the far southwest, alongside French.
