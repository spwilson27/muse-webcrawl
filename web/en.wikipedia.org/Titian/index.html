Tiziano Vecellio (c. 1488/90 – 27 August 1576), known in English as Titian, was an Italian Renaissance painter, the most important artist of the Venetian school. Recognised by his contemporaries as "the sun amidst small stars", he was one of the most versatile of Italian painters, equally adept with portraits, landscapes, mythological and religious subjects; his use of colour and paint profoundly influenced Western art for centuries.

Life

Born in Pieve di Cadore in the Dolomites, Titian was sent to Venice as a boy and apprenticed first to the mosaicist Sebastiano Zuccato and then to Gentile and Giovanni Bellini. He worked alongside Giorgione, and on Giorgione's early death in 1510 completed some of his pictures. His monumental Assumption of the Virgin (1516–18) for the high altar of the Frari caused a sensation and established him as Venice's leading painter. He became official painter to the Venetian Republic and later worked for the courts of Ferrara, Mantua and Urbino.

Habsburg patronage and late work

Titian painted Emperor Charles V repeatedly — the equestrian Charles V at Mühlberg (1548) created the paradigm of the state portrait — and for Philip II of Spain he produced the celebrated poesie, a series of mythological canvases including Diana and Actaeon, Diana and Callisto and The Death of Actaeon. In old age his brushwork became famously loose and expressive, pictures built up from patches of colour resolved only at a distance. He died during the Venetian plague of 1576, leaving the Pietà intended for his own tomb unfinished; it was completed by Palma Giovane.
