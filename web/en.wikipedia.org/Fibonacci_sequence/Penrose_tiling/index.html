Penrose tiling

A Penrose tiling is an example of an aperiodic tiling. Here, a tiling is a covering of the plane by non-overlapping tiles, and aperiodic means that shifting the tiling by any non-zero vector does not produce the same tiling. The tilings are named after mathematician and physicist Roger Penrose, who investigated them in the 1970s.

The three types

Penrose described several families of tilings. The first (P1) uses pentagons, stars, boats and diamonds. The second (P2) uses two quadrilateral shapes called the kite and the dart. The third (P3) uses two rhombi with equal sides but different angles — a "fat" rhombus and a "thin" rhombus. Matching rules, enforced by colouring or by notches, ensure that only aperiodic tilings can be produced.

Properties

Penrose tilings exhibit fivefold symmetry locally, are self-similar under inflation and deflation, and contain copies of any finite patch infinitely often. The ratio of kites to darts (and of fat to thin rhombi) in an infinite tiling tends to the golden ratio φ, a constant that also governs the growth of the Fibonacci sequence and its Lucas sequences.

Quasicrystals

In 1982 Dan Shechtman observed electron diffraction patterns with fivefold symmetry in an aluminium–manganese alloy, an apparently impossible result for periodic crystals. The structure of these quasicrystals is closely modelled by three-dimensional analogues of Penrose tilings, work recognised by the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
