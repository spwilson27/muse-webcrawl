Leonardo Fibonacci (c. 1170 – c. 1250), also known as Leonardo of Pisa, was an Italian mathematician from the Republic of Pisa, considered to be "the most talented Western mathematician of the Middle Ages". His book Liber Abaci (1202) introduced the Hindu–Arabic numeral system to Europe and demonstrated its superiority over Roman numerals for commerce and calculation. He is best known today for the Fibonacci sequence — 0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, … — which he presented in Liber Abaci in a problem about the growth of a rabbit population, though the sequence had been described earlier by Indian mathematicians.

Life

Fibonacci was born around 1170 to Guglielmo Bonacci, a Pisan merchant and customs official posted to Béjaïa (Bugia) in present-day Algeria. Travelling with his father, Leonardo learned the Hindu–Arabic numerals and calculation methods used by Arab merchants, and recognised their advantages. He travelled widely around the Mediterranean — to Egypt, Syria, Greece, Sicily and Provence — studying the arithmetic systems of the places he visited. Returning to Pisa around 1200, he published Liber Abaci in 1202. He was later presented to the court of the Emperor Frederick II, whose scholars posed him mathematical challenges, and he received a salary from the Republic of Pisa from 1240.

Works

Besides Liber Abaci, Fibonacci wrote Practica Geometriae (1220), on geometry and surveying; Flos (1225), containing solutions to problems posed by Johannes of Palermo at Frederick's court, including a remarkably accurate approximation of a root of a cubic equation; and the Liber Quadratorum (1225), the "Book of Squares", on number theory, which includes the identity for sums of squares still named after him and work on congruum numbers.

Legacy

The spread of Hindu–Arabic numerals transformed European commerce, banking and science, though adoption took centuries. The sequence bearing Fibonacci's name arises in the ratios approaching the golden ratio, in nature and combinatorics; its properties were developed by later mathematicians including Édouard Lucas, who named the sequence after Fibonacci in the 19th century. Zeckendorf's theorem states that every positive integer can be uniquely represented as a sum of non-consecutive Fibonacci numbers. A statue of Fibonacci stands in Pisa, and the Fibonacci Association and journal Fibonacci Quarterly continue the study of his sequence.
