In mathematics, two quantities are in the golden ratio if their ratio is the same as the ratio of their sum to the larger of the two quantities. The value, denoted by the Greek letter φ (phi), is approximately 1.6180339887. Algebraically, φ is the positive solution of the quadratic equation x² = x + 1, so φ = (1 + √5)/2. Euclid described the division of a line in "extreme and mean ratio" in the Elements (c. 300 BC). The golden ratio is intimately connected with the Fibonacci sequence: the ratio of consecutive Fibonacci numbers converges to φ.

Properties

The golden ratio has self-similar properties: φ² = φ + 1 and 1/φ = φ − 1. Its continued fraction is [1; 1, 1, 1, …], the slowest-converging of any continued fraction, which makes φ "the most irrational number" — hardest to approximate by rationals. A golden rectangle (sides in ratio φ) can be subdivided into a square and a smaller golden rectangle indefinitely; joining quarter-circles inscribed in the squares approximates a logarithmic (golden) spiral. The golden angle, about 137.5°, is observed in the arrangement of leaves and seeds (phyllotaxis), where it produces optimal packing — for instance in sunflower heads, whose spirals count out consecutive Fibonacci numbers.

History and aesthetics

Luca Pacioli's De Divina Proportione (1509), illustrated by Leonardo da Vinci, celebrated the ratio's mathematical and theological significance, and Johannes Kepler called it one of geometry's treasures. Claims that the golden ratio was used in the Parthenon, the Great Pyramid or the Mona Lisa are popular but disputed by historians. In the 19th and 20th centuries, writers such as Adolf Zeising and Le Corbusier (with his Modulor system) promoted φ as a canon of beauty in art and architecture; experiments on aesthetic preference for golden rectangles have given mixed results, and the ratio's supposed ubiquity in nature and design is often exaggerated.

Mathematics

The golden ratio appears in the geometry of the pentagon and pentagram (whose diagonals divide one another in golden ratio), in Penrose tilings, continued fractions, and the closed-form Binet formula for Fibonacci numbers: F(n) = (φⁿ − ψⁿ)/√5, where ψ = (1 − √5)/2 is the conjugate root. It is related to the Lucas numbers, named for Édouard Lucas, and to the metallic means.
