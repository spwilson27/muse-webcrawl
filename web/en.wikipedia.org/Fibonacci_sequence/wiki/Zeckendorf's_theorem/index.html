Zeckendorf's theorem

Zeckendorf's theorem
FieldNumber theory, combinatorics
Named afterEdouard Zeckendorf
StatementEvery positive integer has a unique representation as a sum of non-consecutive Fibonacci numbers
First published1939 (known to Gerrit Lekkerkerker; formally proved by Zeckendorf, 1972)

Zeckendorf's theorem, named after the Belgian amateur mathematician and army officer Edouard Zeckendorf, states that every positive integer can be represented uniquely as the sum of one or more distinct Fibonacci numbers in such a way that the sum does not include any two consecutive Fibonacci numbers. Such a sum is called the Zeckendorf representation of the number. Using the convention F2 = 1, F3 = 2, F4 = 3, F5 = 5, F6 = 8, …, the representation of, for example, 100 is 89 + 8 + 3, and that of 10 is 8 + 2.

Finding the representation

The greedy algorithm yields the Zeckendorf representation: repeatedly subtract the largest Fibonacci number not exceeding the remainder. Writing the representation as a binary string (1 where a Fibonacci number is used, 0 where not, with F2 as the least significant digit) gives a numeration system — the Zeckendorf (or Fibonacci) coding — in which no two 1s are adjacent; this code is used in data compression as Fibonacci coding, where the appearance of "11" marks the end of a codeword.

Proof sketch

Existence follows by induction: if n is itself a Fibonacci number the claim is immediate; otherwise, take the largest Fibonacci number F ≤ n; by induction n − F has a representation, and F's predecessor cannot appear in it — otherwise n would meet or exceed the next Fibonacci number — so the combined sum has no consecutive terms. Uniqueness uses the lemma that the sum of any set of non-consecutive Fibonacci numbers, each at most F, is strictly less than the next Fibonacci number after F, so the largest term of the representation is forced, and induction completes the argument. The same result holds, mutatis mutandis, for Lucas numbers and generalisations studied by Lekkerkerker and others.

Related topics

Zeckendorf representations support analogues of arithmetic operations studied in "Zeckendorf game" problems and in the theory of numeration systems, and the counting of representations of integers connects to the Stern's diatomic series and the Fibonacci coding used in robotics and signal processing.
