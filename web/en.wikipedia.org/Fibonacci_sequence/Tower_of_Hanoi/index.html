Tower of Hanoi

The Tower of Hanoi (also called the Tower of Brahma or Lucas' Tower) is a mathematical game or puzzle invented by the French mathematician Édouard Lucas in 1883. It consists of three rods and a number of disks of distinct sizes, which can slide onto any rod. The puzzle begins with the disks stacked on one rod in order of decreasing size, the smallest on top — a conical arrangement.

Rules and solution

The objective is to move the entire stack to another rod, obeying three rules: only one disk may be moved at a time; each move consists of taking the upper disk from one stack and placing it on another; and no larger disk may be placed on top of a smaller disk.

The puzzle has a simple recursive optimal solution: to move n disks, move n − 1 disks to the spare rod, move the largest disk to the target, then move the n − 1 disks onto it. The minimum number of moves required is 2n − 1.

The legend

Lucas popularized a legend of an ancient temple (associated with Brahma) where priests move 64 golden disks between diamond needles; when the last move is completed, the world will end. At one move per second, 264 − 1 moves would take about 585 billion years — roughly 42 times the current age of the universe.

Mathematics and computing

The Tower of Hanoi is a standard teaching example for recursion in computer science. Its move sequence is related to the binary reflected Gray code and to the Sierpiński triangle: the state graph of the puzzle, drawn for successive n, converges to the Sierpiński sieve fractal. Variants with more pegs, such as the Reve's puzzle with four pegs, gave rise to the Frame–Stewart conjecture on optimal solutions.
