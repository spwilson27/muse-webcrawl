Hindu–Arabic numeral system

The Hindu–Arabic numeral system (also called the Hindu numeral system, Arabic numeral system, or Hindu–Western Arabic system) is a positional decimal numeral system for representing numbers. Its glyphs are descended from the Indian Brahmi numerals, and it is the most common system for the symbolic representation of numbers in the world today.

The system was invented by Indian mathematicians between the 1st and 4th centuries. By the 9th century it had been adopted in Arabic mathematics, and it became known in Europe through the writings of the Persian mathematician Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī and the Italian mathematician Fibonacci, whose Liber Abaci (1202) promoted the system throughout Europe.

History

The decimal place-value system with a symbol for zero was first developed in India. Brahmi numerals appear from the 3rd century BCE, and by the Gupta period (c. 320–550 CE) Indian mathematicians were using a fully positional decimal system. The concept of zero as a number, with its own arithmetic rules, was articulated by Brahmagupta in his Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628 CE).

The system spread westward through trade and scholarship. Al-Khwārizmī's treatise On the Calculation with Hindu Numerals (c. 825) described the system for the Arabic-speaking world; its Latin translation, Algoritmi de numero Indorum, gave rise to the word "algorithm".

Transmission to Europe

Gerbert of Aurillac (later Pope Sylvester II) encountered the numerals in Spain in the 10th century. The system's widespread European adoption, however, followed Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202), which demonstrated the superiority of the "Modus Indorum" (method of the Indians) for commercial arithmetic compared with Roman numerals and the abacus.

Symbols

The ten digits 0–9 evolved through several regional forms. The western Arabic (Ghubar) numerals used in the Maghreb and al-Andalus developed into the modern European digits, while the eastern Arabic numerals (٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩) remain in use in much of the Middle East.

Impact

Positional decimal notation made arithmetic algorithms uniform and mechanical, enabling advances in algebra, astronomy, accounting, and eventually computing. It is frequently described as one of the most consequential inventions in the history of mathematics.
