Golden ratio

In mathematics, two quantities are in the golden ratio if their ratio is the same as the ratio of their sum to the larger of the two quantities. Denoted by the Greek letter φ (phi), the golden ratio is the irrational number

φ = (1 + √5) / 2 ≈ 1.6180339887…

Algebraically, φ is the positive solution of x² = x + 1. Its reciprocal satisfies 1/φ = φ − 1 ≈ 0.618, and its continued fraction [1; 1, 1, 1, …] is the slowest-converging of all continued fractions, making φ in a precise sense the "most irrational" number.

History

The ratio was studied in Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BC) as the "extreme and mean ratio," arising in the geometry of the pentagon and pentagram. Luca Pacioli's De Divina Proportione (1509), illustrated by Leonardo da Vinci, celebrated its supposed divine properties. Johannes Kepler called it one of geometry's two great treasures.

Relation to the Fibonacci sequence

The ratio of consecutive Fibonacci numbers converges to φ: 1/1, 2/1, 3/2, 5/3, 8/5, 13/8, … approach 1.618…. Binet's formula expresses the nth Fibonacci number directly using φ. The golden angle, derived from φ, governs the arrangement of seeds and florets in many plants (phyllotaxis), producing Fibonacci-numbered spiral arms.

Art, architecture, and claims

The golden ratio has been claimed — often controversially — to underlie the proportions of the Parthenon, the Great Pyramid, and works of Leonardo and Mondrian, and it appears deliberately in some modern design and in Le Corbusier's Modulor system. Many aesthetic claims are disputed by scholars, but the ratio's genuine mathematical properties and its appearance in pentagonal geometry and quasicrystals are well established.
