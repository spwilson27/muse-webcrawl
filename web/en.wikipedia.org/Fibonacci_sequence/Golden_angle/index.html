Golden angle

In geometry, the golden angle is the smaller of the two angles created by sectioning the circumference of a circle in the golden ratio; that is, into two arcs such that the ratio of the length of the smaller arc to the length of the larger arc is the same as the ratio of the length of the larger arc to the full circumference of the circle.

Value

Algebraically, letting the circumference be split into arcs of length a and b with a > b, the golden ratio φ satisfies (a+b)/a = a/b = φ. The golden angle subtended by the smaller arc is then 2π/φ2 radians, approximately 2.399963 radians, or about 137.5077640°. Because φ is the irrational number hardest to approximate by rational numbers, successive points separated by the golden angle never align into simple radial rows.

In nature

The golden angle is significant in phyllotaxis, the arrangement of leaves, seeds, and florets in plants. In sunflowers, pinecones, and many other plants, each new primordium emerges at roughly the golden angle from the previous one; this divergence angle produces the efficient packing and the conspicuous spiral patterns counted by Fibonacci numbers, since convergents of the continued fraction of φ are ratios of consecutive Fibonacci numbers.
