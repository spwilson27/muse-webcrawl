Zeckendorf's theorem

Zeckendorf's theorem, named after the Belgian mathematician Edouard Zeckendorf, states that every positive integer can be represented uniquely as the sum of one or more distinct, non-consecutive Fibonacci numbers. This representation is called the Zeckendorf representation of the number. For example, 100 = 89 + 8 + 3, where 89, 8 and 3 are Fibonacci numbers no two of which are consecutive in the sequence.

Statement and proof idea

Using the Fibonacci numbers F2 = 1, F3 = 2, F4 = 3, F5 = 5, and so on (avoiding the duplicate 1), the theorem asserts existence and uniqueness of a representation with no two consecutive indices. Existence follows from a greedy algorithm: repeatedly subtract the largest Fibonacci number not exceeding the remainder. Uniqueness follows because the sum of any set of non-consecutive Fibonacci numbers with largest term Fk is strictly less than Fk+1, so two different representations can be compared at their largest differing term to give a contradiction.

Generalisation to Lucas sequences

The theorem extends naturally to Lucas sequences. For the Lucas numbers (2, 1, 3, 4, 7, 11, 18, ...), every positive integer likewise has a unique representation as a sum of non-consecutive Lucas numbers, with suitable conventions. More generally, analogous unique "legal decomposition" results hold for the terms of many linear recurrence sequences, a theory developed extensively in modern combinatorics on words and numeration systems.

Applications

Zeckendorf representations give a numeration system — sometimes encoded as Fibonacci coding — used in data compression and in the analysis of algorithms such as the Fibonacci search technique. The representation also appears in the theory of the Wythoff Nim game and in the study of Beatty sequences.
