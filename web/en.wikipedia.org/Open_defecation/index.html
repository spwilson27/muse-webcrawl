Open defecation is the human practice of defecating outside—in fields, forests, bushes, bodies of water, or other open spaces—rather than using a toilet or latrine. It occurs where people lack sanitation infrastructure, and in some communities where it remains a longstanding custom even when facilities exist. The WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme ranks open defecation as the lowest rung of the sanitation ladder.

Globally, the number of people practising open defecation has fallen sharply—from more than 1.3 billion in 1990 to roughly 420 million in the early 2020s—but it remains concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.

Health effects

Open defecation spreads faecal pathogens into soil and water, driving diarrhoeal diseases, cholera, typhoid, polio, and soil-transmitted helminth infections. Diarrhoea remains a leading cause of death in children under five. Chronic exposure to faecal contamination is also linked to environmental enteropathy and childhood stunting. Women and girls face additional risks, including the need to travel to secluded places, often before dawn, exposing them to harassment and assault, and girls may miss school during menstruation where facilities are absent.

Campaigns to end it

The United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 6.2 calls for ending open defecation by 2030. Community-Led Total Sanitation (CLTS), developed in Bangladesh around 2000, uses community mobilisation—rather than hardware subsidies—to trigger collective behaviour change, and has spread to dozens of countries. India's Swachh Bharat Mission, launched in 2014, built more than 100 million household toilets and declared the country open-defecation-free in 2019, though independent surveys found continued practice in some areas, underlining that building toilets and changing habits are distinct challenges.

World Toilet Day

World Toilet Day, observed on 19 November and recognised by the UN since 2013, raises awareness of the global sanitation crisis, of which open defecation is the most visible symptom.
