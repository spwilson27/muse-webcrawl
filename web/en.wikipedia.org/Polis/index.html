Polis

Polis (Greek: πόλις, plural poleis) means "city" in Ancient Greek. In modern scholarship the term is used to describe the ancient Greek city-states, self-governing political communities that dominated the Greek world from the Archaic period through the Hellenistic age. A polis typically consisted of an urban centre — often fortified and containing a citadel (acropolis), an agora or marketplace, and temples — together with the surrounding countryside (chora) that supplied it with food.

At their height there were well over a thousand poleis spread around the Mediterranean and Black Sea coasts, ranging in size from tiny settlements of a few hundred people to Athens and Sparta, which each controlled extensive territories. Despite their diversity, poleis shared a common civic culture: citizenship was restricted to free adult males born to citizen parents, and political life revolved around assemblies, councils, magistracies and law courts.

Origins

The polis emerged during the Greek Dark Ages and took firm shape in the eighth century BC, a process associated with population growth, the revival of trade, the adoption of the alphabet from the Phoenicians, and the appearance of Panhellenic sanctuaries such as Olympia and Delphi. Colonisation carried the polis model from the Aegean to southern Italy and Sicily (Magna Graecia), southern France, Spain, North Africa and the Black Sea.

Government

Poleis experimented with nearly every form of government known to the ancient world. Democracy, most famously at Athens, gave decision-making power to the citizen assembly (ekklesia). Oligarchies restricted rule to a wealthy few, while tyrants — unconstitutional sole rulers — seized power in many cities during the seventh and sixth centuries BC. Sparta developed a distinctive mixed constitution combining two hereditary kings, a council of elders (gerousia), annually elected ephors and an assembly.

Aristotle, in his Politics, treated the polis as the natural and self-sufficient culmination of human association, famously describing man as a "political animal" (politikon zoon) — one whose nature is fulfilled only within the city.

Society

Citizens were a minority of the population in most poleis. Women, resident foreigners (metics), and slaves were excluded from political rights, though they formed the bulk of the workforce in agriculture, crafts and trade. Citizenship carried obligations as well as privileges: male citizens were expected to serve as hoplite infantry or rowers in the fleet, to hold office when chosen (often by lot), and to participate in religious festivals that bound the community together.

Decline

The poleis retained local self-government for centuries after losing their independence, first to Macedon after the Battle of Chaeronea (338 BC) and later within the Roman Empire. Only gradually, in late antiquity, did the civic institutions of the polis give way to new forms of urban organisation.
