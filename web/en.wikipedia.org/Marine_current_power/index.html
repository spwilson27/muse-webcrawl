Marine current power

Marine current power is a form of hydropower that converts the kinetic energy of marine currents — including both tidal streams and steady ocean currents — into electricity. It is closely related to, but distinct from, tidal barrage power: instead of impounding water behind a dam, marine current turbines are placed directly in free-flowing water, much like underwater wind turbines.

Principle

Because seawater is about 800 times denser than air, even a moderate current carries substantial energy. The power available per unit of rotor area grows with the cube of the current speed, so the best sites are where currents are accelerated by constriction — straits, channels between islands, and headland races — where tidal streams may exceed 4 to 5 metres per second. Steady ocean currents such as the Florida Current and the Kuroshio have also been studied as potential resources.

Technology

Most devices use horizontal-axis turbines mounted on the seabed, on monopiles, or on floating platforms moored in the flow. Other designs include oscillating hydrofoils, venturi-effect devices, and vertical-axis rotors. Arrays of turbines are envisaged for commercial-scale generation. The MeyGen project in the Pentland Firth, Scotland, is among the largest tidal stream arrays in operation and expansion.

Advantages and challenges

Marine currents are highly predictable — tidal streams can be forecast years in advance — and the resource is invisible from the surface. Challenges include the harsh marine environment, corrosion and biofouling, maintenance access, cabling costs, and potential impacts on marine life and navigation. As of the mid-2020s the technology remained pre-commercial at scale, with costs well above those of offshore wind.
