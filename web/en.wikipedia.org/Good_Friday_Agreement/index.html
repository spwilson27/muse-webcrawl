Good Friday Agreement

The Good Friday Agreement (GFA), also known as the Belfast Agreement, is a pair of agreements signed on 10 April 1998 — Good Friday — that brought to an end most of the violence of the Troubles, the ethno-nationalist conflict in Northern Ireland that had lasted roughly thirty years. It was a major development in the Northern Ireland peace process of the 1990s and remains the cornerstone of Northern Ireland's devolved system of government.

Background

The Troubles began in the late 1960s amid disputes over civil rights, discrimination, and the constitutional status of Northern Ireland. Paramilitary violence by republican groups such as the Provisional IRA and loyalist groups such as the UVF and UDA, together with security-force operations, killed more than 3,500 people. Ceasefires declared by the IRA in 1994 and restored in 1997 opened the way to multi-party talks chaired by United States Senator George Mitchell.

Structure of the agreement

The agreement consists of two interlocking documents. The multi-party agreement, reached between most of Northern Ireland's political parties, established three "strands" of institutions:

Strand One: devolved government

A Northern Ireland Assembly and a power-sharing Executive were created, allocating ministerial posts to parties broadly in proportion to their electoral strength (using the d'Hondt method) and requiring key decisions to be taken on a cross-community basis.

Strand Two: North–South institutions

A North/South Ministerial Council linked the Northern Ireland administration with the government of Ireland, overseeing cooperation in areas such as agriculture, transport and the environment.

Strand Three: East–West institutions

A British–Irish Council brought together the two sovereign governments with the devolved administrations of Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, and a British–Irish Intergovernmental Conference handled non-devolved matters.

Constitutional provisions

The agreement enshrined the "principle of consent": Northern Ireland would remain part of the United Kingdom unless and until majorities in both Northern Ireland and Ireland voted in simultaneous referendums for a united Ireland. Ireland amended Articles 2 and 3 of its constitution, replacing its territorial claim with an aspiration to unity by consent, and the United Kingdom repealed the Government of Ireland Act 1920.

Other provisions

The agreement also covered policing reform (leading to the Patten Commission and the creation of the Police Service of Northern Ireland), criminal justice, human rights and equality commissions, the decommissioning of paramilitary weapons, the demilitarisation of Northern Ireland, and the early release of paramilitary prisoners within two years.

Referendums and aftermath

On 22 May 1998 the agreement was endorsed in referendums on both sides of the border: 71.1% voted yes in Northern Ireland and 94.4% in Ireland. The institutions were suspended several times in the early 2000s, most seriously from 2002 to 2007, after disputes over decommissioning and policing. The St Andrews Agreement of 2006 paved the way for restoration, and in May 2007 Ian Paisley of the DUP and Martin McGuinness of Sinn Féin took office together as First Minister and deputy First Minister. The agreement's institutions were again challenged by the United Kingdom's withdrawal from the European Union, which raised difficult questions about the Irish border.
