A stellar corona is the outermost layer of a star's atmosphere, consisting of extremely hot, tenuous plasma extending millions of kilometres into space. The Sun's corona is most easily seen during a total solar eclipse as a pearly halo around the darkened disc, and is studied routinely in X-rays and extreme ultraviolet light, where it outshines the photosphere.

Properties

Paradoxically, the corona is far hotter than the surface beneath it: while the Sun's photosphere is about 5,800 K, the corona reaches one to three million kelvin. The mechanism that heats it — the "coronal heating problem" — is attributed to magnetic processes, including dissipation of magnetohydrodynamic waves and countless small reconnection events ("nanoflares"). The corona is structured by the stellar magnetic field into streamers, loops and dark coronal holes, from which the fast solar wind escapes.

Phenomena

Coronal activity includes flares, prominences and coronal mass ejections, which can disturb space weather at Earth. Other stars' coronae are detected through their X-ray emission; young, rapidly rotating stars have much more luminous coronae than the Sun.

Observation

Coronagraphs artificially eclipse a star to image its corona; dedicated solar missions such as SOHO, SDO and the Parker Solar Probe — which has flown through the Sun's corona — have transformed understanding of it.
