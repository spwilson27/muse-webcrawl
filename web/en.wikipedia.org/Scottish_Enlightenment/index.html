Scottish Enlightenment

Leading figures of the Scottish Enlightenment in Edinburgh.

The Scottish Enlightenment was a period of intellectual and scientific accomplishment in Scotland in the 18th and early 19th centuries, centred on the universities of Edinburgh, Glasgow, Aberdeen, and St Andrews. By the second half of the 18th century, Scotland — one of the poorest countries in Western Europe — produced a remarkable concentration of thinkers whose ideas helped shape the modern world.

Key figures

Central figures included the philosopher David Hume, whose empiricism and scepticism reshaped philosophy; Adam Smith, whose The Wealth of Nations (1776) founded modern economics; the moral philosopher Francis Hutcheson; Thomas Reid, founder of the common sense school; the historian William Robertson; the sociologist Adam Ferguson; and the geologist James Hutton, whose Theory of the Earth established the principle of uniformitarianism and a vastly older Earth. James Watt's improvements to the steam engine were developed in Glasgow in dialogue with this intellectual culture.

Institutions and networks

The movement flourished in clubs and societies such as the Select Society and the Poker Club, in reformed universities that rewarded lecturing and research, and in a legal and clerical establishment open to "moderate" ideas. Scotland's relatively widespread literacy, parish schools, and the absence of severe censorship contributed to its success.

Ideas and legacy

Scottish thinkers pioneered conjectural history, the comparative study of societies, empiricist philosophy, and the analysis of commercial society, including Smith's concepts of the division of labour and the "invisible hand". Their influence spread to continental Europe and North America, where it informed the thinking of the American Founding Fathers and the curriculum of universities. The movement faded in the early 19th century as its leading figures died and Scotland's distinct institutions were increasingly absorbed into British ones.
