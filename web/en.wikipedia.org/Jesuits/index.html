Jesuits


The Society of Jesus (Jesuits) is a religious order of the Catholic Church founded by Ignatius of Loyola in 1540.

The Society of Jesus (Latin: Societas Iesu; abbreviated SJ), commonly known as the Jesuits, is a religious order of clerics regular of pontifical right for men in the Catholic Church, headquartered in Rome. It was founded in 1540 by Ignatius of Loyola and six companions, with the approval of Pope Paul III. Members take vows of poverty, chastity, and obedience, and a special fourth vow of obedience to the pope regarding missions.

History

Ignatius, a Basque nobleman and former soldier, gathered his first companions at the University of Paris; they pronounced vows at Montmartre in 1534. The order grew rapidly, becoming a leading force of the Counter-Reformation. Jesuit missionaries — including Francis Xavier in Asia, Matteo Ricci in China, and missionaries in Paraguay and Canada — spread across the globe, often becoming renowned scholars, linguists, astronomers, and cartographers.

Political hostility led to the order's suppression by Pope Clement XIV in 1773; it survived informally in Russia and was restored universally by Pope Pius VII in 1814.

Education and spirituality

The Jesuits are famous for their schools and universities, guided by the Ratio Studiorum (1599) and by Ignatian spirituality, whose central text is Ignatius's Spiritual Exercises. Jesuit institutions include Georgetown University, Boston College, and the Pontifical Gregorian University. In 2013, Jorge Mario Bergoglio became Pope Francis, the first Jesuit pope. The society today numbers roughly 14,000–15,000 members working in over 100 countries.
