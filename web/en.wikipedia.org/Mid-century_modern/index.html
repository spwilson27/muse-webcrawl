Mid-century modern

Mid-century modern (MCM) is a design movement in American product, interior, graphic, and architectural design and urban development that was popular roughly from 1945 to 1969, during the United States' post–World War II period. The term was popularised by Cara Green's 1984 book Mid-Century Modern: Furniture of the 1950s. MCM is characterised by clean lines, gentle organic curves, integration with nature, and the use of new materials and manufacturing methods developed during and after the war.

Architecture

Mid-century modern architecture emphasised flat or low-pitched roofs, large expanses of glass, open floor plans, and post-and-beam construction that blurred the boundary between indoors and outdoors. In Southern California, developer Joseph Eichler built thousands of such homes in suburban tracts, while Palm Springs became a centre of desert modernism, with houses by Richard Neutra, Albert Frey, and others. The Case Study House program (1945–1966), sponsored by Arts & Architecture magazine, produced landmark experimental residences.

Furniture and interiors

Designers such as Charles and Ray Eames, George Nelson, Herman Miller, and Knoll exploited moulded plywood, fibreglass, and plastics to create chairs and storage systems that became icons of the era, including the Eames Lounge Chair (1956) and Saarinen's Tulip chair. Scandinavian design, particularly Danish modern furniture, strongly influenced American interiors of the period.

Legacy

Interest in mid-century modern revived from the 1990s onward, driven by reissued furniture lines, television productions such as Mad Men, and preservation campaigns for threatened postwar buildings. Original MCM furniture and houses now command high prices, and the style remains a pervasive influence on contemporary product and interior design.
