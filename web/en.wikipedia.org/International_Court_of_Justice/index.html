The International Court of Justice (ICJ; French: Cour internationale de justice), sometimes known as the World Court, is the principal judicial organ of the United Nations. It settles legal disputes between states and gives advisory opinions on legal questions referred to it by authorised UN organs and specialised agencies. The Court is seated in the Peace Palace in The Hague, Netherlands, and is the successor of the Permanent Court of International Justice, which operated under the League of Nations.

Composition

The Court consists of 15 judges elected to nine-year terms by the UN General Assembly and Security Council from candidates nominated by national groups in the Permanent Court of Arbitration. No two judges may be nationals of the same state, and elections are staggered so that five seats fall vacant every three years. Parties to a case that have no judge of their nationality on the bench may appoint a judge ad hoc.

Jurisdiction

Only states may be parties in contentious cases. The Court's jurisdiction depends on state consent: states may accept its jurisdiction in advance by declaration under the "optional clause", through compromissory clauses in treaties, or by special agreement. Judgments are binding on the parties and without appeal, though enforcement rests with the Security Council. Well-known cases include the Corfu Channel case (1949), Nicaragua v. United States (1986), and the Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons advisory opinion (1996).

Distinction from the ICC

The ICJ is frequently confused with the International Criminal Court (ICC), which also sits in The Hague but prosecutes individuals for genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity; the ICJ hears disputes between states only.
