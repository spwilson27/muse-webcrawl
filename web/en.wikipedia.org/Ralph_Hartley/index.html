Ralph Vinton Lyon Hartley (November 30, 1888 – May 1, 1970) was an American electronics researcher and inventor. He is best known for inventing the Hartley oscillator, a widely used electronic oscillator circuit, and for Hartley's law, an early formulation relating the amount of information that can be transmitted to bandwidth and time — a precursor of the Shannon–Hartley theorem in information theory.

Early life and education

Hartley was born in Sprucemont, Nevada, and grew up in Utah. He attended the University of Utah, then won a Rhodes Scholarship to study at St John's College, Oxford, where he completed his studies in 1913.

Career

After returning to the United States, Hartley joined the research laboratory of the Western Electric Company, and later Bell Telephone Laboratories. During the First World War he worked on radio receivers for directional listening. At Bell Labs he made contributions to nonlinear circuit analysis and to the theory of transmission of information. His 1928 paper "Transmission of Information", published in the Bell System Technical Journal, introduced a quantitative measure of information proportional to the logarithm of the number of possible message sequences, laying groundwork later developed by Claude Shannon. He also invented the Hartley oscillator in 1915, a circuit using a tapped inductor to provide feedback in vacuum-tube oscillators.

Awards

Hartley was a Fellow of the Institute of Radio Engineers and received the IRE Medal of Honor in 1946 for his work on oscillating circuits and information transmission.
