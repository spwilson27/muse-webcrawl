Bone remodelling

Bone remodelling (or bone remodeling) is a lifelong process in which mature bone tissue is removed from the skeleton (bone resorption) and new bone tissue is formed (ossification). This continuous turnover maintains bone strength, repairs microdamage, and regulates calcium and phosphate homeostasis. Roughly 5–10 percent of the adult skeleton is replaced each year.

The remodelling cycle

Remodelling occurs in discrete packets called basic multicellular units. The cycle proceeds through activation, resorption by multinucleated osteoclasts (lasting a few weeks), a reversal phase, and formation by osteoblasts, which deposit osteoid that subsequently mineralizes over several months. Osteocytes — former osteoblasts embedded in the matrix — sense mechanical strain and microdamage and direct the process.

Regulation

The RANK/RANKL/osteoprotegerin signalling pathway controls osteoclast formation. Parathyroid hormone, calcitriol (vitamin D), calcitonin, oestrogen, and growth factors modulate the balance between resorption and formation. Mechanical loading stimulates formation, while disuse accelerates loss.

Clinical significance

An imbalance favouring resorption causes osteoporosis; excessive or disordered remodelling underlies Paget's disease of bone. Many drugs act on this cycle: bisphosphonates and denosumab suppress resorption, while teriparatide stimulates formation.
