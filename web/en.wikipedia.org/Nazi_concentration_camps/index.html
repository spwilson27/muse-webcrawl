Nazi concentration camps

From 1933 to 1945, Nazi Germany operated more than a thousand concentration camps (Konzentrationslager, abbreviated KZ or KL) across German-occupied Europe. The camps were used to imprison and exploit political opponents, Jews, Roma, homosexuals, Jehovah's Witnesses, Poles, Soviet prisoners of war and many others. Millions of people died in the camp system from execution, starvation, disease, forced labour and medical experiments.

History

The first camp, Dachau, opened in March 1933, weeks after Adolf Hitler came to power, initially to hold political prisoners. Under Heinrich Himmler and the SS, the system was consolidated under the Inspectorate of Concentration Camps, headed by Theodor Eicke, whose Dachau "model" — brutal discipline, forced labour and total SS control — became the template. Major pre-war camps included Sachsenhausen (1936), Buchenwald (1937), Flossenbürg and Mauthausen (1938) and Ravensbrück for women (1939).

Wartime expansion and extermination

During the Second World War the system expanded enormously. Auschwitz, established in 1940 in occupied Poland, became a combined concentration and extermination camp where about 1.1 million people, most of them Jews, were murdered. The separate extermination camps of Operation Reinhard — Bełżec, Sobibór and Treblinka — together with Chełmno and Majdanek, were built primarily for mass murder as part of the Holocaust, the genocide of six million Jews. Prisoners elsewhere were worked to death under the policy of "extermination through labour" (Vernichtung durch Arbeit) for SS enterprises and German industry.

Liberation and aftermath

As Allied forces advanced in 1945, the SS evacuated camps on forced "death marches" that killed tens of thousands. Surviving camps were liberated by Soviet, American, British and French forces, and the conditions found were documented for the Nuremberg trials. Many former camps are now memorials and museums. Historians, drawing on research such as the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum's encyclopedia project, have identified more than 42,500 camps and ghettos across Europe.
