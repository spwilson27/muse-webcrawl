Potential output

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Actual output fluctuates around potential output over the business cycle.

Potential output (also called potential gross domestic product, natural GDP, or full-employment output) is the level of real GDP an economy can produce when its labour and capital are fully employed at a sustainable, non-inflationary rate. It is not directly observable and must be estimated. The difference between actual output and potential output, expressed as a percentage of potential, is the output gap: a negative gap indicates slack in the economy, while a positive gap suggests overheating and inflationary pressure.

Potential output

Field: Macroeconomics

Related measure: Output gap

Estimated by: IMF, OECD, CBO, central banks

Determinants and estimation

Potential output depends on the quantity and quality of the labour force, the capital stock, and total factor productivity. Long-run growth of potential output is therefore driven by population and labour-force growth, investment, education, and technological progress. Estimators use two main approaches: statistical filters that extract a trend from GDP data (such as the Hodrick–Prescott filter) and production-function approaches, used for example by the IMF, OECD, and the US Congressional Budget Office, which model output as a function of labour, capital, and productivity, with labour constrained by the NAIRU — the unemployment rate consistent with stable inflation.

Policy significance

Central banks use estimates of the output gap when setting interest rates, and finance ministries use potential output to calculate cyclically adjusted (structural) budget balances, which determine how much of a deficit is due to the cycle rather than policy. Estimates are uncertain and are frequently revised substantially — a well-known problem in real time, since misjudging potential can lead policy to be too loose or too tight. After the 2008 financial crisis, many institutions revised potential output downward, suggesting the crisis had permanently damaged economies' productive capacity, a phenomenon related to the idea of hysteresis.
