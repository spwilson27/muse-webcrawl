Walkability

Walkability is a concept in urban planning and design describing how friendly an area is to walking — that is, the degree to which the built environment supports safe, convenient, and pleasant pedestrian travel. Walkable places typically combine mixed land uses, connected street networks, density, and safe, comfortable infrastructure, reducing reliance on cars.

Factors

Researchers and practitioners commonly cite factors including: street connectivity (short blocks, many intersections, few cul-de-sacs); land-use mix, so homes, shops, schools, and workplaces are close together; footpath (sidewalk) presence, width, and condition; safety from traffic and crime; shade, lighting, seating, and other comforts; and "eyes on the street", in the phrase of Jane Jacobs, whose 1961 book The Death and Life of Great American Cities is foundational to the field. Urban designer Jeff Speck summarised walkability as requiring that a walk be useful, safe, comfortable, and interesting.

Measurement

Tools such as Walk Score assign addresses a 0–100 rating based on distance to amenities; academic indices combine connectivity, density, and land-use data in GIS. The "15-minute city" concept popularised after 2020 is a related policy goal: daily necessities reachable within a quarter-hour walk or cycle ride.

Benefits and policy

Walkability is associated with better public health (more physical activity, lower obesity), lower transport emissions, higher retail footfall, and increased property values — and in some cities, gentrification pressures. Policies to increase it include zoning reform to allow mixed uses and smaller lots, complete-streets design, traffic calming, and pedestrianisation.
