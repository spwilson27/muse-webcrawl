Auguste Comte

Isidore Auguste Marie François Xavier Comte (19 January 1798 – 5 September 1857) was a French philosopher, mathematician and writer who formulated the doctrine of positivism. He is often regarded as the first philosopher of science in the modern sense of the term, and he coined the word sociology, becoming a founder of the discipline.

Auguste Comte, founder of positivism and sociology.

Life

Born in Montpellier, Comte studied at the École Polytechnique in Paris, from which he was expelled for rebelliousness. He worked as secretary to the social theorist Henri de Saint-Simon, whose ideas strongly influenced him, before their rupture in 1824. From 1830 to 1842 he published the six volumes of the Course of Positive Philosophy, teaching privately after repeated bouts of mental illness and the death of his great love, Clotilde de Vaux, in 1846.

Law of three stages

Comte held that human knowledge in every field passes through three stages: the theological, in which phenomena are explained by gods and spirits; the metaphysical, explained by abstract essences; and the positive or scientific stage, which confines itself to observed relations of succession and resemblance. He arranged the sciences in a hierarchy — mathematics, astronomy, physics, chemistry, biology, and finally sociology — according to their generality and dependence on one another.

Religion of Humanity

In his later work, the System of Positive Polity (1851–1854), Comte proposed a "Religion of Humanity", complete with priests, temples and a positivist calendar of secular saints. The project influenced republican movements in France and Brazil — whose flag still bears Comte's motto "Order and Progress" — and attracted followers such as John Stuart Mill, who later distanced himself from Comte's authoritarian prescriptions.
