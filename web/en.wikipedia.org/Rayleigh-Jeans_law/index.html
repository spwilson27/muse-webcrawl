Rayleigh–Jeans law

The Rayleigh–Jeans law is a classical approximation to the spectral radiance of black-body radiation as a function of wavelength: Bλ(T) = 2ckT/λ4, where k is Boltzmann's constant and T the absolute temperature. Lord Rayleigh first published the λ−4 dependence in 1900, and James Jeans supplied the correct numerical factor by 1905.

The ultraviolet catastrophe

Derived from classical equipartition of energy among the standing-wave modes of a cavity, the law agrees with experiment at long wavelengths but predicts that radiated energy grows without bound at short wavelengths — implying every warm body should emit infinite ultraviolet and X-ray energy. Paul Ehrenfest dubbed this failure the "ultraviolet catastrophe".

Resolution

Max Planck's law (1900), which quantises the energy of the oscillators in units of hν, reduces to the Rayleigh–Jeans law in the long-wavelength limit while matching the full black-body spectrum. The Rayleigh–Jeans law remains useful in radio astronomy, where observations are made far into its valid regime.
