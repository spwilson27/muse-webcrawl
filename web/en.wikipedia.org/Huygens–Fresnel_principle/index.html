Huygens–Fresnel principle

Huygens–Fresnel principle
Named after: Christiaan Huygens and Augustin-Jean Fresnel
Field: Wave optics
Key idea: Every point on a wavefront is a source of secondary spherical wavelets
Later formalised by: Kirchhoff's diffraction formula

The Huygens–Fresnel principle is a method of analysis applied to problems of wave propagation, stating that every point on a wavefront is itself the source of spherical wavelets, and that the secondary wavelets mutually interfere to form the subsequent wavefront. The principle is named for Dutch physicist Christiaan Huygens and French physicist Augustin-Jean Fresnel.

History

Huygens proposed the wavelet construction in his Treatise on Light (1690) to explain rectilinear propagation, reflection and refraction, but his theory lacked interference and could not explain diffraction quantitatively. Fresnel, in work from 1815 to 1819, combined Huygens's construction with the principle of interference, successfully explaining straight-line propagation and diffraction patterns, including the famous bright spot at the centre of a circular obstacle's shadow (the Poisson/Arago spot).

Statement

At any later time, the envelope of the secondary wavelets gives the new wavefront. Amplitudes are summed with attention to phase, so regions of constructive interference build the propagated wave while destructive interference cancels backward propagation. Fresnel introduced an obliquity (inclination) factor to suppress the backward wave.

Applications and refinements

The principle underlies Fresnel diffraction theory, zone plates and much of Fourier optics. Kirchhoff later derived the construction from the wave equation, and quantum electrodynamics generalises the idea in Feynman's path integral formulation, in which waves explore all paths with phase-weighted amplitudes.
