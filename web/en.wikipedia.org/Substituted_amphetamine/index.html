Substituted amphetamine

The substituted amphetamines are a class of compounds based upon the amphetamine structure, sharing a phenethylamine core with an alpha-methyl group. Chemical modifications at various positions produce drugs with stimulant, empathogenic, hallucinogenic, or appetite-suppressant effects.

Substituted amphetamines
Class: Stimulants and related agents
Core: Amphetamine (α-methylphenethylamine)
Examples: Methamphetamine, MDMA, dextroamphetamine

Pharmacology

Most substituted amphetamines act by increasing the release and blocking the reuptake of monoamine neurotransmitters—dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin—though the balance differs by compound. Ring-substituted derivatives such as MDMA act primarily on serotonin, while others are mainly dopaminergic stimulants.

Medical and other uses

Dextroamphetamine and mixed amphetamine salts are prescribed for ADHD and narcolepsy; other members have been used as decongestants and appetite suppressants. Many, including methamphetamine and numerous designer variants, are controlled substances because of their abuse potential.

Structure–activity relationships

Small changes—halogenation, methylation, or methylenedioxy substitution—can shift a compound's potency and subjective effects markedly, a fact exploited by both pharmaceutical research and clandestine chemists.
