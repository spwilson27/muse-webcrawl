Belgian waffle

The Belgian waffle is a variety of waffle distinguished by a larger size, lighter batter, and deeper pockets than the standard North American waffle, cooked in a rectangular or round waffle iron with a pronounced grid pattern. In Belgium itself there is no single "Belgian waffle": the best-known native types are the Brussels waffle (gaufre de Bruxelles) and the Liège waffle (gaufre de Liège), along with regional variants such as the stroopwafel-adjacent Flemish waffles.

Types in Belgium

The Brussels waffle is made from a yeasted batter or batter leavened with beaten egg whites, producing a light, crisp, rectangular waffle, usually eaten with a dusting of powdered sugar and sometimes whipped cream, chocolate, or fruit. The Liège waffle is smaller, denser, and sweeter, adapted from brioche bread dough and studded with pearl sugar that caramelizes on the iron; it is commonly eaten plain and by hand as street food.

In North America

The "Belgian waffle" was popularized in North America by Maurice Vermersch of Brussels, who sold his wife's recipe at the 1964–65 New York World's Fair in Flushing, Queens, under the name "Bel-Gem waffle," served with whipped cream and strawberries. Vermersch had earlier introduced the waffle at Expo 58 in Brussels. North American versions are typically leavened with baking powder rather than yeast, served at breakfast with butter and maple syrup, and also used as a dessert or as the base for chicken and waffles.
