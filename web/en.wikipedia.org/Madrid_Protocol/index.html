Madrid Protocol

The Madrid Protocol, formally the Protocol Relating to the Madrid Agreement Concerning the International Registration of Marks (1989), is a treaty administered by the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) that allows trademark owners to seek protection in many countries through a single international application. Together with the older Madrid Agreement (1891), it forms the Madrid System for the international registration of trademarks.

The Madrid System is administered by WIPO in Geneva, Switzerland.

How it works

An applicant with a trademark application or registration (the "basic mark") in a member country files one international application through their home trademark office, designating the member countries where protection is sought, and pays a single set of fees in Swiss francs. WIPO's International Bureau checks formalities and publishes the international registration. Each designated office then examines the mark under its own law, generally within 12–18 months, and may refuse protection in whole or in part. Once granted, the international registration is managed centrally: renewals (every ten years), changes of ownership, and address changes are recorded with a single filing.

Key features and differences from the Agreement

The Protocol introduced flexibilities that attracted major trading nations: applications may be based on a pending application rather than only a registration; the refusal period may extend to 18 months; fees may be set by individual offices; and a failed international registration can be "transformed" into national applications retaining its date. A notable dependency rule applies: for five years the international registration depends on the basic mark, so if the basic application fails or is cancelled ("central attack"), the international registration falls too — though transformation can preserve rights. The United States joined in 2003, the European Union in 2004, and Canada in 2019.

Advantages and limitations

The system cuts cost and paperwork for brand owners filing abroad, but applicants must still respond to local refusals, often through local agents, and protection remains territorial in substance. Businesses with interests in few countries sometimes find direct national filing equally efficient.
