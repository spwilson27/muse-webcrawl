Sacrilege

Sacrilege is the violation or injurious treatment of a sacred object, place, person or practice. In general terms it describes any act of disrespect toward what a religion holds holy — for example stealing consecrated vessels, damaging a place of worship, misusing a sacrament, or physically assaulting clergy. The word derives from the Latin sacrilegium, from sacer (sacred) and legere (to gather or steal): originally, the theft of sacred things.

In Roman and canon law

Roman law treated sacrilege as a serious crime against the state religion. In Catholic canon law, sacrilege is classified by what it violates: personal sacrilege (against sacred persons), local sacrilege (against sacred places such as churches and cemeteries), and real sacrilege (against sacred things, including the Eucharist and sacred vessels and images). Desecration of the consecrated Eucharistic species is among the gravest offences in canon law, incurring automatic excommunication reserved to the Holy See.

In other traditions

Most religions have analogous concepts. In Islam, desecration of the Quran or mockery of the Prophet is treated as a profound offence; in Sikhism the desecration of the Guru Granth Sahib has provoked major protests; Jewish law strongly protects Torah scrolls, synagogues and burial grounds; and in ancient Greece, crimes of impiety (asebeia) — such as the mutilation of the Herms in Athens in 415 BC or the profanation of the Eleusinian Mysteries — were prosecuted capitally.

Modern usage

In secular legal systems, sacrilege as such has largely disappeared as a crime, replaced by laws against theft, criminal damage, desecration of graves and religiously aggravated offences; blasphemy laws, where they survive, are distinct though related. In colloquial English, "sacrilege" is often used figuratively or humorously for any violation of a cherished custom — "it would be sacrilege to put ice in this wine."
