Disturbance (ecology)

In ecology, a disturbance is a temporary change in environmental conditions that causes a pronounced change in an ecosystem. Disturbances often act quickly and with great effect, altering the physical structure or arrangement of biotic and abiotic elements. A disturbance can also occur over a long period of time and can influence the biodiversity within an ecosystem.

Types of disturbance

Major ecological disturbances include fires, flooding, storms, insect outbreaks and deforestation. They may be natural in origin — part of the normal dynamics of ecosystems such as fire-adapted savannas and floodplain forests — or anthropogenic, including logging, ploughing, mining, dam construction and the introduction of invasive species. Disturbances are described by their frequency, intensity, severity, spatial extent and return interval, together forming a disturbance regime.

Ecological effects

Disturbance alters resource availability, substrate and the physical environment, killing or displacing some organisms and creating opportunities for others. The sequence of recolonization and community change that follows is ecological succession: pioneer species exploit the opened space, followed by later-successional species. Many species are adapted to particular disturbance regimes; for example, the seeds of some pines germinate only after the heat of a fire, and lodgepole pine forests regenerate as even-aged stands after stand-replacing burns.

Intermediate disturbance hypothesis

Joseph Connell's intermediate disturbance hypothesis (1978) proposes that local species diversity is highest at intermediate levels of disturbance: at low disturbance, competitive dominants exclude other species, while at high disturbance only a few stress-tolerant species survive. The hypothesis has been influential but is debated, with reviews finding mixed support across systems.

Disturbance and management

Suppressing natural disturbances can itself be harmful: decades of fire exclusion in western North American forests allowed fuel to accumulate, contributing to unusually severe fires. Restoration ecology and modern land management therefore sometimes reintroduce disturbance through prescribed burning, managed flooding or grazing, while climate change is shifting disturbance regimes worldwide — altering fire seasons, storm intensities and pest outbreaks.
