Dahomey Amazons

The Dahomey Amazons (Fon: Mino, "our mothers") were a Fon all-female military regiment of the Kingdom of Dahomey, in present-day Benin, which existed from the 17th century until the kingdom's defeat by France in 1894. European observers named them after the Amazons of Greek legend. They were among the very few documented all-female combat units in modern history, and formed a substantial part of Dahomey's army, prized for their discipline and ferocity.

Origins and recruitment

Tradition traces the corps to elephant-hunter units or palace guards under King Houegbadja (r. 1645–1685); it was formalised under Queen Hangbè in the early 18th century and greatly expanded by King Ghezo (r. 1818–1858). Recruits were volunteers, women offered by their families, captives, and wives of the king; they took vows of celibacy and were considered married to the king.

Training and warfare

Training was deliberately brutal, including storming thorn barricades barefoot and exercises in insensitivity to pain. The women fought with muskets, machetes, and clubs, organised in companies with their own officers, and campaigned in Dahomey's wars against the Oyo Empire, the Egbado, and neighbouring peoples, often in connection with the slave trade. They fought French colonial forces in 1890 and 1892–94 before the regiment was dissolved.

Legacy

The Mino inspired later depictions of African warrior women, including the Dora Milaje in Marvel's Black Panther comics and films, and the 2022 historical film The Woman King, centred on a fictionalised general of the corps. In Benin they are remembered as symbols of female strength and national history.
