Home movies

A home movie is a short amateur film or video typically made just to preserve a visual record of family activities, a vacation, or a special event, and intended for viewing at home by family and friends. Originally, home movies were made on photographic film in formats that reduced the cost of film stock compared to 35 mm stock used in professional productions. In the 1970s and 1980s, video formats such as VHS and Video8 became popular, and later digital formats on camcorders, smartphones, and other devices largely replaced film.

History

The 9.5 mm format was introduced by Pathé in 1922 as part of the Pathé Baby system. Kodak introduced 16 mm film in 1923 as a less expensive amateur alternative to 35 mm, and 8 mm film followed in 1932, halving costs again. Double 8 was run through the camera twice, exposing one side at a time, and slit after processing. Super 8, introduced in 1965, made loading easier with cartridges and became the dominant home-movie format until home video took over.

Preservation and cultural value

Home movies have become valued historical documents, recording everyday life, landscapes, and customs that professional media often ignored. Archives and events such as Home Movie Day, founded in 2002, encourage the preservation and screening of amateur films. Historians and documentarians frequently draw on home movies for footage of ordinary life and of notable events — the Zapruder film of the assassination of John F. Kennedy is the most famous example of a home movie with major historical significance.
