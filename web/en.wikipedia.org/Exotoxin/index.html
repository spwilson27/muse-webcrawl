Exotoxin

An exotoxin is a toxin secreted by bacteria into their surrounding environment. Exotoxins are proteins produced inside the bacterial cell and released, either during growth or upon lysis; they are among the most potent poisons known and can damage the host far from the site of infection. They contrast with endotoxins, which are components of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria released mainly when the cells disintegrate.

Types and examples

  • Neurotoxins — tetanus toxin and botulinum toxin from Clostridium species act on the nervous system; botulinum toxin is lethal in nanogram quantities.
  • Enterotoxins — cholera toxin and the heat-stable enterotoxin of E. coli act on the intestinal lining, causing profuse watery diarrhea.
  • Cytotoxins — diphtheria toxin halts protein synthesis in host cells; many exotoxins share an A-B structure, in which a binding (B) subunit delivers an enzymatically active (A) subunit into the cell.
  • Superantigens — such as toxic shock syndrome toxin-1 of Staphylococcus aureus, overstimulate the immune system by cross-linking receptors on T cells.

Medical significance

Because exotoxins are proteins, they are strongly immunogenic and can be chemically inactivated with formaldehyde to form toxoids, which retain their immunogenicity and are used as vaccines — the diphtheria and tetanus components of DTaP/Td vaccines are toxoids. Antitoxins (antibodies against a toxin) provide passive treatment for diseases such as diphtheria and botulism. Exotoxins are generally heat-labile, unlike the heat-stable endotoxins.
