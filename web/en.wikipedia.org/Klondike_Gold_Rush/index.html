Klondike Gold Rush

The Klondike Gold Rush was a migration by an estimated 100,000 prospectors to the Klondike region of Yukon, in north-western Canada, between 1896 and 1899. Gold was discovered there by local miners on August 16, 1896; when news reached Seattle and San Francisco the following year, it triggered a stampede of would-be prospectors. Some became wealthy, but the majority went in vain.

Discovery

On August 16, 1896, George Carmack, Skookum Jim Mason (Keish), and Dawson Charlie (K̲áa Goox̱) found gold on Rabbit Creek, soon renamed Bonanza Creek, a tributary of the Klondike River. The creek claims were quickly staked. News travelled slowly; the rush began only when ships docked at Seattle and San Francisco in July 1897 carrying miners and, in the press's phrase, "a ton of gold."

The stampede

Stampeders travelled mainly through the Alaskan ports of Dyea and Skagway, then over the Chilkoot or White Pass trails into Canada. The North-West Mounted Police famously required each traveller to carry a year's supplies — roughly a ton of goods — to prevent starvation. Dawson City boomed at the confluence of the Klondike and Yukon rivers, reaching a population of some 30,000 by mid-1898. Figures such as "Soapy" Smith in Skagway and writer Jack London, who joined the rush, became part of its lore.

Decline

By 1899 the easily worked claims were exhausted and gold was discovered at Nome, Alaska, drawing stampeders away. The Klondike rush faded, though dredge mining continued for decades and the region is still mined today.

Legacy

The rush is commemorated in Canada and the United States by Klondike Gold Rush national historical parks, by Charlie Chaplin's film The Gold Rush (1925), and by the works of Jack London and Robert W. Service. Indigenous peoples of the region, particularly the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in, suffered displacement and disease, a history recognised in the 2023 UNESCO World Heritage listing of Tr'ondëk-Klondike.
