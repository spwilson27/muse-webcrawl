Judaea (Roman province)

Judaea (Latin: Iudaea)
Roman province established: AD 6
Capital: Caesarea Maritima
Preceded by: Herodian kingdom
Succeeded by: Syria Palaestina (after AD 135)

Judaea was a Roman province encompassing the regions of Judea, Samaria, and Idumaea in the southern Levant. It was established in AD 6, when the emperor Augustus deposed Herod Archelaus and placed his territory under direct Roman administration, initially as part of the province of Syria and governed by prefects of equestrian rank based at Caesarea Maritima.

History

The census conducted by Quirinius in AD 6, associated with the province's creation, provoked unrest led by Judas the Galilean. Judaea was governed by a succession of prefects, the most famous of whom was Pontius Pilate (r. 26–36), under whom Jesus of Nazareth was crucified. From 41 to 44 the province was briefly restored to a client kingdom under Herod Agrippa I, before reverting to procuratorial rule.

Roman misgovernment and rising tensions culminated in the First Jewish–Roman War (66–73/74), during which Jerusalem and the Second Temple were destroyed by Titus in AD 70; the last rebel stronghold at Masada fell in 73 or 74. After the war, Judaea became a separate senatorial province governed by a legate, and the Legio X Fretensis was stationed in Jerusalem.

Later history

The province saw further revolts, including the Kitos War (115–117) and the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–136). After crushing the latter, Hadrian rebuilt Jerusalem as the pagan colony Aelia Capitolina and, around AD 135, merged Judaea into the renamed province of Syria Palaestina, a designation that reflected the region's older Philistine associations and effaced its Jewish name.
