Lineweaver–Burk plot

In biochemistry, the Lineweaver–Burk plot (or double reciprocal plot) is a graphical representation of the Lineweaver–Burk equation of enzyme kinetics, described by Hans Lineweaver and Dean Burk in 1934. It is a double-reciprocal plot of the Michaelis–Menten equation:

1/v = (Km/Vmax)(1/[S]) + 1/Vmax

where v is the reaction velocity, Km is the Michaelis constant, Vmax is the maximum reaction velocity, and [S] is the substrate concentration.

Use

Plotting 1/v against 1/[S] yields a straight line whose y-intercept equals 1/Vmax, whose x-intercept equals −1/Km, and whose slope equals Km/Vmax. Before computer curve-fitting became routine, the Lineweaver–Burk plot was widely used to estimate Km and Vmax from kinetic data, because the parameters can be read off a straight-line fit. It is also useful for distinguishing types of enzyme inhibition: competitive, non-competitive and uncompetitive inhibition produce characteristic patterns of intersecting lines.

Criticism

The plot distorts the experimental error: taking reciprocals gives disproportionate weight to the least accurate measurements (those at low substrate concentration), so parameter estimates from double-reciprocal plots are unreliable. Modern practice prefers non-linear regression directly on the Michaelis–Menten equation, or alternative linearizations such as the Hanes–Woolf and Eadie–Hofstee plots, which distribute error more evenly.
