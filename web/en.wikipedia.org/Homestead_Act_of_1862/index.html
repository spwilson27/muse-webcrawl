Homestead Act of 1862

The Homestead Act of 1862 was a United States federal law signed by President Abraham Lincoln on May 20, 1862. It granted adult citizens (or intended citizens) who had never borne arms against the United States up to 160 acres (65 hectares) of public land, provided they lived on it, built a dwelling, and cultivated it for a term of five years. After five years, or sooner if the settler paid $1.25 per acre, the settler could file for a deed of title.

Background

Land homesteading had been a goal of the Free Soil movement and the young Republican Party, but Southern congressmen repeatedly blocked it. Secession during the Civil War removed that opposition and allowed passage. Earlier bills had been vetoed by President James Buchanan in 1860.

Effects

By 1934, over 1.6 million successful homestead applications had been processed and more than 270 million acres — about 10% of all U.S. lands — were transferred to individuals. The last claim under the act was made in Alaska in 1988. The act is often credited with accelerating settlement of the West, though it also displaced Native Americans from their lands and was subject to extensive fraud, with speculators and companies acquiring land through dummy claimants. Women and formerly enslaved people were eligible, and single women filed many claims in their own names.
