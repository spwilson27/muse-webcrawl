Tetrarchy

The Tetrarchy (from Greek tetrarchia, "rule of four") was the system of government instituted by the Roman emperor Diocletian in 293 AD to restore order after the Crisis of the Third Century. The empire was divided between two senior emperors, the Augusti, each assisted by a junior emperor and designated successor, the Caesares. Diocletian ruled the East from Nicomedia and Maximian the West from Mediolanum (Milan); their Caesars were Galerius and Constantius Chlorus respectively.

Organisation

The arrangement was not a formal partition of the empire into separate states — legislation was issued in the names of all four rulers, and the empire remained legally indivisible — but a practical division of military and administrative responsibility, allowing an imperial presence near threatened frontiers on the Rhine, Danube, and in the East. Each ruler maintained his own court and capital, and the system was reinforced by dynastic marriages. Diocletian also undertook sweeping administrative, military, and economic reforms, including a division of provinces and the Edict on Maximum Prices.

Collapse

In 305 Diocletian and Maximian abdicated on schedule, the only voluntary abdication in Roman imperial history, and the Caesars succeeded them. The system quickly broke down: the exclusion of Constantine, son of Constantius, and of Maxentius, son of Maximian, provoked civil wars from 306. The Conference of Carnuntum (308) failed to restore stability. Constantine's victory at the Milvian Bridge in 312 and his defeat of Licinius in 324 reunited the empire under a single ruler, ending the tetrarchic experiment.

Legacy

Although the tetrarchy itself lasted barely a generation, its administrative framework — smaller provinces, separated civil and military offices, and new imperial capitals — shaped the late Roman and early Byzantine state.
