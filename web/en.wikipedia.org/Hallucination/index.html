Hallucination

A hallucination is a perception in the absence of an external stimulus that has the qualities of a real perception. Hallucinations are vivid, substantial, and are perceived to be located in external objective space. They are distinguishable from illusions (misperceptions of real stimuli), dreams, and vivid imagery. Hallucinations can occur in any sensory modality — auditory (hearing voices), visual, olfactory, gustatory, tactile (somesthetic), proprioceptive and others.

Causes

Hallucinations are associated with psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia (in which auditory hallucinations, especially voices commenting or conversing, are common), bipolar disorder and major depression with psychotic features. They also arise from neurological conditions (Parkinson's disease, Lewy body dementia, epilepsy, migraine, Charles Bonnet syndrome following vision loss), from sleep phenomena (hypnagogic and hypnopompic hallucinations), sensory deprivation, fever and delirium, bereavement, and from psychoactive substances including psychedelics, stimulants and alcohol withdrawal (delirium tremens).

Prevalence

Hallucinations are not always a sign of illness. Surveys suggest that a substantial minority of the general population — estimates range from about 5% to 15% over a lifetime — experience hallucinations, most often in sleep-wake transitions, without any disorder.

Assessment and treatment

Clinical assessment seeks the underlying cause: psychiatric evaluation, medication and substance review, and neurological examination or imaging where indicated. Treatment is directed at the cause; antipsychotic medication is used for hallucinations in psychotic disorders, while hallucinations due to medical illness require treatment of the underlying condition. Psychological approaches, including cognitive behavioural therapy for psychosis, help some people cope with distressing voices.
