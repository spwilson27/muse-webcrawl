Tide gauge

A tide gauge (also called a mareograph, marigraph, sea-level recorder, or tide recorder) is a device for measuring the change in sea level relative to a vertical datum at a specific location. Networks of tide gauges around the world's coasts provide the longest instrumental records of sea-level change and are essential for tidal prediction, navigation and chart datum determination, coastal engineering, storm surge and tsunami warning, and monitoring long-term sea level rise due to climate change.

Types

Historically, sea level was read by eye from a graduated staff (a tide staff). Self-recording gauges developed in the 19th century typically used a float in a stilling well — a vertical pipe that damps wave action — connected by wire to a pen tracing on a rotating chart drum; the float gauge at a station could operate for many decades and produced the classic long records. Modern gauges more often use:

  • Pressure gauges, which infer water depth from hydrostatic pressure, including bubbler systems and sub-sea transducers.
  • Acoustic gauges, which time the echo of a sound pulse reflected from the water surface down a sounding tube.
  • Radar gauges, which measure the distance to the sea surface from above with microwave pulses and require no contact with the water.

Records and sea-level change

Some gauges have operated since the 18th and 19th centuries — for example at Stockholm (1774), Brest, Liverpool, and Sheerness — and the Permanent Service for Mean Sea Level (PSMSL, founded 1933) archives monthly and annual mean sea level data from over 2,000 stations. Interpretation of tide-gauge records requires correcting for vertical land motion (from glacial isostatic adjustment, subsidence, or tectonics), increasingly measured by co-located GNSS receivers. Tide-gauge analyses indicate global mean sea level rose at roughly 1.4–1.9 mm per year over the 20th century, with satellite altimetry (since 1993) showing acceleration to over 3 mm per year. Global networks such as GLOSS (Global Sea Level Observing System) coordinate standards and data sharing among some 290 stations.
