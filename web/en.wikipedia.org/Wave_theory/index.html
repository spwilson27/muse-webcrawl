Wave theory

Wave theory is the branch of physics that treats phenomena — such as light, sound, and water motion — as propagating waves: disturbances that transfer energy through a medium or space without the net transport of matter. The theory provides the mathematical framework of oscillations, superposition, interference, diffraction, and dispersion that is applied across optics, acoustics, electromagnetism, and quantum mechanics.

Historical development

Robert Hooke and Christiaan Huygens argued in the 17th century that light behaves as a wave, with Huygens' principle (1678) describing how each point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets. Isaac Newton's competing corpuscular theory dominated through the 18th century, until Thomas Young's double-slit experiment (1801) and Augustin-Jean Fresnel's mathematical theory of diffraction demonstrated interference effects that only a wave model could explain. James Clerk Maxwell's equations (1865) then showed light to be an electromagnetic wave, and Heinrich Hertz confirmed electromagnetic waves experimentally in 1887.

Core principles

The classical wave equation relates the curvature of a disturbance to its acceleration, giving a characteristic propagation speed. Because the equation is linear for many systems, waves superpose: the total disturbance is the sum of the parts. This linearity explains interference patterns, standing waves, beats, and Fourier synthesis. Wave theory also predicts the Doppler effect, reflection and refraction at boundaries, and polarization in transverse waves.

Beyond classical theory

In the 20th century, quantum mechanics extended wave theory into new territory: particles show wave-like interference, and their states are described by a wave function whose evolution follows Schrödinger's wave equation. The duality of wave and particle descriptions remains a cornerstone of modern physics.
