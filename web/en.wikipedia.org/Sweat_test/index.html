Sweat test

The sweat test measures chloride concentration in sweat.

The sweat test measures the concentration of chloride found in sweat. It is used to screen for and diagnose cystic fibrosis (CF). The most commonly used method is the quantitative pilocarpine iontophoresis test, described by Gibson and Cooke in 1959.

Procedure

A small electric current is used to deliver the drug pilocarpine to a patch of skin, usually on the arm or leg, stimulating local sweating. The sweat is collected on filter paper, gauze, or in a coiled tube for about 30 minutes, and its chloride concentration is then measured in a laboratory.

Interpretation

In people with cystic fibrosis, defective CFTR chloride channels cause sweat to contain abnormally high levels of salt. A chloride concentration of 60 mmol/L or higher is generally considered diagnostic of CF, values of 30–59 mmol/L are intermediate, and lower values make CF unlikely. Because newborn screening can miss some cases, the sweat test remains the confirmatory "gold standard" test.
