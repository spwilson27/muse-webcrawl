Alphabet

An alphabet is a standard set of written symbols that represent the phonemes (distinct speech sounds) of a language. Each symbol, called a letter, typically represents a consonant or a vowel, or a combination thereof. The word alphabet comes from the first two letters of the Greek alphabet, alpha and beta.

History

The first fully phonemic script, the Proto-Canaanite script, later known as the Phoenician alphabet, is considered to be the first alphabet and is the ancestor of most modern alphabets, including Arabic, Cyrillic, Greek, Hebrew, and Latin. It emerged in the Levant around the 11th century BCE from earlier Proto-Sinaitic signs derived in part from Egyptian hieroglyphs. The Greeks adapted the Phoenician script around the 8th century BCE, introducing independent letters for vowels—an innovation that defines the “true alphabet” in many classifications.

Types of writing systems

Alphabets contrast with abjads, in which consonants are primary and vowels are optional or marked by diacritics (as in Arabic and Hebrew); abugidas, in which consonant letters carry inherent vowels modified by marks (as in Devanagari); syllabaries, in which symbols represent syllables (as in Japanese kana); and logographic systems, in which symbols represent words or morphemes (as in Chinese characters).

Largest and smallest alphabets

The Latin alphabet is the most widely used in the world. Among alphabets proper, Rotokas of Papua New Guinea has one of the smallest inventories with 12 letters, while Khmer has one of the largest, with 74 letters including subscripts.
