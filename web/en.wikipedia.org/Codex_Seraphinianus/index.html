The Codex Seraphinianus, originally published in 1981, is an illustrated encyclopedia of an imaginary world, created by the Italian artist, architect and industrial designer Luigi Serafini between 1976 and 1978. It is written in an invented, undeciphered script and filled with hand-drawn, coloured-pencil images of surreal flora, fauna, machines, foods and customs.

Structure

The book is divided into eleven chapters treating the natural world, physics and chemistry, biology, machines, the human body and medicine, history and customs, food and clothing, games, linguistics, and architecture. The illustrations are bizarre and often humorous: bleeding fruit, a couple who metamorphose into a crocodile, fish shaped like eyes, and hybrid machines. Its name makes it a modern, deliberately mysterious counterpart to ancient manuscripts such as the Codex Argenteus.

The script

The writing system has resisted decipherment. Serafini has said the script is asemic — having no specific meaning — and that the experience of reading the Codex should resemble that of a child who cannot yet read: the words look like sentences but convey sense only through the pictures. Analysts have nonetheless found structure in its page-numbering system, which appears to be based on a variant of base 21.

Reception

Initially issued in a limited luxury edition by publisher Franco Maria Ricci, the Codex became a cult work of outsider and surrealist art, compared with the Voynich manuscript and the works of M. C. Escher and Hieronymus Bosch. Later editions, including a 2013 Rizzoli edition with additional material by Serafini, brought it to a wider audience.
