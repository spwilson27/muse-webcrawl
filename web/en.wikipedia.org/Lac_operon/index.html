lac operon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The lac operon (lactose operon) is an operon required for the transport and metabolism of lactose in the bacterium Escherichia coli and many other enteric bacteria. It was the first genetic regulatory mechanism to be understood clearly, and became the paradigmatic example of prokaryotic gene regulation, through the work of François Jacob and Jacques Monod, who shared the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Structure

The operon contains three structural genes: lacZ, encoding β-galactosidase, which cleaves lactose into glucose and galactose; lacY, encoding β-galactoside permease, which transports lactose into the cell; and lacA, encoding β-galactoside transacetylase. Upstream lie the promoter, the operator, and the regulatory gene lacI, which encodes the lac repressor.

Regulation

In the absence of lactose, the repressor binds the operator and blocks transcription. When lactose is present, a small amount is converted to allolactose, which binds the repressor and causes it to release the operator, inducing the operon. A second layer of control, catabolite repression, acts through the CAP–cAMP complex: when glucose is abundant, cAMP levels fall, CAP does not bind, and the operon is expressed only weakly. The bacterium thus preferentially uses glucose, exhibiting diauxic growth.
