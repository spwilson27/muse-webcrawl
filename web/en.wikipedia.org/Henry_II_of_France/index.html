Henry II of France

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Henry II (French: Henri II; 31 March 1519 – 10 July 1559) was King of France from 1547 until his death in 1559. The second son of Francis I and Duchess Claude of Brittany, he pursued his father's wars against the Habsburgs and his father's patronage of the arts, while persecuting Protestants with increasing severity. His reign ended abruptly when he was mortally wounded in a jousting accident.

Early life

In 1526, under the Treaty of Madrid, the eight-year-old Henry and his elder brother Francis were sent to Spain as hostages for their father, who had been captured at the Battle of Pavia. The boys spent more than four years in harsh confinement, an experience said to have marked Henry's character for life. Released in 1530, he married Catherine de' Medici in 1533, at the age of fourteen; although the marriage eventually produced ten children, Henry's affections centred on his mistress Diane de Poitiers, twenty years his senior, who exercised great influence at court. He became heir on his brother's death in 1536.

Reign

Crowned in 1547, Henry continued the struggle with Emperor Charles V and his son Philip II of Spain. French forces seized the Three Bishoprics of Metz, Toul, and Verdun in 1552 and defeated the Imperial army at Renty (1554); after setbacks including the disaster at Saint-Quentin (1557), France recovered by capturing Calais — England's last possession on the continent — in January 1558. The Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis (1559) ended the Italian Wars, with France retaining Calais and the Three Bishoprics while renouncing most claims in Italy.

Domestically, Henry intensified the repression of Protestantism, establishing the chambre ardente to try heresy cases and issuing the Edict of Écouen (1559) prescribing death for convicted heretics. The crown's finances, strained by war, relied on loans and the sale of offices.

Death

To celebrate the peace treaty and the marriage of his daughter Elisabeth to Philip II, Henry held a tournament in Paris. On 30 June 1559, jousting against Gabriel de Lorges, Count of Montgomery, captain of his Scottish Guard, Henry was struck when Montgomery's lance splintered and fragments pierced his visor, driving into his eye and brain. Despite the care of the surgeons Ambroise Paré and Andreas Vesalius, he died in agony ten days later, on 10 July 1559. He was succeeded by his sickly son Francis II, and his death opened the way to the instability of the French Wars of Religion.

The accident was later popularly linked to a prophecy attributed to Nostradamus (Century 1, Quatrain 35, "the young lion shall overcome the older").
