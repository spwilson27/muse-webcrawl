The Abbasid Caliphate (Arabic: الخلافة العباسية, al-Khilāfah al-ʿAbbāsīyah) was the third caliphate to succeed the Islamic prophet Muhammad. Founded by a dynasty descended from Muhammad's uncle, Abbas ibn Abd al-Muttalib, it overthrew the Umayyad Caliphate in 750 CE and ruled from its new capital of Baghdad for over five centuries. At its height the Abbasid realm stretched from the Atlantic coast of North Africa to the Indus valley, and its early centuries are remembered as the golden age of Islamic civilisation — an era of advances in science, mathematics, medicine, philosophy and literature, and of the translation movement centred on Baghdad's House of Wisdom.

Although political power fragmented after the 9th century, with provinces breaking away under local dynasties and the caliphs themselves falling under the control of Buyid and later Seljuk protectors, the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad remained the symbolic head of Sunni Islam until the Mongol conquest of 1258. A line of Abbasid shadow caliphs was subsequently maintained by the Mamluk sultans in Cairo until the Ottoman conquest of Egypt in 1517.

History

The Abbasid Revolution

The Abbasids built a clandestine propaganda movement (the daʿwa) in Khurasan, exploiting resentment of Umayyad rule among non-Arab converts (mawālī) and Arab settlers alike. Under the organiser Abu Muslim, the revolution erupted in 747; Umayyad armies were defeated, and after the decisive Battle of the Zab in January 750 the last Umayyad caliph, Marwan II, was killed in Egypt. Abu al-Abbas al-Saffah was proclaimed the first Abbasid caliph in Kufa. One Umayyad prince, Abd al-Rahman I, escaped to al-Andalus, where he founded an independent emirate, permanently detaching Spain from Abbasid rule.

The move to Baghdad

Al-Saffah's brother and successor, al-Mansur (r. 754–775), founded Baghdad in 762 as a purpose-built circular capital on the Tigris, called Madinat al-Salam, "the City of Peace." The empire's centre of gravity shifted eastward from Syria to Iraq and Persia, and Persian administrators such as the Barmakid family became central to government. Under Harun al-Rashid (r. 786–809) — the caliph of the Thousand and One Nights — Baghdad became perhaps the largest city in the world, a hub of trade reaching from China to the Atlantic. Harun's death was followed by a ruinous civil war between his sons al-Amin and al-Ma'mun (809–813), from which al-Ma'mun emerged victorious.

Samarra and the loss of control

The spiral Malwiya minaret of the Great Mosque of Samarra, built under the Abbasids in the 9th century.

To escape Baghdad's turbulence, al-Mu'tasim (r. 833–842) moved the capital to Samarra in 836 and built a new guard of Turkic slave-soldiers (ghilmān). The remedy proved worse than the disease: during the "Anarchy at Samarra" (861–870) the Turkic officers made and murdered caliphs at will. The capital returned to Baghdad in 892, but by then the empire had fractured: the Tulunids and Ikhshidids in Egypt, the Saffarids and Samanids in the east, the Hamdanids in Syria, and the breakaway Fatimid Caliphate in North Africa (from 909) all reduced the caliph's effective domain, often to Iraq alone. In 945 the Shi'a Buyid emirs occupied Baghdad and held the caliphs as pensioned figureheads for a century; in 1055 the Sunni Seljuk Turks replaced them as protectors, holding the title of sultan while the caliph retained religious authority.

The Islamic Golden Age

Abbasid patronage drove the Graeco-Arabic translation movement, in which the works of Greek, Persian and Indian learning were rendered into Arabic — famously at Baghdad's House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Ḥikmah) under al-Ma'mun. Scholars such as al-Khwarizmi (whose name gives us "algorithm" and whose book gave algebra its name), the physician al-Razi, the philosopher al-Kindi, the astronomer al-Battani, and the polymath Ibn al-Haytham made foundational contributions. Persian court culture flourished under the Barmakids and later viziers, and Arabic literature saw the compilation of the Thousand and One Nights and the poetry of Abu Nuwas and al-Mutanabbi. Baghdad's position on the trade routes between China, India and Europe enriched the caliphate and spread technologies such as paper-making, learned from Chinese prisoners after the Battle of Talas (751).

Administration and society

The caliph stood at the head of both state and faith. Government was exercised through the vizier, who supervised the dīwāns (bureaus) of finance, the army, correspondence and the post-intelligence service (barīd). Provinces were ruled by governors (amīrs) whose remittances of tax sustained the court. The economy rested on irrigated agriculture in Iraq and Egypt, long-distance trade, and a monetised urban culture. Non-Muslim subjects — Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians — held protected dhimmī status in return for the jizya tax, and frequently served as physicians, translators and officials.

Decline and fragmentation

From the mid-9th century the caliphate's unity was more legal fiction than fact. Regional dynasties acknowledged the caliph in the Friday sermon (khuṭba) and on coinage while ruling independently. The caliphs al-Nasir (r. 1180–1225) briefly revived real power in Iraq, but the end came in 1258, when the Mongol army of Hulagu stormed Baghdad after a brief siege, killed the last Baghdad caliph, al-Musta'sim, and massacred much of the population — an event remembered as one of the great catastrophes of Islamic history. In 1261 the Mamluk sultan Baybars installed a surviving Abbasid prince as caliph in Cairo, where the line continued, largely ceremonial, until Selim I's conquest of Egypt in 1517.

Legacy

The Abbasid era defined classical Islamic civilisation: its law schools, theology, science, architecture and literature became the reference points for later Muslim societies, and its black banner and the title of caliph remained symbols of Sunni legitimacy for centuries. Baghdad's memory as a cosmopolitan capital of learning still shapes the cultural imagination of the Islamic world.
