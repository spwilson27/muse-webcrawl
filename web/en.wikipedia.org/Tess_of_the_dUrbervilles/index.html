Tess of the d'Urbervilles

Tess of the d'Urbervilles: A Pure Woman Faithfully Presented
Author: Thomas Hardy
Published: 1891
Publisher: James R. Osgood, McIlvaine & Co.
Genre: Novel, tragedy

Tess of the d'Urbervilles: A Pure Woman Faithfully Presented is a novel by Thomas Hardy, published in 1891. It initially appeared in a censored and serialised version in the British illustrated newspaper The Graphic that year, and in book form in three volumes. Now considered a major work of English literature, the novel tells the story of Tess Durbeyfield, a poor country girl whose family discovers a supposed link to the aristocratic d'Urberville family, setting in motion a chain of events that ends in tragedy.

Plot

Tess Durbeyfield is the eldest daughter of a poor pedlar in the village of Marlott, in Hardy's fictional Wessex. When her father learns he is descended from the ancient d'Urberville family, Tess is sent to "claim kin" with the wealthy Stoke-d'Urberville household, where she is pursued — and ultimately seduced or raped, the text leaves deliberate ambiguity — by Alec d'Urberville. She returns home, bears a child who dies in infancy (she baptises him Sorrow herself), and later finds work as a dairymaid at Talbothays, where she falls in love with Angel Clare, a parson's son learning farming. She confesses her past on their wedding night; Angel, despite his own earlier lapse, rejects her and goes to Brazil. Destitute, Tess works at the harsh Flintcomb-Ash farm and is driven back into Alec's keeping. When Angel returns, repentant, Tess murders Alec, flees briefly with Angel, and is arrested at Stonehenge. She is executed at Wintoncester (Winchester).

Themes and reception

The subtitle "A Pure Woman" was a direct challenge to Victorian moral conventions, and the novel's treatment of sexuality, religion, and social class provoked controversy; early manuscripts were rejected by several publishers and the serial version was bowdlerised. Hardy explores fate, coincidence, the harshness of rural poverty, the decline of old families and agricultural communities, and a bleak, indifferent universe — the novel closes with the narrator's remark that "the President of the Immortals" had ended his sport with Tess. Critical reputation grew steadily, and the novel is now among Hardy's most studied works.

Adaptations

The novel has been adapted many times, notably as Roman Polanski's film Tess (1979), the BBC serial Tess of the d'Urbervilles (2008), and the Indian-set film Trishna (2011).
