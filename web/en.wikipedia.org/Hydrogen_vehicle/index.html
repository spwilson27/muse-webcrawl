Hydrogen vehicle

A fuel cell electric vehicle refuelling with compressed hydrogen

A hydrogen vehicle is a vehicle that uses hydrogen as its fuel. The term most commonly refers to fuel cell electric vehicles (FCEVs), in which an on-board fuel cell combines hydrogen with oxygen from the air to generate electricity, which drives electric motors; the only tailpipe emission is water vapour. A smaller number of vehicles burn hydrogen directly in an internal combustion engine. Hydrogen vehicles are promoted as a means of decarbonising transport, especially where fast refuelling and long range are valued.

Fuel cell cars

Production fuel cell cars have included the Toyota Mirai (introduced in 2014, one of the first mass-produced hydrogen vehicles), the Honda Clarity Fuel Cell, and the Hyundai Nexo. Their hydrogen is stored in carbon-fibre tanks as compressed gas, typically at 700 bar, giving driving ranges of roughly 500–650 km and refuelling times of about five minutes. Sales have remained small compared with battery electric vehicles, concentrated in California, Japan, and South Korea, where refuelling networks exist.

Advantages and challenges

Advocates cite zero tailpipe emissions, rapid refuelling, and suitability for heavy-duty applications such as trucks, buses, and trains. Critics point to fundamental efficiency losses — electricity used to produce "green" hydrogen by electrolysis, compress it, and convert it back in a fuel cell returns only around a quarter to a third of the original energy to the wheels, versus roughly three-quarters for a battery electric vehicle. Most hydrogen today is produced from natural gas by steam reforming, with associated CO2 emissions. Other challenges include the cost of fuel cells and platinum catalysts, the expense of refuelling infrastructure, and the difficulty of storing a very light, leak-prone gas.

Other applications

Hydrogen trains (such as the Alstom Coradia iLint, in service in Germany), buses, forklifts, and prototype aircraft have all been demonstrated, and hydrogen is considered a candidate fuel for shipping and long-haul aviation, where batteries are too heavy.
