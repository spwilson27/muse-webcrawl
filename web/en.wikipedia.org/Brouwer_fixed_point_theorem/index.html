Brouwer fixed point theorem

Every continuous map of a disk into itself leaves some point fixed

The Brouwer fixed point theorem is a fundamental result of algebraic topology, named after Dutch mathematician L. E. J. Brouwer. It states that for any continuous function f mapping a closed ball (or any compact convex set, or indeed any space homeomorphic to a closed ball) into itself, there is a point x₀ with f(x₀) = x₀ — a fixed point.

Statement and intuition

In one dimension the theorem is a consequence of the intermediate value theorem. In two dimensions it says that if a disc is deformed continuously within itself — no tearing, no part pushed outside — some point stays in its original place. Familiar illustrations include: stirring a cup of coffee, some point of the liquid ends where it began; and a map of a region placed within that region has some point of the map lying exactly over the point it represents ("you are here" points must exist).

History

Special cases were proved by Piers Bohl (1904), and Brouwer gave the general result around 1909–1912 using his new notion of the degree of a mapping; proofs by Hadamard, Poincaré, and others followed. The theorem is non-constructive: it guarantees existence without giving an algorithm.

Generalisations and applications

The Schauder and Kakutani fixed-point theorems extend the result to infinite-dimensional and set-valued settings. Kakutani's version underlies John Nash's proof of the existence of Nash equilibria in game theory, and fixed-point arguments are foundational in economics (existence of general equilibrium), differential equations, and the theory of computation — searching for Brouwer fixed points defines the complexity class PPAD.
