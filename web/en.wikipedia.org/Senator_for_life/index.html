Senator for life

Senator for life
TermFor the holder's lifetime
Countries (current)Italy, Paraguay, Burundi, Rwanda, DR Congo, others
Countries (former)France, Spain, Peru, Venezuela, Chile, Brazil (Empire)

A senator for life is a member of a senate who holds the office for life rather than for a fixed term. The title is conferred in several ways: by appointment of a head of state, by election to a lifetime seat, or ex officio — automatically — upon former heads of state leaving office. Lifetime membership is intended to bring experience and continuity to a legislature and to insulate its holders from electoral pressure, though critics regard it as undemocratic.

Italy

The most prominent modern example is Italy, whose Constitution provides for senatori a vita. Former presidents of the Republic become senators for life by right, and the president may appoint up to five citizens who have "honoured the Fatherland through their outstanding merits in the social, scientific, artistic, and literary fields." Distinguished appointees have included the conductor Arturo Toscanini (who declined), the scientist Rita Levi-Montalcini, and the architect Renzo Piano.

Elsewhere

France's Third Republic had 75 life senators (sénateurs inamovibles) under the Constitution of 1875, a provision abolished in 1884. Several Latin American constitutions have made former presidents senators for life — Chile's 1980 constitution did so (a provision that seated Augusto Pinochet and was abolished in 2005), and Paraguay retains the institution. In Africa, Burundi, Rwanda, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo give former heads of state lifetime senate seats, and the practice also exists in various forms in smaller states and in the composition of some upper houses elsewhere.
