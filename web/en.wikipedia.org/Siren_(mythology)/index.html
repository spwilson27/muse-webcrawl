Siren (mythology)

Odysseus and the Sirens, from a red-figure stamnos (c. 480–470 BC).

In Greek mythology, the Sirens (Greek: Σειρῆνες, Seirênes) were dangerous creatures who lured nearby sailors with their enchanting music and singing voices to shipwreck on the rocky coast of their island. Typically depicted as part woman and part bird, they are most famous from Homer's Odyssey, in which Odysseus, warned by Circe, has his crew plug their ears with wax and is himself tied to the mast so that he may hear their song and live.

Mythology

Ancient authors vary in the Sirens' number — often two or three — their parentage (usually daughters of the river god Achelous), and their names. In the Argonautica of Apollonius of Rhodes, the Argonauts pass safely because Orpheus plays more beautiful music and drowns out their song. Later traditions said the Sirens were fated to die if anyone heard them and escaped; after Odysseus passed, they threw themselves into the sea. In Ovid, they were companions of Persephone, given wings to search for her after her abduction by Hades.

Depiction and later meaning

Early Greek art shows the Sirens as birds with large women's heads; vase painters and sculptors used them as funerary figures, and their images appear on grave monuments into the Roman period. In medieval bestiaries, they were gradually conflated with mermaids, acquiring fish tails. The word "siren" passed into modern usage for alarm devices and, in Romance languages, for mermaids, and the creatures remain a byword for dangerous allure — hence the "siren song" of something that lures one to ruin.
