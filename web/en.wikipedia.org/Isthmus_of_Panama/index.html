Isthmus of Panama

The Isthmus of Panama (Spanish: Istmo de Panamá), historically known as the Isthmus of Darien, is the narrow strip of land between the Caribbean Sea and the Pacific Ocean that links North and South America. It contains the country of Panama and is crossed by the Panama Canal. The isthmus measures about 700 km (430 mi) from east to west and between roughly 50 and 200 km from north to south, reaching its narrowest point near the canal.

Formation and biogeography

The isthmus formed as the Caribbean tectonic plate collided with South America; conventional dating holds that the seaway between the oceans finally closed about 3 million years ago (with some research arguing for an earlier or more gradual closure). Its emergence triggered the Great American Interchange, in which land mammals — horses, tapirs, llamas, glyptodonts, ground sloths and terror birds among them — migrated between the two continents, while the severing of ocean currents reshaped global climate and marine evolution on either side.

History

The isthmus was a Spanish colonial crossroads after Vasco Núñez de Balboa sighted the Pacific from its heights in 1513; treasure fleets carried Peruvian silver across it by mule train. The Panama Railway (1855) served California gold-rush traffic, and after a failed French attempt under Ferdinand de Lesseps, the United States completed the Panama Canal in 1914 following Panama's independence from Colombia in 1903. The canal, returned to Panama in 1999, remains one of the world's great trade chokepoints.
