Disassortative mating

Disassortative mating (also known as negative assortative mating or heterogamy) is a mating pattern in which individuals with dissimilar genotypes or phenotypes mate with one another more frequently than would be expected under random mating. It is the opposite of assortative mating.

Mechanisms and examples

Disassortative mating tends to increase genetic heterozygosity and maintain polymorphism in a population. A well-studied example is mate choice based on the major histocompatibility complex (MHC): in several vertebrates, experimental and observational studies suggest preferences for mates with dissimilar MHC alleles, which can produce offspring with broader immune competence. In plants, self-incompatibility systems enforce disassortative mating by preventing fertilisation between individuals sharing incompatibility alleles.

Consequences

By pairing dissimilar individuals, disassortative mating can counter inbreeding depression and help stabilise rare alleles. It is also invoked in explanations of disassortative preferences for traits such as colour morphs in some birds and symmetry deviations in humans.
