Gestational diabetes

Gestational diabetes is a condition characterised by high blood sugar (hyperglycaemia) that develops during pregnancy and usually resolves after giving birth. It occurs when pregnancy hormones and other factors interfere with the action of insulin, producing insulin resistance that the pancreas cannot fully overcome. It differs from pre-existing diabetes in pregnancy, in which diabetes is present before conception.

Gestational diabetes generally produces few symptoms, so screening of pregnant women is recommended, typically with an oral glucose tolerance test at 24–28 weeks of gestation. Untreated, it increases the risks of pre-eclampsia, caesarean delivery, and macrosomia (an excessively large baby), and both mother and child have a higher long-term risk of type 2 diabetes.

Causes and risk factors

During pregnancy the placenta secretes hormones — including human placental lactogen, progesterone and cortisol — that antagonise insulin. In women whose pancreatic beta cells cannot increase insulin output sufficiently, blood glucose rises. Risk is higher with obesity, advanced maternal age, a family history of type 2 diabetes, certain ethnic backgrounds, and a previous large baby or gestational diabetes.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually made with a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, with thresholds for fasting and post-load glucose set by bodies such as the IADPSG and the World Health Organization. Diagnostic criteria vary between countries, which affects reported prevalence.

Management

First-line treatment is dietary modification, physical activity and self-monitoring of blood glucose. If targets are not met, insulin is the traditional medication of choice; metformin and glyburide are also used in some settings. After delivery, glucose usually returns to normal, but women are advised to have lifelong periodic screening for type 2 diabetes.
