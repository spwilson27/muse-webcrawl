Puna de Atacama

The Puna de Atacama is a high, arid plateau in the central Andes of northern Chile and northwestern Argentina, forming the southern portion of the Altiplano-Puna plateau complex. Lying at an average elevation of about 4,500 metres (14,800 ft), it is one of the driest high-elevation regions on Earth, a cold desert of salt flats (salares), endorheic basins, and some of the world's tallest volcanoes.

Geography

The region is bounded by the Cordillera Occidental to the west, with peaks such as Llullaillaco (6,739 m) and Ojos del Salado (6,893 m, the world's highest active volcano), and descends eastward toward the Argentine provinces of Jujuy, Salta, and Catamarca. Interior drainage creates extensive salt pans, including the Salar de Atacama in Chile and the Salinas Grandes in Argentina. Precipitation is extremely low and mostly falls as summer snow; diurnal temperature swings are severe.

History

The Puna was inhabited for millennia by Atacameño (Likan Antay) and related Andean peoples, who herded llamas and alpacas and farmed oasis valleys. It came under Inca influence in the fifteenth century. After South American independence, the region was disputed between Chile, Bolivia, and Argentina; the Atacama border dispute over the Puna was submitted to arbitration by the United States envoy William Buchanan in 1899, which awarded most of the territory to Argentina and the remainder to Chile.

Economy and environment

The salares hold some of the world's richest lithium brine deposits, making the "Lithium Triangle" of Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia a strategic mining region; borates, copper, and gold are also extracted. The fragile high-altitude wetlands (bofedales and lagunas) support flamingos, vicuñas, and endemic species, and areas are protected in reserves such as Los Flamencos National Reserve. The clear, dry skies host major astronomical facilities on nearby peaks, and the town of San Pedro de Atacama is a center for tourism.
