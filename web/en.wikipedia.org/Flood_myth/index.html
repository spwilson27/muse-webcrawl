Flood myth

The deluge, a motif found in mythologies around the world.

A flood myth or a deluge myth is a myth in which a great flood, usually sent by a deity or deities, destroys civilization, often in an act of divine retribution. Parallels are often drawn between the flood waters of these myths and the primeval waters that appear in certain creation myths, as the flood waters are described as a measure for the cleansing of humanity in preparation for rebirth. Most flood myths also contain a culture hero, who "represents the human craving for life".

The flood myth motif is found among many cultures as seen in the Mesopotamian flood stories, the Genesis flood narrative, the Hindu story of Manu, Deucalion's flood in Greek mythology, and flood stories from the lore of Native American, Chinese, African, and other peoples worldwide.

Mesopotamian flood stories

The earliest known flood stories come from Mesopotamia. In the Sumerian and Akkadian traditions, the hero — Ziusudra, Atrahasis, or Utnapishtim in the Epic of Gilgamesh — is warned by a god (Enki/Ea) to build a boat and preserve the seed of life, because the chief god Enlil, annoyed by human noise or wickedness, has resolved to destroy mankind with a flood. The parallels between the Gilgamesh account — including the release of birds to find dry land — and the later biblical narrative are striking and widely discussed.

Genesis flood narrative

In the Book of Genesis, God, seeing the wickedness of humanity, sends a flood over the whole earth, but spares Noah, who at God's command builds an ark and takes aboard his family and pairs of every animal. After the waters subside, God makes a covenant with Noah, symbolised by the rainbow, never again to destroy all life by flood.

Other traditions

  • Greek myth — Deucalion and Pyrrha survive Zeus's flood in a wooden chest and repopulate the earth by casting stones behind them.
  • Hindu tradition — the fish Matsya warns Manu of a coming deluge and guides his boat to safety; Manu becomes the ancestor of the new humanity.
  • Chinese myth — the Great Flood of the Gun–Yu tradition is a long struggle to control the waters rather than a divine punishment.
  • Norse myth — Bergelmir survives the flood of Ymir's blood in a vessel.

Explanations

Scholars have proposed that flood myths preserve the memory of real events — catastrophic river floods, post-glacial sea-level rise, or tsunamis — though no single geological event explains all versions. Others see them as symbolic expressions of creation renewed, or as products of cultural diffusion along trade routes. The near-universality of the motif in river-valley and coastal civilisations is unsurprising given the destructive power of real floods.
