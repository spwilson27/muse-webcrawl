Satellite state

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A satellite state is a country that is formally independent but under heavy political, economic, and military influence or control by another country. The term was coined by analogy to planetary objects orbiting a larger body and was used mainly during the Cold War to describe central and eastern European countries that were aligned with, and dominated by, the Soviet Union.

History of the term

The expression gained currency after the Second World War. The nations of the Eastern Bloc—including Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, and East Germany—were members of the Warsaw Pact and were widely described in the West as Soviet satellites. The Soviet Union intervened militarily to keep them aligned, notably in the Hungarian Revolution of 1956 and the Prague Spring of 1968.

Historians note that satellite status was a matter of degree: Romania pursued a relatively autonomous foreign policy from the 1960s, while Albania broke with Moscow entirely in 1961. Yugoslavia, although communist, was never a Soviet satellite after the Tito–Stalin split of 1948.

Related concepts

A satellite state differs from a puppet state, whose government is installed and directly controlled by a foreign power, and from a client state, which depends on a patron for economic, security, or diplomatic support but retains more autonomy. Some analysts describe such relationships in terms of a sphere of influence. During the Second World War, the term was also applied to minor Axis states aligned with Nazi Germany, and in the post–Cold War era it has occasionally been used for states closely aligned with other major powers.
