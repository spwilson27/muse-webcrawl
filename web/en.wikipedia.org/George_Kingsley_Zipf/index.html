George Kingsley Zipf (January 7, 1902 – September 25, 1950) was an American linguist and philologist who studied statistical occurrences in different languages. He taught at Harvard University and is eponymously associated with Zipf's law, the observation that the frequency of a word in a text is inversely proportional to its rank in the frequency table.

George Kingsley Zipf, founder of quantitative linguistics.

Work

Zipf's law implies that in any large body of text, the most frequent word occurs roughly twice as often as the second most frequent, three times as often as the third, and so on. Similar rank-size regularities were found in city populations, incomes, and many other phenomena. Zipf attempted to unify these findings in his 1949 book Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort, arguing that people organise their behaviour — including language — so as to minimise probable average work.

Legacy

Zipf's work founded much of quantitative linguistics and remains heavily cited in corpus linguistics, information theory, and network science, where power-law distributions are ubiquitous. The related Zipf–Mandelbrot law refines his rank-frequency formula with an additional parameter.
