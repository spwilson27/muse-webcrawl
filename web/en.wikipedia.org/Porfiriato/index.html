Porfiriato

The Porfiriato (Spanish: Porfiriato) is the period in the history of Mexico during which the country was under the authoritarian leadership of General Porfirio Díaz, from 1876 until his resignation and exile in May 1911, with a brief interregnum (1880–1884) under his ally Manuel González. The era is named after Díaz himself and is characterized by political stability, economic modernization, and deepening social inequality.

Order and progress

Governing under the motto "poca política y mucha administración" ("little politics and much administration"), Díaz and his circle of technocratic advisers, the Científicos, pursued positivist policies of "order and progress." Foreign investment from the United States and Europe poured into railways, mining, oil, and plantations; Mexico's railway network grew from a few hundred kilometres to roughly 19,000 km, exports boomed, and the federal government achieved budget surpluses.

Social costs and repression

The benefits of growth were unevenly distributed. Land laws enabled hacienda owners and survey companies to absorb village communal lands, leaving much of the rural population landless and in debt peonage. Political opposition was repressed through the rurales (rural police), censorship, and manipulation of elections; strikes such as those at Cananea (1906) and Río Blanco (1907) were violently suppressed.

Fall

An aging Díaz's 1908 interview suggesting Mexico was ready for democracy emboldened opposition, and Francisco I. Madero's anti-re-election campaign ignited the Mexican Revolution in 1910. Díaz resigned on May 25, 1911, and sailed into exile in Paris, where he died in 1915. Historians continue to debate the Porfiriato's balance of modernization and repression.
