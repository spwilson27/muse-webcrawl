The Prut (Ukrainian and Romanian: Prut; also spelled Pruth in older sources) is a river 953 kilometres long in Eastern Europe, a left tributary of the Danube. It rises in the Ukrainian Carpathians near Mount Hoverla, flows through the city of Chernivtsi, and then forms almost the entire border between Romania (to the west) and Moldova (to the east) before joining the Danube near Giurgiulești, not far from Galați.

History

The river is known from antiquity — Herodotus lists it among the tributaries of the Danube, under a name rendered as Porata or Pyretus. In modern history it marked the border between the Russian Empire's Bessarabia and the Romanian principality of Moldavia after the Treaty of Bucharest (1812), and again between Romania and the Soviet Union after 1940. The Treaty of the Pruth (1711) ended Peter the Great's disastrous Pruth campaign against the Ottoman Empire, in which the Russian army was encircled by the river. In June–July 1940 and again in 1941 and 1944 the river line featured in the shifting frontiers and military operations of the Second World War period.

Geography and use

The Prut is navigable by small craft only in its lower reaches. The Stânca–Costești hydroelectric dam, a joint Romanian–Moldovan project completed in 1978, is the largest structure on the river. The middle and lower Prut valley contains important wetlands, including the Lower Prut Lakes in Moldova, recognised for bird habitat.
