Surrender at Perevolochna

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The surrender at Perevolochna was the capitulation of almost the entire Swedish army under General Adam Ludwig Lewenhaupt on 30 June (Old Style) / 1 July 1709, three days after the Battle of Poltava, during the Great Northern War. The surrender took place at the village of Perevolochna on the Dnieper River, where the retreating Swedish army found itself trapped against the unfordable river by pursuing Russian forces under Prince Alexander Menshikov.

Background

After the catastrophic Swedish defeat at Poltava on 27–28 June 1709, King Charles XII of Sweden escaped southward across the Dnieper with an escort of some 1,500–2,000 men, accompanied by the Cossack hetman Ivan Mazepa, seeking refuge in the Ottoman Empire. Command of the remaining field army — some 14,000 to 20,000 men, exhausted and demoralised — devolved upon General Lewenhaupt, who marched the army to the Dnieper near Perevolochna only to find that boats were sufficient only for the king's party.

The surrender

On 30 June, Menshikov's Russian force, roughly 9,000 strong, caught up with the Swedes. Lewenhaupt, unwilling to sacrifice his men in a hopeless stand and judging the position indefensible, put the question of capitulation to a council of senior officers and agreed to surrender on negotiated terms, despite his army outnumbering its immediate pursuers. The terms allowed Swedish officers and men to keep their personal belongings, but the army laid down its arms and its artillery, colours, and treasury were forfeited. In accordance with the capitulation, the Cossacks and Zaporozhians in Swedish service were largely handed over or scattered; many were executed. Mazepa and Charles had already crossed the river and escaped.

Consequences

Together with Poltava, the surrender at Perevolochna destroyed Sweden's main field army and effectively decided the Great Northern War: Sweden never recovered its great-power status. Thousands of Swedish prisoners were transported deep into Russia and Siberia, many being employed on construction works including the building of Saint Petersburg; most never returned home. Lewenhaupt himself died in captivity in 1719. Charles XII spent five years in Ottoman exile at Bender before returning to Sweden.
