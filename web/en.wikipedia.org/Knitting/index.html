Knitting is a method for creating textiles by interlocking loops of yarn with a pair of needles or a machine. Unlike weaving, in which two sets of threads cross at right angles, a knitted fabric consists of a single yarn (or set of yarns) pulled through previously formed loops, producing a stretchy, elastic material well suited to garments such as sweaters, socks, hats and scarves.

Techniques

All knitting rests on two fundamental stitches: the knit stitch, in which the new loop is pulled through the old loop from front to back, and the purl stitch, its reverse. Combining them produces textures such as garter stitch, stockinette (stocking) stitch, ribbing, moss stitch and intricate cable patterns formed by crossing stitches over one another. Lace knitting uses yarn-overs paired with decreases to create openwork designs.

Hand knitters generally work either in the "English" style, throwing the yarn with the right hand, or the "Continental" style, picking it with the left. Work may be done flat on two needles or in the round using circular or double-pointed needles. Garment size is controlled by gauge — the number of stitches and rows per unit length — usually checked with a swatch.

History

The earliest surviving knitted objects are finely patterned cotton socks from Egypt, dating roughly to the 11th–14th centuries CE. The craft spread through the Islamic world into Spain and then across Europe, where knitting guilds were established by the 13th century. Knitted stockings became a major industry; the invention of the stocking frame by William Lee in 1589 began the mechanisation of the trade. Hand knitting remained essential domestic work for centuries and saw revivals during both World Wars, when civilians knitted garments for soldiers, and again in the early 21st century as a popular hobby and craft movement.

Machine knitting

Modern knitting machines range from domestic flat-bed machines to industrial whole-garment systems that produce a complete seamless sweater in under an hour. Computer-controlled machines can knit complex colourwork (jacquard), shaping and three-dimensional structures, and knitted constructions are used not only in fashion but in technical textiles, medical implants and aerospace composites.

Social and cultural aspects

Knitting circles and "stitch and bitch" groups, online communities such as Ravelry, and charity knitting projects have made the craft a social activity as much as a practical one. Studies have associated repetitive crafts like knitting with reduced stress and improved wellbeing.
