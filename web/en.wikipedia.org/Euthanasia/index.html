Euthanasia
EtymologyGreek: "good death"
TypesVoluntary, non-voluntary, involuntary; active vs passive
Legal in (voluntary active)Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, Spain, Canada, Colombia, parts of Australia and New Zealand, among others

Euthanasia is the practice of intentionally ending a life to relieve pain and suffering. It is distinguished from assisted dying (where the final act is the person's own) and from withholding or withdrawing futile treatment. Classification turns on consent: voluntary (at the patient's request), non-voluntary (patient unable to consent), and involuntary (against the patient's will, generally regarded as murder).

Ethical debate

Supporters argue from autonomy and relief of unbearable suffering; opponents argue from the sanctity of life, risks of coercion of vulnerable people ("slippery slope"), and the availability of palliative care. Major religious traditions differ: Roman Catholic teaching prohibits euthanasia, while many liberal and secular ethicists defend regulated access.

Legal status

The Netherlands (2002) and Belgium (2002) were the first countries to legalise voluntary euthanasia under due-care criteria, including unbearable suffering and repeated voluntary request. Canada (MAID, 2016), Spain (2021), New Zealand (2021), and several Australian states have since adopted frameworks, typically limited to competent adults, often with terminal illness requirements. In much of the world euthanasia remains a criminal offence. The Nazi "Aktion T4" programme, which murdered disabled people, is historically conflated with the term but was involuntary killing, not euthanasia in the modern voluntary sense.

Animal euthanasia

The term also covers humane killing of animals, performed by injection in shelters and veterinary practice to end suffering or manage populations.
