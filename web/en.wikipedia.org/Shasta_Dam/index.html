Shasta Dam (called Kennett Dam before its renaming in 1944) is a concrete arch-gravity dam across the Sacramento River in Northern California. Built between 1938 and 1945 by the United States Bureau of Reclamation, it is the keystone of the Central Valley Project, impounding Shasta Lake — California's largest reservoir — and providing irrigation water, flood control, hydroelectric power, and municipal water. At 183 m (602 ft) high, it is one of the tallest dams in the United States; its spillway, with a drop roughly three times the height of Niagara Falls, is among the largest ever built.

Construction

Construction began in 1938 under contractor Pacific Constructors Inc., after the purpose-built town of Toyon (and the workers' settlements of the project area) sprang up to house thousands of workers. Materials arrived by a conveyor belt system and rail; some 12 million tons of aggregate were moved. A specially built cableway head tower — the largest of its kind — placed 6.5 million cubic yards of concrete. Power generation began in 1944, before the dam was formally completed in 1945, supplying wartime industry. Chief engineer Frank Crowe, fresh from Hoover Dam, supervised the work.

Functions

Shasta Lake stores winter runoff from the Sacramento, Pit, and McCloud Rivers for release during California's dry summers. The dam's powerhouse, with seven generators totalling about 710 MW, is the largest hydroelectric plant by capacity wholly within the state reclamation system of the Central Valley Project. Stored water flows down the Sacramento River to the Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta, from which it is exported south to irrigate millions of acres of Central Valley farmland and to serve urban users.

Impacts and controversies

Flooding the upper Sacramento canyon inundated the lands of the Winnemem Wintu people, whose villages, burial grounds, and fishing sites were submerged without the compensation and replacement land promised under federal law; the tribe continues to campaign for recognition and for the restoration of salmon above the dam. The dam also blocked Chinook salmon from their historical spawning grounds in the upper Sacramento, McCloud, and Pit rivers, contributing to the decline of winter-run Chinook, now listed as endangered; a trap-and-haul and reintroduction programme for the McCloud River has been under development.

Proposals to raise Shasta Dam by up to 5.6 m (18.5 ft) to enlarge storage have been studied by the Bureau of Reclamation for decades and remain controversial, opposed by the Winnemem Wintu, fishing and environmental groups, and the State of California, whose rivers law protects the McCloud as a wild and scenic river.
