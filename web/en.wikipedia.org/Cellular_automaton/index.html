Cellular automaton

A cellular automaton (plural: cellular automata, abbreviated CA) is a discrete model of computation studied in automata theory. It consists of a regular grid of cells, each in one of a finite number of states, evolving in discrete time steps according to a fixed rule based on the states of neighbouring cells. Despite their simple rules, cellular automata can produce complex behaviour and some are capable of universal computation.

History

Cellular automata were pioneered in the 1940s by Stanislaw Ulam and John von Neumann at Los Alamos, where von Neumann developed a self-reproducing automaton with 29 states per cell. Martin Gardner's 1970 Scientific American column popularized Conway's Game of Life, the best-known cellular automaton. In the 1980s Stephen Wolfram systematically studied one-dimensional elementary cellular automata and classified their behaviour into four classes.

Examples

In the Game of Life, a live cell with two or three live neighbours survives, a dead cell with exactly three live neighbours becomes alive, and all other cells die or stay dead. Elementary cellular automata such as Rule 30 generate apparent randomness, while Rule 110 has been proved Turing complete. John von Neumann's and later constructions demonstrate universal construction and computation within CA rules.

Applications

Cellular automata are used to model physical systems (fluid flow via lattice gas automata), biological pattern formation, traffic flow, forest fires, cryptography, and procedural generation in computer graphics and games.
