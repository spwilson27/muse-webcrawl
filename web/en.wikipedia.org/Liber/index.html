Liber

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Liber, the Roman god of viticulture, wine, fertility and freedom.

In ancient Roman religion and mythology, Liber (Latin: Līber, "the free one"), also known as Liber Pater ("the free Father"), was a god of viticulture and wine, male fertility, and freedom. He was a patron deity of Rome's plebeians and was part of the Aventine Triad with his mother Ceres and his sister or consort Libera. His festival, the Liberalia, held on 17 March, became associated with free elections, coming of age, and popular freedom of speech; on that day Roman boys assumed the toga virilis, the garment of adult citizenship.

Mythology and cult

Liber was closely identified with the Greek god Dionysus and his Roman counterpart Bacchus, absorbing much of their mythology of wine, ecstasy, and theatrical festivity. The temple of Ceres, Liber and Libera on the Aventine Hill, traditionally founded in 493 BC, was a centre of plebeian religious and political organization, housing the archives of the plebeian aediles.

The Bacchanalia affair

In 186 BC, the Roman Senate moved to suppress the Bacchanalia, ecstatic rites associated with Liber/Bacchus, fearing conspiracy and moral corruption; the decree (the Senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus) restricted the cult throughout Italy. The historian Livy gives a dramatic, possibly embellished, account of the affair.

Legacy

Liber's name is related to the Latin word for "free" and to the notion of liberation, and he remains associated with the freedoms of speech and of the citizen body in Roman political thought.
