The Oklahoma Land Rush of 1889 was the first land run into the Unassigned Lands of the former Indian Territory, which are now part of the U.S. state of Oklahoma. The land run started at high noon on April 22, 1889. An estimated 50,000 people lined up for their piece of the available two million acres (8,100 km²).

The Unassigned Lands were considered some of the best unoccupied public land in the United States. A number of earlier land rushes into other areas of the territory followed, but the 1889 run is the best known and gave Oklahoma its nickname, the "Sooner State."

Background

Following the American Civil War, several Native American tribes in Indian Territory were forced to cede portions of their land to the federal government. The central "Unassigned Lands" were not assigned to any tribe. Pressure from settlers, railroad companies, and "Boomers" — advocates led by figures such as David L. Payne and William L. Couch — mounted through the 1880s, with Boomers repeatedly entering the territory illegally and being removed by federal troops.

In 1889, Congress passed legislation authorizing the opening of the lands, and President Benjamin Harrison signed a proclamation on March 23, 1889, setting the date and terms of the run. Under the Homestead Act provisions, settlers could claim 160 acres.

The Run

At noon on April 22, 1889, cannons and bugles sounded along the borders of the district, and tens of thousands of people on horseback, in wagons, on trains, and on foot surged across the line. Those who crossed early or hid in advance to claim prime land were nicknamed "Sooners," initially a term of derision for cheaters, though it later became a badge of pride.

By nightfall, tent cities had sprung up: Oklahoma City and Guthrie each had populations estimated at 10,000 by the end of the first day. Claims disputes were widespread, and federal land offices were overwhelmed.

Later land runs

Additional openings followed: the Iowa, Sac and Fox, Shawnee, and Pottawatomie lands in 1891; the Cheyenne and Arapaho lands in 1892; the Cherokee Outlet in 1893 — the largest run, with some 100,000 participants racing for over six million acres; and the Kickapoo lands in 1895. Later distributions, such as the Kiowa-Comanche-Apache lands in 1901, were handled by lottery instead.

Legacy

The land runs displaced Native American communities further and set the stage for Oklahoma statehood in 1907. The 1889 rush is commemorated by the Land Run Monument in Oklahoma City, a large bronze sculpture depicting the chaos of the run.
