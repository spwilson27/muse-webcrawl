Fallschirmjäger

BranchLuftwaffe (German Air Force)
TypeAirborne infantry / paratroopers

The Fallschirmjäger (German for "parachute hunters/riflemen") were the paratrooper arm of the German Luftwaffe during the Second World War. Formed from 1935 under General Kurt Student, they were among the first airborne forces to be used in large-scale combat operations, and later in the war fought primarily as elite ground infantry.

Early operations

Fallschirmjäger made their combat debut in April 1940 in Norway and Denmark, and in May 1940 spearheaded the invasions of the Netherlands and Belgium, most famously capturing the Belgian fortress of Eben-Emael with glider-borne engineers using shaped charges. In May 1941 some 22,000 paratroopers and mountain troops took Crete from the air in Operation Mercury — a victory won at such cost (about 4,000 killed) that Hitler forbade further large airborne operations. Nevertheless a celebrated rescue of Benito Mussolini at Gran Sasso was carried out by Fallschirmjäger and SS commandos in September 1943.

Ground role

From 1942 the Fallschirmjäger divisions fought as infantry renowned for tenacious defence: at Monte Cassino in Italy (1944), where Allied troops nicknamed them the "Green Devils" for their camouflage smocks, in Normandy, at Arnhem, in the Battle of the Bulge, and in the final defence of Germany. Elite status, rigorous selection, and distinctive equipment — the rimless M38 paratrooper helmet, jump smock ("bonesack"), gravity knife, and later the FG 42 automatic rifle designed specifically for them — marked them out from ordinary units. Their German RZ-series parachutes, however, required a vulnerable head-first exit and prevented jumping with a weapon in hand.

Legacy

The Bundeswehr's modern parachute troops (Fallschirmjäger of Division Schnelle Kräfte) continue the name. Post-war assessments credit the Fallschirmjäger with pioneering airborne warfare while noting their involvement in reprisals, notably on Crete.
