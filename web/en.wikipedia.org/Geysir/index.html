Geysir, sometimes known as the Great Geysir, is a geyser in the Haukadalur geothermal area of southwestern Iceland. It is the first geyser known to Europeans and the origin of the English word geyser: its name derives from the Icelandic verb geysa, "to gush". The name is first mentioned in written sources in the thirteenth century, following an earthquake that may have activated the spring.

Activity

Geysir's eruptions have varied greatly over the centuries, driven by earthquakes that open or close its conduit. In its most active periods it threw boiling water tens of metres into the air; a height of 170 metres was reported in 1630. During much of the twentieth century it was largely dormant, and eruptions were sometimes artificially induced with soap on special occasions. An earthquake in 2000 reawakened it briefly. In recent decades Geysir erupts only rarely and irregularly, while its smaller neighbour Strokkur, about 50 metres away, erupts reliably every few minutes to heights of 15–20 metres and is the main attraction of the site, which lies on Iceland's Golden Circle tourist route.

Ownership

The surrounding land was sold by a local farmer in 1894 to James Craig (later Lord Craigavon), who charged admission; it later passed through several hands before the Icelandic director Sigurður Jónasson bought it in 1935 and donated it to the Icelandic people.
