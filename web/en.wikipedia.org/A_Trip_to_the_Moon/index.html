A Trip to the Moon (French: Le Voyage dans la Lune) is a 1902 French silent science fiction adventure film directed by Georges Méliès. Inspired by Jules Verne's novels From the Earth to the Moon (1865) and Around the Moon (1870), the film follows a group of astronomers who travel to the Moon in a cannon-propelled capsule, explore the lunar surface, escape from its underground Selenite inhabitants, and return to Earth.

Plot

At a meeting of the Astronomic Club, Professor Barbenfouillis proposes an expedition to the Moon. A capsule shaped like a bullet is built and fired from a huge cannon. It lands in the eye of the Man in the Moon — the film's most famous image. The astronomers explore the surface, watch an Earthrise, and sleep; underground they are captured by insectoid Selenites and taken to their king, but break free, fight their way back to the capsule, and fall back to Earth, splashing down in the ocean and being celebrated with a parade.

Production and themes

Méliès, a former stage magician, used innovative special effects including substitution splices, multiple exposures and dissolves. The film satirises nineteenth-century scientific societies and colonial exploration: the astronomers are pompous academics, and the Selenites are treated as colonial subjects. At the time it was by far the longest and most ambitious film Méliès had made, costing about 10,000 francs.

Reception and legacy

The film was an enormous international success and was widely pirated in the United States. It is regarded as one of the most influential films ever made — the first science fiction film and a landmark of early cinema. A hand-coloured print, long thought lost, was recovered in the 1990s and restored, premiering at Cannes in 2011.
