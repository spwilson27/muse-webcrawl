Machpelah

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cave of Machpelah

Location: Hebron, West Bank

Coordinates: 31.5247° N, 35.1107° E

Also known as: Cave of the Patriarchs; Ibrahimi Mosque

Biblical account: Genesis 23

Machpelah (Hebrew: Me'arat ha-Makhpelah, "Cave of the Double Tombs") is the name given in the Hebrew Bible to the cave and field in Hebron purchased by the patriarch Abraham as a family burial place. According to the Book of Genesis, Abraham bought the field of Ephron the Hittite, together with the cave in it, for four hundred shekels of silver, and buried his wife Sarah there. Abraham himself, Isaac and Rebekah, Jacob and Leah were later buried in the cave according to biblical tradition; Rachel alone of the matriarchs was buried elsewhere, near Bethlehem.

The site

The cave lies beneath a massive rectangular enclosure of finely dressed stone blocks, generally attributed to the reign of Herod the Great in the first century BCE, which still dominates the old city of Hebron. Inside the enclosure stand large cenotaphs commemorating the patriarchs and matriarchs. After the Muslim conquest the structure became a mosque, known as the Ibrahimi Mosque; a church and synagogue occupied it in other periods. Access to the caves themselves has been restricted for centuries; a small opening in the floor is the traditional point of descent.

Significance

The site is revered in Judaism as the second holiest place after the Temple Mount in Jerusalem, and it is also venerated in Islam, where Abraham (Ibrahim) is honoured as a prophet and patriarch. Since 1967 the building has been divided between a mosque and a synagogue, with separate entrances for Muslim and Jewish worshippers, and days of full access allocated to each community. The site has also been a flashpoint of conflict, most tragically in the 1994 massacre in which a Jewish settler shot dead 29 Muslim worshippers. In 2017 UNESCO inscribed the old town of Hebron, including the cave complex, as a Palestinian World Heritage Site. See also the article on Maat for another ancient Near Eastern sacred tradition.
