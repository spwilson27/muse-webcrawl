Bevel

A bevelled edge (UK) or beveled edge (US) is an edge of a structure that is not perpendicular to the faces of the piece — that is, a sloped or angled surface at the edge rather than a square (90°) corner. The words bevel and chamfer overlap in usage: a chamfer is usually understood as a cut at a 45° angle, whereas a bevel may be at any angle and is often larger and more gradual. A bevel that does not extend the entire width of the piece is sometimes called a stopped bevel.

Applications

Bevels are used to soften sharp edges for safety and handling, to reduce stress concentration and prevent chipping, to ease the assembly of mating parts, to prepare metal edges for welding (bevelled groove joints), and for decoration in woodworking, masonry, and glasswork — bevelled glass is a hallmark of mirrors, windows, and Tiffany-style lamps. In machining, bevel gears transmit motion between intersecting shafts, and in manufacturing generally, bevelled edges help parts slide into fixtures and reduce wear. Knife and tool blades are ground to a bevel whose angle trades sharpness against durability, and ski and snowboard edges are bevelled to tune grip and release.

Bevel gears and measurement

Bevel gears are gears with intersecting axes and conical tooth-bearing faces, used for example in differential drives and hand drills. The angle of a bevel is commonly measured with a bevel protractor or a sliding bevel (bevel gauge), an adjustable tool used by carpenters and machinists to copy and transfer angles.
