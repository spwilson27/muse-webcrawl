LNG carrier

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An LNG carrier is a tank ship designed for transporting liquefied natural gas (LNG). LNG is carried at about −162 °C (−260 °F) at atmospheric pressure, reducing the gas to about 1/600 of its gaseous volume and making long-distance transport by sea economical.

Design

Two main containment systems dominate: the self-supporting spherical Moss tanks, recognisable by their aluminium spheres protruding above deck, and membrane systems (such as the GTT Mark III and NO96 designs), in which a thin stainless-steel or Invar membrane backed by insulation is supported by the hull itself, giving typical capacities of 125,000–175,000 m³. The largest vessels, Qatar's Q-Max class, carry about 266,000 m³. Boil-off gas is commonly burned as fuel in the ship's engines.

History and trade

The first ocean-going LNG carrier, the converted Methane Pioneer, sailed from Louisiana to the United Kingdom in 1959, demonstrating the trade's feasibility. Commercial shipments began from Algeria in 1964. The world LNG carrier fleet has grown to several hundred vessels as gas trade expanded, especially to Japan, South Korea, China and Europe. Specialised vessels include floating storage and regasification units (FSRUs), which can receive LNG and deliver gas ashore without a land terminal.
