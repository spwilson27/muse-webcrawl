Derg

The Derg (Amharic: ደርግ, "committee" or "council"), officially the Provisional Military Administrative Council (PMAC), was the military junta that ruled Ethiopia from 1974, after deposing Emperor Haile Selassie and the Ethiopian Empire, until 1987, when the military leadership formally abolished itself in favour of a nominally civilian constitution and established the People's Democratic Republic of Ethiopia under the leadership of Mengistu Haile Mariam, who remained in power until 1991. The Derg implemented a programme of socialism, nationalisation, and land reform, and is associated with the Red Terror campaign and with the mishandling of the 1983–85 famine.

Rise to power

In early 1974, unrest spread through Ethiopia: soldiers mutinied over pay and conditions, teachers and taxi drivers struck, and the government's concealment of a famine in Wollo province discredited Emperor Haile Selassie's regime. In June 1974, representatives of the armed forces formed the Coordinating Committee of the Armed Forces, Police and Territorial Army — the Derg — to present demands. Through the summer the committee steadily arrested officials and dismantled the imperial government, and on 12 September 1974 it deposed Haile Selassie, who died the following year in disputed circumstances. General Aman Andom, the Derg's first chairman, was killed in November 1974 during a struggle for control of the junta ("Bloody Saturday"), and power contests within the committee continued until Mengistu Haile Mariam emerged dominant after the February 1977 shootings of chairman Tafari Benti and others.

Policies

The Derg declared Ethiopia a socialist state, nationalised banks, insurance companies, and major industries, and in March 1975 proclaimed a sweeping land reform that abolished tenancy and redistributed land to peasants — its most popular measure. Urban land and extra housing were nationalised and kebele (neighbourhood) associations created. The regime aligned with the Soviet Union and Cuba, receiving massive military aid, especially during the Ogaden War of 1977–78, when Somalia invaded Ethiopia's Somali region and Soviet- and Cuban-supported Ethiopian forces repelled it. At the same time, the Derg fought internal wars against Eritrean independence movements and the Tigray People's Liberation Front, and conflicts in other regions.

Red Terror and famine

From 1976–78 the Derg waged the Red Terror (Qey Shibir) against rival leftist groups, principally the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Party, and other opponents. Tens of thousands — estimates range widely, commonly from 30,000 to as many as 500,000 — were killed, imprisoned, or tortured; bodies were at times left in public as a warning. In 1983–85, drought combined with the regime's war policies, forced resettlement and villagisation programmes, and restrictions on movement to devastate northern Ethiopia in a famine that killed an estimated 400,000 to one million people and drew global attention through televised reports and the Live Aid concerts. The regime's resettlement programme moved hundreds of thousands from the north to the southwest, with high mortality.

Fall

In 1984 the Derg founded the Workers' Party of Ethiopia, and in 1987 a new constitution created the People's Democratic Republic of Ethiopia with Mengistu as president. But military defeats mounted as Soviet support waned with the Cold War's end. In May 1991, as rebels of the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front advanced on Addis Ababa, Mengistu fled to Zimbabwe; the regime collapsed days later. Mengistu was later convicted in absentia by an Ethiopian court of genocide and sentenced to death; many other Derg officials were tried in lengthy proceedings. The Derg period remains one of the most contested chapters in Ethiopian history, remembered for both its egalitarian land reform and its mass violence.
