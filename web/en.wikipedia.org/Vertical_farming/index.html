Vertical farming
Practice: Growing crops in vertically stacked layers indoors
Methods: Hydroponics, aeroponics, aquaponics
Advantages: Land and water efficiency, year-round production, no pesticides
Drawbacks: High energy and capital costs

Vertical farming is the practice of growing crops in vertically stacked layers, often in controlled-environment agriculture facilities such as warehouses or purpose-built towers, using soilless techniques — hydroponics, aeroponics, or aquaponics — and artificial LED lighting.

Leafy greens under LED lighting in a vertical farm.

Advantages

  • Yield per area: stacking layers can produce many times the crop of the same footprint of farmland.
  • Water: recirculating hydroponic systems use up to ~95% less water than field agriculture.
  • Local and year-round: farms near cities cut transport; controlled conditions eliminate seasons, weather, and most pests, sharply reducing pesticide use.

Challenges

Lighting and climate control consume large amounts of electricity, so vertical farming is generally economical only for high-value, fast-growing crops such as leafy greens and herbs; staple grains remain impractical. The 2020s saw heavy venture investment followed by high-profile failures (e.g., Planty, AeroFarms' bankruptcy restructuring), as energy prices exposed thin margins.
