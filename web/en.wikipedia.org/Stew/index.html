Stew

A beef stew: solid ingredients simmered in liquid until tender.

A stew is a combination of solid food ingredients that have been cooked in liquid and served in the resultant gravy. Ingredients can include any combination of vegetables, meat (especially tougher cuts suited to slow cooking), seafood, beans and flavourings. Stewing is one of the oldest cooking methods, practised since antiquity wherever a fireproof pot was available.

Method

Unlike soup, a stew contains relatively little liquid, which thickens — or is thickened with roux, starch or puréed vegetables — into a sauce coating the ingredients. The food is simmered at low temperature (braising in the oven or on the stovetop), which tenderises connective tissue in meat and melds flavours. Browning meat before stewing adds depth through the Maillard reaction.

Varieties

Notable stews include French boeuf bourguignon and bouillabaisse, Hungarian goulash, Irish stew, Japanese nikujaga and oden, Indian curries, Ethiopian wat, Korean jjigae, and American Brunswick stew and gumbo. Ragù, cassoulet and chili con carne are also forms of stew, and the distinction from braises and soups is often a matter of convention.

History

Evidence of stewing goes back to the earliest pottery; Herodotus described Scythians cooking meat in the animal's own paunch. Medieval potage — a perpetual stew kept simmering and topped up — was a staple of European households for centuries.
