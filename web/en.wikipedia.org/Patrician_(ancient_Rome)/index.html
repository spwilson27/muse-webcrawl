Patrician (ancient Rome)

The patricians (Latin: patricii) were the hereditary aristocratic class of ancient Rome. Membership was restricted to descendants of the original senatorial families (gentes maiores and gentes minores), and patricians enjoyed exclusive privileges in early Roman religion and politics.

Origins

Tradition held that Romulus appointed the first hundred senators, whose descendants formed the patriciate. Patrician families included the Claudii, Cornelii, Fabii, Julii, and Valerii. Originally only patricians could hold magistracies, priesthoods, and seats in the Senate.

Conflict of the Orders

From the early Republic, the plebeians struggled against patrician monopoly in the Conflict of the Orders (c. 494–287 BCE). Reforms opened the consulship (367 BCE), most priesthoods, and finally granted plebiscites binding force through the Lex Hortensia of 287 BCE.

Late Republic and Empire

By the late Republic, wealth and political power mattered more than birth, and emperors could elevate commoners to patrician rank. Under Constantine and his successors, "patrician" became a high court title of honour, surviving in the Byzantine and early medieval West.
