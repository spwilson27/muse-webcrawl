A crucible is a ceramic or metal container in which metals or other substances may be melted or subjected to very high temperatures. While crucibles were historically usually made from clay, they can be made from any material that withstands temperatures high enough to melt or otherwise alter its contents.

History and use

Crucibles have been used since the Chalcolithic and Bronze Age for smelting and casting copper, bronze, gold and silver; archaeologically they are among the key witnesses to early metallurgy. The Huntsman process (1740s) used clay crucibles to produce high-quality crucible steel in Sheffield. Modern laboratory crucibles are made of porcelain, alumina, or platinum and used in gravimetric analysis and ashing; industrial foundry crucibles are typically clay-graphite or silicon carbide and are lifted with tongs or tilted furnaces for pouring.

Material choice depends on temperature and chemical compatibility: platinum crucibles resist many reagents but are costly; porcelain serves to about 1,050 °C; graphite performs well in reducing atmospheres but oxidises in air at high temperature.
