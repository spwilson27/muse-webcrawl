In re Winship

In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), was a United States Supreme Court decision that held that the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt in juvenile delinquency proceedings when a juvenile is charged with an act that would constitute a crime if committed by an adult.

Background

Samuel Winship, a 12-year-old boy in New York, was accused of stealing $112 from a woman's purse — an act that would have been larceny if done by an adult. Under New York's Family Court Act, a judge found delinquency by a "preponderance of the evidence," the civil standard, and expressly noted that proof beyond a reasonable doubt was not required. Winship was ordered to a training school.

Decision

Writing for a 5–3 majority, Justice William J. Brennan Jr. held that the constitutional requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt — "a prime instrument for reducing the risk of convictions resting on factual error" — is fundamental to a fair trial and applies during the adjudicatory stage when a juvenile is charged with an act that would be a crime for an adult. The Court extended the reasonable-doubt standard, already binding on the states in adult criminal cases, to juvenile proceedings.

Significance

In re Winship is a landmark of the Supreme Court's juvenile justice jurisprudence of the 1960s and 1970s, alongside In re Gault (1967), which extended rights to notice, counsel, and confrontation to juveniles, and McKeiver v. Pennsylvania (1971), which declined to extend the right to a jury trial. It constitutionalized the highest burden of proof as a basic protection for accused juveniles.
