Labour theory of value

The labour theory of value holds that a commodity's value derives from the labour required to produce it

The labour theory of value (LTV) is a theory of value that argues the exchange value of a good or service is determined by the total amount of "socially necessary labour" required to produce it. It was central to classical economics and to Karl Marx's critique of political economy, and stands in contrast to the subjective theory of value that underlies modern neoclassical economics.

Classical development

Antecedents appear in John Locke's labour theory of property and in the writings of William Petty. Adam Smith, in The Wealth of Nations (1776), held that in an "early and rude state of society" the value of goods was proportioned to the labour embodied in them, though he also suggested a "labour commanded" measure. David Ricardo, in his Principles of Political Economy and Taxation (1817), refined the embodied-labour account, treating labour as the principal (though not sole) determinant of relative prices.

Marxian formulation

Marx distinguished a commodity's use value from its exchange value and argued that exchange value expresses value, whose substance is abstract human labour and whose magnitude is socially necessary labour time — the labour time required under normal conditions of production with average skill and intensity. On this basis Marx developed his account of surplus value: workers sell their labour power at its value but produce more value than that during the working day, the difference being appropriated by capital as profit. Debates over the "transformation problem" — converting values into prices of production — have occupied Marxian economists since Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk's critique in the 1890s.

Criticism and legacy

From the marginal revolution of the 1870s, mainstream economics largely replaced cost-of-production theories with subjective utility and supply-and-demand analysis; critics contend the LTV cannot explain prices of non-reproducible goods, land, or goods produced with different capital intensities without auxiliary assumptions. The theory remains influential in Marxian economics and heterodox traditions.
