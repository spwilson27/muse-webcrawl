In Norse mythology, Ragnarök (Old Norse: "fate of the gods") is a series of foretold events, including a great battle, that ultimately leads to the death of a number of major figures (including the gods Odin, Thor, Týr, Freyr, and Loki), the occurrence of various natural disasters, and the subsequent submersion of the world in water. After these events, the world is said to resurface anew and fertile, the surviving and returning gods meet, and the world is repopulated by two human survivors.

Ragnarök is an important theme in Norse mythology and has been the subject of scholarly discourse and theory. The events are principally attested in the Poetic Edda, compiled in the 13th century from earlier traditional sources, and in the Prose Edda, written in the 13th century by Snorri Sturluson.

The foretold events

The seeress of the poem Völuspá prophesies a sequence of signs: a three-year winter (Fimbulvetr) without summers, moral breakdown and war among kin, and the breaking of bonds — the wolf Fenrir frees himself, the World Serpent Jörmungandr rises from the sea, and Loki breaks loose from his captivity to steer the ship Naglfar, built from the nails of the dead, carrying the hosts of Hel.

The battle

On the plain of Vígríðr the gods and the einherjar of Valhalla face the giants and monsters. Odin is swallowed by Fenrir and avenged by his son Víðarr, who tears the wolf's jaws apart. Thor kills Jörmungandr but takes nine steps and falls dead from its venom. Freyr, having given away his sword, falls to the fire giant Surtr. Týr and the hound Garmr kill each other, as do Loki and Heimdallr. Finally Surtr flings fire over the earth and the world sinks into the sea.

Aftermath

The earth rises green from the waters. Baldr and Höðr return from the dead; the surviving sons of Thor inherit his hammer Mjölnir. A man and a woman, Líf and Lífþrasir, who hid in Hoddmímis' wood, emerge to repopulate the world, and a new sun — the daughter of the old — shines.

Reception

Ragnarök has inspired artists and writers for centuries, from Richard Wagner's Götterdämmerung (the German rendering of the name) to modern novels, comics, and films.
