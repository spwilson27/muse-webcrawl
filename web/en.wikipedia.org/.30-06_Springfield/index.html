.30-06 Springfield

The .30-06 Springfield, also known in its military designation as the .30-06 U.S. Cartridge Model of 1906, is a centerfire rifle cartridge introduced to the United States Army in 1906. Its name derives from its caliber (.30 inch) and the year of adoption (1906). It served as the standard U.S. military rifle and machine gun cartridge for roughly half a century before being replaced by the 7.62×51mm NATO round in the 1950s.

History

The .30-06 was developed after experience in the Spanish–American War highlighted the shortcomings of the earlier .30-40 Krag cartridge used in the Krag–Jørgensen rifle. The Army adopted the improved .30-03 round in 1903 alongside the M1903 Springfield rifle, then revised the design in 1906 with a lighter, pointed ("spitzer") bullet of 150 grains and a slightly shortened case neck, producing the .30-06.

The cartridge saw extensive service in both World Wars, chambered in the M1903 Springfield, the M1917 Enfield, the M1 Garand, and machine guns such as the Browning M1917 and M1919. It was also used in the Korean War before the adoption of the shorter 7.62×51mm round and the M14 rifle.

Performance

The standard military ball loading fired a 150-grain bullet at a muzzle velocity of approximately 2,740 feet per second (835 m/s), producing on the order of 2,820 foot-pounds of muzzle energy. The later M2 ball loading used a 150-grain flat-base bullet at about 2,740 ft/s. Match and armor-piercing loadings were also produced, including the black-tipped M2 AP round.

Civilian use

The .30-06 remains one of the most popular big-game hunting cartridges in North America, suitable for deer, elk, black bear, and other medium to large game. Its combination of power, flat trajectory, and enormous variety of factory loadings and rifles has kept it in continuous use by hunters and sport shooters for well over a century.
