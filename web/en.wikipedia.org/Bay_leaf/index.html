Bay leaf

The bay leaf is an aromatic leaf commonly used as a herb in cooking. It can be used whole, either dried or fresh, in which case it is usually removed from the dish before consumption, or less commonly in ground form. The term most often refers to the leaves of the bay laurel (Laurus nobilis), but leaves from several other plants are also sold as bay leaves, including the California bay laurel (Umbellifera californica), Indian bay leaf (Cinnamomum tamala) and Indonesian bay leaf (Syzygium polyanthum).

Culinary use

Bay leaves are used to flavour soups, stews, braises, rice dishes and sauces across Mediterranean, European, Indian and other cuisines. They are a component of the French bouquet garni and of Indian spice mixtures. The dried leaf releases its flavour slowly during long cooking; because the leaves remain stiff and can be a choking or digestive hazard, they are typically removed before serving.

Flavour and chemistry

Bay laurel leaves are rich in the aromatic compound 1,8-cineole (eucalyptol), which can make up a large share of their essential oil, along with eugenol and linalool. Other "bay" plants differ chemically: Indian bay leaf has a more cinnamon-like character because it comes from a relative of cinnamon.

History and symbolism

In ancient Greece and Rome, wreaths of laurel symbolised victory and honour, and the term "baccalaureate" and the phrase "resting on one's laurels" derive from this tradition. Bay leaves have also been used in folk medicine and as a stored-grain insect deterrent.
