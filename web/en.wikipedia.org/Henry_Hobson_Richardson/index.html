Henry Hobson Richardson (September 29, 1838 – April 27, 1886) was an American architect, best known for his work in a style that became known as Richardsonian Romanesque. He is regarded as one of the most important American architects of the nineteenth century, and his Trinity Church in Boston was voted among the ten most significant buildings in the United States.

Early life and education

Richardson was born on Priestley Plantation in St. James Parish, Louisiana, and raised in New Orleans. He attended Harvard College, graduating in 1859, and then travelled to Paris, where he was admitted to the École des Beaux-Arts — only the second American, after Richard Morris Hunt, to study there. The American Civil War cut off his family funding, and he supported himself working in Paris architectural offices, including that of Théodore Labrouste.

Career

Returning to the United States in 1865, Richardson settled in New York and won the competition for the Church of the Unity in Springfield, Massachusetts. His breakthrough came with the commission for Trinity Church, Boston (1872–1877), a polychromatic Romanesque Revival design with a massive central tower. In 1874 he moved his practice and family to Brookline, Massachusetts.

Style and major works

Mature works such as the Allegheny County Courthouse and Jail in Pittsburgh (1884–1888), the Marshall Field Wholesale Store in Chicago (1885–1887), the Ames Gate Lodge in North Easton, Massachusetts, and a series of small suburban railroad stations and libraries display his characteristic vocabulary: rock-faced rusticated stone, deep round arches, cavernous entrances, and heavy, sculptural massing. His style was widely imitated across the United States and Canada in the 1880s and 1890s.

Death and legacy

Richardson died of Bright's disease in 1886 at the age of 47, at the height of his career. His practice was continued by the successor firm Shepley, Rutan and Coolidge. His influence shaped architects including Louis Sullivan and influenced the development of modern architecture.
