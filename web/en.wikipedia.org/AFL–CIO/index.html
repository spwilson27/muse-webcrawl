The American Federation of Labor and Congress of Industrial Organizations (AFL–CIO) is the largest federation of labor unions in the United States. It is a voluntary federation of national and international unions, whose affiliated members together represent about 12.5 million workers.

History

The federation was created in 1955 by the merger of two rival centres: the American Federation of Labor (AFL), founded in 1886 under Samuel Gompers and dominated by craft unions, and the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO), which broke away in 1935 under John L. Lewis to organize workers industrially in mass-production industries. The merger, led by George Meany (its president until 1979) and Walter Reuther, reunified most of the US labor movement at the peak of union density.

Role and politics

The AFL–CIO does not bargain directly; it coordinates political action, lobbying, and organizing support among affiliates, which include unions such as the AFSCME, the teachers' unions (AFT and NEA is independent), and the United Steelworkers. In 2005 several large unions (including the Teamsters and SEIU) split to form the rival Change to Win federation, though some later returned. The federation traditionally allies with the Democratic Party and campaigns on labor law reform, minimum wages, trade, and workplace safety.
