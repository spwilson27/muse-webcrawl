Low-key lighting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Low-key lighting is a style of lighting for photography, film and television in which a scene is predominantly dark, with a high ratio between the key light and the fill light. Low-key images are characterised by large areas of shadow, strong contrast, and muted or absent mid-tones in much of the frame. The opposite approach is high-key lighting, in which the scene is brightly and evenly lit with minimal shadow.

Technique

In a conventional three-point lighting setup — key light, fill light and back light — low-key work uses a powerful key light with little or no fill, producing lighting ratios of 8:1 or higher (compared with roughly 2:1 for high-key). Shadows are allowed to fall to black, and the subject is often rim-lit or lit from the side so that only part of the face or object is visible. Cinematographers may add a weak back light or "kicker" for separation from dark backgrounds, and use flags, grids and negative fill to control spill. In photography, low-key portraits are often shot against black backdrops with a single directional source, sometimes stopped down or filtered to deepen the blacks.

History and use

The style descends from chiaroscuro in Renaissance and Baroque painting — the dramatic contrast of light and dark used by Caravaggio and Rembrandt — and from German Expressionist cinema of the 1920s. It became a signature of film noir in 1940s Hollywood, where cinematographers such as John Alton used hard, low-key lighting to evoke moral ambiguity, danger and psychological tension on low budgets. Low-key lighting remains standard in horror, thriller and crime genres, and in moody portrait and product photography. Modern practitioners include cinematographers who "light for darkness", exposing for highlights and letting shadows crush — a look associated with films such as The Godfather (1972), shot by Gordon Willis, and the chiaroscuro interiors of many neo-noirs.
