Panchakarma

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Panchakarma (Sanskrit: पञ्चकर्म, "five actions" or "five treatments") is a method of cleansing and rejuvenation in Ayurveda, the traditional system of medicine of India. It is described in the classical Ayurvedic texts, including the Charaka Samhita, as a set of procedures intended to eliminate accumulated toxins (ama) and restore balance among the three doshas: vata, pitta and kapha.

The five procedures

The classical five actions are:

  1. Vamana – therapeutic vomiting, used principally to expel excess kapha.
  2. Virechana – therapeutic purgation, used to clear excess pitta from the digestive tract.
  3. Basti – medicated enema, considered the most important of the five for disorders of vata; it is administered in forms using decoctions (niruha) or oils (anuvasana).
  4. Nasya – administration of medicated oils or powders through the nasal passages, for disorders of the head and neck.
  5. Raktamokshana – therapeutic bloodletting, traditionally by leeches or venesection, now rarely practised.

Stages

A full course of Panchakarma is traditionally given in three stages. The preparatory stage, purvakarma, includes oleation (snehana), in which oils or ghee are applied externally or taken internally, and sudation (svedana), the induction of sweating, intended to loosen toxins. The main procedures follow, and the course concludes with paschatkarma, a period of dietary regulation and gradual return to normal activity.

Practice and evidence

Panchakarma is widely practised in Ayurvedic hospitals and clinics in India and in wellness centres elsewhere, usually over courses lasting one to several weeks under the supervision of an Ayurvedic physician. Scientific evidence for its efficacy is limited; some procedures, such as induced vomiting, purgation and bloodletting, can pose risks if performed without proper medical supervision, and practitioners caution against its use in pregnancy, severe debility and certain chronic conditions.
