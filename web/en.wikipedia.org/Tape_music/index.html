Tape music

Tape music is an early form of electronic and electroacoustic music created by recording, editing, and manipulating sounds on magnetic tape. Developed in the late 1940s and 1950s, it allowed composers to work directly with recorded sound — splicing, looping, reversing, changing speed, and layering — producing fixed works intended for playback over loudspeakers rather than live performance of a score.

Origins

Tape music grew out of two post-war schools. In Paris, Pierre Schaeffer founded musique concrète at French radio (RTF) in 1948, composing studies from recorded everyday sounds, initially on disc and then tape. In Cologne, the WDR studio of Herbert Eimert and Karlheinz Stockhausen pursued elektronische Musik, generating tones electronically and structuring them serially. The two approaches converged in works such as Stockhausen's Gesang der Jünglinge (1956), which fused voices and synthesized sound.

Centres and composers

In the United States, Vladimir Ussachevsky and Otto Luening demonstrated tape composition at Columbia in 1952, leading to the Columbia-Princeton Electronic Music Center (1959), where Milton Babbitt composed with the RCA Mark II synthesizer. Edgard Varèse's Déserts (1954) and Poème électronique (1958) were landmarks. Studios at the BBC Radiophonic Workshop, NHK in Tokyo, and in Milan made tape the medium of the post-war avant-garde.

Techniques and legacy

Core techniques — the splice, the tape loop (boucle), variable-speed transposition, reversal, and echo — prefigured sampling, turntablism, and digital audio editing. Steve Reich's early loop pieces ("It's Gonna Rain", 1965) and the Beatles' tape experiments on "Tomorrow Never Knows" (1966) carried tape methods into minimalism and popular music. By the 1970s, digital synthesis and later the DAW absorbed tape music into broader electroacoustic practice.
