Put and take

Put and take is a gambling game played with a many-sided spinning top known as a put-and-take top (or teetotum). The game was especially popular in Britain and the United States during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, when put-and-take tops were commonly made of brass and carried in the pocket for impromptu wagering in pubs and on street corners. It is a game of pure chance, closely related to the dreidel game played at Hanukkah.

Play

Each player contributes an equal stake to a central pool. Players take turns spinning the top; when it falls, the letter or instruction uppermost determines the outcome. A typical six-sided top is marked: Put One, Put Two, Take One, Take Two, Take All, and All Put (every player puts another stake into the pool). "Take All" wins the entire pool, after which all players re-ante to start a new round. Eight- and ten-sided tops add further instructions such as "Put Three" or "Take Three".

History

Spinning tops with instructions on their faces date back centuries and were used for games of chance in many cultures. The put-and-take form became a craze in the 1890s and again in the 1920s; during the First World War, versions of the game were popular among soldiers. Because it is unambiguously a gambling device, put-and-take tops were periodically targeted by anti-gambling campaigns and local ordinances, and they were sometimes disguised or marketed as novelties. Antique brass put-and-take tops are now collected as folk and gambling memorabilia.
