Behavioral modernity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Behavioral modernity is a suite of behavioural and cognitive traits believed to distinguish anatomically modern humans (Homo sapiens) from other archaic humans and primates, including abstract thinking, planning depth, symbolic behaviour, music and dance, long-distance trade, and elaborate tool technologies.

Origins debate

One model holds that behavioural modernity emerged suddenly in Europe around 40,000–50,000 years ago in an Upper Palaeolithic "revolution" associated with cave art, ornamentation and blade tools. An alternative model argues the traits accumulated gradually in Africa over tens of thousands of years, citing evidence such as the engraved ochre and shell beads from Blombos Cave (c. 75,000 years old) and heat treatment of stone at Pinnacle Point.

Evidence

Archaeological markers include personal ornaments, figurative art, bone tools, microliths, long-distance exchange of materials, and structured hearths. Whether modern-looking behaviour appeared as a package or piecemeal remains debated.
