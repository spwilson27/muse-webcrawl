Dengue fever is a mosquito-borne disease caused by dengue virus, one of four (or five) related serotypes. It is transmitted principally by Aedes aegypti mosquitoes and is common in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide. The World Health Organization estimates tens of millions of infections occur each year, with numbers rising sharply in recent decades.

Signs and symptoms

Many infections are asymptomatic. When symptoms occur they typically begin 4–10 days after infection and include high fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, muscle and joint pain (giving the disease its nickname "breakbone fever"), nausea and a characteristic rash. A small proportion of cases progress to severe dengue, with plasma leakage, bleeding and organ impairment, which can be fatal without treatment.

Prevention and treatment

There is no specific antiviral treatment; care is supportive, centred on fluid replacement and paracetamol, while aspirin and other NSAIDs are avoided because of bleeding risk. Prevention relies on mosquito control and personal protection. Vaccines have been licensed in some countries, with use restricted by serostatus and local epidemiology because infection with one serotype followed by another can increase the risk of severe disease through antibody-dependent enhancement.
