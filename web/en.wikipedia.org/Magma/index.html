Magma

Magma (from Ancient Greek μάγμα, mágma, "thick unguent") is the molten or semi-molten natural material from which all igneous rocks are formed. Magma is found beneath the surface of the Earth, and evidence of magmatism has also been discovered on other terrestrial planets and some natural satellites. Besides molten rock, magma may also contain suspended crystals and gas bubbles.

Magma that reaches the surface is called lava.

Physical and chemical properties

Magma consists of liquid rock, typically silicate in composition, with temperatures generally between 700 and 1,300 °C. Its viscosity varies enormously with silica content: basaltic magmas (about 50% SiO2) flow readily, whereas rhyolitic magmas (over 70% SiO2) are extremely viscous. Dissolved volatiles—chiefly water and carbon dioxide—greatly influence eruption behaviour.

Formation

Magma forms by partial melting of the mantle or crust, mainly through three mechanisms: decompression melting at mid-ocean ridges and mantle plumes; flux melting where water lowers the melting point of rock in subduction zones; and heat-transfer melting where hot magma intrudes cooler crust.

Magma chambers and eruption

Buoyant magma rises and may accumulate in magma chambers, where it can fractionally crystallise, assimilate wall rock and mix with other magmas before either solidifying underground as intrusive (plutonic) rock or erupting at a volcano. Once magma reaches the surface it is termed lava; its solidified products form extrusive (volcanic) rocks.

Types

  • Basaltic (mafic) magma – low silica, low viscosity
  • Andesitic (intermediate) magma
  • Rhyolitic (felsic) magma – high silica, high viscosity
  • Ultramafic magma, including komatiites of the early Earth
