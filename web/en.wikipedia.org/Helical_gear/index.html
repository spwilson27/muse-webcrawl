Helical gear

This article is about gears with angled teeth. For the related worm drive, see Worm gear.

Helical gears connected on parallel shafts, showing the angled teeth that distinguish them from spur gears.

A helical gear is a type of cylindrical gear in which the teeth are cut at an angle to the face of the gear, forming segments of a helix. When two helical gear teeth engage, the contact starts at one end of the tooth and gradually spreads as the gear rotates, until the two teeth are in full engagement. This gradual engagement makes helical gears operate much more smoothly and quietly than spur gears, which is why they are used in almost all car transmissions and many other high-speed applications.

The angled teeth also engage more gradually than spur gear teeth, causing them to run more smoothly and quietly. With parallel helical gears, each pair of teeth first makes contact at a single point on one side of the gear wheel; a moving curve of contact then grows gradually across the tooth face to a maximum and then recedes. The final contact is therefore not a sudden line across the whole face width, as with spur gears, but a progressive engagement.

Advantages and disadvantages

The angled teeth of helical gears produce axial thrust loads when they mesh, requiring thrust bearings to absorb the axial forces. However, the smooth engagement allows higher loads and higher speeds than equivalent spur gears. Helical gears can also be used between non-parallel shafts — in this configuration they are called crossed helical gears or screw gears — although the contact is a point rather than a line and the load capacity is lower.

The disadvantage of the axial thrust can be removed by using a double helical gear, also called a herringbone gear, which has two sets of teeth cut at opposite angles on the same blank, so that the axial forces cancel. A herringbone gear has a groove in the middle where the two helices meet, whereas a double helical gear in the strict sense is made from two separate helical gears of opposite hand mounted together.

Manufacture

Helical gears are typically manufactured by hobbing, shaping, or milling. The helix angle is usually between 15° and 30°; a helix angle of 45° gives the maximum efficiency for crossed helical gears. The normal module and normal pressure angle are the standard values used for the cutting tools, and the transverse values follow from the helix angle by geometric relations.

Applications

Helical gears are the most common gears used in transmissions, including automotive gearboxes, because they operate quietly, particularly at high speeds, and can handle greater loads than comparably sized spur gears. They are also used in industrial machinery, printing presses, and marine propulsion systems.
