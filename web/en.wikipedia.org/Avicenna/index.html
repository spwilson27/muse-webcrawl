Avicenna

Born: c. 980, Afshana, near Bukhara, Samanid Empire

Died: June 1037, Hamadan, Kakuyid Emirate

Era: Islamic Golden Age

Fields: Medicine, philosophy, logic, astronomy, physics

Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā, c. 980 – June 1037) was a Persian Muslim polymath regarded as one of the most significant physicians, astronomers, philosophers and writers of the Islamic Golden Age. Often called the "father of early modern medicine", he wrote some 200 works, of which about half survive.

Major works

His Canon of Medicine (al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb, 1025) systematised medical knowledge from Greek, Indian and Persian sources, described contagious diseases, quarantine and clinical trials, and remained a standard textbook in European universities into the 17th century. The Book of Healing (Kitāb al-Shifā) — a vast scientific and philosophical encyclopedia — covers logic, physics, mathematics and metaphysics, while its summary, The Book of Salvation, and his later Pointers and Reminders refined his thought.

Philosophy

Avicenna's metaphysics distinguishes essence from existence and argues for a Necessary Existent (God) whose existence is identical with its essence. His "Flying Man" thought experiment — a person suspended in air, deprived of all senses, would still affirm his own existence — anticipates later arguments about self-consciousness. He synthesised Aristotelian and Neoplatonic thought, profoundly influencing Islamic philosophy and Latin scholasticism, including Aquinas.

Life

A child prodigy who had memorised the Quran by age ten, Avicenna served as court physician and vizier under several rulers, travelling between Bukhara, Gorgan, Ray, Hamadan and Isfahan amid the era's political upheavals. He died in Hamadan in 1037; his mausoleum stands there today.
