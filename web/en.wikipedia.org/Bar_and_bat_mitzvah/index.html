Bar and bat mitzvah

A bar mitzvah (for a boy) or bat mitzvah (for a girl) is a Jewish coming-of-age ritual. According to Jewish law, when Jewish children reach the age of majority — traditionally 13 for boys and 12 or 13 for girls — they become responsible for their own actions and accountable for observing the commandments (mitzvot). The terms mean "son of the commandment" and "daughter of the commandment" respectively.

The ceremony

Traditionally the bar mitzvah boy is called up to the Torah for an aliyah — reciting blessings over the reading — during a Shabbat or weekday morning service, and often chants the week's Torah portion and the accompanying haftarah (a reading from the Prophets). He may also lead parts of the service and deliver a short speech or d'var Torah. The bat mitzvah developed in the twentieth century — the first widely recognised American bat mitzvah was that of Judith Kaplan in 1922 — and in Reform, Reconstructionist, and most Conservative congregations girls mark the occasion in essentially the same way as boys; practices in Orthodox communities vary and are often marked with a synagogue ceremony or festive meal rather than a Torah reading by the girl.

Celebration

After the religious service, families commonly host a celebratory meal or party (seudat mitzvah). In North America especially, receptions can be elaborate affairs with music, dancing, and gifts — traditionally money in multiples of 18, the numerical value of chai ("life"). Many communities encourage a charitable or social-action project as part of the preparation, and preparation itself typically involves a year or more of study of Hebrew, prayer, and the assigned Torah portion.

Significance

Strictly, a Jewish child becomes bar or bat mitzvah automatically upon reaching the prescribed age, whether or not a ceremony is held; the ceremony publicly marks the transition. From that point the young person counts in a minyan (prayer quorum) and assumes adult religious obligations such as fasting on Yom Kippur.
