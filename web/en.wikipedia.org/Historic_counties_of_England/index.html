Historic counties of England

The historic counties of England are areas of England that were established for administration by the Normans, in many cases based on earlier kingdoms and shires established by the Anglo-Saxons and others. They are alternatively known as the ancient counties or traditional counties. There are 39 historic counties, whose origins can be traced back more than a thousand years in some cases.

History

The system of shires developed during the Anglo-Saxon period, as kingdoms such as Wessex, Mercia and Northumbria were divided into administrative districts governed by a shire reeve (sheriff) under an ealdorman. After the Norman Conquest the shires were retained and gradually became known by the French-derived term "county". Some counties, such as those of the Welsh Marches and the palatinates of Chester, Durham and Lancaster, enjoyed special privileges.

Changes in administration

During the nineteenth century, elected county councils were created in 1889, and the administrative counties they governed began to diverge from the historic counties. Major reorganisations followed in 1965 (in London) and under the Local Government Act 1972, which in 1974 abolished many familiar counties for administrative purposes, created new metropolitan counties, and redrew boundaries, notably in the north-west, the north-east and around Bristol. Despite these changes, the historic counties were never formally abolished in law, and they continue to be used for cultural and sporting purposes, in postal history, and as the basis for county flags and local identity.

Cultural role

County cricket has been organised on historic-county lines since the nineteenth century, and bodies such as the Association of British Counties campaign for the use of the historic counties in addressing, signage and public life. In 2019 the UK government formally recognised the historic counties as an important part of English heritage and identity.
