Parietal lobe

The parietal lobe is one of the four major lobes of the cerebral cortex in the brain of mammals. It is positioned above the temporal lobe and behind the frontal lobe and central sulcus. The parietal lobe integrates sensory information from different modalities, especially the sense of spatial position and navigation (proprioception), the main sensory receptive area for the sense of touch (somatosensation) in the somatosensory cortex, and the dorsal stream of the visual system. It also plays important roles in language processing and in mathematical cognition.

Anatomy

The parietal lobe is bounded in front by the central sulcus, below by the lateral sulcus, and behind by an imaginary line from the parieto-occipital sulcus. Its major gyri include the postcentral gyrus (containing the primary somatosensory cortex), and the superior and inferior parietal lobules, the latter comprising the supramarginal and angular gyri. The name derives from the parietal bone of the skull, which overlies it.

Function

The somatosensory cortex maps touch, temperature, pain, and body position in the form of the sensory homunculus. Posterior parietal areas combine vision and proprioception to guide reaching and grasping, contribute to spatial attention, and support aspects of number processing and calculation. Damage to the parietal lobe can produce characteristic deficits: lesions of the non-dominant (usually right) parietal lobe can cause hemispatial neglect, in which a patient ignores one side of space; lesions involving the angular gyrus can contribute to Gerstmann syndrome, with agraphia, acalculia, finger agnosia, and left–right confusion; and bilateral damage can cause Balint syndrome, with simultanagnosia and optic ataxia.
