Constructive proof

In mathematics, a constructive proof is a method of proof that demonstrates the existence of a mathematical object by creating or providing a method for creating the object. This is in contrast to a non-constructive proof (also known as an existence proof or pure existence theorem), which proves the existence of a particular kind of object without providing an example.

Example

A classic illustration is the theorem that there exist irrational numbers a and b such that ab is rational. The famous non-constructive proof considers √2√2: if it is rational, take a = b = √2; otherwise take a = √2√2 and b = √2, giving (√2√2)√2 = 2, rational. The proof shows one of two cases works but does not say which (although the Gelfond–Schneider theorem later established that √2√2 is in fact irrational, indeed transcendental). A constructive proof instead gives explicit numbers, e.g. a = √2 and b = 2·log2 3, since √22 log2 3 = 3.

Constructivism and intuitionistic logic

Constructive proofs are required in constructive mathematics and intuitionistic logic, which reject the unrestricted law of excluded middle. Under the Curry–Howard correspondence, a constructive proof of an existence statement corresponds to a program that computes a witness. Philosophers and mathematicians such as L. E. J. Brouwer, Arend Heyting, and Errett Bishop developed the constructivist programme; in Bishop's constructive analysis, numerical meaning is paramount: a proof that an object exists must in principle allow one to find it.
