Anaximenes of Miletus

Anaximenes of Miletus (c. 586 – c. 526 BCE) was an Ancient Greek Pre-Socratic philosopher active in the late 6th century BCE, the third and last of the Milesian school. He is best known for his doctrine that air (aer) is the arche, the originating substance of all things, and for explaining change through the processes of rarefaction and condensation.

Life

Little is known of Anaximenes' life. He was a younger contemporary and associate of Anaximander, and according to tradition he was a pupil of Anaximander, following Thales as the third thinker of Miletus. He is said to have flourished around the time of the fall of Sardis (546/5 BCE).

Philosophy

Where Thales had proposed water and Anaximander the indefinite (apeiron) as the first principle, Anaximenes chose air — a definite, observable substance that he regarded as encompassing the world and sustaining life, analogous to the soul (psyche) holding the body together. From air, all things arise by rarefaction and condensation: rarefied, air becomes fire; condensed, it becomes wind, cloud, water, earth, and finally stone. Anaximenes gave a quantitative, mechanical account of qualitative differences, a notable advance in physical explanation.

Astronomy and meteorology

Anaximenes held that the Earth is flat, shaped like a table, and rides on air like a lid. The heavenly bodies were fiery and also flat, floating on air. He explained rain and hail as products of condensation, and associated earthquakes with periods of drought cracking the earth.

Legacy

Only fragments of his thought survive, mainly through later doxographers such as Theophrastus. His condensation–rarefaction theory influenced later Greek natural philosophy, and his use of analogy between the cosmos and the human soul ("as our soul, being air, holds us together, so breath and air encompass the whole world") is one of the earliest statements of the microcosm–macrocosm parallel.
