Longevity

Jeanne Calment (1875–1997), the oldest verified human, lived 122 years

Longevity may refer to a population's life expectancy or to an individual's long lifespan, especially beyond the typical expectation. The longest verified human lifespan is that of Jeanne Calment of France (1875–1997), who lived 122 years and 164 days. People who live to 100 are called centenarians; those reaching 110 are supercentenarians.

Determinants

Studies of twins and families suggest genetics account for roughly 20–30% of variation in human lifespan, with the heritable component growing in importance at extreme ages — siblings of centenarians are far more likely than average to reach 100 themselves. Environmental and lifestyle factors dominate for most people: not smoking, moderate alcohol use, regular physical activity, maintaining a healthy weight, diet, education and socioeconomic status all correlate with longer life. So-called "Blue Zones" — regions such as Okinawa, Sardinia and Ikaria with unusually many centenarians — have popularised Mediterranean- and plant-rich diets, strong social ties and habitual low-intensity activity as longevity practices.

Biology of ageing

Biogerontology studies the mechanisms of ageing, including telomere shortening, accumulated DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence and epigenetic change. Longevity research examines interventions shown to extend lifespan in model organisms — caloric restriction, rapamycin, metformin and senolytic drugs — and candidate longevity genes such as FOXO3 and APOE variants. Maximum lifespan varies enormously across species, from weeks in insects to centuries in the Greenland shark and ocean quahog.

Historical trends

Global average life expectancy roughly doubled during the 20th century, from about 32 years in 1900 to over 70 by the 2000s, driven mainly by reduced child mortality, sanitation, nutrition and medicine. Whether human maximum lifespan is approaching a fixed limit remains debated among demographers.
