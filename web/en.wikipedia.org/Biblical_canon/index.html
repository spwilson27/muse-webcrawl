A biblical canon is a set of texts (also called "books") which a particular Jewish or Christian religious community regards as part of the Bible — that is, as authoritative scripture. The term derives from the Greek kanōn, a measuring rod or standard. Different traditions accept different canons: the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), the Protestant Old Testament, the Catholic and Orthodox Old Testaments (which include the deuterocanonical books), and the distinct canons of Oriental Orthodox churches such as the Ethiopian Tewahedo canon, the largest of all, with 81 books.

Development

The canon developed over many centuries. The Torah was broadly fixed by the Persian period; the Prophets by about the second century BC; and the Writings remained fluid longer, with the Hebrew canon generally regarded as closed by around the end of the first century AD. In Christianity, lists of authoritative New Testament books circulated from the second century; the first list matching exactly the twenty-seven books now universally accepted in Western Christianity appears in Athanasius's Festal Letter of AD 367. Regional councils at Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) affirmed a canon including the deuterocanon.

Differences between traditions

During the Reformation, Martin Luther and other Protestants restricted the Old Testament to the books of the Hebrew canon, reclassifying the deuterocanonical books (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and 1–2 Maccabees, among others) as "Apocrypha". The Council of Trent (1546) responded by dogmatically defining the Catholic canon. Eastern Orthodox churches accept additional books, such as 3 Maccabees and Psalm 151, while the Ethiopian canon includes Enoch and Jubilees — works preserved in full only in Ge'ez.
