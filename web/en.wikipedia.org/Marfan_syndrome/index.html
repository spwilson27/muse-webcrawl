Marfan syndrome

SpecialtyMedical genetics, cardiology
CauseMutation in FBN1 gene
InheritanceAutosomal dominant
Frequency1 in 5,000–10,000

Marfan syndrome (MFS) is a genetic disorder that affects the connective tissue. People with the condition tend to be tall and thin, with long arms, legs, fingers and toes. They may also have a flexible joints and scoliosis.

Signs and symptoms

The most serious complications involve the heart and aorta, with an increased risk of mitral valve prolapse and aortic aneurysm or dissection. The lungs, eyes, bones and spinal cord covering may also be affected. Ocular features include dislocation of the lens of the eye (ectopia lentis) and severe myopia.

Cause

Marfan syndrome is caused by a mutation in FBN1, the gene encoding fibrillin-1, a glycoprotein essential for the formation of elastic fibres in connective tissue. It is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, although about a quarter of cases arise from a new mutation. The condition is named after Antoine Marfan, the French paediatrician who first described it in 1896.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on the revised Ghent criteria, combining clinical features across organ systems, aortic root measurements, genetic testing and family history.

Management

There is no cure, but regular cardiovascular monitoring, beta blockers or angiotensin receptor blockers to slow aortic dilation, avoidance of strenuous isometric exercise, and prophylactic aortic surgery when the root enlarges can bring life expectancy close to normal.
