External audit

An external audit is an independent examination of an organization's financial statements, performed by an auditor who is not an employee of the organization, in order to express an opinion on whether the statements present fairly, in all material respects, the organization's financial position and performance in accordance with an applicable accounting framework such as GAAP or IFRS.

Scope and process

External audits are required by law for public companies and many other entities. The auditor plans the engagement around assessed risks of material misstatement, tests internal controls, and gathers evidence through inspection, confirmation with third parties, recalculation, and analytical procedures. At the conclusion, the auditor issues a report: an unqualified (clean) opinion, or a qualified, adverse, or disclaimed opinion if problems are found. In the United States, audits of public companies are overseen by the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB), and professional standards are set by bodies such as the AICPA and the IAASB.

Distinctions

An external audit differs from an internal audit, which is conducted by an organization's own staff to evaluate controls and operations, and from a review or compilation, which provide lower levels of assurance. Concerns about auditor independence — sharpened by collapses such as Enron and the failure of Arthur Andersen in 2001–2002 — led to reforms including the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002, which restricts non-audit services and requires rotation of lead audit partners. Most large-company audits worldwide are performed by the "Big Four" firms: Deloitte, PwC, EY, and KPMG.
