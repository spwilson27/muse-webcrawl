Poynting–Robertson effect

Poynting–Robertson effect
Named afterJohn Henry Poynting; Howard P. Robertson
First described1903 (Poynting); 1937 (Robertson)
FieldCelestial mechanics, astrophysics

The Poynting–Robertson effect, also known as Poynting–Robertson drag, is a process by which solar radiation causes a dust grain orbiting a star to lose angular momentum relative to its orbit around the star. It is related to radiation pressure tangent to the grain's motion.

This makes dust that moves in a spiral slowly inwards to the star. This effect was first described in 1903 by English physicist John Henry Poynting, who used Maxwell's theory of electromagnetism to show that a small body absorbing and re-radiating heat from the Sun would be subject to the "stellar radiation pressure". In 1937, Howard P. Robertson revisited the phenomenon using general relativity.

Explanation

The drag can be understood as a consequence of the aberration of light: in the rest frame of the dust particle, sunlight appears to come from slightly ahead due to the particle's orbital motion. Because the re-radiated light is isotropic in the particle's frame but the absorbed light carries momentum in a slightly sunward-tilted direction, the net effect includes a force component opposing the grain's motion around the star. This tangential force removes orbital angular momentum, causing the grain to spiral inward.

Applications

The effect plays an important role in the dynamics of dust in the Solar System. It is responsible for the continual depletion of the zodiacal cloud, sweeping dust grains into the Sun on timescales of tens of thousands to millions of years depending on grain size, and it affects the evolution of planetary rings and asteroid dust bands. It also matters for the interpretation of debris disks around other stars.
