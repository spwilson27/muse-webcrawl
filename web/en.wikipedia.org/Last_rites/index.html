Last rites

Last rites
TraditionPrimarily Roman Catholic; analogues in Eastern Christianity and some other churches
ElementsPenance (confession), Anointing of the Sick, Viaticum (final Communion), Apostolic Pardon
Administered toThe dying, or those in danger of death

The last rites, also called the Commendation of the Dying, are the prayers and ministrations given to an individual of the Christian faith, when possible, shortly before death. In the Roman Catholic Church the term most properly refers to the combination of three sacraments administered in sequence to a person in danger of death: Penance (confession), the Anointing of the Sick, and Viaticum — Holy Communion given as "food for the journey" — together with the Apostolic Pardon.

Practice

Popular usage often equates the last rites simply with the Anointing of the Sick (formerly called Extreme Unction), but since the Second Vatican Council the anointing is intended for serious illness generally, not only the point of death, while Viaticum is regarded as the proper sacrament of the dying. The rites conclude with prayers of commendation asking the saints and angels to receive the departing soul. Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox churches have analogous ministrations, including confession, communion, and (in some traditions) the office of Holy Unction; many Protestant traditions retain bedside prayer and commendation without sacramental anointing.

Cultural usage

The phrase has passed into general English: to "read someone the last rites" or declare something's "last rites" is a metaphor for pronouncing a final, irrevocable end. The administration of the rites to executed prisoners and to the mortally wounded in war by military chaplains is a familiar theme in literature and film.
