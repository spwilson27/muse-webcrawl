Impossible object

An impossible object (also called an impossible figure or undecidable figure) is a type of optical illusion consisting of a two-dimensional figure that is readily interpreted by the visual system as representing a three-dimensional object which could not physically exist. Familiar examples include the Penrose triangle, the Penrose stairs and the blivet.

History

The Swedish artist Oscar Reutersvärd drew an early impossible triangle in 1934. In the 1950s the physicist Roger Penrose and his father Lionel Penrose popularised the triangle and staircase, and the Dutch artist M. C. Escher built celebrated works such as Ascending and Descending and Waterfall around them.

Constructing impossible objects

Although such objects cannot exist as ordinary solids in Euclidean three-dimensional space, carefully crafted models can appear impossible from one specific viewpoint, because the eye is deceived about the object's true geometry.

In psychology

Impossible figures are studied in perceptual psychology because they reveal how the visual system constructs a three-dimensional interpretation from flat retinal images.
