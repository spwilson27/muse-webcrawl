An arsenate is any compound containing the arsenate ion, AsO43−, a tetrahedral oxyanion in which arsenic is in the +5 oxidation state. Arsenates closely resemble phosphates in structure and chemistry, and salts of arsenic acid (H3AsO4) — a weak triprotic acid with pKa values of about 2.1, 6.8 and 11.8 — are widely distributed in minerals and soils.

Occurrence

Arsenate minerals include erythrite (hydrated cobalt arsenate, "cobalt bloom"), scorodite (hydrated iron arsenate) and olivenite (copper arsenate hydroxide). Arsenate also forms naturally by oxidation of arsenic-bearing sulphide ores and enters groundwater in many parts of the world, notably South and Southeast Asia.

Chemistry and biology

Because arsenate mimics phosphate, it is taken up by phosphate transporters and interferes with oxidative phosphorylation — a process called arsenolysis — in which arsenate substitutes for phosphate in forming ATP analogues that hydrolyse immediately, uncoupling energy production. This similarity underlies much of arsenate's toxicity, and also its historical use in herbicides and wood preservatives such as chromated copper arsenate (CCA).

Applications

Lead arsenate and calcium arsenate were once common insecticides, and copper arsenate compounds remain important in treated timber, though their use is increasingly restricted. Some bacteria can respire arsenate, reducing it to arsenite, and a few organisms even incorporate arsenate into biomolecules, though a 2010 claim of arsenic-based DNA in the bacterium GFAJ-1 was subsequently refuted.
