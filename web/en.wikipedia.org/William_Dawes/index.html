William Dawes Jr. (6 April 1745 – 25 February 1799) was an American tanner and militia officer known for his role in the "Midnight Ride" of April 1775. On the night of 18–19 April 1775, Dawes rode from Boston by the land route over Boston Neck to Lexington and Concord to warn John Hancock and Samuel Adams and rouse the colonial militia that British regulars were marching to seize military stores at Concord. His ride paralleled the better-known water-route ride of Paul Revere.

The ride

Dawes, who regularly passed in and out of Boston on business and was less likely to be stopped, was dispatched by Dr Joseph Warren by the southern land route, slipping through the British sentries at Boston Neck just before the gates closed. He reached Lexington about half an hour after Revere, rousing Hancock and Adams. Dawes and Revere then rode on together toward Concord with the physician Samuel Prescott. Near Lincoln, a British patrol captured Revere; Dawes escaped but was unhorsed and forced to walk back, while Prescott alone carried the alarm to Concord.

Later life and legacy

Dawes served as a quartermaster in the Continental Army and fought at the Battle of Bunker Hill. After the war he returned to his tannery and grocery business in Boston, later moving to Marlborough, where he died in 1799. Although Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's 1860 poem made Revere famous, historians note that Dawes — whose descendants campaigned for recognition in the 1890s — completed the same mission by the more dangerous route. He is commemorated by a statue at Lexington and by Dawes Island in Boston Harbor.
