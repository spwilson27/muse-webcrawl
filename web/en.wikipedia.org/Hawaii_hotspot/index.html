Hawaii hotspot

The Hawaii hotspot is a volcanic hotspot located near the namesake Hawaiian Islands, in the northern Pacific Ocean. It is one of the world's most famous and intensively studied hotspots, widely interpreted as the surface expression of a mantle plume rising from deep within the Earth. As the Pacific Plate moves over the relatively stationary hotspot, magma punches through the plate to build volcanoes, which then drift away from the heat source, erode and subside — producing a long chain of islands and seamounts of progressively greater age.

The Hawaiian–Emperor chain

The hotspot has produced the Hawaiian–Emperor seamount chain, a trail of more than 80 volcanoes stretching about 6,200 kilometres (3,900 mi) from the Aleutian Trench in the northwest to the young submarine volcano Lōʻihi, southeast of the Island of Hawaiʻi. A prominent bend in the chain, dated to about 47 million years ago, separates the Emperor seamounts (running north) from the Hawaiian ridge (running northwest) and records a major change in the direction of Pacific Plate motion — though research suggests the hotspot itself also drifted southward during the Emperor phase.

Current activity

Today the hotspot underlies the southeastern end of the Hawaiian archipelago. Kīlauea has been one of the most active volcanoes on Earth, and Mauna Loa is the world's largest shield volcano. Submarine Lōʻihi, still about 975 metres below sea level, is the youngest volcano in the chain and may eventually form a new island.

Scientific significance

Proposed by J. Tuzo Wilson in 1963, the hotspot idea was key evidence for the theory of plate tectonics. Seismic tomography images a low-velocity conduit beneath Hawaii extending deep into the mantle, supporting the plume hypothesis, though debate continues over the plume's depth of origin and exact structure.
