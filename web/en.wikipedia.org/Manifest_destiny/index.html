Manifest destiny

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: "American Progress" by John Gast (1872)]

Manifest destiny was a 19th-century cultural belief in the United States that American settlers were destined—and divinely ordained—to expand across North America. The phrase was coined by journalist John L. O'Sullivan in 1845, in connection with the annexation of Texas and the Oregon boundary dispute.

Themes

Historian Frederick Merk identified three core themes: the special virtues of the American people and their institutions; the mission to spread those institutions and redeem the West; and an irresistible destiny given by God. The idea justified territorial acquisitions including the Louisiana Purchase's consolidation, the annexation of Texas (1845), the Oregon Treaty (1846), and the Mexican Cession (1848) after the Mexican–American War.

Consequences

Manifest destiny accelerated western settlement but had devastating consequences for Native American peoples, whose lands were seized and populations displaced or killed, and it intensified the national conflict over the expansion of slavery. Critics, including Abraham Lincoln and Ulysses S. Grant, opposed the war with Mexico. The ideology later informed American overseas imperialism, including the annexation of Hawaii and the Spanish–American War of 1898.

John Gast's painting

American Progress (1872) by John Gast, a widely reproduced chromolithograph, personifies the idea: a luminous female figure ("Progress") strings telegraph wire westward as settlers displace Native Americans and bison.
