Alaska Natives

Alaska Natives (also known as Native Alaskans) are the Indigenous peoples of Alaska. They comprise numerous distinct peoples and cultures, including the Iñupiat, Yup'ik, Aleut (Unangax), Alutiiq (Sugpiaq), Eyak, Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, and the Athabascan peoples of the interior.

Peoples and cultures

Alaska Native cultures are traditionally grouped by region and subsistence pattern: the whaling and sealing cultures of the Arctic coast, the salmon-fishing peoples of the Yukon-Kuskokwim delta and the Northwest coast rainforest, and the caribou-hunting peoples of the interior. Alaska Native languages belong to several language families, including Eskimo-Aleut, Na-Dene, and Haida.

Land claims and ANCSA

In 1971 the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA) extinguished aboriginal land claims in exchange for about 44 million acres and $962.5 million, organized through thirteen regional corporations and more than 200 village corporations — a corporate model unique among Indigenous land settlements in the United States.

Today

Alaska Natives make up roughly 15–20 percent of Alaska's population. Issues of subsistence rights, tribal sovereignty, language revitalization, and the effects of climate change on coastal villages remain central concerns.
