Earth's magnetic field

Earth's magnetic field, also known as the geomagnetic field, is the magnetic field that extends from Earth's interior out into space, where it interacts with the solar wind, a stream of charged particles emanating from the Sun. The field ranges in magnitude from about 25 to 65 microteslas (0.25 to 0.65 gauss) at Earth's surface — much weaker than a typical refrigerator magnet.

Origin

The field is generated by the geodynamo: electric currents flowing in the liquid iron-nickel outer core, driven by convection, Earth's rotation and heat escaping from the core. The field is mostly that of a dipole tilted about 11 degrees from the rotational axis, as if a bar magnet were placed near Earth's centre — with the notable feature that the magnetic pole near the geographic North Pole is physically a magnetic south pole, attracting the north-seeking ends of compass needles.

Behaviour and importance

The field varies over timescales from milliseconds to millions of years. The magnetic poles drift — the north magnetic pole is accelerating from Canada toward Siberia — and, at irregular intervals averaging a few hundred thousand years, the field reverses polarity, as recorded in seafloor magnetic stripes and volcanic rocks. The magnetosphere deflects most of the solar wind and cosmic radiation, protecting the atmosphere and surface life, and produces auroras near the poles. Many animals, including birds and sea turtles, sense the field for navigation (magnetoreception).
