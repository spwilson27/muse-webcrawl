Human rights in Uzbekistan
Country: Uzbekistan
Constitution adopted: 1992, revised 2023
Key issues: Freedom of expression, assembly, religion, forced labour, torture prevention

Human rights in Uzbekistan are governed by the constitution of Uzbekistan and international treaties to which the country is party, but international organisations have long criticised restrictions on political opposition, independent media, civil society and religious practice. The government of President Islam Karimov (1991–2016) was accused of systematic abuses; after Shavkat Mirziyoyev succeeded Karimov in 2016, some reforms were introduced, including the release of some political prisoners and measures to end systematic forced labour in the cotton harvest.

History

Uzbekistan became independent from the Soviet Union in 1991. Under Karimov the state maintained tight control over politics and society. The 2005 Andijan unrest, in which government forces killed hundreds of protesters according to witnesses and rights groups, became the most widely cited episode. Relations with Western governments and human rights monitors deteriorated sharply afterwards.

Civil and political rights

Elections have repeatedly been assessed by OSCE observers as falling short of democratic standards, with genuine opposition parties unable to register or campaign freely. Freedom of assembly is restricted by permit requirements, and independent journalists and bloggers have faced prosecution, including under laws on defamation and extremism. Reforms after 2016 expanded media discussion of some social issues, but red lines around the president and security services remained.

Forced labour

For decades the cotton sector relied on mass mobilisation of students, teachers and public employees. International pressure, including a boycott by hundreds of brands, and ILO monitoring led the government to ban child labour and reduce forced adult labour; in 2022 the Cotton Campaign ended its boycott after Uzbek Forum reported no systematic forced labour in the 2021 harvest.

Religion and torture

The state regulates Islam through the Muslim Board and restricts unregistered religious activity; thousands of Muslims were imprisoned on extremism charges in the Karimov era. Torture was widely reported in detention; after 2016 the government closed the Jaslyk prison colony in 2019 and ratified additional safeguards, though monitors continued to report cases.
