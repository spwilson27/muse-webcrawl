Nostra Aetate (Latin: "In our time") is the Declaration on the Relation of the Church with Non-Christian Religions of the Second Vatican Council. Promulgated by Pope Paul VI on 28 October 1965, it is the shortest of the Council's documents but one of the most consequential, fundamentally reshaping the Catholic Church's approach to Judaism, Islam, Hinduism, and Buddhism.

Content

The declaration affirms that the Church "rejects nothing that is true and holy" in other religions and urges Catholics to engage in dialogue and collaboration with their followers. Section 2 speaks appreciatively of Hinduism and Buddhism; Section 3 expresses esteem for Muslims, recalling their worship of the one God and reverence for Jesus as a prophet and for Mary.

On the Jews

Section 4, the declaration's most famous part, rejects the charge of deicide against the Jewish people, declaring that the death of Christ "cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today", and condemns antisemitism in all its forms. Rooted in Paul's Letter to the Romans, it affirms the Church's Jewish origins and the irrevocability of God's covenant with Israel. The section grew out of Pope John XXIII's initiative and his meeting with Jewish historian Jules Isaac in 1960.

Legacy

Nostra Aetate laid the groundwork for formal Catholic–Jewish dialogue, papal visits to synagogues and Israel, and documents such as the 2015 Vatican reflection "The Gifts and the Calling of God are Irrevocable". Its 50th anniversary in 2015 was widely commemorated by interfaith bodies worldwide.
