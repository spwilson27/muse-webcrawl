Organ of Corti

The organ of Corti, or spiral organ, is the receptor organ for hearing and is located in the cochlea of the mammalian inner ear. It consists of a strip of sensory epithelial tissue running along the partition between the scalae of the cochlea, converting sound-driven vibrations into electrical signals that are transmitted to the brain via the cochlear nerve.

The organ was named after the Italian anatomist Alfonso Corti, who described it in his 1851 paper on the mammalian auditory system.

Structure

The organ of Corti rests on the basilar membrane within the scala media. It contains about 15,000 hair cells arranged in rows: a single row of inner hair cells, which are the primary sensory receptors, and three rows of outer hair cells, which amplify and tune the motion of the basilar membrane. Supporting cells include the pillar cells, Deiters' cells and Hensen's cells. The hair cells' stereocilia project toward the overlying tectorial membrane; deflection of the stereocilia opens mechanically gated ion channels, generating receptor potentials.

Function

Sound vibrations transmitted through the ossicles cause pressure waves in the cochlear fluids, which displace the basilar membrane. Because the membrane's stiffness and width vary along its length, different frequencies produce maximal displacement at different places — a tonotopic organization. Inner hair cells at each location transduce the motion into neurotransmitter release onto afferent fibres of the auditory nerve, encoding the frequency and intensity of sound.

Clinical significance

Damage to the organ of Corti, particularly to its hair cells, is a common cause of sensorineural hearing loss. Mammalian cochlear hair cells do not regenerate, so damage from noise, ototoxic drugs or aging is generally permanent.
