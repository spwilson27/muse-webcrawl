Data visualization

Data visualization (or data visualisation) is the representation of data through use of common graphics, such as charts, plots, infographics and even animations. These visual displays of information communicate complex data relationships and data-driven insights in a way that is easy to understand.

Overview

Visualizing data exploits the human visual system's ability to detect patterns, trends, and outliers far faster than scanning tables of numbers. Common chart types include bar charts, line charts, scatter plots, histograms, pie charts, box plots, heat maps, and choropleth maps. Effective visualization requires matching the graphic to the data type and the question being asked, and avoiding distortions such as truncated axes or misleading area scaling.

History

The field traces back to early maps and thematic cartography. William Playfair invented the line, bar, and pie charts in his Commercial and Political Atlas (1786) and Statistical Breviary (1801). Famous historical examples include Charles Joseph Minard's 1869 flow map of Napoleon's Russian campaign, John Snow's 1854 cholera map of London, and Florence Nightingale's polar-area ("rose") diagrams of mortality in the Crimean War. In the 20th century, Jacques Bertin's Sémiologie graphique (1967) and Edward Tufte's The Visual Display of Quantitative Information (1983) set out systematic principles, including the data-ink ratio and the avoidance of "chartjunk."

Modern practice

Computer software has made visualization interactive and ubiquitous: tools range from spreadsheet charts to libraries such as D3.js, ggplot2, matplotlib, and Tableau, and to scientific visualization of simulations and medical imaging. Related fields include information visualization, visual analytics, and statistical graphics. Critics caution that persuasive visualizations can mislead, and research continues into perception, colour use (including accessibility for colour-vision deficiency), and storytelling with data.
