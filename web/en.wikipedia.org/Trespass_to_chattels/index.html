Trespass to chattels

Trespass to chattels is a tort in common law jurisdictions whereby a person intentionally interferes with another's lawful possession of a chattel (movable personal property). The interference must be substantial — amounting to dispossession, impairment of the property's condition, quality, or value, or deprivation of the owner's use for a substantial time. Unlike conversion, which involves a serious exercise of dominion justifying forced sale, trespass to chattels covers lesser interferences and the remedy is compensation for actual damage.

Elements

A plaintiff must show lack of consent, intent to interfere (though not intent to harm), interference with possession, and resulting harm. Historically any touching of another's property was actionable, but modern law, as restated in the Restatement (Second) of Torts, requires demonstrable harm for most forms of intermeddling.

Modern applications

The doctrine has been applied to cyberspace disputes, notably in cases where automated queries burdened computer servers, such as eBay v. Bidder's Edge (2000), though later decisions such as Intel Corp. v. Hamidi (2003) narrowed the requirement of actual damage.
