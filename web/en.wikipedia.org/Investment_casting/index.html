Investment casting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Investment casting is an industrial metal-forming process based on lost-wax casting, one of the oldest known casting techniques. A pattern of the desired part is made in wax (or another sacrificial material), coated ("invested") with a refractory ceramic shell, and the wax is melted and burned out; molten metal is poured into the resulting mould, and the ceramic is broken away to reveal the casting. The process produces components with excellent surface finish, tight dimensional tolerances and intricate internal geometry, and is used for turbine blades, aerospace and medical components, firearms parts, jewellery and dental work.

Process

  1. Pattern making: wax is injected into a metal die to form patterns, commonly assembled onto a wax "tree" with sprues and gating.
  2. Shell building: the assembly is dipped repeatedly into ceramic slurry and dusted with refractory stucco until a shell of adequate thickness forms, then dried.
  3. Dewaxing: the wax is melted out, typically in a steam autoclave, and the shell is fired to burn out residue and develop strength.
  4. Pouring: molten metal is cast into the hot shell by gravity, pressure, vacuum or centrifugal force.
  5. Finishing: the shell is knocked off, parts are cut from the tree, and heat treatment, machining and inspection complete the component.

Advantages and limitations

Investment casting can reproduce fine detail and thin sections in alloys that are difficult to machine, including nickel and cobalt superalloys; single-crystal turbine blades grown by directional solidification in investment moulds are a landmark application. Offsetting these advantages are the high cost of tooling and the multi-step process, which make it most economical for complex parts, high-value alloys, or larger production volumes.

History

Lost-wax casting is more than 5,000 years old, with examples from ancient Mesopotamia, Egypt, China and pre-Columbian America. Its industrial form developed in the 1930s–40s, spurred by dentistry, jewellery manufacture, and Second World War demand for precision aircraft components such as turbo-supercharger parts.
