Water politics

Water politics, sometimes called hydropolitics, is the study of politics affected by the availability of water and water resources. Because water is essential to agriculture, industry, energy, and human survival, decisions about dams, irrigation, allocation, pricing, and pollution are inherently political at local, national, and international levels.

Transboundary politics

Most of the world's major rivers cross national borders, so upstream dam construction and diversions can reshape the water security of downstream states. Water politics therefore interacts with diplomacy and international law, producing institutions such as river basin organizations and treaties like the Indus Waters Treaty and the Nile Basin Initiative. Scholars debate whether scarcity drives conflict or cooperation; the historical record shows hundreds of water treaties and few outright "water wars."

Domestic politics

Domestically, water politics includes disputes over irrigation entitlements, urban water supply and privatization, groundwater regulation, flood control, and the construction of large dams, which often displace communities and alter ecosystems. Water pricing and subsidy policy are recurring electoral issues in many countries, and droughts can destabilize governments.

Concepts

Related ideas include water security, virtual water (the water embedded in traded goods), and the human right to water, recognized by the United Nations General Assembly in 2010.
