Bacchus

Bacchus was the Roman name for the Greek god Dionysus, god of wine, festivity, theatre and religious ecstasy. His cult, imported from Greece and associated in Italy with the native god Liber, became famous — and at times notorious — for its ecstatic rites, the Bacchanalia.

Mythology

In Greek myth Dionysus was the son of Zeus and the mortal Semele, born from Zeus's thigh after Semele's death. He travelled widely, spreading the cultivation of the vine, accompanied by a retinue of satyrs, sileni and maenads, and carrying the pine-cone-tipped staff called the thyrsus.

The Bacchanalia

The Bacchanalia were Roman festivals of Bacchus that came to official attention in 186 BC, when the Senate, alarmed by reports of crime and conspiracy within the cult, suppressed it by decree across Italy — an episode described by the historian Livy.

In art

Bacchus is a frequent subject in Western art, from Michelangelo's drunken Bacchus statue to paintings by Caravaggio, Titian and Rubens, usually shown as a youthful, often inebriated figure crowned with ivy and vine leaves. See also Roman mythology and Wine.
