The Third Estate (French: Tiers État) was one of the three estates of the realm in pre-revolutionary France, comprising everyone who was neither clergy (First Estate) nor nobility (Second Estate) — roughly 98 percent of the population, from peasants and urban workers to the wealthy bourgeoisie.

Role in the Estates General

In the Estates General, each estate deliberated and voted as a single body, so the Third Estate could always be outvoted two to one despite representing nearly the whole nation. When the Estates General convened at Versailles in May 1789 for the first time since 1614, the Third Estate's deputies demanded voting by head rather than by order.

Revolution

In January 1789 the Abbé Sieyès published his famous pamphlet What Is the Third Estate?, arguing that the Third Estate was "everything." On 17 June 1789 the deputies declared themselves the National Assembly, and days later swore the Tennis Court Oath, setting the French Revolution in motion. The estate system itself was abolished in August 1789.
