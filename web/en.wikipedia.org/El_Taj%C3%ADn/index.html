El Tajín is a pre-Columbian archaeological site in the present-day state of Veracruz, Mexico, near the city of Poza Rica. It was one of the largest and most important cities of the Classic and early Postclassic periods on the Gulf Coast, reaching its height between about 600 and 1200 CE after the fall of Teotihuacan.

Architecture

The site's best-known building is the Pyramid of the Niches, a six-tiered stepped pyramid whose façades are pierced by 365 square niches — a count usually interpreted as a solar calendar reference. El Tajín also has an exceptional number of Mesoamerican ballcourts — at least 17 — several decorated with carved relief panels depicting ritual scenes, including human sacrifice associated with the ballgame and the pulque cult.

Art and culture

Classic Veracruz art produced at and around El Tajín includes "yokes", "hachas" and "palmas" — stone objects connected with the ballgame — and smiling-face figurines (sonrientes). The identity of the city's builders is debated; the historic Totonac people claimed the site, though its florescence may predate their dominance of the region.

Rediscovery

Hidden in dense jungle, the ruins were reported to the colonial authorities in 1785 and were excavated and restored during the twentieth century. El Tajín was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1992, and hosts the annual Cumbre Tajín festival. The flying ritual of the Voladores de Papantla is performed at the site.
