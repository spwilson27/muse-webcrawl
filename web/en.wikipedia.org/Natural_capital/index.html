Natural capital

Natural capital is the stock of renewable and non-renewable natural resources — including geology, soil, air, water, forests, fisheries, and all living organisms — that yield a flow of benefits to people, known as ecosystem services. The concept extends the economic idea of capital (means of production) to goods and services provided by the natural environment.

History of the concept

The term was popularised in the early 1990s by ecological economists such as Robert Costanza and Herman Daly, and by E. F. Schumacher's earlier notion of natural capital in Small Is Beautiful (1973). A landmark 1997 study led by Costanza estimated the value of the world's ecosystem services at US$33 trillion per year. The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2005) and The Economics of Ecosystems and Biodiversity (TEEB) initiative further mainstreamed the idea.

Valuation and accounting

Natural capital accounting seeks to record the state and value of environmental assets in national statistics, complementing GDP. The United Nations adopted the System of Environmental-Economic Accounting (SEEA) as a statistical standard. Governments including the United Kingdom (through its Natural Capital Committee) and corporations have piloted natural capital accounts, though valuing services such as pollination, flood regulation, and carbon storage raises methodological and ethical questions.

Criticism

Critics argue that pricing nature risks commodifying it and implying that natural capital can be substituted by produced capital ("weak sustainability"), whereas ecological economists often insist that critical natural capital is irreplaceable ("strong sustainability").
