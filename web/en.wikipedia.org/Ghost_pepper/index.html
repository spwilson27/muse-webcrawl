The ghost pepper, also known by its native name bhut jolokia (Assamese: ভূত জলকীয়া), is an interspecific hybrid chili pepper cultivated in Northeast India. It is a hybrid of Capsicum chinense and Capsicum frutescens. In 2007, Guinness World Records certified the bhut jolokia as the world's hottest chili pepper, roughly 400 times hotter than Tabasco sauce; it was later surpassed by the Trinidad Scorpion Butch T (2011) and the Carolina Reaper (2013).

Names and etymology

"Bhut" in Assamese means "ghost," so bhut jolokia is literally "ghost chili"; a folk explanation holds that the name refers to the pepper's heat sneaking up on the eater, or that the peppers are so hot they could scare away ghosts. In neighboring regions it is called naga jolokia (after the Naga people) and in Nagaland raja mircha, "king of chillies."

Characteristics

The plant is a perennial shrub growing about 45–120 cm tall. The fruits ripen from green through orange to a deep red, measuring roughly 6–8.5 cm long, with characteristically thin, wrinkled, bumpy skin. DNA studies by India's Defence Research Laboratory and others confirmed its hybrid origin between the two Capsicum species, unusual among landraces.

Heat

Testing at New Mexico State University's Chile Pepper Institute measured bhut jolokia samples at just over one million Scoville heat units (about 1,001,304–1,041,427 SHU), making it the first chili documented to exceed one million SHU. The capsaicinoid content concentrates in the placental tissue, as in other C. chinense peppers.

Uses

In Northeast India the pepper is eaten raw or cooked in curries, pickles and chutneys in small quantities. Villagers smear it on fences or use it in smoke bombs to deter wild elephants, and the Indian military has experimented with ghost-pepper-based hand grenades and aerosols as non-lethal crowd-control and personal-defense agents. Abroad it became a staple of extreme-heat sauces and eating challenges, and a parent in breeding later record-holders.
