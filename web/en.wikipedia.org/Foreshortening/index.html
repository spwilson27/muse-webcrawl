Foreshortening

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Foreshortening is the visual effect or optical illusion that an object or distance appears shorter than it actually is because it is angled toward the viewer. In painting and drawing, foreshortening is a technique used in perspective to create the illusion of depth: objects receding into the picture plane are compressed so that those angled toward the viewer appear shortened.

History

Although rudimentary attempts appear in ancient art, foreshortening was systematically developed during the Italian Renaissance. Paolo Uccello experimented obsessively with perspective, and the technique reached a famous early climax in Andrea Mantegna's Lamentation over the Dead Christ (c. 1480), in which the body of Christ is viewed from the feet, dramatically compressed along the viewer's line of sight.

In Renaissance and Baroque art

Michelangelo's figures on the Sistine Chapel ceiling, and the illusionistic ceiling paintings of the Baroque — such as Andrea Pozzo's Allegory of the Missionary Work of the Society of Jesus in Sant'Ignazio, Rome — rely on extreme foreshortening, known in Italian as di sotto in sù ("from below, upward"), to make painted figures appear to soar above the viewer. The related quadratura technique extended painted architecture into real space.

Technique

Artists achieve convincing foreshortening by observing how overlapping forms and proportions change with angle: a limb pointing at the viewer shows its cross-sections as a series of overlapping shapes, with the nearest parts largest. Instruction in life drawing commonly teaches measurement by sighting and the construction of forms as rotated geometric solids.

In photography and film

In photography, foreshortening describes the compression of depth produced by long telephoto lenses, which make distant objects appear closer together, the opposite of the exaggerated depth produced by wide-angle lenses at close range.
