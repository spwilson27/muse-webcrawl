In geometry, the hyperplane separation theorem is a family of results about disjoint convex sets in n-dimensional Euclidean space. In its basic form, it states that if two disjoint nonempty convex sets are given, there exists a hyperplane that separates them — the sets lie in opposite closed half-spaces determined by the hyperplane, and at least one version guarantees a positive distance ("strict separation") when the sets are closed, disjoint, and one of them is compact.

Statement

Let A and B be two disjoint nonempty convex subsets of Rn. Then there exist a nonzero vector v and a real number c such that ⟨x, v⟩ ≥ c for all x in A and ⟨y, v⟩ ≤ c for all y in B; that is, the hyperplane {x : ⟨x, v⟩ = c} separates A and B.

Proof and generalisations

A standard proof for the strict version uses the point of minimum distance between the two sets. In infinite-dimensional spaces the result is a consequence of the Hahn–Banach theorem, whose "geometric" form is often itself called the separation theorem. Counterexamples show the hypotheses cannot be dropped: two disjoint convex sets that approach each other asymptotically may admit no strictly separating hyperplane.

Applications

The theorem underlies the duality theory of linear programming, the separating-hyperplane step in the theory of support-vector machines (where the maximum-margin classifier is a separating hyperplane), and separating arguments in welfare economics and general equilibrium theory.
