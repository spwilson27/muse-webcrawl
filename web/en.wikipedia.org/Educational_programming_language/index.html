Educational programming language

An educational programming language is a programming language designed primarily as a learning instrument rather than as a tool for writing production code. Such languages prioritise a gentle learning curve, immediate feedback, and the teaching of programming concepts over raw performance or brevity.

History and examples

BASIC (1964), designed at Dartmouth College, was created so that all students — not just scientists — could use computers, and became the first language of the home-computer generation. Logo (1967), developed by Seymour Papert and colleagues, embodied a constructionist philosophy of learning, famous for its turtle graphics. Pascal, designed by Niklaus Wirth, dominated university teaching in the 1970s and 1980s for its emphasis on structured programming.

Later examples include Scratch, a block-based visual language from MIT used by millions of children; Python, a general-purpose language widely adopted in teaching for its readability; Alice, Greenfoot, and BlueJ for object-oriented concepts; and domain-specific teaching languages such as Racket (from the How to Design Programs curriculum) and Karel the Robot.

Design considerations

Designers of educational languages weigh a low threshold (easy to start), a high ceiling (powerful enough for real projects), and "wide walls" (supporting many kinds of projects). Debates continue over whether students should begin with block-based languages or text, and whether teaching languages should be the same languages used professionally.
