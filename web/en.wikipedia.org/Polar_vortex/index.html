Polar vortex

The polar vortex is a persistent cyclone of cold air above each pole.

The polar vortex is a large area of low pressure and cold air surrounding both of Earth's poles, consisting of strong upper-level winds (the polar night jet in the stratosphere and a tropospheric circulation) that circulate counter-clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere. The term "vortex" refers to the counter-clockwise flow of air that helps keep the colder air near the poles.

Structure

There are two distinct vortices: a stratospheric polar vortex, which forms in the autumn as polar temperatures fall and dissipates in spring, and a tropospheric polar vortex, which exists year-round. The Antarctic vortex is stronger and more stable than the Arctic one because of the distribution of land and ocean.

Effects on weather

When the Arctic polar vortex is strong, cold air is well contained near the pole. When it weakens — sometimes following a sudden stratospheric warming event — the jet stream can become wavier and lobes of frigid air can plunge southward into mid-latitudes, bringing severe cold outbreaks to North America, Europe, or Asia. Such events brought extreme cold to North America in early 2014 and February 2021 (including the Texas power crisis).

Scientists continue to debate how Arctic amplification — the faster warming of the Arctic relative to the rest of the planet under climate change — affects the stability of the vortex and the frequency of cold outbreaks.
