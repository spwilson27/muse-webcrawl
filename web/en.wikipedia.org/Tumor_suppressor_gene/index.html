A tumor suppressor gene (TSG), or anti-oncogene, is a gene that regulates a cell during cell division and replication. If the cell grows uncontrollably, it will result in cancer. When a tumor suppressor gene is mutated, it results in a loss or reduction in its function; in combination with other genetic changes, this could allow the cell to grow abnormally.

Tumor suppressor genes code for proteins that slow down cell division (the cell cycle), repair DNA mistakes, or tell cells when to die (apoptosis, or programmed cell death). Loss of tumor suppressor function is one of the hallmarks of cancer.

Two-hit hypothesis

Unlike oncogenes, tumor suppressor genes generally follow the "two-hit hypothesis", which implies that both alleles coding for a particular gene must be affected before an effect is manifested. This was first proposed by Alfred G. Knudson in 1971, based on his study of retinoblastoma, a cancer of the eye in children. If only one allele of the gene is damaged, the second can still produce the correct protein. In hereditary cancer syndromes, one defective copy is inherited (first hit) and the second is lost somatically (second hit), greatly increasing cancer risk.

Categories

Tumor suppressor proteins can be grouped into categories including caretaker genes (which maintain genomic stability, e.g. DNA repair genes such as BRCA1 and MLH1), gatekeeper genes (which directly regulate cell growth, e.g. RB1, APC, TP53), and landscaper genes (which control the surrounding stromal environment).

Important examples

TP53, which encodes the protein p53, is the most commonly mutated gene in human cancers and is called the "guardian of the genome". The RB1 gene was the first tumor suppressor discovered, cloned in 1986. BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations predispose to hereditary breast and ovarian cancer. Loss of APC initiates most colorectal cancers, and loss of PTEN is common in prostate, endometrial, and brain tumors.
