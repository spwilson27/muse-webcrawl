The United States Bill of Rights comprises the first ten amendments to the United States Constitution. Proposed following the often bitter 1787–88 debate over ratification of the Constitution, and written primarily by James Madison to respond to Anti-Federalist demands for protection of individual liberties, the amendments were adopted as a single unit on December 15, 1791.

The Bill of Rights guarantees, among other protections, freedom of speech, press, assembly and religion; the right to keep and bear arms; protection against unreasonable search and seizure; rights of criminal defendants, including the right to a speedy public trial by jury, protection against self-incrimination and double jeopardy; and protection against cruel and unusual punishment. The Ninth and Tenth Amendments reserve rights and powers not delegated to the federal government to the people and the states.

Background

The Constitution as drafted in 1787 contained no bill of rights. Anti-Federalists such as George Mason — whose Virginia Declaration of Rights of 1776 strongly influenced Madison's draft — objected to ratification without one. Several state conventions ratified the Constitution only on the understanding that amendments would follow, and Madison, honouring a campaign pledge, introduced a series of amendments in the First Congress in June 1789. Congress approved twelve articles of amendment and sent them to the states; ten were ratified by the required three-fourths of the states, with Virginia's ratification on December 15, 1791 making them effective.

Of the two articles not ratified in 1791, one (on congressional pay) was ratified in 1992 as the Twenty-seventh Amendment; the other, concerning the size of the House of Representatives, remains technically pending.

The amendments

First: freedom of religion, speech, press, assembly and petition. Second: right to keep and bear arms. Third: no quartering of soldiers in private homes in peacetime. Fourth: protection against unreasonable searches and seizures; warrants require probable cause. Fifth: grand jury indictment, no double jeopardy, no self-incrimination, due process, just compensation for takings. Sixth: speedy and public trial, impartial jury, informed of charges, confrontation of witnesses, assistance of counsel. Seventh: jury trial in civil cases. Eighth: no excessive bail or fines, no cruel and unusual punishment. Ninth: enumeration of rights does not deny others retained by the people. Tenth: powers not delegated to the United States are reserved to the states or the people.

Application

Originally the Bill of Rights limited only the federal government; the Supreme Court confirmed this in Barron v. Baltimore (1833). After ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment (1868), the Court gradually "incorporated" most of its provisions against the states through the Due Process Clause, a process largely completed during the 20th century. The original engrossed copy is displayed in the Rotunda of the National Archives in Washington, D.C., alongside the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution. December 15 is observed as Bill of Rights Day.
