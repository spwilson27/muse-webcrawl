Stonehenge

Stonehenge is a prehistoric megalithic structure on Salisbury Plain in Wiltshire, England, 2 miles (3 km) west of Amesbury. It consists of an outer ring of vertical sarsen standing stones, each around 13 feet (4.0 m) high, 7 feet (2.1 m) wide, and weighing around 25 tons, topped by connecting horizontal lintel stones. Inside is a ring of smaller bluestones. Inside these are free-standing trilithons, two bulkier vertical sarsens joined by one lintel. The whole monument, now ruinous, is aligned towards the sunrise on the summer solstice.

Archaeologists believe that Stonehenge was constructed in several phases from around 3100 BC to 1600 BC. The surrounding circular earth bank and ditch, which constitute the earliest phase of the monument, have been dated to about 3100 BC. Radiocarbon dating suggests that the first bluestones were raised between 2400 and 2200 BC, although they may have been at the site as early as 3000 BC.

Construction

The monument was built in stages over roughly 1,500 years. The earliest work was a circular ditch and bank (a henge) with a ring of 56 pits known as the Aubrey holes. The great sarsen stones were probably brought from the Marlborough Downs, about 20 miles (32 km) away, while the smaller bluestones were transported from the Preseli Hills in Wales, some 150 miles (240 km) distant — a feat that continues to impress researchers. Recent geochemical analysis has suggested the Altar Stone may have originated in northern Scotland.

Purpose

The purpose of Stonehenge remains debated. Proposed functions include a burial ground (cremated remains have been found in and around the monument), a place of healing, a solar calendar, and a monument to ancestor worship. The alignment of the Avenue with the summer solstice sunrise and winter solstice sunset is widely accepted as deliberate. The site and its surroundings were added to UNESCO's list of World Heritage Sites in 1986.

Modern history

Stonehenge was privately owned until 1915, when Cecil Chubb bought it at auction and later gave it to the nation. It is now managed by English Heritage on behalf of the Crown. More than a million people visit each year, and the monument remains a focal point for modern Druid and pagan celebrations of the solstices.
