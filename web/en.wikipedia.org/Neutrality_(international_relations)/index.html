Neutrality (international relations)

Neutrality is the legal status arising from the abstention of a state from all participation in a war between other states, and the attitude of impartiality toward the belligerents. The rights and duties of neutral states are chiefly codified in the Hague Conventions of 1907 (Conventions V and XIII).

Types

Neutrality may be temporary, applying to a single conflict, or permanent, where a state binds itself by treaty or constitution to remain neutral in all future wars — as with Switzerland (recognised in 1815), Austria (1955) and Turkmenistan. Traditionally neutral states include Ireland and, until their NATO accession in 2023–2024, Finland and Sweden.

Rights and duties

Belligerents must respect neutral territory; neutrals must prevent its use for military operations, intern belligerent troops entering their territory, and apply any restrictions impartially. Neutral trade with belligerents is generally permitted, subject to contraband and blockade rules. Since 1945 the UN Charter's collective security system has qualified, but not abolished, the law of neutrality.
