Gap junction

Gap junctions are membrane channels between adjacent cells that allow the direct transfer of ions, second messengers and small metabolites between cells — a process known as direct intercellular communication. They are composed of connexon pairs in vertebrates or innexin channels in invertebrates, each connexon consisting of six connexin proteins.

Structure

A gap junction channel is formed when two hemichannels (connexons) from neighbouring cells dock across a narrow extracellular gap of about 2–4 nm. Thousands of such channels can aggregate into a dense plaque. More than 20 connexin genes are known in humans (e.g., connexin 43, encoded by GJA1), and different connexins confer different permeability and gating properties. Channels typically pass molecules up to roughly 1 kDa, including ions, cAMP, IP3 and small metabolites.

Functions

Gap junctions synchronise electrical activity in cardiac and smooth muscle, coordinate metabolic activity in tissues such as the liver, allow signalling in the developing embryo, and couple neurons at electrical synapses. Mutations in connexin genes cause human diseases including some forms of deafness (connexin 26), Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease, and skin disorders.
