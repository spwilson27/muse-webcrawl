Iran crisis of 1946

Date1945–1946
LocationNorthern Iran (Azerbaijan, Kurdistan)
PartiesIran, Soviet Union; United States and United Nations involved
OutcomeSoviet withdrawal; separatist republics collapsed
SignificanceOne of the first crises of the Cold War

The Iran crisis of 1946, also known as the Iran–Azerbaijan crisis, was one of the first crises of the Cold War. It arose when the Soviet Union refused to withdraw its troops from northern Iran after the agreed deadline following the Second World War, and sponsored two separatist states there.

Background

In August 1941 British and Soviet forces invaded Iran to secure supply routes to the USSR and protect the oilfields, forcing Reza Shah to abdicate. Under the Tripartite Treaty of 1942 the occupiers pledged to leave within six months of the war's end — by 2 March 1946.

The crisis

Backed by Soviet forces, the Azerbaijan People's Government was proclaimed in Tabriz in November 1945, and the Kurdish Republic of Mahabad followed in January 1946. When the deadline passed with Soviet troops still in place, Iran brought the first complaint ever heard by the new United Nations Security Council. Diplomatic pressure from the United States and the UN, combined with negotiations by Iranian prime minister Ahmad Qavam — who promised Moscow an oil concession the Majlis later refused to ratify — led Stalin to withdraw Soviet forces by May 1946.

Aftermath

Deprived of Soviet protection, both separatist republics collapsed as Iranian troops re-entered Azerbaijan and Mahabad in December 1946. The crisis hardened American thinking about Soviet expansionism and is often cited as an early application of the policy that became the Truman Doctrine of containment.
