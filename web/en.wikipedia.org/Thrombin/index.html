Thrombin

Thrombin (EC 3.4.21.5), also called factor IIa, is a serine protease enzyme that in humans is encoded by the F2 gene. It is the central effector of blood coagulation: thrombin converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble strands of fibrin, which form the mesh of a blood clot, and it activates several other clotting factors and platelets. Thrombin is generated from its inactive precursor, prothrombin (factor II), by the prothrombinase complex (factor Xa with factor Va) on activated platelet surfaces in the presence of calcium ions.

Function in coagulation

Thrombin performs multiple roles in the coagulation cascade:

  • Cleaves fibrinogen to fibrin monomers, which polymerise into a clot.
  • Activates factor XIII, which cross-links and stabilises the fibrin clot.
  • Activates factors V and VIII (positive feedback, accelerating its own generation) and factor XI.
  • Activates platelets via protease-activated receptors (PARs).
  • Bound to thrombomodulin on endothelial cells, it activates protein C, which (with protein S) inactivates factors Va and VIIIa — an important anticoagulant pathway.

Thrombin also has roles outside haemostasis, including inflammation, cell proliferation, and embryonic development; mice lacking prothrombin die in utero or perinatally from haemorrhage.

Regulation and clinical relevance

Circulating thrombin is rapidly inactivated by antithrombin, especially in the presence of heparin, which is the basis of heparin therapy. Direct thrombin inhibitors include the parenteral drugs hirudin (from leech saliva), bivalirudin, and argatroban, and the oral drug dabigatran, used as an anticoagulant. Conversely, topical thrombin preparations (bovine, human, or recombinant) are used in surgery as haemostatic agents. Mutations in the prothrombin gene, notably the G20210A variant, raise prothrombin levels and increase the risk of venous thrombosis. A thrombin generation assay is used in research to measure overall coagulation potential.
