Spice trade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Maritime spice routes between Asia and Europe

The spice trade refers to the trade between historical civilisations in Asia, Northeast Africa and Europe in spices such as cinnamon, cassia, cardamom, ginger, pepper, nutmeg, cloves and turmeric. Spices were among the most valuable commodities of the ancient and medieval worlds, used in cooking, medicine, religious ritual and embalming, and their trade helped drive global exploration and colonisation.

History

From antiquity, spices from India and the East Indies reached the Mediterranean through Arab and Indian Ocean intermediaries, passing through Egypt and the Levant to Venice and Genoa, whose merchants dominated European distribution. The high prices charged along the route motivated Portuguese voyages of discovery: Vasco da Gama reached India by sea in 1498, breaking the Venetian monopoly. The Portuguese, and later the Dutch East India Company (VOC) and English East India Company, fought for control of the producing regions, notably the Maluku Islands — the "Spice Islands" proper — source of nutmeg and cloves.

Legacy

Competition for spices spurred the European Age of Discovery, the first circumnavigation of the globe, and the establishment of colonial empires in Asia and the Americas. As cultivation spread and transport costs fell in the 18th and 19th centuries, spices lost their extraordinary value and became ordinary commodities.
