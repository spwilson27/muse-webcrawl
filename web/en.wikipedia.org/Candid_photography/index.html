Candid photography

Candid photography is photography that captures people in spontaneous, unposed moments, typically without the subject's knowledge or awareness of the camera. By avoiding posing and direction, candid photographs aim to record genuine expressions, gestures, and social situations. The genre is central to street photography, photojournalism, and documentary work, and was transformed in the 1920s–30s by small, quiet cameras such as the Leica.

History

Early photography's long exposures made true candids impractical, though Paul Martin photographed Londoners unawares with a disguised camera in the 1890s. The candid era began with fast lenses and compact 35 mm cameras: Erich Salomon used an Ermanox to photograph conferences and courts in the late 1920s, and LIFE-era photojournalists made the unposed image the visual language of news. Henri Cartier-Bresson's theory of the "decisive moment" (1952) gave candid practice its most famous artistic rationale.

Technique

Candid photographers use small or unobtrusive cameras, wide-angle lenses, zone focusing, shooting from the hip, and available light to remain inconspicuous. The aim is that subjects continue natural behaviour; telephoto lenses allow candids at a distance, a practice associated with paparazzi.

Genres and practitioners

Street photographers such as Robert Frank, William Klein, Daidō Moriyama, and Vivian Maier built major bodies of candid work. Wedding and event photography now frequently advertise a "photojournalistic" candid style, and snapshot aesthetics in fashion and social media descend from candid practice.

Ethics and law

Photographing strangers without consent raises ethical questions about privacy and exploitation, and laws differ by jurisdiction: photography in public places is broadly legal in many countries, but publication, commercial use, and certain venues are restricted, and some jurisdictions grant subjects stronger rights over their image. The paparazzi's pursuit of celebrities has prompted specific anti-harassment statutes.
