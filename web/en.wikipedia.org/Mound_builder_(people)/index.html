Mound builder (people)

The Mound Builders were various Indigenous peoples of the Americas who, over a period of some 5,000 years, built earthen mounds for religious, ceremonial, burial, and elite residential purposes across the eastern United States, especially in the Mississippi and Ohio river valleys.

Cultures

Mound building began in the Archaic period, with sites such as Poverty Point in Louisiana (c. 1700–1100 BCE). The Woodland period saw the Adena and Hopewell cultures raise elaborate burial mounds and geometric earthworks. The later Mississippian culture (c. 800–1600 CE) built great platform-mound cities such as Cahokia, whose Monks Mound is the largest pre-Columbian earthwork in the Americas.

The "lost race" myth

Nineteenth-century settlers, unwilling to credit Native Americans with the earthworks, popularized the myth that a vanished "race of Mound Builders" — variously identified with Vikings, Lost Tribes of Israel, or others — had built them and been exterminated. Cyrus Thomas of the Bureau of Ethnology conclusively debunked the myth in 1894, demonstrating the mounds were built by ancestors of living Native American peoples, a conclusion confirmed by modern archaeology.
