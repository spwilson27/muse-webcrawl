Chaldean Neo-Aramaic is a Northeastern Neo-Aramaic language spoken by Chaldean Catholics, an ethnic Assyrian people whose traditional homeland lies in northern Iraq (the Nineveh Plain and surrounding areas), with large diaspora communities in metropolitan Detroit (Michigan), San Diego (California), Sydney (Australia), and parts of Europe. The name refers to the Chaldean Catholic Church rather than to the ancient Chaldeans of Babylon; the language is closely related to, and partly mutually intelligible with, Assyrian Neo-Aramaic (Sureth).

History and dialects

The language descends from the Aramaic dialects spoken in Mesopotamia for over two millennia. It is written in the Madnhāyā ("eastern") form of the Syriac alphabet. Dialects cluster around villages and towns such as Alqosh, Tel Keppe, Batnaya, Karamlesh and Tesqopa; the Alqosh dialect carries particular prestige because of its role in 17th–19th century manuscript and early printed literature. Speech in the southern Nineveh Plain is relatively homogeneous, while mountain dialects farther north differ more.

Status

Chaldean Neo-Aramaic is considered endangered: decades of emigration, urbanization, the 2003 Iraq War and the 2014 ISIS invasion of the Nineveh Plain displaced most of its speaker base. In Iraq it has some official recognition in Assyrian-majority areas, and diaspora communities maintain it through churches, satellite television, and community schools, though younger generations increasingly shift to Arabic, English or other local languages.

Features

Like other Northeastern Neo-Aramaic languages, Chaldean has extensively restructured the classical Aramaic verb system into analytic tense forms, lost many pharyngeal consonants in some dialects, and borrowed vocabulary from Arabic, Kurdish, Turkish and Persian. Phonology, verbal morphology and word order vary noticeably between village dialects.
