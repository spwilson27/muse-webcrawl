Contempt of court

Contempt of court, often referred to simply as "contempt", is a behaviour that opposes or defies the authority, justice and dignity of a court. A person found in contempt may be punished by fines, imprisonment, or both, and courts use the power both to compel compliance with their orders and to punish disobedience or disruption.

Types

Common-law systems distinguish civil contempt, which is coercive or remedial — for example, jailing a witness until they testify or comply with a court order — from criminal contempt, which punishes past disobedience, such as violating an injunction or insulting the court. Contempt may also be direct (committed in the court's presence) or constructive/indirect (committed outside it, such as breaching a court order in private).

Procedure and penalties

Direct contempt may be punished summarily, while indirect contempt generally requires notice and a hearing. Penalties range from fines to indefinite imprisonment in coercive civil cases — the contemnor is said to "hold the keys to the cell" because release follows compliance. In England and Wales, the maximum sentence for contempt is two years' imprisonment under the Contempt of Court Act 1981.

Publication contempt

Some jurisdictions punish publications that create a substantial risk of serious prejudice to legal proceedings ("sub judice" rules), a doctrine that can conflict with freedom of the press. In the United States, the First Amendment sharply limits punishment of out-of-court speech about pending cases.
