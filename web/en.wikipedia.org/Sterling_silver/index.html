Sterling silver

Sterling silver flatware, stamped with the 925 fineness mark.

Sterling silver is an alloy of silver containing 92.5% by mass silver and 7.5% other metals, usually copper. Its minimum millesimal fineness is 925, hence the common ".925" stamp. The copper content strengthens the otherwise soft pure silver, making it suitable for tableware, jewellery and coinage-adjacent uses.

History

The sterling standard was used for English coinage and plate from the Middle Ages; the word "sterling" is often traced to the Easterling merchants of the Hanseatic League, or to the Old English steorling, "coin with a small star". The standard was codified in law over centuries and administered through hallmarks applied by assay offices. England's Britannia standard (1697–1720) required an even higher fineness of 95.84%.

Properties and care

Sterling silver work-hardens, can be cast, soldered and polished to a bright finish, and tarnishes as the copper-bearing surface reacts with sulfur compounds in air to form silver sulfide. Modern variants such as Argentium replace part of the copper with germanium to resist tarnish.

Uses

Beyond tableware and jewellery, sterling silver has been used for musical instruments such as flutes, medical and scientific apparatus, and commemorative items. In the United States, large quantities of sterling flatware were produced by makers such as Tiffany & Co., Gorham and Reed & Barton.
