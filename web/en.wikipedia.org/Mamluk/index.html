A mamluk (Arabic: مملوك, plural mamālīk, "one who is owned") was a soldier recruited as a slave, typically of Turkic, Circassian, or other Eurasian steppe origin, who converted to Islam and was trained for military service in the medieval Muslim world. Unusually, mamluks often rose to the highest levels of power: mamluk dynasties ruled Egypt and Syria (1250–1517) and, briefly, part of India (the Delhi Sultanate's "Slave dynasty", 1206–1290).

Origins

The institution appeared under the Abbasid caliphs in the 9th century, notably al-Mu'tasim, who built a guard of Turkic slave-soldiers at Samarra to counterbalance Arab tribal troops. Boys purchased on the steppe were converted, educated in barracks (ṭibāq), trained in horsemanship, archery and the martial arts (furūsiyya), and freed upon graduation into their master's household corps, bound by loyalty to their patron and comrades (khushdāshiyya).

Mamluk Sultanate

In Egypt, the mamluks of the Ayyubid sultan al-Salih seized power in 1250. Their sultanate—the Bahri (1250–1382) and Burji (1382–1517) regimes—defeated the Mongols at Ain Jalut (1260), expelled the Crusaders, and made Cairo a great centre of Islamic art and learning before falling to the Ottoman sultan Selim I in 1517. Mamluk beys nevertheless remained Egypt's effective rulers until Muhammad Ali massacred them in 1811.
