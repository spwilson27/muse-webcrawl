The Battle of Mantinea of 362 BC was the last battle of the Theban general Epaminondas, fought near Mantinea in Arcadia between Thebes and its allies and a coalition of Sparta, Athens, Mantinea and Elis. Epaminondas, employing the deepened left-wing phalanx and combined cavalry tactics that had shattered Sparta at Leuctra nine years earlier, again broke the opposing line — but was mortally wounded at the moment of victory. With his death the Theban pursuit dissolved, and the battle ended in strategic exhaustion for all of Greece. Xenophon, whose son Gryllus died fighting on the Athenian side, concluded his Hellenica by remarking that the battle produced "even more confusion and disorder in Greece after the battle than before."

Background

Theban hegemony, established after Leuctra (371 BC) and the liberation of Messenia, was increasingly resented by Thebes's own allies, including the Arcadian League, which quarrelled with Thebes over control of the federal government. In 362 BC the Arcadians split; Mantinea seceded and aligned with Sparta and Athens. Epaminondas marched into the Peloponnese to restore Theban influence, manoeuvring against Sparta itself — which he nearly captured while King Agesilaus was absent — before turning on the allied army assembling at Mantinea.

The battle

Epaminondas massed his best Boeotian infantry in an exceptionally deep column on his left, supported by cavalry interspersed with light infantry, and advanced obliquely, first deceiving the enemy with a feigned halt and lowering of weapons as if encamping. His hammer-blow attack broke the Spartan-led right wing of the coalition. As the Thebans pressed the pursuit, Epaminondas was struck down — according to tradition by a Spartan named Anticrates, or in another account by the Athenian Gryllus. Carried from the field still alive, he is said to have asked whether his shield was safe and whether the enemy had fled, and, hearing "yes" to both, declared that he had lived long enough and let the weapon's point be drawn out, dying at once.

Aftermath

Both sides set up trophies and recovered their dead under truce, each claiming victory. The real consequence was the collapse of Theban leadership: Epaminondas's successors could not sustain the hegemony he had built, and no Greek state was again strong enough to dominate the others. Within a generation, the exhausted city-states fell under the power of Philip II of Macedon, whose victory at Chaeronea in 338 BC ended the era of the independent polis.
