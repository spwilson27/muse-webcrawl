Rope access

Rope access, also known as industrial climbing or commercial abseiling, is a form of work positioning, initially developed from techniques used in climbing and caving, which applies practical ropework to allow workers to access difficult-to-reach or at-height locations without the use of scaffolding, cradles or aerial work platforms. Rope access technicians descend, ascend, and traverse ropes for access and work while suspended by their harnesses.

System and safety

The defining feature of professional rope access is a two-rope system: a working line that supports the technician and a safety (back-up) line attached to an independent anchorage and a fall-arrest device, so that failure of any single item cannot cause a fall. Technicians are trained and certified in levels of competence — under the IRATA (Industrial Rope Access Trade Association) scheme, Level 1 workers operate under supervision, Level 2 technicians can rig and perform rescues, and Level 3 supervisors carry responsibility for site safety. Industry statistics have historically shown rope access to have a low accident rate compared with other access methods.

Applications

Typical work includes inspection, non-destructive testing, maintenance, painting, and cleaning on offshore oil and gas platforms, wind turbines, bridges, dams, silos, stadiums, and high-rise buildings, as well as geotechnical work on cliffs and cuttings. It is often far cheaper and faster to mobilise than scaffolding and causes minimal disruption.

Regulation

Trade bodies such as IRATA (founded in the UK in the late 1980s) and SPRAT (in North America) set training, certification, and audit standards, and national work-at-height regulations impose duties on employers regarding planning, equipment inspection, and rescue provision.
