Doppler echocardiography

Doppler echocardiography is a procedure that uses Doppler ultrasonography to examine the heart. An echocardiogram uses high-frequency sound waves to create an image of the heart, while the Doppler component allows the speed and direction of blood flow through the heart's chambers and valves to be measured and displayed, most commonly with colour.

Principle

The technique exploits the Doppler effect: ultrasound reflected from red blood cells changes in frequency in proportion to the velocity of the blood. From the measured velocities, clinicians can derive pressure gradients across valves and within chambers using the simplified Bernoulli equation, ΔP = 4v². This permits non-invasive estimation of, for example, the severity of aortic stenosis, pulmonary artery pressures, and cardiac output.

Techniques

  • Pulsed-wave (PW) Doppler — measures velocities at a specific location but is limited in the maximum velocity it can record.
  • Continuous-wave (CW) Doppler — measures very high velocities along the whole ultrasound beam but cannot localize them precisely.
  • Colour Doppler — superimposes a colour map of blood-flow velocity and direction on the two-dimensional image, used to detect valvular regurgitation and shunts.
  • Tissue Doppler imaging — measures the much slower velocities of the myocardium itself, used to assess systolic and diastolic function.

Clinical uses

Doppler echocardiography is central to the diagnosis and follow-up of valvular heart disease, heart failure, congenital heart disease, and cardiomyopathies, and to the assessment of diastolic dysfunction and prosthetic valve function. It may be performed transthoracically or, for higher-resolution views, transoesophageally.
