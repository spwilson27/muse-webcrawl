Mikhail Sergeyevich Gorbachev (1931–2022) was the last leader of the Soviet Union. As General Secretary of the Communist Party from 1985, he launched the reforms of glasnost (openness) and perestroika (restructuring) that transformed Soviet society, ended the Cold War — and, unintentionally, led to the dissolution of the USSR in 1991.

Rise to power

Gorbachev was born to a peasant family in the Stavropol region and joined the Communist Party while studying law at Moscow State University. He rose through the party apparatus in Stavropol, entered the Politburo in 1980, and became the protégé of Yuri Andropov. On Konstantin Chernenko's death in March 1985, Gorbachev — at 54, young by Soviet leadership standards — was chosen General Secretary.

Reforms and the end of the Cold War

Faced with economic stagnation, Gorbachev promoted glasnost, loosening censorship and releasing political prisoners such as Andrei Sakharov, and perestroika, introducing limited markets and contested elections. The Chernobyl disaster of 1986 deepened his push for openness. Abroad he pursued arms control with US President Ronald Reagan (the 1987 INF Treaty) and his successor George H. W. Bush, withdrew Soviet troops from Afghanistan, and refused to use force to hold Eastern Europe, allowing the Berlin Wall to fall in 1989. He received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1990.

Collapse and later life

Reform unleashed forces he could not control: republics from the Baltics to his native Russia demanded sovereignty, the economy deteriorated, and hardliners attempted a coup in August 1991. Though the coup failed, power flowed to Boris Yeltsin; Gorbachev resigned on 25 December 1991 and the Soviet Union ceased to exist. Reviled by many Russians for the collapse yet honoured in the West, he founded environmental and political foundations, ran quixotically for president in 1996, and remained a commentator on world affairs until his death in Moscow in 2022.
