Cataphract

TypeHeavy cavalry
OriginIranian peoples of the Eurasian steppe and Near East
Erac. 4th century BC – late antiquity / medieval period
ArmourScale, mail, or lamellar covering rider and horse
Primary weaponKontos (long lance)

A cataphract was a form of armoured heavy cavalryman found in many Eurasian armies, in which both the rider and his horse were covered from head to foot in scale, mail, or lamellar armour. The word derives from the Greek kataphraktos, "covered over" or "fully armoured".


A reconstruction of a Parthian or Sasanian cataphract and armoured horse.

Origins

Fully armoured cavalry developed among Iranian peoples — Sarmatians, Scythians, and Parthians — by about the 4th century BC. Parthian cataphracts famously destroyed a Roman army at the Battle of Carrhae in 53 BC, where their long lances (the kontos, wielded two-handed) and armour defeated Roman legionaries in combination with horse archers.

Equipment and tactics

The cataphract's principal weapon was the kontos, a lance some 3.5–4.5 m long, supplemented by a long sword and sometimes a mace or bow. Armour of iron or bronze scales, mail, or lamellar plates covered the rider's body, limbs, and face, and the horse wore a chamfron for its head and barding of similar construction. Cataphracts charged in close formation at relatively controlled speed, relying on weight and armour to break enemy lines; they were expensive to equip and were typically drawn from aristocratic classes.

Use by later empires

The Sasanian Empire maintained cataphracts (the aswaran) as its elite arm. Rome adopted the type from the 2nd century AD, raising units of equites cataphractarii and the still more heavily armoured clibanarii. The Byzantine Empire fielded cataphracts for centuries, describing their wedge-formation charges in manuals such as the Praecepta Militaria. Armoured lancer traditions descended from the cataphract persisted across the Near East and steppe into the medieval period, influencing later heavy cavalry.
