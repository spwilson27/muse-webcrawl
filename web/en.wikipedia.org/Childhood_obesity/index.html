Childhood obesity is a condition where excess body fat negatively affects a child's health or well-being. As methods to determine body fat directly are difficult, the diagnosis of obesity is often based on body mass index (BMI). Using growth charts, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention define obesity in children aged 2–19 years as a BMI at or above the 95th percentile for children of the same age and sex, and overweight as the 85th to below the 95th percentile. Owing to the rising prevalence of obesity among children and its many adverse health effects, it is recognized as a serious public health concern.

Epidemiology

The prevalence of childhood obesity has increased dramatically since the 1970s. Globally, the number of overweight or obese infants and young children rose from about 32 million in 1990 to roughly 41 million by 2016, and the World Health Organization estimates that over 390 million children and adolescents aged 5–19 were overweight in 2022, including about 160 million living with obesity. In the United States, about one in five children and adolescents (roughly 20%) has obesity, with higher rates among lower-income families and some racial and ethnic groups.

Causes

Childhood obesity results from a sustained imbalance between energy intake and expenditure, shaped by genetic susceptibility and environment. Contributing factors include diets high in energy-dense foods and sugar-sweetened beverages, large portion sizes, low levels of physical activity, increased sedentary screen time, short sleep duration, parental obesity, and an obesogenic built environment with limited access to healthy food and safe play space. Rare causes include single-gene disorders (e.g., leptin or MC4R mutations), syndromes such as Prader–Willi, hypothyroidism, and medications such as some antipsychotics and glucocorticoids.

Health effects

Children with obesity are more likely to have high blood pressure, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes, metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease, obstructive sleep apnea, asthma, and orthopaedic conditions such as slipped capital femoral epiphysis. Psychosocial consequences — weight stigma, bullying, low self-esteem, and depression — are also common. Obesity in childhood strongly tracks into adulthood, raising lifetime risk of cardiovascular disease and premature mortality; it is a major concern of cardiology and preventive medicine.

Management and prevention

First-line treatment is intensive, family-based behavioural intervention addressing diet, physical activity, sleep, and screen time. Public-health measures include improving school food standards, restricting marketing of unhealthy foods to children, taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages (as adopted in Mexico, the United Kingdom, and other jurisdictions), and urban design promoting active transport. For adolescents with severe obesity, guidelines increasingly support adjunct pharmacotherapy (including GLP-1 receptor agonists) and metabolic and bariatric surgery in specialized centres.
