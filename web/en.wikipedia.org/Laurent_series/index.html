Laurent series

A Laurent series converges on an annulus between two circles

FieldComplex analysis

In mathematics, the Laurent series of a complex function f(z) is a representation of that function as a power series which includes terms of negative degree. It may be used to express complex functions in cases where a Taylor series expansion cannot be applied. The Laurent series was named after and first published by Pierre Alphonse Laurent in 1843; Karl Weierstrass had derived it earlier, in 1841, but did not publish.

f(z) = Σn=−∞∞ an(z − z₀)n,   with   an = (1/2πi) ∮ f(z)/(z − z₀)n+1 dz

Convergence and the principal part

The series converges on an annulus centred at z₀ whose inner radius is set by the nearest singularity inside and outer radius by the nearest singularity outside. The part of the series with negative powers is called the principal part; its form classifies an isolated singularity: if the principal part is zero the singularity is removable; if it has finitely many terms it is a pole, whose order is the index of the highest negative term; if infinitely many, it is an essential singularity, near which the function behaves wildly (Casorati–Weierstrass and Picard theorems).

Residues

The coefficient a−1 is the residue of f at z₀, and by the residue theorem the integral of f around a closed contour equals 2πi times the sum of the residues inside — the principal tool for evaluating real integrals by complex methods. Formal Laurent series, with coefficients in a ring and only finitely many negative terms, also play a central role in algebra and in the theory of vertex operator algebras.

Example

The function e1/z about z₀ = 0 has Laurent series 1 + 1/z + 1/(2!z²) + …, with infinitely many negative powers, reflecting its essential singularity at the origin. By contrast, 1/(z(z−1)) expanded about 0 in the annulus 0 < |z| < 1 has principal part just 1/z, a simple pole.
