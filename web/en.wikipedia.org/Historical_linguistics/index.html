Historical linguistics

Historical linguistics, also called diachronic linguistics, is the scientific study of language change over time. Its principal concerns include describing and accounting for observed changes in particular languages, reconstructing the pre-history of languages and their grouping into language families, developing theories of linguistic change, and describing the history of speech communities.

Methods

The central tool of the discipline is the comparative method, which infers features of an unattested ancestor language (a proto-language) from systematic correspondences among its descendants. Regular patterns of sound change, such as Grimm's law describing consonant shifts between Proto-Indo-European and the Germanic languages, revealed that sound change proceeds with striking regularity. Related methods include internal reconstruction, which works from patterns within a single language, and the study of borrowing and contact. Glottochronology, which attempts to date language splits from vocabulary retention, is more controversial.

History of the discipline

The field is usually dated from Sir William Jones's 1786 observation of systematic resemblances among Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin, and it flourished in the 19th century with scholars such as Jacob Grimm, Franz Bopp, August Schleicher, and the Neogrammarians. Modern historical linguistics also encompasses grammaticalisation, language contact, and computational phylogenetics.
