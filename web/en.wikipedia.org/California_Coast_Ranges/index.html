California Coast Ranges

LocationCalifornia, United States
Length~640 km (400 mi)
Highest pointMount Linn (South Yolla Bolly Mountain), 2,468 m
GeologyFranciscan Complex, accretionary wedge

The California Coast Ranges are a series of mountain ranges that run roughly parallel to the Pacific coast of California for about 640 kilometres (400 miles), from the Klamath Mountains in the north to the Santa Ynez Mountains near Santa Barbara in the south. They form the western wall of California's Central Valley in their northern and central sections and enclose many of the coastal valleys and basins, including the San Francisco Bay Area.

Geography

The Coast Ranges are divided into the North Coast Ranges and the South Coast Ranges, separated approximately by San Francisco Bay. Northern ranges include the King Range, the Mendocino Range, and the Mayacamas Mountains; central and southern ranges include the Santa Cruz Mountains, the Diablo Range, the Santa Lucia Range of Big Sur, and the Temblor and Caliente ranges bordering the San Joaquin Valley. Although rugged, the ranges are relatively low, mostly between 600 and 1,800 metres in elevation.

Geology

Much of the Coast Ranges consists of the Franciscan Complex, a chaotic assemblage of sedimentary and metamorphic rocks scraped off the ocean floor and accreted to the continent as the Farallon Plate subducted beneath North America. The ranges remain tectonically active: the San Andreas Fault and related faults slice through them, and uplift continues today.

Ecology

The Coast Ranges support famous redwood and Douglas-fir forests in the north, oak woodland and chaparral in drier interior valleys, and the steep coastal scrub of Big Sur. The region is part of the California Floristic Province, a biodiversity hotspot with many endemic species. Rainfall varies enormously from north to south, from over 2,500 mm annually in the King Range to semi-arid conditions in the Temblor Range.
