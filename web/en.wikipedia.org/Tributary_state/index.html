Tributary state

A tributary state is a state that is subordinate to a more powerful state (or empire), to which it sends regular tribute — payments in goods, money, or symbolic gifts — in acknowledgment of its subordinate position. In return, the subordinate ruler is typically left in control of internal affairs and may receive investiture, protection, and trading privileges.

Characteristics

Tributary relationships lie on a spectrum between full sovereignty and direct rule, and the meaning of tribute differed sharply between the parties: a dominant power might regard the gifts as submission, while the sender considered them the price of trade and peace. Tribute systems rarely involved administration of the tributary by the overlord, distinguishing them from colonies and provinces, and differ from vassalage chiefly in scale and in the regularity of the payments.

Historical examples

The most elaborate tributary system was that of imperial China, whose tributary relations with Korea, Vietnam, the Ryukyu Kingdom, and states of Southeast and Central Asia were managed for centuries through the Ministry of Rites and framed by the ideology of the Mandate of Heaven. Elsewhere, the Aztec Empire extracted tribute in kind from subject city-states, recorded in codices such as the Matrícula de Tributos; the Ottoman Empire received tribute from vassal principalities including Wallachia, Moldavia, and the Crimean Khanate; and medieval European kings and the Byzantine Empire variously paid and received tribute.

Decline

The system declined as the Westphalian model of formally equal sovereign states spread in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and colonial expansion and treaty-port diplomacy dismantled the East Asian tributary order in particular.
