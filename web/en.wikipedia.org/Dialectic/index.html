Dialectic

Dialectic (Greek: διαλεκτική, dialektikḗ) is a method of philosophical argument that involves a discourse between two or more people holding different points of view, seeking to establish the truth through reasoned argumentation. It has been a central concept in Western philosophy since antiquity.

Ancient philosophy

In ancient Greece, dialectic was associated with Socrates, whose method of questioning (the elenchus) exposed contradictions in his interlocutors' beliefs. Plato used dialogue as his literary and philosophical form, and for Aristotle dialectic was reasoning from generally accepted opinions (endoxa), contrasted with demonstration from first principles.

Hegel and Marx

For G. W. F. Hegel, dialectic describes the process by which concepts and history develop through the resolution of contradictions — commonly (if simplistically) summarised as thesis, antithesis, and synthesis. Karl Marx adapted the method into dialectical materialism, applying it to social and economic history, where contradictions between classes drive historical change.

Other uses

In medieval universities, dialectic (logic) formed part of the trivium alongside grammar and rhetoric. The term also appears in Eastern philosophy, and in modern critical theory and rhetoric.
