The xun (Chinese: 塤; pinyin: xūn) is a globular vessel flute of China, one of the oldest surviving Chinese musical instruments. Made of fired clay, stone or ceramic, the egg-shaped instrument has a blowing hole on top and finger holes on the sides, and produces a soft, hollow, mournful tone.

History

Excavated examples date back some 7,000 years to Neolithic sites such as Hemudu. In the bayin classification of Chinese instruments the xun belongs to the earth category. It was a standard instrument of Zhou-dynasty court ritual music (yayue), where its deep, austere sound symbolised humility and harmony with the earth. Legend associates its invention with the mythical era and its refinement with court musicians of antiquity.

Construction and playing

Traditional xun have five finger holes — three in front and two behind — giving a pentatonic range of about an octave. Modern concert xun may have eight to ten holes and a range exceeding two octaves. The player blows across the top aperture as with a bottle rim, covering and uncovering the holes to change pitch. The Korean hun and Japanese tsuchibue derive from the same vessel-flute tradition.

Repertoire

Standard pieces include "Chu Ge" (Song of Chu) and settings evoking autumn wind and parting. The instrument largely vanished from popular use after the Song dynasty but was revived by 20th-century performers and makers, and is now heard in Chinese orchestras, film scores and meditative music.
