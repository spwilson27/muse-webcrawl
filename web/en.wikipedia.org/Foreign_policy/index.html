Foreign policy

Foreign policy governs how a state pursues its interests in relation to other states and international bodies.

A state's foreign policy or external policy is its objectives and activities in relation to its interactions with other states, unions, and international organizations, whether bilaterally or through multilateral platforms. It comprises the goals a government seeks abroad — security, prosperity, prestige, and the promotion of values — and the instruments used to pursue them, including diplomacy, alliances, trade policy, foreign aid, sanctions, intelligence cooperation, and, at the extreme, war. The study of foreign policy is a central field of international relations.

Making foreign policy

Foreign policy is typically formulated by the executive branch — a head of state or government together with a foreign ministry headed by a foreign minister or secretary of state — with input from defence, trade, finance, and intelligence agencies. Legislatures may shape it through treaty ratification, funding, and oversight. Domestic politics, public opinion, economic interests, and bureaucratic competition all influence outcomes, a theme developed in Graham Allison's bureaucratic-politics model of decision-making. In international organizations such as the European Union, member states also conduct a common foreign and security policy alongside national policies.

Instruments

The classic instruments of foreign policy range along a spectrum of coercion. Diplomacy — negotiation and representation — is the primary peaceful tool, supported by public diplomacy and cultural exchange ("soft power", in Joseph Nye's term). Economic instruments include trade agreements, aid, loans, and sanctions. Military instruments range from arms sales and alliances to deterrence and the use of force. States also act through international law and institutions such as the United Nations.

Theories and analysis

Foreign policy analysis examines policy at several levels: the international system, the state, and the individual decision-maker. Realists stress power and national interest; liberals emphasize institutions, interdependence, and democratic peace; constructivists focus on identity and norms. Doctrines — often named statements of principle such as the Monroe Doctrine, the Truman Doctrine, or a policy of non-alignment — publicly frame a state's foreign policy orientation, while grand strategy seeks to align ends and means over the long term.
