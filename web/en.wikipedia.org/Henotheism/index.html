Henotheism

Henotheism is the worship of a single god while not denying the existence or possible worship of other deities. The term derives from the Greek hen ("one") and theos ("god"). It was coined by the German philosopher Friedrich Schelling and popularised by the philologist Max Müller, who used it to describe the religion of the Vedas, in which individual gods such as Indra, Agni or Varuna are each addressed in hymns as if supreme.

Related concepts

Henotheism is distinguished from monolatry, the exclusive worship of one god by a community that accepts other gods exist — the two terms overlap substantially and are sometimes used interchangeably. Müller also proposed kathenotheism for the worship of one god at a time in succession. All differ from monotheism, which asserts that only one god exists, and from polytheism, in which many gods are worshipped as a pantheon without one being singled out as supreme for the worshipper.

Examples

Scholars have applied the concept to several traditions. In Vedic Hinduism, hymns exalt whichever deity is being addressed to the highest rank, a tendency later systematised in the idea that the many gods are forms of one reality. Some interpretations of ancient Egyptian religion treat the exaltation of Amun-Ra in the New Kingdom as henotheistic. The early religion of Israel is often described by historians as monolatrous or henotheistic before the emergence of strict monotheism in the exilic period, since texts such as the commandment "You shall have no other gods before me" presuppose the existence of other gods while demanding exclusive loyalty to Yahweh. Certain forms of modern Hindu devotion to a chosen deity (ishta-devata), and the Sikh and Zoroastrian emphases on one supreme god alongside other divine beings, have also been discussed under this heading.

Criticism

The term has been criticised as imprecise and as reflecting 19th-century evolutionary schemes of religion, in which henotheism was cast as a halfway stage between polytheism and monotheism. Nevertheless it remains useful in describing devotional patterns in which one god is treated as supreme without a doctrine denying the others.
