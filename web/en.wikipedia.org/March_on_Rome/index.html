March on Rome

The March on Rome (Italian: Marcia su Roma) was an organised mass demonstration in October 1922 which resulted in Benito Mussolini's National Fascist Party ascending to power in the Kingdom of Italy. In the march, Fascist paramilitary squads — the Blackshirts — converged on Rome from across Italy. Although the march itself was more bluff than military threat, it pressured King Victor Emmanuel III to refuse to sign a state of siege against the Fascists and instead to invite Mussolini to form a government on 29 October 1922.

Background

Post-war Italy was marked by economic crisis, inflation, unemployment and the Biennio Rosso (1919–1920), when strikes and factory occupations raised conservative fears of a socialist revolution. Fascist squads, funded by landowners and industrialists, violently suppressed socialist and trade-union organisations. Mussolini's party entered parliament in 1921 with 35 deputies, and by 1922 successive liberal governments (under Bonomi and Facta) had failed to restore order or contain Fascist violence.

The march

At the Fascist congress in Naples on 24 October 1922, Mussolini declared that either the government would be given to the Fascists or it would be seized. On 27–28 October, Fascist columns began mobilising toward Rome, in reality poorly armed and supplied, numbering perhaps 25,000–30,000 men, and were in no position to defeat the regular army had it resisted. Prime Minister Luigi Facta asked the King to proclaim a state of siege; Victor Emmanuel III refused — from fear of civil war, doubts about the army's loyalty and calculation about his own position. Facta resigned, and on 29 October the King telegraphed Mussolini, who arrived from Milan by train on 30 October and formed a coalition cabinet.

Consequences

Fascist mythology later exalted the march as a heroic revolution; in fact power was handed over constitutionally, and the regime's dictatorship was consolidated only afterwards, through the Acerbo Law, the violence surrounding the 1924 elections, the Matteotti murder crisis and the exceptional laws of 1925–26. The anniversary of the march, 28 October, became the Fascist regime's New Year, and the event's date opened the "Era Fascista" calendar.
