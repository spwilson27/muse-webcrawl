Secretin is a hormone that regulates water homeostasis throughout the body and influences the environment of the duodenum by regulating secretions in the stomach, pancreas and liver. It is a peptide hormone produced in the S cells of the duodenum, which are located in the intestinal glands. In humans, the secretin peptide is encoded by the SCT gene.

History

Secretin was the first hormone to be identified. In 1902, William Bayliss and Ernest Starling at University College London showed that acid introduced into the duodenum caused the pancreas to secrete, even after all nervous connections to the gut had been cut, and that an extract of duodenal mucosa injected into the blood reproduced the effect. They called the active substance "secretin", and Starling introduced the term "hormone" (from Greek hormao, "I excite") in 1905 — founding the science of endocrinology. The peptide was purified and sequenced by Viktor Mutt and J. Erik Jorpes in Stockholm in the 1960s; its 27-amino-acid sequence resembles that of glucagon, VIP and GIP, members of the same hormone family.

Function

When acidic chyme (pH below about 4.5) from the stomach enters the duodenum, S cells release secretin into the bloodstream. Secretin stimulates the pancreatic duct cells to secrete a bicarbonate-rich fluid that neutralises the acid; stimulates bile production and bicarbonate secretion in the liver; and inhibits gastric acid secretion and gastric emptying, forming the humoral arm of the "intestinal phase" of digestion. It also potentiates the action of cholecystokinin (CCK) on enzyme secretion by pancreatic acinar cells and plays roles in osmoregulation in the brain and kidney.

Clinical use

Synthetic human secretin is used diagnostically in the secretin stimulation test for gastrinoma (Zollinger–Ellison syndrome) and to assess pancreatic exocrine function; secretin-enhanced MRCP improves imaging of the pancreatic duct. Unsubstantiated claims in the 1990s that secretin treated autism were not borne out by controlled trials and are not supported by evidence.
