Vajrayana (Sanskrit: वज्रयान, "Diamond Vehicle" or "Thunderbolt Vehicle"), also known as Tantric Buddhism and Esoteric Buddhism, is a tradition within Mahayana Buddhism that developed in medieval India and spread to Tibet, Nepal, Bhutan, Mongolia, and parts of East Asia. It is characterised by the use of tantras, mantras, maṇḍalas, mudrās, initiations (abhiṣeka), and esoteric ritual practices believed to offer a faster path to enlightenment than the gradual methods of other vehicles.

The word vajra denotes both a thunderbolt and a diamond, symbolising the indestructibility and luminosity of awakened mind. Vajrayana practitioners hold that, with proper empowerment from a qualified guru (lama), meditative techniques such as deity yoga — visualising oneself as a Buddha — can lead to Buddhahood in a single lifetime.

History

Esoteric Buddhist practices began to emerge in India around the 5th–7th centuries CE, drawing on Mahayana philosophy (Madhyamaka and Yogācāra) and on ritual elements influenced by Shaivism. Major tantric cycles such as the Guhyasamāja Tantra, the Hevajra Tantra, and later the Kālacakra Tantra were composed between roughly the 7th and 11th centuries. In the 8th century, the Indian master Padmasambhava and the abbot Śāntarakṣita helped establish Buddhism in Tibet. A second dissemination in the 11th–12th centuries, associated with figures such as Atiśa, gave rise to the schools of Tibetan Buddhism: Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya, and Gelug.

Practices

Central practices include the recitation of mantras (such as Om mani padme hum), the construction and contemplation of maṇḍalas, guru yoga, generation-stage and completion-stage meditation, and in some traditions the "six yogas" associated with Nāropa. Transmission requires initiation from a lineage holder, and many practices are traditionally kept secret from the uninitiated.
