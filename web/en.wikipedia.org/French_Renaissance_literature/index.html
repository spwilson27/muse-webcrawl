French Renaissance literature

French Renaissance literature is the body of writing produced in France during the 16th century, shaped by humanism, the rediscovery of classical antiquity, the growth of printing, and the religious upheavals of the Reformation and the Wars of Religion.

Prose

François Rabelais produced the great comic prose cycle Gargantua and Pantagruel (1532–1564), mixing erudition, satire, and earthy humour. Marguerite de Navarre wrote the Heptaméron, a collection of stories modelled on Boccaccio. The century culminated in Michel de Montaigne's Essais (1580–1595), which invented the personal essay as a literary form.

Poetry

Clément Marot and the Lyon school, including Louise Labé, brought new lyric forms to French. The poets of La Pléiade, led by Pierre de Ronsard and Joachim du Bellay, sought to elevate the French language to rival Latin and Greek; Du Bellay's manifesto La Défense et illustration de la langue française (1549) set their programme, and Ronsard's Odes and Amours made him the "prince of poets".

Humanism and the language

The Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539) mandated French in official documents, and humanist scholars such as Guillaume Budé, supported by the Collège de France founded in 1530, advanced the study of Greek and Latin texts that nourished the literature of the age.
