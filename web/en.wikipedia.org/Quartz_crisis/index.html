The quartz crisis (also called the quartz revolution) was an upheaval in the watchmaking industry during the 1970s and early 1980s caused by the advent of quartz watches, which largely replaced mechanical watches around the world. Quartz movements, regulated by the vibrations of a quartz crystal rather than a balance wheel, were far more accurate and far cheaper to manufacture than mechanical movements.

Early quartz watches transformed the watch market in the 1970s

Background

Ironically, the quartz wristwatch was partly a Swiss invention: the Centre Électronique Horloger in Neuchâtel developed the Beta 21 quartz movement in the late 1960s. But Swiss manufacturers, organized around small workshops and mechanical expertise, were slow to exploit it. On December 25, 1969, the Japanese company Seiko released the Astron 35SQ, the first commercially produced quartz wristwatch. It was accurate to within five seconds a month — far better than any mechanical watch — and although it initially cost as much as a car, prices collapsed as production scaled, especially with the arrival of digital LCD watches such as those made by Texas Instruments and Casio.

Impact on the Swiss industry

The consequences for Switzerland were devastating. Between 1970 and the early 1980s, employment in the Swiss watch industry fell from about 90,000 to under 30,000, and the number of watch companies shrank from roughly 1,600 to about 600. Exports of Swiss mechanical watches plummeted as inexpensive, accurate quartz watches from Japan and Hong Kong seized the world market; by the late 1970s Japan had overtaken Switzerland as the largest watch exporter. The American industry, including Hamilton and Bulova, was similarly transformed, and venerable firms failed or were sold.

Recovery

The Swiss industry recovered through consolidation and reinvention. The two largest struggling groups were merged in 1983 into the group that became the Swatch Group, under the leadership of Nicolas Hayek. The launch of the Swatch in 1983 — a cheap, fashionable, Swiss-made quartz watch with radically simplified construction — restored volume production. At the same time, traditional brands repositioned mechanical watches as luxury goods and objects of craftsmanship. By the 1990s Switzerland once again dominated the world watch market by value, even though quartz watches dominate by unit volume to this day.

Legacy

The quartz crisis is a standard case study in disruptive innovation: an industry leader, possessing the new technology, failed to adapt because its structure and expertise were invested in the old one. Mechanical watchmaking survived by moving upmarket, and quartz remains the dominant watch technology by units sold.
