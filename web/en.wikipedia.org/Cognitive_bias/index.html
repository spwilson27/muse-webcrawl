Cognitive bias

A cognitive bias is a systematic pattern of deviation from norm or rationality in judgment. Individuals create their own "subjective reality" from their perception of the input. An individual's construction of reality, not the objective input, may dictate their behavior in the world. Cognitive biases may sometimes lead to perceptual distortion, inaccurate judgment, illogical interpretation, and irrationality.

Overview

While cognitive biases were first systematically studied by psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in the 1970s, the concept has roots in earlier work on heuristics. Tversky and Kahneman introduced the heuristics and biases research program, demonstrating that people rely on mental shortcuts — representativeness, availability, and anchoring and adjustment — that predictably produce errors.

Types

Many biases have been documented, including confirmation bias (favoring information that confirms prior beliefs), anchoring (over-relying on an initial piece of information), hindsight bias (seeing past events as predictable), the Dunning–Kruger effect (overestimating one's competence in a domain where one is unskilled), availability bias (judging likelihood by ease of recall), and framing effects (decisions influenced by how options are presented). Biases can affect memory, attention, belief formation, and social attribution.

Explanations and debiasing

Some biases are explained as byproducts of information-processing shortcuts that are adaptive in most contexts; others stem from motivational factors, such as the desire to maintain self-esteem. Awareness alone rarely eliminates bias, but techniques such as considering the opposite, using base rates, checklists, and structured decision procedures can reduce its effects. The field has faced replication debates, and some classic bias findings have been re-examined, though the overall framework remains influential in psychology, behavioral economics, and decision science.
