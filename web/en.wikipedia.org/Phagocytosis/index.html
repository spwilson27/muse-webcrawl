Phagocytosis

Phagocytosis (from Greek phagein, "to eat", and kytos, "cell") is the process by which a cell engulfs a particle, such as a bacterium, dead cell, or debris, enclosing it in an internal compartment called a phagosome. It is a major mechanism of the innate immune system and, in single-celled organisms such as amoebae, a means of feeding.

Steps

The process begins when surface receptors recognise a target, often one coated (opsonised) with antibodies or complement proteins. The cell extends pseudopods around the particle and internalises it as a phagosome, which then fuses with lysosomes to form a phagolysosome, where enzymes and reactive oxygen species digest the contents.

History

The phenomenon was described by Élie Metchnikoff in the 1880s from observations of starfish larvae and water fleas; his cellular theory of immunity, initially controversial, later earned him a share of the 1908 Nobel Prize. Professional phagocytes — macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells — also present antigens to lymphocytes, linking innate and adaptive immunity. Some pathogens, such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis, survive by blocking phagosome–lysosome fusion. See also immune system and endocytosis.
