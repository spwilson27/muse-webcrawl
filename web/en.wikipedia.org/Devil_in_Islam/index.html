Devil in Islam

In Islam, the Devil is known as Iblis (Arabic: إبليس), also referred to as Shaytan (شيطان, "Satan"). Iblis is a jinn created from smokeless fire who refused God's command to bow before Adam and was expelled from Paradise, thereafter devoting himself to leading humanity astray until the Day of Judgment.

In the Quran

The Quran recounts that when God created Adam, He commanded the angels to prostrate before him. Iblis refused, arguing that he, made of fire, was superior to Adam, made of clay. For this pride and disobedience he was cast out, but granted respite until the end of time, during which he vowed to tempt humans from God's path. Unlike in some Christian traditions, Iblis is not a fallen angel in mainstream Islamic theology, since angels are considered incapable of disobedience; he is instead one of the jinn, beings endowed with free will.

Role and nature

Iblis and his offspring, the shayatin (devils), whisper temptations to humans but have no power to compel them; human beings remain responsible for their own choices. Reciting verses such as Ayat al-Kursi and seeking refuge in God ("A'udhu billahi min ash-shaytan ir-rajim") are traditional protections. During the Hajj, pilgrims symbolically stone the devil at Mina in the ritual of Ramy al-Jamarat.

Theological debates

Sufi traditions sometimes portray Iblis more ambiguously, as a tragic monotheist who refused to bow to other than God. Some modern and heterodox interpretations differ, but orthodox scholarship regards Iblis's refusal as arrogant disobedience deserving punishment in Hell.
