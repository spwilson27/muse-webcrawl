Mexican War of Independence

Miguel Hidalgo's Cry of Dolores in 1810 began the war.

The Mexican War of Independence (16 September 1810 – 27 September 1821) was an armed conflict and political process that freed Mexico from Spanish colonial rule. It was a complex, multi-sided struggle involving insurgents, royalist forces, and shifting alliances across New Spain.

Outbreak

The war began with the Cry of Dolores, issued by the priest Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla on 16 September 1810, calling for revolt against the colonial government. Hidalgo's largely indigenous and mestizo army won early victories but was defeated in 1811; Hidalgo was captured and executed. Leadership passed to José María Morelos, who organised a congress and issued the Sentiments of the Nation before his own capture and execution in 1815.

Consummation

After years of guerrilla warfare under figures such as Vicente Guerrero, the tide turned when the royalist officer Agustín de Iturbide defected and proclaimed the Plan of Iguala in 1821, uniting conservatives and insurgents around independence, Catholicism, and equality. The Army of the Three Guarantees entered Mexico City on 27 September 1821, and independence was formalised by the Treaty of Córdoba.

Legacy

The war devastated the economy and mining industry of Mexico and inaugurated decades of political instability. Hidalgo is honoured as the father of the nation, and Independence Day is celebrated on 16 September.
