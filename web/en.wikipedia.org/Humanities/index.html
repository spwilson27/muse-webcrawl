Humanities

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The humanities are academic disciplines that study aspects of human society and culture, including certain fundamental questions asked by humans. During the Renaissance, the term contrasted with divinity and referred to what is now called classics, the main area of secular study in universities at the time. Today, the humanities are frequently defined as any fields of study outside of natural sciences, social sciences, formal sciences (such as mathematics), and applied sciences.

Fields

The humanities include the disciplines of philosophy, history, literature, languages and linguistics, religious studies, art history, musicology, and the performing arts. They use methods that are primarily critical, interpretive, or speculative, distinguishing them from the empirical approaches of the sciences. Scholars in the humanities are called humanities scholars or humanists.

History

The term derives from the Renaissance Latin studia humanitatis — grammar, rhetoric, history, poetry, and moral philosophy — promoted by humanists such as Petrarch and Erasmus as the core of a liberal education. In the nineteenth century, thinkers such as Wilhelm Dilthey argued for the distinctness of the Geisteswissenschaften ("human sciences") from the natural sciences.

Contemporary debates

The humanities face ongoing debate about funding, enrolment, and public value. Defenders argue they cultivate critical thinking, cultural literacy, and ethical judgement, while newer fields such as digital humanities apply computational methods to traditional questions.
