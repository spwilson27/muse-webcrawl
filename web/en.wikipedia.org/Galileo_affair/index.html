Galileo affair

The Galileo affair (Italian: il processo a Galileo Galilei) was a sequence of events beginning in the early 17th century surrounding Galileo Galilei's support for heliocentrism, the astronomical model in which the Earth and planets revolve around the Sun. The affair culminated in Galileo's trial by the Roman Inquisition in 1633, his conviction for "vehement suspicion of heresy," and his house arrest for the remainder of his life. It remains a defining episode in the history of the relationship between science and religious authority.

Background

Galileo's telescopic discoveries of 1609–1610 — the moons of Jupiter, the phases of Venus, sunspots, and the rugged surface of the Moon — undermined key features of Aristotelian cosmology and the Ptolemaic system, though they did not by themselves prove Earth's motion (the phases of Venus, for example, were also consistent with Tycho Brahe's geo-heliocentric model). Galileo's advocacy of Copernicanism drew opposition from Aristotelian philosophers and from some theologians who cited scriptural passages, such as Joshua's command that the Sun stand still.

The 1616 proceedings

In 1615 Galileo traveled to Rome to defend Copernicanism. In February 1616, consultants to the Inquisition judged heliocentrism "foolish and absurd in philosophy, and formally heretical," and the Congregation of the Index suspended Copernicus's De revolutionibus "until corrected." Cardinal Bellarmine admonished Galileo not to hold or defend the doctrine; the precise terms of this injunction — and of a stricter alleged order not even to discuss it — became central to the later trial.

The Dialogue and the trial of 1633

In 1632 Galileo published the Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, with an imprimatur obtained in Florence. The book presented the Copernican case through a dialogue among Salviati, Sagredo, and the Aristotelian Simplicio; Pope Urban VIII, formerly Galileo's admirer, took offense that his own argument about divine omnipotence had been placed in Simplicio's mouth. Galileo was summoned to Rome and tried in 1633. Facing the threat of torture and imprisonment, he abjured, and was sentenced to house arrest, served first in Siena and then at his villa at Arcetri until his death in 1642. The Dialogue was placed on the Index of Forbidden Books, where it remained until 1835. The famous story that he muttered "Eppur si muove" ("And yet it moves") is almost certainly apocryphal.

Later assessments

The affair has been retold as a simple morality tale of science versus dogma, but historians emphasize its complexity: court politics, Galileo's own abrasive style, biblical hermeneutics (Galileo himself argued, following Augustine, that Scripture teaches "how one goes to heaven, not how heaven goes"), and the absence at the time of direct proof of Earth's motion all played roles. In 1992, Pope John Paul II acknowledged errors by Church officials in the case, following a commission study he had initiated in 1981.
