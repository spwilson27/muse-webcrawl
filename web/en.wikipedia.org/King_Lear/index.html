King Lear

King Lear is a tragedy written by William Shakespeare, probably between 1603 and 1606. It is based on the mythological Leir of Britain, a pre-Roman Celtic king. The play depicts the consequences of the aging Lear's decision to divide his kingdom among his three daughters according to their declarations of love: flattered by Goneril and Regan, he disinherits the honest Cordelia, and descends into madness, destitution and death amid civil strife.

Plot

Lear, king of Britain, asks his daughters to profess their love in return for shares of the kingdom. Goneril and Regan flatter him extravagantly; Cordelia, refusing to inflate her sincere devotion, is banished and marries the King of France. The Earl of Kent is exiled for defending her. Goneril and Regan then strip Lear of his retinue and dignity; turned out into a storm, Lear goes mad on the heath, accompanied by his Fool and the disguised Kent.

In a parallel subplot, the Earl of Gloucester is gulled by his bastard son Edmund into disowning his loyal son Edgar; Gloucester is later blinded by Regan and her husband Cornwall for aiding Lear. Cordelia returns with a French army; she is defeated, and Edmund orders her hanged. Lear dies of grief over her body. Edmund, Edgar, Goneril, Regan and Gloucester also die; the kingdom passes to the survivors, Edgar and Albany.

Sources and texts

Shakespeare drew on the chronicle account of King Leir in Holinshed, the earlier play King Leir (c. 1590), Sidney's Arcadia for the Gloucester subplot, and Montaigne's essays. The play survives in two substantially different early texts — the 1608 Quarto ("The History of King Lear") and the 1623 Folio ("The Tragedy of King Lear") — treated by many modern editors as distinct versions of a revised work.

Reception

From 1681 to 1838 the stage knew mostly Nahum Tate's adaptation with a happy ending, in which Cordelia lives and marries Edgar. Restored to its tragic form in the 19th century, the play is now widely judged among Shakespeare's supreme achievements and one of the greatest tragedies in English; its storm scenes, the Fool, and Lear's speeches on unaccommodated man have had vast influence on literature, opera and film (notably Kurosawa's Ran, 1985).
