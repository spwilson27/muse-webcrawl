Affirmation in law

In law, an affirmation is a solemn declaration allowed to those who conscientiously object to taking an oath. An affirmation has the same legal effect as an oath: the person undertakes to tell the truth and faces the same penalties, including prosecution for perjury, for false statements. Affirmations are used by witnesses in court, office-holders, and others required to swear.

History

In England, Quakers and Moravians, whose religious beliefs forbade swearing oaths, were permitted to affirm from the late seventeenth century. The right was gradually extended to all persons regardless of belief; the Oaths Act 1978 governs affirmations in the United Kingdom today. Many other jurisdictions, including the United States and Commonwealth countries, similarly allow affirmation as an alternative to a religious oath.

Form

A typical affirmation begins, "I do solemnly, sincerely and truly declare and affirm..." The person affirming raises no religious text and invokes no deity, but the declaration binds their conscience in law exactly as an oath does.
