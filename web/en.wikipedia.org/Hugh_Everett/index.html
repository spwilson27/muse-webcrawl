Hugh Everett III (11 November 1930 – 19 July 1982) was an American physicist who, in his 1957 Princeton PhD thesis, proposed the relative state formulation of quantum mechanics, later popularized as the many-worlds interpretation. It holds that all outcomes of quantum measurements are realized in a branching universal wavefunction, with no collapse.

Early life and education

Everett studied chemical engineering at the Catholic University of America and then physics at Princeton, where he worked on game theory and military operations research before turning to the foundations of quantum mechanics under John Archibald Wheeler. His thesis, "On the Foundations of Quantum Mechanics," argued that the wavefunction should be treated as universally valid and that observers themselves are quantum-mechanical systems correlated with the states they measure.

Reception and career change

The theory was largely ignored or opposed — notably by Niels Bohr's Copenhagen school — and Everett left academic physics soon after his doctorate. He became a pioneer of defense analysis and computing, applying Lagrangian multipliers to optimization (the "Everett algorithm"), working at the Pentagon's Weapons Systems Evaluation Group and co-founding the Lambda Corporation. He grew wealthy from defense contracting and database work.

Later recognition

Interest in Everett's ideas revived in the 1970s through the work of Bryce DeWitt, who coined the term "many-worlds." The interpretation is now one of the major contenders in the foundations of quantum mechanics, and Everett has been the subject of biographies and documentaries. His son is Mark Everett, frontman of the rock band Eels.
