Snapshot (photography)

The snapshot made photography an everyday, informal practice

A snapshot is an informal photograph taken quickly and casually, typically with a simple camera and without artistic or journalistic intent. The term was popularised after the introduction of cheap roll-film cameras such as the Kodak Brownie (1900), whose slogan "You press the button, we do the rest" put photography in the hands of amateurs.

History and characteristics

Before the snapshot, photographs were usually formal, posed and made by professionals. Snapshots instead recorded birthdays, holidays, family gatherings and everyday scenes, often with technical imperfections — tilted horizons, awkward framing, motion blur — that came to define the snapshot aesthetic. The mass accumulation of snapshots in family albums created a vast vernacular archive of twentieth-century life.

Snapshot aesthetic

From the mid-twentieth century, art photographers deliberately adopted snapshot conventions: Robert Frank, William Klein, Nan Goldin and Martin Parr are among those associated with diaristic, unvarnished styles. The Museum of Modern Art's exhibitions of vernacular photography and works such as John Szarkowski's writings helped legitimise the snapshot as an artistic form. In the smartphone era, the snapshot has merged into everyday digital communication through platforms for sharing casual images.
