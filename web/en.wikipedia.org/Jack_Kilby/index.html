Jack St. Clair Kilby (8 November 1923 – 20 June 2005) was an American electrical engineer who, while working at Texas Instruments, realised the first working integrated circuit in 1958. He shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics for his part in the invention. He is also credited as a co-inventor of the handheld calculator and the thermal printer.

Early life and education

Kilby grew up in Great Bend, Kansas, where his father ran a small electric company; a youthful experience keeping communications alive during an ice storm sparked his interest in electronics. After wartime service as a radio engineer, he earned a degree in electrical engineering from the University of Illinois in 1947 and a master's degree from the University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee in 1950 while working at Centralab in Milwaukee.

The integrated circuit

Kilby joined Texas Instruments in Dallas in 1958. Because he was a new employee, he had not yet earned vacation time and worked alone through the company's summer shutdown. Reflecting on the "tyranny of numbers" — the growing difficulty of wiring ever more components together — he concluded that resistors and capacitors could be made from the same semiconductor material as transistors, and that an entire circuit could be built in a single piece of material. On 12 September 1958 he demonstrated the first working integrated circuit, an oscillator built in germanium. Texas Instruments filed a patent in February 1959. Independently, Robert Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor developed a silicon version with a practical interconnection method, and the two men are jointly regarded as co-inventors of the integrated circuit.

Later work

Kilby led teams that built the first military systems and computers using integrated circuits. In 1967 he co-invented the Pocketronic, the first handheld calculator, based on integrated circuitry. He held more than 60 patents. He took a leave of absence from Texas Instruments in 1970 to work as an independent inventor, and from 1978 to 1984 was a professor of electrical engineering at Texas A&M University.

Honours

Kilby shared half of the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics "for his part in the invention of the integrated circuit". He also received the US National Medal of Science, the National Medal of Technology, and the Kyoto Prize. He died of cancer in Dallas in 2005.
