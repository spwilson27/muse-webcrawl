Tetrapod

A tetrapod (from Greek τετρα- tetra- "four" and πούς poús "foot") is any four-limbed vertebrate animal of the superclass Tetrapoda. Tetrapods include all living and extinct amphibians and amniotes, the latter in turn including reptiles (including birds) and mammals. Tetrapods evolved from finned tetrapodomorph fishes in the middle of the Devonian period, about 390 million years ago.

Evolution

The first tetrapods evolved from lobe-finned fish ("tetrapodomorphs") in freshwater or brackish habitats. Forms such as Tiktaalik (c. 375 Ma) had fin skeletons with recognizably limb-like bones, while Acanthostega and Ichthyostega (c. 365 Ma) possessed true digits — eight on each limb in Acanthostega — yet remained largely aquatic. The five-digit (pentadactyl) limb became standard later. Fossil trackways from Poland dated to about 395 Ma suggest tetrapods may have originated earlier than the body-fossil record shows.

Diversity

By the Carboniferous, tetrapods had diversified into fully terrestrial forms. The key innovation of the amniotes — an egg with membranes that freed reproduction from open water — split the lineage into the synapsids (leading to mammals) and sauropsids (leading to reptiles and birds). Some tetrapod lineages secondarily lost limbs entirely, notably snakes and caecilians, and others returned to the water, such as whales and ichthyosaurs; all remain tetrapods by descent. There are more than 33,000 described living tetrapod species.
