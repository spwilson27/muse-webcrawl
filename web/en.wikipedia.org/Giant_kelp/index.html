Giant kelp

Giant kelp (Macrocystis pyrifera) is a species of large brown alga and the largest of all kelps. It forms extensive underwater forests in cold, nutrient-rich coastal waters of the Pacific Ocean and the Southern Hemisphere, and it is one of the fastest-growing organisms on Earth.

Description

Giant kelp grows from a root-like holdfast attached to rocky seabed. Its long stipes (stems) bear blades (leaves), each buoyed by a gas-filled float called a pneumatocyst at its base, which lifts the fronds toward the sunlit surface. Mature plants commonly exceed 30 metres in length and can reach 45 metres or more. Under ideal conditions it can grow as much as 60 centimetres (2 feet) in a single day.

Distribution and ecology

The species is found along the Pacific coast of North America from Alaska to Mexico, off South America, and around islands of the southern oceans including New Zealand, Tasmania, and South Africa. Kelp forests built by giant kelp are among the most productive and diverse ecosystems in the sea, sheltering fish, sea otters, abalone, sea urchins, and hundreds of other species.

Uses

Giant kelp is harvested commercially as a source of algin, a thickening and gelling agent used in food, pharmaceuticals, and industry. It has also been used as fertiliser and animal feed, and has been investigated as a feedstock for biofuels. Aquaculture of the species is practised in several countries.
