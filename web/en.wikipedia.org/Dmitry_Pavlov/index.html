Dmitry Grigoryevich Pavlov (Russian: Дмитрий Григорьевич Павлов; 4 November 1897 – 22 July 1941) was a Soviet general who commanded the Western Front during the initial stage of the German invasion of the Soviet Union (Operation Barbarossa) in June 1941. After his forces were heavily defeated within the first days of the war, he was recalled to Moscow, arrested, tried for military incompetence and executed.

Career

Pavlov served in the First World War and joined the Red Army in 1919, fighting in the Russian Civil War. He studied at the Frunze Military Academy and became a leading specialist in armoured warfare. As a Soviet adviser in the Spanish Civil War (under the pseudonym "Pablo") he commanded a tank brigade and was made a Hero of the Soviet Union. His conclusion from Spain that tanks should support infantry rather than operate independently influenced Soviet doctrine controversially.

June 1941 and execution

From January 1941 Pavlov commanded the Western Special Military District, which became the Western Front on 22 June 1941. German forces encircled much of his command in the Białystok–Minsk pocket; Soviet losses were catastrophic. Pavlov was arrested on 4 July, convicted of "cowardice, unauthorised abandonment of strategic points ... and failure to direct his troops" and shot on 22 July 1941. He was rehabilitated posthumously in 1956 during de-Stalinisation.

Assessment

Historians debate Pavlov's responsibility: his dispositions left forces exposed in the Białystok salient, and he lost communication with his armies early in the attack, but he also operated under strict orders from Stalin forbidding "provocative" responses to German build-ups, and much of the failure stemmed from systemic unpreparedness.
