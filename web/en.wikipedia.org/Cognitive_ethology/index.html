Cognitive ethology

Cognitive ethology is a branch of ethology concerned with the influence of conscious awareness and intention on the behaviour of an animal. Donald Griffin, a zoologist and the discoverer of animal echolocation, founded the study of cognitive ethology in the 1970s with his books The Question of Animal Awareness (1976) and Animal Thinking (1984), arguing that the mental experiences of animals are a legitimate subject for scientific inquiry.

Scope

The field studies cognitive processes such as perception, learning, memory, problem solving, communication, and self-recognition in animals, typically in naturalistic or semi-natural settings. It blends classical ethology — the study of animal behaviour under natural conditions, associated with Konrad Lorenz and Niko Tinbergen — with cognitive science. Researchers investigate such topics as whether animals plan for the future, attribute mental states to others (theory of mind), count, or experience emotions.

Debate

Cognitive ethology has been controversial. Critics influenced by behaviorism and by Morgan's canon warn against anthropomorphism and argue that mentalistic explanations are untestable. Defenders reply that evolutionary continuity between humans and other animals makes some degree of mental experience plausible, and that hypotheses about animal minds can generate testable predictions. The field overlaps with comparative psychology and animal cognition research, and it has contributed to modern debates about animal sentience and welfare.
