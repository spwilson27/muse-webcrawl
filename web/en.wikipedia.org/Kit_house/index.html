Kit house

A kit house is a type of prefabricated house sold as a set of pre-cut lumber, framing, roofing, and fittings, shipped to the customer — historically by rail — for assembly on site by the owner or a local builder. Kit houses were a major feature of North American housing in the first half of the 20th century.

History

The best-known seller was Sears, Roebuck and Co., whose Modern Homes programme (1908–1942) sold roughly 70,000 houses in nearly 450 designs — from modest cottages to the nine-bedroom Magnolia — marketed through its mail-order catalogue with mortgages attached. Competitors included the Aladdin Company of Bay City, Michigan (a pioneer from 1906), Montgomery Ward, Gordon-Van Tine, and the Canadian Eaton's chain. Pieces arrived numbered and labelled with a detailed instruction manual; balloon and platform framing with standardised lumber made self-building feasible.

Identification and survival

Most kit houses survive in the Midwest and eastern United States and Canada. They can be identified by stamped lumber part numbers, shipping labels, distinctive floor plans matching catalogue illustrations, and hardware from the supplying company. Preservationists and historians document surviving examples, and several Sears models are listed on historic registers.

Modern equivalents

The concept persists in modern log-home kits, panelised housing, and flat-pack designs sold by manufacturers worldwide, sold on the same principle of factory precision reducing on-site costs.
