Japanese invasion of Manchuria

The Japanese invasion of Manchuria began on 18 September 1931, when the Imperial Japanese Army's Kwantung Army invaded Manchuria in northeastern China immediately following the Mukden Incident. By February 1932 the Japanese had occupied the whole of Manchuria and established the puppet state of Manchukuo, nominally ruled by the former Qing emperor Puyi. The invasion is widely regarded as a decisive step on the road to the Second World War, exposing the weakness of the League of Nations and inaugurating fifteen years of Japanese expansion in Asia.

Background

Japan had held extensive rights in southern Manchuria since its victory in the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–05: the lease of the Kwantung Peninsula, the South Manchuria Railway, and the right to station the Kwantung Army to guard the railway zone. In the 1920s, Chinese nationalism and the warlord Zhang Xueliang's alignment with the Nationalist government in Nanjing increasingly threatened these privileges, while the Great Depression intensified pressure within Japan for territorial and economic expansion. Radical officers of the Kwantung Army, notably Colonel Seishirō Itagaki and Lieutenant Colonel Kanji Ishiwara, planned a pretext for seizing Manchuria outright.

The Mukden Incident and invasion

On the night of 18 September 1931, Japanese officers detonated a small quantity of dynamite beside the South Manchuria Railway track near Mukden (Shenyang) — damage so slight that a train passed safely minutes later. Blaming Chinese troops, the Kwantung Army attacked the Chinese barracks at Beidaying and seized Mukden, then fanned out across southern Manchuria. Acting on its own initiative and in defiance of the civilian government in Tokyo, the army expanded operations northward; reinforcements from Korea crossed the Yalu without authorisation.

Zhang Xueliang, following a policy of non-resistance associated with Chiang Kai-shek's priority of defeating the Communists first, withdrew most of his Northeastern Army south of the Great Wall. Isolated resistance did occur: Ma Zhanshan fought the Jiangqiao Campaign in Heilongjiang in November 1931, and Chinese forces defended Jinzhou briefly before abandoning it in January 1932. Harbin fell in February 1932, completing the conquest. Anti-Japanese volunteer armies continued guerrilla resistance across Manchuria for years afterwards.

Manchukuo

In March 1932 Japan sponsored the proclamation of the "independent" state of Manchukuo, installing Puyi — the last emperor of the Qing dynasty — first as Chief Executive and, in 1934, as Emperor Kangde. Manchukuo was recognised only by Japan and a handful of sympathetic states; the Japanese ambassador doubled as commander of the Kwantung Army and effective ruler. The new state became a base for Japanese industrial investment, settlement, and the notorious biological warfare programme of Unit 731 near Harbin.

International reaction

China appealed to the League of Nations, which appointed the Lytton Commission. Its report, published in October 1932, concluded that Manchuria was Chinese territory, that the Japanese operations of 18 September could not be regarded as legitimate self-defence, and that Manchukuo was not the product of a genuine independence movement. Japan responded by withdrawing from the League in 1933. The United States, under Secretary of State Henry L. Stimson, issued the Stimson Doctrine of non-recognition of territorial changes effected by force — but neither power imposed effective sanctions, a failure that encouraged further aggression by Japan, Italy, and Germany.

Consequences

The invasion fatally weakened Japan's party governments — Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi was assassinated in May 1932 — and entrenched military dominance of policy. Manchuria supplied coal, iron, and agricultural land, and served as the springboard for Japan's advance into North China, culminating in the Marco Polo Bridge Incident of July 1937 and the full-scale Second Sino-Japanese War. In Chinese historical memory, 18 September is commemorated as National Humiliation Day.
