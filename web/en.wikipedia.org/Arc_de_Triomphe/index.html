The Arc de Triomphe de l'Étoile is one of the most famous monuments in Paris, standing at the western end of the Champs-Élysées at the centre of Place Charles de Gaulle, from which twelve avenues radiate. It honours those who fought and died for France, and its design, by Jean Chalgrin, draws on the Arch of Titus in Rome.

History

Napoleon commissioned the arch in 1806 after his victory at Austerlitz, but construction proceeded slowly and it was completed only in 1836 under King Louis-Philippe. Its sculptures, including François Rude's Departure of the Volunteers of 1792 (La Marseillaise), celebrate French military triumphs and resistance. The names of 558 generals and the major victories of the Revolutionary and Napoleonic wars are inscribed on its surfaces. Napoleon's remains passed under the arch in 1840 on their way to the Invalides, as did Victor Hugo's coffin in 1885.

Tomb of the Unknown Soldier

Beneath the arch lies the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier from the First World War, interred in 1921, with an eternal flame lit in 1923 and rekindled daily. The monument is a focal point for national ceremonies, notably Bastille Day parades.
