Classical Greek sculpture
Period: c. 480–323 BC
Materials: Marble, bronze
Key figures: Phidias, Polykleitos, Praxiteles, Skopas, Lysippos
Hallmarks: Contrapposto, ideal proportions, naturalism

Classical Greek sculpture refers to the sculpture of ancient Greece during the Classical period (roughly the 5th and 4th centuries BC), following the Archaic period and preceding the Hellenistic. It is celebrated for its development of naturalistic yet idealized representations of the human body, achieved principally in marble and bronze.

Early Classical (Severe Style)

The transition from Archaic stiffness is visible in works such as the Kritios Boy (c. 480 BC), which introduces a relaxed, weight-shifted stance known as contrapposto, and in the pedimental sculptures of the Temple of Zeus at Olympia. The Charioteer of Delphi and the bronze gods recovered from the sea (such as the Artemision Bronze) exemplify the "Severe Style".

High Classical

In the mid-5th century BC, Polykleitos codified ideal proportions in his treatise the Canon, embodied in the Doryphoros (Spear-Bearer). Phidias directed the sculptural program of the Parthenon in Athens, whose pediments, metopes, and frieze define High Classical art. Myron's Discobolus captures an athlete mid-motion. Most Greek bronzes survive only in Roman marble copies.

Late Classical

In the 4th century BC, Praxiteles introduced the sensuous S-curve and the first life-size female nude, the Aphrodite of Knidos; Skopas conveyed intense emotion, and Lysippos, court sculptor to Alexander the Great, refined proportions with a smaller head and more slender body, paving the way for Hellenistic sculpture.
