Special relativity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Special relativity
ProposedAlbert Einstein, 1905 ("On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies")
Postulates(1) Laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames; (2) Speed of light in vacuum is constant for all observers
Key resultsTime dilation, length contraction, relativity of simultaneity, E = mc²
FrameworkMinkowski spacetime; Lorentz transformations

Special relativity is a scientific theory of the relationship between space and time, published by Albert Einstein in 1905. It applies to systems moving at constant velocity relative to one another (inertial frames) and replaces the Newtonian picture of absolute space and time with a unified four-dimensional spacetime.

Postulates and consequences

From the two postulates it follows that measurements of time intervals and lengths depend on the observer's motion: moving clocks run slow (time dilation), moving objects contract along their direction of travel, and events simultaneous in one frame need not be in another. No object or signal can exceed the speed of light in vacuum, which is the same for all observers. Mass and energy are equivalent, expressed by the famous equation E = mc².

Development

The theory drew on the work of Lorentz, Poincaré and the null result of the Michelson–Morley experiment, which failed to detect the luminiferous aether. Minkowski's geometric formulation (1908) recast it in terms of spacetime. Einstein extended the theory to accelerated frames and gravitation in general relativity (1915).

Evidence

Special relativity is confirmed by countless experiments, from the extended lifetimes of fast-moving muons to the daily corrections applied to GPS satellite clocks, and it is foundational to particle physics and quantum field theory.
