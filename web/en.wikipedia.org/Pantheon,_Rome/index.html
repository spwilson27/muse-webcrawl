Pantheon, Rome

Pantheon Location: Rome, Italy
Built: c. 113–125 AD (Hadrianic rebuild)
Dedicated to: All gods; later St Mary and the Martyrs
Architectural style: Roman
Notable feature: World's largest unreinforced concrete dome

The Pantheon (from Greek Πάνθειον, "temple of all the gods") is a former Roman temple and, since the year 609 AD, a Catholic church (Basilica di Santa Maria ad Martyres) in Rome, Italy, on the site of an earlier temple commissioned by Marcus Agrippa during the reign of Augustus (27 BC – 14 AD). It was rebuilt by the emperor Hadrian and probably dedicated around 126 AD. Its date of construction is uncertain, because Hadrian chose not to inscribe the new temple but rather to retain the inscription of Agrippa's older temple, which had burned down.

The building is cylindrical with a portico of large granite Corinthian columns (eight in the front and two groups of four behind) under a pediment. A rectangular vestibule links the porch to the rotunda, which is under a coffered concrete dome, with a central opening (oculus) to the sky. Almost two thousand years after it was built, the Pantheon's dome is still the world's largest unreinforced concrete dome. The height to the oculus and the diameter of the interior circle are the same, 43 metres (142 ft).

History

The original Pantheon was built by Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa, son-in-law of Augustus, in 27–25 BC as part of a complex in the Campus Martius. That building burned in the fire of 80 AD, was rebuilt by Domitian, and burned again in 110 AD after being struck by lightning. The present structure was completed under Hadrian (reigned 117–138 AD), probably around 126 AD, though the pedimental inscription still reads "M·AGRIPPA·L·F·COS·TERTIVM·FECIT" — "Marcus Agrippa, son of Lucius, made [this building] in his third consulship."

In 609, the Byzantine emperor Phocas donated the building to Pope Boniface IV, who consecrated it as the church of St Mary and the Martyrs on 13 May 609. This consecration is widely credited with saving the Pantheon from the neglect, destruction, and spoliation that befell most of ancient Rome's buildings during the early medieval period.

Architecture

The rotunda's walls are 6.1 metres (20 ft) thick, and the dome is composed of concrete whose aggregate grows progressively lighter toward the top — heavy travertine near the base, then brick and tufa, and finally light pumice around the oculus. The dome's coffers, arranged in five rings of 28, reduce weight and were probably decorative as well as structural. The oculus, 8.9 metres (29 ft) across, is the building's only source of natural light besides the doorway, and it also serves as a cooling and ventilation method; rain that enters drains away through a system of holes in the marble floor.

The interior contains tombs of notable figures, including the painters Raphael and Annibale Carracci, the composer Arcangelo Corelli, and the kings Vittorio Emanuele II and Umberto I of Italy, as well as Umberto's wife, Queen Margherita.

Influence

The Pantheon is one of the best-preserved of all ancient Roman buildings, largely because it has been in continuous use throughout its history, and it has served as a model for countless later domed buildings, from Brunelleschi's dome in Florence to the United States Capitol and university libraries across the Western world. Michelangelo is said to have remarked that it was the design of angels, not of men.
