Life on Mars

Mars today is cold and dry, but ancient river valleys suggest a wetter past.

The possibility of life on Mars is a major subject of interest in astrobiology. While there is no confirmed evidence that life exists — or ever existed — on Mars, geological evidence shows that early Mars had liquid water on its surface and a thicker atmosphere, making its ancient environment potentially habitable for microorganisms.

Habitability

Ancient Mars (the Noachian period, more than 3.7 billion years ago) featured lakes, rivers and possibly oceans. The Curiosity rover found organic molecules and evidence of an ancient freshwater lake in Gale Crater, while the Perseverance rover has catalogued organic-bearing samples in Jezero Crater, a former lake bed. Liquid water may still exist transiently as brines, and large quantities are locked in polar ice and subsurface reservoirs.

Searches for life

The Viking landers of 1976 performed the first direct experiments to detect life in Martian soil. The labelled release experiment produced results initially suggestive of metabolism, but the consensus interpretation attributed the reactions to reactive soil chemistry, in particular perchlorates, which were confirmed on Mars by the Phoenix lander in 2008.

The Martian meteorite ALH84001, found in Antarctica, prompted claims in 1996 of fossilised microbial structures; these claims remain controversial and are not generally accepted as evidence of past life. Seasonal variations in atmospheric methane, reported by orbiters and rovers, have also been debated, since methane can be produced geologically as well as biologically.

Present-day possibilities

If life persists on Mars today, it would most plausibly be microbial and located beneath the surface, sheltered from ultraviolet radiation and the thin, dry atmosphere. Extremophile organisms on Earth that thrive in deep aquifers, Antarctic dry valleys and high-radiation environments are studied as analogues.

Planetary protection

Space agencies follow planetary protection protocols to avoid contaminating Mars with Earth microbes, which could produce false positives or harm any native biosphere. Sample return missions are designed to bring cached Martian material to Earth for analysis in specialised containment facilities.
