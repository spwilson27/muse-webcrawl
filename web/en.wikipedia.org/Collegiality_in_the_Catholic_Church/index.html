Collegiality in the Catholic Church

Collegiality in the Catholic Church is the doctrine that the bishops of the Church, as a college (body) in union with its head, the pope, share responsibility for the governance and teaching of the whole Church. The idea is rooted in the belief that the college of bishops succeeds the college of the apostles, whose head was Saint Peter, succeeded by the bishop of Rome.

Vatican II

The doctrine was prominently developed at the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965). The dogmatic constitution Lumen gentium taught that episcopal consecration confers membership in the college of bishops, which, together with the pope and never without him, exercises supreme authority over the Church — most solemnly in an ecumenical council. A controversial explanatory note (Nota explicativa praevia) clarified that the college acts as such only in communion with its head, preserving papal primacy defined at Vatican I.

Structures and debate

Practical expressions of collegiality include the Synod of Bishops (established 1965), episcopal conferences, and councils. Since Vatican II, theologians and popes have debated the balance between collegiality and primacy: John Paul II and Benedict XVI stressed that collegiality does not limit the pope's personal authority, while Pope Francis emphasized "synodality" and convened synods with broader consultation, including lay participation, culminating in the Synod on Synodality (2021–2024). Critics at both extremes argue collegiality has been either insufficiently implemented or pushed toward a quasi-parliamentary model foreign to Catholic ecclesiology.
