Luray Caverns

Luray Caverns
Location: Luray, Page County, Virginia, USA
Discovered: 13 August 1878
Discoverers: Andrew Campbell, William Campbell, Benton Stebbins
Depth: up to ~164 ft (50 m) below entrance
Temperature: 54 °F (12 °C) year-round
Designation: U.S. National Natural Landmark (1974)
Features: Giant's Hall, Dream Lake, Great Stalacpipe Organ

Luray Caverns are the largest caverns in the eastern United States, located west of Luray, Virginia, in the Shenandoah Valley. The cave system, formed in dolomite and limestone of the Cambrian-Ordovician Beekmantown formation, is celebrated for its profuse and colourful speleothems — stalactites, stalagmites, flowstone, columns and mirrored pools — and draws roughly half a million visitors a year. The caverns were designated a U.S. National Natural Landmark in 1974.

Discovery and development

The caverns were discovered on 13 August 1878 by tinsmith Andrew Campbell, his nephew William Campbell, and local photographer Benton Stebbins, who noticed cool air rising from a sinkhole on Cave Hill. The discoverers kept the find secret until they could buy the land; ensuing litigation over the purchase — the sellers claimed fraud because the land's true value had been concealed — reached the Virginia courts before the caverns were developed as a show cave under Sam Buracker and later the Graves family, whose descendants still operate them.

Features

The paved tour route, about 1.25 miles (2 km), passes cathedral-sized chambers including Giant's Hall, whose double columns rise some 47 feet, and the Pluto's Ghost column. Dream Lake creates a near-perfect mirror illusion of the stalactites above it. The cave's most famous attraction is the Great Stalacpipe Organ, built in the 1950s by mathematician and electronics engineer Leland W. Sprinkle: solenoid-actuated rubber mallets tap tuned stalactites across 3.5 acres of the cavern, wired to an organ console — billed as the world's largest musical instrument. Other attractions at the site include the Car and Carriage Caravan Museum and the Luray Valley Museum.

Geology

The caverns formed as slightly acidic groundwater dissolved the host carbonate rock along fractures. Formations grow extremely slowly — roughly one cubic inch per 120 years — as mineral-laden water deposits calcite; the white, amber and red colours come from calcite, iron oxide and other mineral impurities. The cave air stays at a constant 54 °F (12 °C).
