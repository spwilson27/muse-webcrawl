Rule of inference

In logic, a rule of inference (also called an inference rule or transformation rule) is a logical form consisting of a function that takes premises, analyses their syntax, and returns a conclusion or conclusions. Rules of inference are the basic building blocks of formal proofs in deductive systems: a valid rule preserves truth, so that if the premises are true, the conclusion must also be true.

Form and standard rules

A rule is typically written with the premises above a horizontal line and the conclusion below it. Standard examples in propositional and predicate logic include modus ponens (from P and "if P then Q", infer Q), modus tollens, hypothetical syllogism, disjunctive syllogism, and universal instantiation and existential generalisation for quantifiers. Systems of natural deduction, introduced by Gerhard Gentzen in the 1930s, are built almost entirely from such rules, with introduction and elimination rules for each logical connective; Hilbert-style systems, by contrast, rely on many axiom schemas and very few rules, often modus ponens alone.

Properties

A rule is sound if it preserves validity (or truth); a set of rules is complete if every semantically valid argument can be derived with them. A distinction is drawn between rules that are primitive and "admissible" rules — those whose conclusions are derivable whenever their premises are — a famous admissible-but-not-derivable rule appearing in the study of structural proof theory. Rules of inference also underpin automated theorem proving and logic programming, where resolution and backward chaining apply rules mechanically.
