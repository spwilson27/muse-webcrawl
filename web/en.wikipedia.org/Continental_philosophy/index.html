Continental philosophy is a label for philosophical traditions that developed in mainland Europe from the late 18th century onward, in contrast to the analytic philosophy dominant in the English-speaking world. The term is retrospective and contested — few of the philosophers so described used it of themselves — but it groups together movements that share historical roots in Kant and Hegel and an interest in history, interpretation, and the conditions of human experience.

Major movements

The tradition is usually traced through German idealism (Kant, Fichte, Schelling, Hegel) and its critics (Schopenhauer, Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Marx). In the 20th century it diversified into phenomenology (Husserl, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty), existentialism (Sartre, Beauvoir, Camus), hermeneutics (Gadamer, Ricoeur), the critical theory of the Frankfurt School (Adorno, Horkheimer, Habermas), and French structuralism and post-structuralism (Lévi-Strauss, Foucault, Derrida, Deleuze).

Themes and style

Continental philosophers often treat concepts as historically situated, focus on lived experience, language, power and embodiment, and engage closely with literature, art and politics. Critics in the analytic tradition have charged the style with obscurity; defenders reply that the analytic/continental divide is increasingly artificial, with growing overlap in topics and methods since the late 20th century.

Influence

Continental thought has been especially influential in literary studies, critical theory, film studies, architecture and parts of theology and political theory, where figures such as Foucault and Derrida are canonical.
