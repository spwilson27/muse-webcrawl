Federal Project Number One

Posters like this one advertised the plays, concerts, and exhibitions of Federal Project Number One.

Federal Project Number One, commonly called Federal One, was a New Deal programme of the United States Works Progress Administration (WPA) created in 1935 to employ artists, writers, musicians, actors, and historians during the Great Depression. It comprised five cultural projects: the Federal Art Project, the Federal Music Project, the Federal Theatre Project, the Federal Writers' Project, and the Historical Records Survey. At its peak, Federal One employed more than 40,000 cultural workers and is credited with sustaining American culture — and the careers of a generation of artists — through the Depression.

The five projects

The Federal Art Project, directed by Holger Cahill, produced murals, sculptures, posters, and easel paintings for public buildings and ran community art centres across the country; artists later famous in the Abstract Expressionist movement, including Jackson Pollock and Willem de Kooning, worked for it. The Federal Music Project, under Nikolai Sokoloff, organised orchestras and bands that gave thousands of free or cheap concerts and copied and performed American music. The Federal Theatre Project, directed by Hallie Flanagan, staged productions nationwide — including Orson Welles's all-Black "Voodoo" Macbeth in Harlem (1936) and the "Living Newspaper" plays dramatising current events — and admitted millions of first-time theatregoers, many free of charge.

The Federal Writers' Project produced the American Guide Series of state guidebooks, collected folklore, and recorded some 2,300 life histories of formerly enslaved people, the famous Slave Narrative Collection; writers employed included Ralph Ellison, Richard Wright, Studs Terkel, and Zora Neale Hurston. The Historical Records Survey catalogued archives, manuscripts, and county records, work foundational to American archival practice.

Controversy and end

Federal One was controversial: conservatives attacked some productions as left-wing propaganda, and the Federal Theatre Project was singled out by the House Un-American Activities Committee. Congress terminated the theatre project in 1939, and the remaining projects were devolved to state sponsorship or wound down by 1943 as the economy moved onto a war footing.

Legacy

The murals, posters, guidebooks, recordings, and slave narratives created under Federal One remain in public collections — many murals still decorate post offices and schools — and the project is remembered as the United States' most ambitious experiment in direct federal patronage of the arts.
