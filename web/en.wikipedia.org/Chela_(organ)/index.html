Chela (organ)

A chela (/ˈkiːlə/, from Greek χηλή, khēlē, "claw"; plural chelae) is a pincer-like organ at the end of certain limbs of some arthropods. The name comes from Greek through classical Latin chēla. Chelae are most familiar as the claws of crabs, lobsters, crayfish, and scorpions, but they occur in many other arthropod groups.

Structure

A chela is formed from two opposed elements: a fixed finger, which is an extension of the propodus (or analogous segment), and a movable finger, the dactyl, which articulates against it. Muscles close the movable finger against the fixed finger, producing a grasping or crushing action. In many decapod crustaceans the chelae are enlarged into chelipeds, and in males of fiddler crabs one chela is dramatically larger than the other and is used in display and combat.

Chelae across groups

Chelae occur in true crabs, lobsters, and shrimp (Decapoda); in scorpions and pseudoscorpions, where the pedipalps end in chelae; and in various other chelicerates and crustaceans. The chelate legs of some insects serve similar grasping functions. Animals bearing chelae are said to be chelate; limbs modified into chelae are called chelipeds when they are walking legs adapted for that purpose.
