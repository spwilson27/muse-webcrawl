Fresnel zone plate

Fresnel zone plate
TypeOptical element
PrincipleDiffraction
UsesX-ray focusing, microscopy, imaging

A Fresnel zone plate is a device used to focus light or other waves, consisting of a set of concentric annular zones that alternately block and transmit the incoming wave. Named after the French physicist Augustin-Jean Fresnel, the zone plate produces a focus by constructive interference of the transmitted portions of the wavefront, functioning much like a lens but relying on diffraction rather than refraction.

Principle

The radii of the zones are chosen so that waves passing through adjacent transmitting zones arrive at the focal point with path lengths differing by whole wavelengths, producing constructive interference. The radius of the nth zone grows roughly with the square root of n times the wavelength times the focal length. Because the focal length depends on wavelength, a zone plate acts as a strongly chromatic focusing element.

Applications

Zone plates are especially important in X-ray optics, where conventional refractive lenses absorb strongly and mirrors must be used at grazing incidence. They are used in X-ray microscopes, telescopes, and in soft X-ray beamlines at synchrotrons. Binary zone plates block alternate zones entirely, while phase zone plates shift the phase of alternate zones to improve efficiency.

History

Fresnel described the underlying zone construction in his work on diffraction in the early nineteenth century. The first practical zone plates for light were made in the 1870s, and Lord Rayleigh and R. W. Wood later developed photographic methods of producing them.
