Melanoma is the most dangerous type of skin cancer; it develops from the pigment-producing cells known as melanocytes. Melanomas typically occur in the skin, but may also occur in the eye (uveal melanoma), the mouth, intestines or other mucosal surfaces. Melanoma is caused mainly by ultraviolet light exposure in people with low levels of skin pigment.

Signs and symptoms

Early signs are changes in an existing mole or a new pigmented or unusual-looking growth. The "ABCDE" guide summarises warning features: Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Colour variation, Diameter greater than about 6 mm, and Evolving (changing) appearance. Types include superficial spreading, nodular, lentigo maligna and acral lentiginous melanoma.

Causes and diagnosis

Ultraviolet radiation causes DNA damage in melanocytes; mutations in genes such as BRAF (present in roughly half of melanomas), NRAS and CDKN2A drive growth. Diagnosis is by skin examination, dermoscopy and excisional biopsy, with prognosis strongly related to the Breslow thickness of the tumour and whether it has spread (metastasised) to lymph nodes or distant organs.

Treatment and prognosis

Early melanoma is usually cured by wide surgical excision. Since the 2010s, treatment of advanced melanoma has been transformed by immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as pembrolizumab and ipilimumab) and by targeted drugs against BRAF and MEK in BRAF-mutant tumours. Melanoma is relatively uncommon compared with other skin cancers but causes a majority of skin-cancer deaths; incidence is highest in Australia and New Zealand.
