Peanut butter and jelly sandwich


A peanut butter and jelly sandwich

The peanut butter and jelly sandwich, or PB&J, is a sandwich popular in North America, especially the United States, made with one or more layers of peanut butter and fruit preserves ("jelly") on bread, usually sliced white sandwich bread. It is a staple of children's lunchboxes and school cafeterias.

History

The ingredients only came together in the early 20th century: peanut butter had been promoted in the 1890s by John Harvey Kellogg and others as a health food; Welch's introduced commercial grape jelly (Grapelade) in 1917; and pre-sliced bread arrived in the late 1920s, making sandwich assembly easy for children. The first known printed reference to the combination appeared in 1901 in the Boston Cooking-School Magazine. During the Second World War, both peanut butter and jelly appeared on U.S. military ration lists, and returning soldiers spread the sandwich nationwide. Its popularity grew with postwar prosperity and the marketing of brands such as Jif, Skippy, and Smucker's — which later sold a crustless, sealed version, the Uncrustable.

Preparation and variations

Typically peanut butter is spread on one slice of bread and jelly or jam on the other, then the slices are pressed together; spreading peanut butter on both slices prevents the jelly from soaking through. Variations include the peanut butter and banana sandwich, the "fluffernutter" (with marshmallow creme, popular in New England), and versions with honey. The sandwich has also been deep-fried or grilled in some regional treatments.

Culture

The PB&J is a cultural shorthand for simple, cheap, comforting American food, though peanut allergies have led many schools to restrict or ban it.
