RNA splicing

RNA splicing
Post-transcriptional modification
Introns removed and exons joined in precursor mRNA

RNA splicing is a form of RNA processing in which a newly made precursor messenger RNA (pre-mRNA) transcript is transformed into a mature messenger RNA (mRNA). It works by removing all the introns (non-coding regions of RNA) and splicing back together exons (coding regions). For nuclear-encoded genes, splicing occurs in the nucleus either during or immediately after transcription, performed by the RNA polymerase-associated machinery known as the spliceosome.

Pathways

Splicing occurs by several mechanisms. The dominant pathway in eukaryotes is spliceosomal splicing, in which a large molecular machine of small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs) recognizes the 5′ splice site, branch point and 3′ splice site, then catalyzes two transesterification reactions. Self-splicing introns (group I and group II) fold into ribozymes that catalyze their own removal.

Alternative splicing

In alternative splicing, different combinations of exons from a single gene are joined, producing multiple distinct mRNA and protein isoforms. Alternative splicing occurs in most multi-exonic genes in higher eukaryotes and greatly expands proteomic diversity; it is regulated by splicing enhancer and silencer sequences and their binding proteins.

Errors and disease

Mutations in splice sites or splicing factors cause numerous human diseases, including spinal muscular atrophy, many cancers and various inherited disorders.
