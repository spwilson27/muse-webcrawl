Environmental determinism

Field: Geography

Related: Possibilism, environmental possibilism, geographic determinism

Environmental determinism (also known as climatic determinism or geographical determinism) is the study of how the physical environment predisposes societies and states towards particular economic or social developmental (or even more generally, cultural) trajectories. It stresses the presumption that physical and geographic forces shape human cultures and histories.

History

The idea has ancient roots: Hippocrates' Airs, Waters, Places and Aristotle's Politics linked climate to the character of peoples, and Montesquieu's The Spirit of the Laws (1748) gave the doctrine influential modern form. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, geographers such as Friedrich Ratzell, Ellen Churchill Semple, and Ellsworth Huntington developed systematic environmental determinism, arguing that climate and terrain determined civilization's vigor and distribution.

Criticism and decline

Critics objected that such theories were simplistic, often served to justify colonialism and racial hierarchies, and ignored human agency. French geographers led by Paul Vidal de la Blache advanced possibilism, in which the environment offers possibilities that cultures select among. By the mid-twentieth century determinism had fallen into disrepute within geography.

Neo-environmental determinism

Recent decades have seen renewed interest in the role of geography and environment in development, in works such as Jared Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel (1997) and economic studies relating climate, disease burden, and crop geography to prosperity — positions their authors distinguish from older doctrines while critics question the distinction.
