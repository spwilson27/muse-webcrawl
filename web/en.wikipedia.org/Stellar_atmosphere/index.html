Stellar atmosphere

The stellar atmosphere is the outer region of the volume of a star, lying above the stellar core, radiation zone, and convection zone. It is divided into several regions of distinct character: the photosphere, the lowest and coolest layer from which the star's visible light is emitted; the chromosphere above it; a narrow transition region; and the hot, tenuous corona, the outermost part.

Structure (the Sun)

For the Sun, the photosphere is about 400 km thick at a temperature near 5,800 K; the chromosphere extends roughly 2,000 km and reaches tens of thousands of kelvin; the corona extends millions of kilometres into space with temperatures of one to three million kelvin, heated by mechanisms still under study. Nearly all information about a star's composition, temperature, and surface gravity comes from analysing the spectrum of light formed in its atmosphere.

Modelling

Stellar atmosphere models compute how radiation, convection, and (in cooler stars) molecules and dust transport energy and shape the emergent spectrum, often assuming local thermodynamic equilibrium and hydrostatic equilibrium. Spectral lines are broadened and strengthened in the atmosphere, so abundance analyses and classifications (such as the Harvard spectral classification) are fundamentally studies of stellar atmospheres. Active phenomena — starspots, flares, prominences, and stellar winds — also originate in atmospheric layers.
