The Juris Doctor (Latin for "Doctor of Law"), abbreviated J.D., is a graduate-entry professional degree in law. In the United States it is the standard degree required to practise law: graduates of law schools accredited by the American Bar Association may sit for the bar examination in any state. The J.D. replaced the Bachelor of Laws (LL.B.) as the primary American law degree during the twentieth century.

Programme structure

The J.D. normally requires three years of full-time study after a bachelor's degree. The first year (1L) covers foundational subjects such as contracts, torts, civil procedure, criminal law, property and constitutional law, usually taught by the case method and Socratic questioning. Upper years are largely elective, with opportunities for clinics, journals and moot court.

History

The degree was first proposed by the University of Chicago Law School in 1902, reflecting the view that law is a graduate discipline. By the 1960s and 1970s the J.D. had become nearly universal in the United States. Although styled a doctorate, the J.D. is classified in U.S. federal statistics as a "doctor's degree — professional practice"; the research doctorate in law is the S.J.D. (Doctor of Juridical Science), and the LL.M. is a postgraduate master's degree.

Outside the United States

J.D. programmes also exist in Canada, Australia, Japan and several other countries, where they function as graduate-entry qualifying law degrees, analogous in level to other professional master's or bachelor's law qualifications.
