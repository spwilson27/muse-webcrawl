Shrinkage estimator

In statistics, a shrinkage estimator is an estimator that, either explicitly or implicitly, incorporates the effects of shrinkage. In loose terms this means that a naive or crude raw estimate is improved by combining it with other information. The resulting estimate, although biased, often has lower overall error — typically measured by mean squared error — than the unbiased estimator it replaces.

Background

The idea became prominent through the work of Charles Stein in the 1950s. Stein's paradox showed that when simultaneously estimating three or more unrelated means, the ordinary sample means are inadmissible: there exist combined estimators that perform better on average no matter what the true values are. The James–Stein estimator is the best-known example.

Methods

Common shrinkage techniques include ridge regression, the lasso, empirical Bayes methods, and regularized estimation more generally. In each case, estimates are pulled ("shrunk") toward a central value such as zero or a grand mean by an amount depending on the noise in the data.

Applications

Shrinkage estimators are widely used in genomics, small-area estimation, portfolio selection, and signal processing, where many related quantities must be estimated simultaneously from limited data.
