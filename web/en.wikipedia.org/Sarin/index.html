Sarin

Sarin (military designation GB) is an extremely toxic synthetic organophosphorus nerve agent. It is a colourless, odourless liquid that evaporates readily, and it attacks the nervous system by irreversibly inhibiting the enzyme acetylcholinesterase, causing acetylcholine to accumulate at nerve junctions. Even tiny doses can be lethal through respiratory failure.

History

Sarin was discovered in 1938 by German scientists at IG Farben — including Gerhard Schrader — while they were developing new pesticides; its name is an acronym of the discoverers' surnames. Nazi Germany produced it during the Second World War but did not use it in battle. After the war the United States and Soviet Union stockpiled sarin and related nerve agents such as tabun, soman, and VX.

Use and prohibition

Sarin was used by Iraq in the Iran–Iraq War, by the Japanese cult Aum Shinrikyo in the 1994 Matsumoto incident and the 1995 Tokyo subway attack, and by Syrian government forces in attacks during the Syrian civil war, including Ghouta in 2013 and Khan Shaykhun in 2017. The 1993 Chemical Weapons Convention prohibits its production and stockpiling, and declared stockpiles have been destroyed under international verification.

Effects and treatment

Symptoms of exposure include pinpoint pupils, salivation, sweating, vomiting, convulsions, and paralysis. Antidotes include atropine and pralidoxime, administered as quickly as possible, together with protective equipment and decontamination.
