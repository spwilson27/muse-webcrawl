Critique of political economy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The critique of political economy is an approach to the study of economic life that questions the categories, assumptions, and social foundations of economics itself rather than merely analyzing markets within them. The phrase is most closely associated with Karl Marx, whose magnum opus Capital carries the subtitle A Critique of Political Economy, and who published A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy in 1859.

Meaning of "critique"

In this tradition, "critique" does not mean simple rejection. Following an immanent method, the critic takes the categories of classical political economy — commodity, value, money, capital, wage labour — and shows how they arise from historically specific social relations, contain internal contradictions, and tend to present those relations as natural and eternal. Marx's target was the work of Adam Smith, David Ricardo, and their successors, whose insights he both built upon and systematized as historically limited.

Themes

Central themes include the analysis of the commodity form and commodity fetishism, the theory of surplus value, the reproduction of capitalist social relations, and the tendency of economic categories to obscure exploitation. Later writers extended the critique to finance, the state, social reproduction, ecology, and globalization, and the term now also denotes a broader interdisciplinary field overlapping with economic sociology, heterodox economics, and critical theory.
