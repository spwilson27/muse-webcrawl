The Cossacks are a predominantly East Slavic, Eastern Orthodox Christian people originating in the Pontic–Caspian steppe of eastern Ukraine and southern Russia. Historically, they were a freebooting, semi-militarized people, famed as horsemen, who established self-governing communities — called hosts (voiska) — in return for military service to the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Tsardom of Russia, and later the Russian Empire. The name derives from a Turkic word meaning "free man" or "adventurer".

History

Cossack communities coalesced in the 15th and 16th centuries among runaway serfs, adventurers, and frontiersmen living beyond the reach of the states, hunting, fishing, and raiding along the Dnieper (the Zaporozhian Cossacks below the Dnieper rapids) and the Don. The Zaporozhian leader Bohdan Khmelnytsky's uprising of 1648 against Poland created the Cossack Hetmanate; subsequent treaties brought it under Russian suzerainty. Cossack revolts — led by Stenka Razin (1670–71), Kondraty Bulavin (1707–08), and Yemelyan Pugachev (1773–75) — shook the empire, which progressively restricted Cossack autonomy while relying on Cossack regiments as light cavalry and border guards in the conquest of Siberia, the Caucasus, and Central Asia.

20th century and revival

During the Russian Civil War, Cossacks fought on both sides; the Bolsheviks responded with "decossackization", collectivization, and famine that devastated Cossack lands. Cossack units were restored in the Red Army in 1936 and fought in the Second World War, while some Cossacks collaborated with Nazi Germany. Since 1991, registered Cossack organizations have been revived in Russia and Ukraine, variously as cultural societies, state-affiliated patrol and military formations, and symbols of Ukrainian national identity.
