Residue theorem

The residue theorem, also known as Cauchy's residue theorem, is a powerful tool to evaluate line integrals of analytic functions over closed curves in the complex plane; it can in particular be used to compute definite integrals of real-valued functions, including improper integrals that are hard or impossible to evaluate by elementary means.

Statement

Let U be a simply connected open subset of the complex plane containing a positively oriented, piecewise smooth, simple closed contour C and finitely many points a1, …, an inside C. If f is holomorphic on U except at those points, then:

∮C f(z) dz = 2πi · Σk=1..n Res(f, ak)

where Res(f, ak) denotes the residue of f at ak — the coefficient of (z − ak)−1 in the Laurent series expansion of f around ak.

Computing residues

For a simple pole at a, the residue is the limit of (z − a)f(z) as z → a. For a pole of order m, the residue is obtained from the (m − 1)-th derivative of (z − a)mf(z), divided by (m − 1)!. For a quotient f = p/q with q having a simple zero at a, Res(f, a) = p(a)/q′(a).

Applications

By choosing suitable contours — semicircles closed in the upper or lower half-plane, keyhole contours, indented contours — the residue theorem evaluates many real integrals: rational functions over the whole line, Fourier-type integrals (using Jordan's lemma), integrals involving logarithms and fractional powers, and infinite series summed via residues at the poles of π cot πz. The concept of the residue at infinity extends the theorem so that the sum of all residues including infinity equals zero.

History

Cauchy announced the theorem in 1825 in connection with his integral theorem; the general statement with Laurent series followed Laurent's work in 1843. It remains a centrepiece of every course in complex analysis.
