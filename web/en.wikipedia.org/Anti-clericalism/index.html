Anti-clericalism

Anti-clericalism is opposition to the influence and power of religious institutions and clergy — especially the Roman Catholic Church — in political and public life. It is distinct from atheism and from anti-religious sentiment generally: historically many anti-clericals were themselves believers who opposed the clergy's wealth, privileges, political interference, or control of education, marriage, and censorship.

History

Anti-clerical ideas surfaced in the Reformation and the Enlightenment, whose philosophes attacked clerical privilege, and exploded in the French Revolution, which confiscated church lands and briefly attempted dechristianisation. In the nineteenth century, liberal states across Catholic Europe and Latin America fought "culture wars" with the Church: Bismarck's Kulturkampf in Germany, the suppression of religious orders and secular education laws in France (culminating in the 1905 law on the separation of churches and the state), and the Italian Risorgimento's annexation of the Papal States.

Twentieth century

The most violent anti-clerical episodes occurred in Mexico, where the 1917 constitution's restrictions provoked the Cristero War (1926–29) against the Calles government's enforcement; in Spain, where anti-clerical violence during the Civil War killed thousands of clergy and religious; and in communist states, where ruling parties combined anti-clericalism with state atheism and persecution of all religions. Anti-clerical rhetoric and humour have also drawn on clerical scandals, from medieval satires to modern responses to abuse crises.
