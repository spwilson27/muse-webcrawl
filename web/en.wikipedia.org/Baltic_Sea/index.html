The Baltic Sea is an arm of the Atlantic Ocean in Northern Europe, enclosed by Sweden and Finland to the north, the Baltic states and Russia to the east, Poland and Germany to the south, and Denmark to the west. It is connected to the North Sea through the narrow Danish straits and the Kattegat. One of the largest brackish inland seas in the world, the Baltic receives great volumes of fresh water from the rivers of its catchment, which covers an area about four times the size of the sea itself and is home to some 85 million people.

Geography

The sea extends from about 53°N to 66°N latitude. Its principal subdivisions include the Gulf of Bothnia between Sweden and Finland, the Gulf of Finland extending eastward toward Saint Petersburg, and the Gulf of Riga between Latvia and Estonia. Major islands include Gotland, Öland, Åland, Saaremaa, and Hiiumaa. Because the Baltic is shallow and its connection to the ocean narrow, its water renews only slowly, over a period of roughly thirty years.

Hydrology and environment

Surface salinity is low — far below that of the open ocean — and decreases from the Danish straits eastward and northward; the innermost gulfs are nearly fresh. The sea freezes in winter, with ice cover historically reaching much of the surface in severe years. Eutrophication from agricultural and municipal nutrient runoff has produced widespread oxygen-depleted "dead zones" on the sea floor, and the Baltic is a major focus of international environmental cooperation through the Helsinki Commission (HELCOM).

History and economy

In the early Middle Ages the Baltic was traversed by Viking trade routes; from the thirteenth century the Hanseatic League dominated its commerce from ports such as Lübeck, Gdańsk, Riga, and Tallinn. Control of the sea was contested for centuries by Denmark, Sweden, Russia, and Germany. Today the Baltic is one of the world's busiest shipping regions, carrying large volumes of container traffic, ferries, and Russian oil exports, and it is ringed by major ports including Stockholm, Helsinki, Gdańsk, and Saint Petersburg.
