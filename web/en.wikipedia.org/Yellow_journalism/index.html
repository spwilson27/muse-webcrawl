Yellow journalism

Yellow journalism
Era1890s (United States)
Key figuresJoseph Pulitzer; William Randolph Hearst
Named afterThe Yellow Kid comic strip

Yellow journalism is a style of newspaper reporting that emphasises sensationalism, scandal, and eye-catching headlines over factual accuracy. The term emerged in the mid-1890s during the circulation war between Joseph Pulitzer's New York World and William Randolph Hearst's New York Journal.

Origin of the term

Both papers published Richard F. Outcault's comic strip Hogan's Alley, featuring the Yellow Kid, a character in a yellow nightshirt. Rival papers poached the strip, and editor Erwin Wardman of the New York Press coined "yellow journalism" for the competing papers' style: huge scare headlines, lavish illustrations, stunt reporting, crusades, and a heavy reliance on anonymous or dubious sources.

Spanish–American War

The yellow press is often credited with inflaming American public opinion against Spain over Cuba, particularly after the explosion of the USS Maine in Havana harbour in 1898, helping to push the United States into the Spanish–American War. Historians debate how decisive the papers' influence actually was, but the episode fixed the term in the language as a byword for irresponsible sensational reporting.
