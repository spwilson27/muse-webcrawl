Dry dock

A dry dock is a narrow basin or vessel that can be flooded to allow a ship to float in, then drained so that the ship rests on supports out of the water, enabling work to be carried out on parts of the hull normally below the waterline. Dry docks are used for ship construction, maintenance and repair, especially for cleaning, inspection, repainting and work on propellers and rudders.

Types

A graving dock is a permanent basin excavated in a shipyard, closed by a gate or caisson and pumped dry. A floating dry dock is a submersible pontoon structure that sinks to receive a vessel and then raises it by pumping out its ballast tanks. Smaller craft may instead be lifted on a slipway, marine railway or boat lift.

Operation

Before docking, keel blocks and side shores are arranged to match the ship's hull form. The vessel is manoeuvred in, the dock is dewatered, and the hull settles onto the blocks as the water level falls. Classification rules generally require ships to be dry-docked for hull surveys at intervals of a few years.

History

Simple dry docks existed in the ancient world; a notable early example was built at Portsmouth, England, in 1495 under Henry VII. Modern graving docks serve the largest vessels afloat, including aircraft carriers and ultra-large container ships.
