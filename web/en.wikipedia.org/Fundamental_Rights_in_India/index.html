Fundamental Rights in India

The Fundamental Rights in India are the basic human rights enshrined in Part III of the Constitution of India, which guarantees civil liberties such that all Indians can lead their lives in peace and harmony as citizens of India. They are applicable to every citizen of India and, in several cases, to all persons within the territory of India.

Part III of the Constitution, described by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar as its "heart and soul", originally contained seven groups of rights; the right to property was removed from the list by the 44th Amendment in 1978 and made a constitutional legal right under Article 300A. The six remaining groups are:

The six Fundamental Rights

Right to Equality (Articles 14–18): equality before the law, prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth, equality of opportunity in public employment, and abolition of untouchability and of titles.

Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22): protection of six freedoms — speech and expression, assembly, association, movement, residence, and profession — together with protection in respect of conviction for offences, protection of life and personal liberty (Article 21), and protection against arrest and detention in certain cases.

Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24): prohibition of human trafficking and forced labour, and of child labour in hazardous work.

Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28): freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality and health.

Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30): protection of the interests of minorities, including their right to establish and administer educational institutions.

Right to Constitutional Remedies (Articles 32–35): the right to move the Supreme Court directly for enforcement of Fundamental Rights; the Court may issue writs including habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari.

Enforcement and significance

Fundamental Rights are justiciable: laws that contravene them can be struck down by the courts. Landmark cases such as Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) established that while Parliament may amend the Constitution, it cannot alter its "basic structure"; Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) greatly expanded the interpretation of Article 21; and Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) recognised privacy as a Fundamental Right. During a national emergency, enforcement of most Fundamental Rights may be suspended, though Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended even then.
