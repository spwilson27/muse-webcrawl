Hidden curriculum

The hidden curriculum is a concept in the sociology of education describing the unwritten, unofficial, and often unintended lessons, values, and perspectives that students learn in school. While the formal curriculum consists of the courses and content students are explicitly taught, the hidden curriculum conveys social norms, cultural values, and expectations that shape behaviour and belief.

Origins of the term

The phrase is commonly attributed to the sociologist Philip W. Jackson, whose 1968 book Life in Classrooms examined how the crowds, praise, and power structures of school life taught children lessons distinct from academic content. Earlier educational theorists such as John Dewey had explored related ideas about the collateral learning that accompanies schooling.

Content of the hidden curriculum

Through daily routines, rules, reward systems and teacher expectations, schools may implicitly teach punctuality, obedience to authority, competition, gender roles, and acceptance of social hierarchies. Critics such as Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis argued, in their "correspondence principle", that these lessons prepare working-class students for subordinate roles in the capitalist economy, while defenders note that socialisation into shared norms can also support cooperation and civic life.

Criticism and related concepts

Related ideas include the "null curriculum" — what schools teach by leaving topics out — and the distinction between formal, informal and hidden curricula. Scholars continue to debate how visible and intentional hidden-curriculum effects are, and how educators might make them explicit.
