Salt evaporation pond

A salt evaporation pond (also called a saltern, salt pan or salt works) is a shallow artificial pond designed to extract salts from seawater, salty lake water or mineral-rich spring water. The water is directed into a series of connected ponds in which evaporation, driven by sun and wind, progressively concentrates the brine until the desired salts crystallise and can be harvested.

Process

Seawater contains roughly 35 grams of dissolved salts per litre, about 85 percent of which is sodium chloride. In a typical saltern, water is admitted through sluice gates into large, shallow evaporation ponds, where it may spend months slowly concentrating. As the brine densifies it is moved through a sequence of ponds; because different salts precipitate at different concentrations, operators can separate gypsum, halite (common salt) and finally the bitter magnesium and potassium salts known as bitterns.

The colours of working salt ponds are famous: as salinity rises, halophilic algae such as Dunaliella salina and colonies of brine shrimp tint the water shades of orange, pink and red, creating striking aerial patterns, as seen in the ponds of San Francisco Bay, the Camargue, and Western Australia.

History

Solar salt production is ancient: coastal salterns operated in China, the Mediterranean world and pre-Columbian America for millennia, and salt was a strategically vital commodity for food preservation. Traditional salterns survive in places such as Guérande in France, where fleur de sel is still hand-raked, and Trapani in Sicily. Today most of the world's salt still comes from a combination of solar evaporation, rock salt mining and vacuum evaporation.

Ecology

Although artificial, evaporation ponds often become important wetland habitats. Their range of salinities supports brine shrimp, algae and the migratory shorebirds that feed on them, and many former commercial ponds — such as those around San Francisco Bay — are being restored to tidal marsh while others are retained as managed bird habitat.
