Trial by combat
Also calledJudicial duel; wager of battle
OriginsEarly medieval Germanic law

Trial by combat (also known as the judicial duel or wager of battle) was a method of Germanic law used in medieval Europe to settle accusations — most often disputes over land or serious criminal charges — in the absence of witnesses or confession. Two parties, or their champions, fought in single combat under judicial supervision; the victor was held to be right, since it was believed that God (the judicium Dei) would grant victory to the party whose cause was just.

Origins and practice

The practice was codified in early medieval law codes, including the Burgundian code of King Gundobad (c. 501), who promoted it partly as an alternative to oath-swearing, which invited perjury. It spread across Europe with Germanic custom and was brought to England in developed form after the Norman Conquest, where "wager of battle" became a standard procedure in disputes over land (writs of right) and in appeals of felony. Parties could fight personally or appoint champions; women, clergy, the elderly and the infirm were entitled or required to use champions, producing a class of professional hired fighters. Ecclesiastical disapproval grew steadily — the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) forbade clerical participation in ordeals, undermining trial by ordeal generally — and judicial duels were increasingly restricted to cases where other proof was impossible.

Regional variants

Forms varied widely. In the Holy Roman Empire, German fechtbücher (fighting manuals) such as Hans Talhoffer's describe elaborate rules for judicial duels, including unusual combats — famously, a form in which a man stood waist-deep in a pit with one arm tied, fighting a woman armed with a stone in a cloth. In France, the last judicial duel authorised by the Parlement of Paris was fought in 1547 between Jarnac and La Châtaigneraie before Henri II, an event whose unexpected outcome (Jarnac's hamstringing cut, the coup de Jarnac) scandalised the court. Scandinavian, Polish and Hungarian law also recognised judicial combat, and the practice survived longest in military and chivalric settings as the duel of honour, which is legally distinct.

Decline and survival in English law

Trial by combat fell out of use in England after the sixteenth century but was never formally abolished, until it was unexpectedly claimed in 1818 in Ashford v Thornton: Abraham Thornton, acquitted of the murder of Mary Ashford, was charged again by her brother on a private appeal and demanded wager of battle, throwing down his glove in court. The court held that the right still existed in law; the appellant withdrew, Thornton went free, and Parliament abolished appeals of felony and trial by battle the following year (1819). The last known judicial combat in France took place in 1583, while the last in the Holy Roman Empire is generally dated to the early seventeenth century, though ceremonial and unsanctioned forms lingered longer.
