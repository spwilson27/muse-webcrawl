Extended mind thesis

The extended mind thesis, in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, holds that the mind does not reside exclusively in the brain or the body, but can extend into the physical world: objects and processes outside the skull may literally constitute part of a cognitive process. The thesis was given its canonical formulation in the 1998 paper "The Extended Mind" by Andy Clark and David Chalmers.

The parity principle and the case of Otto

Clark and Chalmers propose a parity principle: if a part of the external world functions in a way that, were it in the head, we would unhesitatingly count it as part of a cognitive process, then it is part of that process. Their famous example contrasts Inga, who recalls from biological memory that the Museum of Modern Art is on 53rd Street, with Otto, who has Alzheimer's disease and consults a notebook he carries everywhere. The notebook, they argue, plays the same functional role for Otto as memory does for Inga — so Otto's belief about the museum's location is partly constituted by the notebook's contents.

Criticism and development

Critics such as Fred Adams, Ken Aizawa, and Robert Rupert objected that the thesis confuses the coupling of a cognitive system to external tools with the constitution of cognition, and warned of "cognitive bloat," in which the mind expands without limit. Clark replied with criteria of trust and glue — constant access, easy availability, automatic endorsement, and prior conscious endorsement. The thesis has influenced work on embodied and embedded cognition, distributed cognition, and the philosophy of technology.
