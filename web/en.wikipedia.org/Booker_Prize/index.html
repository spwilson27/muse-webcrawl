Booker Prize

The Booker Prize, formerly the Booker–McConnell Prize (1969–2001) and the Man Booker Prize (2002–2019), is a prestigious literary award conferred each year for the best novel written in the English language and published in the United Kingdom or Ireland. Winning the Booker can transform an author's career and sales; the prize money is £50,000, with each shortlisted author receiving £2,500.

History

The prize was established in 1969 by the food company Booker plc, modelled partly on France's Prix Goncourt. P. H. Newby won the first award for Something to Answer For. From 2002 to 2019 it was sponsored by the Man Group and known as the Man Booker Prize; since 2019 the sponsor has been the Crankstart foundation and the name reverted to the Booker Prize. Since 2005 a separate International Booker Prize has honoured fiction in translation (initially biennial, then annual from 2016), with the prize split between author and translator.

Rules and judging

Originally restricted to citizens of the Commonwealth and Ireland, eligibility was extended in 2014 to any novel in English published in the UK or Ireland, a change that provoked debate about American dominance. A panel of judges — writers, critics, academics and public figures, changing each year — selects a longlist (the "Booker dozen", usually 12 or 13 titles), then a shortlist of six, then the winner, announced in the autumn. Publishers may submit a limited number of titles, with extra slots for previously shortlisted authors.

Notable winners

Four authors have won twice: J. M. Coetzee, Peter Carey, Hilary Mantel (for Wolf Hall and Bring Up the Bodies) and Margaret Atwood, who shared the 2019 prize controversially with Bernardine Evaristo. Salman Rushdie's Midnight's Children (1981) won both the Booker and the special "Booker of Bookers" awards in 1993 and 2008. Other celebrated winners include The Remains of the Day by Kazuo Ishiguro, Life of Pi by Yann Martel, and Lincoln in the Bardo by George Saunders.
