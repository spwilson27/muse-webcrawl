The Swedish Empire (Swedish: Stormaktstiden, "the Era of Great Power") was the period in Swedish history from about 1611 to 1721 during which Sweden was one of the great powers of Europe, controlling most of the shores of the Baltic Sea and territories in northern Germany. Its rise began under Gustavus Adolphus and ended with the death of Charles XII and the Treaty of Nystad that closed the Great Northern War.

Rise

Sweden emerged from the Kalmar Union as a centralised state under Gustav Vasa in the sixteenth century. Under Gustavus Adolphus (reigned 1611–1632), Sweden intervened in the Thirty Years' War, won the Battle of Breitenfeld in 1631, and became the leading Protestant military power in Europe, supported by Chancellor Axel Oxenstierna's administrative reforms. Treaties of Brömsebro (1645) and Roskilde (1658, under Charles X Gustav) added Danish provinces including Scania, and the Peace of Westphalia (1648) gave Sweden possessions in northern Germany such as Western Pomerania and Bremen-Verden.

Territory and government

At its greatest extent the empire included present-day Sweden and Finland, Estonia, Latvia (Swedish Livonia), parts of modern Russia (Ingria and Karelia), coastal northern Germany, and briefly overseas colonies including New Sweden on the Delaware River (1638–1655) and a fort on the Gold Coast of Africa. The empire fought repeated wars with Denmark-Norway, Poland-Lithuania, and Russia to defend a Baltic that functioned almost as a Swedish lake.

Decline

Charles XI's reduktion recovered crown lands and funded a standing army through the allotment system. His son Charles XII won brilliant early victories in the Great Northern War (1700–1721), notably at Narva (1700), but the invasion of Russia ended in catastrophic defeat at Poltava in 1709. Charles XII was killed at Fredriksten in 1718, and by the Treaty of Nystad (1721) Sweden ceded its Baltic provinces to Russia, ending the age of greatness and leaving Russia as the dominant Baltic power.
