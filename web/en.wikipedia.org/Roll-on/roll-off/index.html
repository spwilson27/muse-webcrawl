Roll-on/roll-off

Roll-on/roll-off (RORO or ro-ro) ships are cargo ships designed to carry wheeled cargo, such as cars, motorcycles, trucks, semi-trailer trucks, buses, trailers, and railroad cars, that are driven on and off the ship on their own wheels or using a platform vehicle, such as a self-propelled modular transporter. This is in contrast to lift-on/lift-off (LoLo) vessels, which use a crane to load and unload cargo.

Design

RORO vessels have either built-in or shore-based ramps or ferry slips that allow cargo to be efficiently rolled on and off when in port. Large vehicle carriers have multiple internal decks connected by ramps and lifts, with capacities measured in car-equivalent units (CEU). Pure car carriers and pure car and truck carriers dominate the international trade in new automobiles, moving millions of vehicles per year between manufacturing countries and markets.

Types

Types of RORO ships include ferries, cruiseferries, cargo ships, and barges. Passenger ferries with car decks are a familiar form of RORO vessel on short sea routes. RoPax ferries combine large vehicle capacity with passenger accommodation. Military sealift ships also use RORO designs to transport vehicles and equipment.

Safety

The large, open vehicle decks and high freeboard of RORO ships have raised safety concerns after several high-profile losses, including the Herald of Free Enterprise (1987) and the Estonia (1994). Regulations were tightened regarding bow door design, subdivision, and stability following these disasters.

History

Railway train ferries of the 19th century were early RORO vessels. The modern RORO cargo ship developed in the 1950s and 1960s alongside the growth of the international automobile trade and roll-on/roll-off ferry services across the English Channel and other short sea crossings.
