Roll-on/roll-off

Roll-on/roll-off (RORO or ro-ro) ships are cargo ships designed to carry wheeled cargo, such as cars, motorcycles, trucks, semi-trailer trucks, buses, trailers, and railroad cars, that are driven on and off the ship on their own wheels or using a platform vehicle, such as a self-propelled modular transporter. This is in contrast to lift-on/lift-off (LoLo) vessels, which use cranes to load and unload cargo.

Design

RORO vessels have built-in or shore-based ramps, usually at the stern and sometimes the bow or side, that allow cargo to be efficiently rolled on and off when in port. Large car carriers have multiple vehicle decks, internal ramps and lifts, and a distinctive tall, boxy superstructure. Pure car carriers (PCC) and pure car and truck carriers (PCTC) can carry thousands of vehicles; capacity is measured in car equivalent units (CEU). ConRo vessels combine RORO decks with container capacity on top, and RoPax ships combine freight vehicles with passenger accommodation, as on many ferries.

History

The principle dates to 19th-century train ferries, and early car ferries appeared in the early 20th century. Purpose-built ocean-going RORO ships expanded after the Second World War — the US military's use of landing ships demonstrated the concept — and the trade grew with the export booms of the Japanese and later Korean and Chinese car industries. Specialised operators such as Wallenius Wilhelmsen and Höegh Autoliners run global fleets.

Safety

Free vehicle decks and large external doors make RORO ships sensitive to flooding and stability problems when damaged; losses such as the Herald of Free Enterprise (1987) and Estonia (1994) led to stricter design and operating rules. Fire risk from vehicle cargoes, including electric vehicles, is a continuing concern.
