Container ship

A container ship (also called a boxship or spelled containership) is a cargo ship that carries all of its load in truck-sized intermodal containers, in a technique called containerization. Container ships are a common means of commercial intermodal freight transport and now carry most seagoing non-bulk cargo. Capacity is measured in twenty-foot equivalent units (TEU).

Design

Modern container ships are built around cellular holds fitted with vertical guide rails, so that standard 20-foot and 40-foot containers stack precisely below deck; additional containers are lashed on deck in stacks that can reach ten or more high. The engine room and accommodation block are typically aft. Gearless ships rely entirely on shore cranes, while smaller geared feeder vessels carry their own cranes and can serve ports without container terminals.

History

Container shipping began in 1956 when Malcolm McLean loaded 58 truck trailers onto the converted tanker Ideal X. Standardisation of container sizes and corner fittings in the 1960s allowed the system to spread worldwide, transforming ports, slashing handling costs, and enabling global supply chains. Ships grew steadily: Panamax designs filled the old Panama Canal locks, and later classes — post-Panamax, New Panamax, and ultra-large container vessels (ULCVs) — exceeded 20,000 TEU. The Ever Given, at about 20,000 TEU and 400 metres long, became famous when it blocked the Suez Canal in March 2021.

Categories and operations

Vessels range from small feeders under 1,000 TEU serving regional ports to ULCVs above 14,500 TEU on Asia–Europe trunk routes. Operations depend on giant ship-to-shore gantry cranes and computerised stowage planning; even so, hundreds of containers are estimated to be lost overboard each year. Container ships are also significant sources of emissions, and the industry is experimenting with alternative fuels such as methanol and LNG.
