Motoo Kimura (1924–1994) was a Japanese theoretical biologist and population geneticist, best known for developing the neutral theory of molecular evolution. Working mainly at Japan's National Institute of Genetics in Mishima, he applied stochastic (diffusion-equation) methods to problems of allele frequency change, helping to found the mathematical theory of molecular evolution.

Neutral theory

In a landmark 1968 paper, "Evolutionary rate at the molecular level", Kimura argued that the great majority of evolutionary changes at the molecular level are caused not by natural selection but by random genetic drift of mutant alleles that are selectively neutral — that is, they do not affect fitness. A companion theory by King and Jukes ("non-Darwinian evolution") appeared in 1969. The neutral theory provoked the "neutralist–selectionist debate" and later became the standard null hypothesis of molecular evolution; Tomoko Ohta, Kimura's student, extended it as the nearly neutral theory.

Other contributions

Kimura's mathematical work included the diffusion theory of allele frequencies, the infinite-alleles and infinite-sites models, and estimates of the rates of amino-acid substitution that anticipated the molecular clock. His 1983 monograph The Neutral Theory of Molecular Evolution consolidated the field.

Honours

He was elected a foreign member of the Royal Society and of the US National Academy of Sciences, received the Darwin Medal of the Royal Society (1992), and was awarded Japan's Order of Culture.
