Photographic film

Photographic film is a strip or sheet of transparent plastic film base coated on one side with a light-sensitive emulsion containing microscopically small silver halide crystals. When exposed briefly to light through a camera lens, an invisible latent image forms; chemical development makes the image visible as a negative or positive.

History

Film photography grew from the work of pioneers such as Louis Daguerre and William Henry Fox Talbot. George Eastman's Kodak company introduced flexible roll film in the 1880s, and 35 mm film — originally a motion-picture format — became the standard still format in the 20th century. Colour processes such as Kodachrome (1935) and later chromogenic films made colour photography universal before digital sensors displaced film in the 2000s.

Types

Black-and-white negative film, colour negative (print) film, colour reversal (slide) film and instant film are the main families. Films are rated by sensitivity (ISO speed), grain, contrast and colour response. Infrared and technical films serve scientific uses.

Structure

A colour film typically has three emulsion layers sensitive to blue, green and red light, separated by filter layers, on an acetate or polyester base with an anti-halation backing.
