The Social War (from the Latin bellum sociale, "war of the allies"), also called the Italian War or the Marsic War, was fought from 91 to 87 BC between the Roman Republic and several of its autonomous allies (socii) in Italy. The conflict was triggered by Rome's refusal to grant Roman citizenship to its Italian allies, who supplied much of the manpower of Roman armies yet lacked political rights.

Causes

Attempts to enfranchise the Italians had repeatedly failed. Marcus Livius Drusus, tribune in 91 BC, proposed extending citizenship but was assassinated, and his laws were annulled. His death convinced many allies that reform from within Rome was impossible. The spark came at Asculum, where the population massacred the Roman residents after a Roman official threatened the town.

Course of the war

The rebels organised a rival state, Italia, with its capital at Corfinium—renamed Italica—and minted coins bearing imagery of the Italian bull goring the Roman wolf. Eight peoples formed the initial confederation, divided into a northern (Marsic) group under Quintus Poppaedius Silo and a southern (Samnite) group under Gaius Papius Mutilus. The fighting was exceptionally bloody; Rome suffered defeats, including the death of the consul Rutilius Lupus at the river Tolenus in 90 BC.

Rome responded politically as well as militarily. The lex Julia (90 BC) granted citizenship to allies who had remained loyal or laid down their arms, and the lex Plautia Papiria (89 BC) extended the offer further, undercutting the rebellion. Commanders such as Gaius Marius, Pompeius Strabo, and the young Lucius Cornelius Sulla won major victories; Sulla's campaigns in the south in 89 BC effectively broke Samnite resistance, though Samnite holdouts fought on and the last rebels were crushed only after the war merged into the Roman civil wars.

Aftermath

Rome won the war but conceded its central demand: citizenship was extended to virtually all free inhabitants of Italy south of the Po River (Cisalpine Gaul followed in 49 BC). The enfranchisement transformed Roman politics and administration, though new citizens were at first registered in a limited number of voting tribes, diluting their power—a grievance that fed directly into the civil wars of the 80s BC.
