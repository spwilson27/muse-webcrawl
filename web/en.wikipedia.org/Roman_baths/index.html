Imperial thermae combined bathing, exercise, libraries and gardens.

Roman baths were public bathing facilities found throughout the Roman world. In Latin, large imperial complexes were called thermae and smaller establishments balneae. Bathing was a central part of daily urban life in Rome and the provinces, combining hygiene, exercise, socialising and business in a single, usually inexpensive, institution.

The bathing routine

Bathers typically progressed through a sequence of rooms of increasing temperature. After undressing in the apodyterium (changing room) and exercising in the palaestra, they entered the warm tepidarium, then the hot, steamy caldarium, where they might be oiled and scraped clean with a curved metal strigil, and finally plunged into the cold water of the frigidarium. Large complexes added swimming pools (natatio), gardens, libraries, lecture halls and food stalls.

Engineering

The baths depended on sophisticated engineering: aqueducts supplied vast quantities of water, and the hypocaust system heated the rooms — hot air from furnaces circulated beneath raised floors supported by stacks of tiles (pilae) and through hollow tiles in the walls. Glazed windows, including in the great Baths of Caracalla in Rome (dedicated 216 AD), helped retain heat.

Social role

Admission cost a token fee, and men and women bathed either at different times or in separate facilities. The baths were among the most democratic institutions of Roman society, used by nearly all classes. Emperors built ever grander thermae as acts of public benefaction; by the 4th century AD Rome had over 800 smaller baths and eleven great thermae. The tradition continued in the Byzantine East and influenced the Islamic hammam and later European spas.
