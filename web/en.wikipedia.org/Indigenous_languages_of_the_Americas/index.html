Indigenous languages of the Americas

The Indigenous languages of the Americas are the languages that were spoken in the Western Hemisphere before the arrival of Christopher Columbus in 1492 and their modern descendants. They consist of more than a thousand languages belonging to dozens of distinct language families as well as many language isolates. Estimates suggest that at the time of European contact, over 1,000 languages were spoken in South America and several hundred in North America; today many are extinct or severely endangered.

Classification

Joseph Greenberg's controversial 1987 classification proposed just three families for all Native American languages — Eskimo–Aleut, Na-Dene, and a vast "Amerind" group — corresponding to three waves of migration. Most specialists reject the Amerind grouping and recognise many independent families, including Algic, Uto-Aztecan, Mayan, Quechuan, Aymaran, Arawakan, Tupian and Oto-Manguean. The most widely spoken indigenous languages today include Quechua (around 7 million speakers), Guarani (official in Paraguay), Aymara, Nahuatl and the Mayan languages.

Characteristics

Indigenous American languages display enormous structural diversity: polysynthetic languages such as Inuktitut build whole sentences into single words; many have evidentiality systems grammatically marking the source of information; and some, like the Mayan languages, historically developed writing systems — Maya glyphs being the only fully developed script in the pre-Columbian Americas, alongside other systems such as Zapotec and Mixtec pictography and the Andean khipu.

Endangerment and revitalisation

European colonisation caused catastrophic language loss through disease, displacement and assimilationist schooling. Most surviving languages have few speakers and are endangered, but revitalisation programmes — immersion schools, master-apprentice schemes and official recognition such as Bolivia's and Paraguay's multilingual constitutions — aim to reverse the decline.
