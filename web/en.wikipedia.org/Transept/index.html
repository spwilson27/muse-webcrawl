Transept

A transept is a transverse part of any building whose main portion is longitudinal, or, in the cruciform plan characteristic of many churches, the transverse arm of the cross. In church architecture, the transept runs perpendicular to the nave and aisles, separating them from the sanctuary and chancel, and the intersection of nave and transept is called the crossing.

Description

The two ends of the transept are sometimes called the north transept and south transept (in churches oriented with the altar to the east). Transepts often contain chapels, altars or tombs, and their end walls frequently feature large windows, such as rose windows, which illuminate the crossing. In some churches the transept has its own aisles; in others, particularly Cistercian churches, the transept projects only modestly or is absent.

History

The transept developed in early Christian and Romanesque architecture. In Old St Peter's Basilica in Rome a continuous transept gave the church a tau-cross (T-shaped) plan. Romanesque and Gothic cathedrals developed the Latin cross plan with a projecting transept; some large English cathedrals, including Salisbury, Lincoln and Canterbury, have two transepts. The transept also served liturgical functions, providing space for additional altars and for processions, and in monastic churches access between the cloister and the church.
