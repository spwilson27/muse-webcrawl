Alcohol intoxication

Alcohol intoxication, also known as alcohol poisoning or drunkenness, is the negative behaviour and physical effects caused by the consumption of ethanol (alcohol). Symptoms at lower doses include mild sedation, euphoria, and reduced inhibition; at higher doses they progress to slurred speech, impaired balance and judgement, nausea, and confusion, and at very high blood alcohol concentrations to vomiting, unconsciousness, respiratory depression, coma, and death.

Mechanism

Ethanol enhances the effect of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA and inhibits the excitatory transmitter glutamate, depressing central nervous system activity. It is absorbed from the stomach and small intestine and metabolised mainly in the liver by alcohol dehydrogenase and aldehyde dehydrogenase. Blood alcohol concentration (BAC) is the standard measure of intoxication, and legal driving limits in many jurisdictions are set at 0.05% or 0.08%.

Stages and risks

Intoxication is commonly described in stages from subclinical effects below 0.03% BAC through euphoria, excitement, and confusion to stupor and coma above roughly 0.30–0.40%. Acute risks include accidents, aspiration of vomit, hypothermia, and alcohol poisoning; chronic heavy use leads to dependence and organ damage. Treatment is mainly supportive — monitoring, airway protection, and treating low blood sugar — while the body clears the alcohol.
