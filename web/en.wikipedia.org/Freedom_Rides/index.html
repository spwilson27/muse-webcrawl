Freedom Rides

The Freedom Rides were a series of political protests against racial segregation in the United States in 1961, during the civil rights movement. Civil rights activists called Freedom Riders rode interstate buses into the segregated Southern United States to challenge the non-enforcement of U.S. Supreme Court decisions — Morgan v. Virginia (1946) and Boynton v. Virginia (1960) — which had ruled that segregated public buses and interstate terminal facilities were unconstitutional.

The 1961 rides

The first Freedom Ride, organized by the Congress of Racial Equality (CORE), left Washington, D.C., on May 4, 1961, with thirteen riders, Black and white, bound for New Orleans. After relatively mild resistance in the upper South, the riders met extreme violence in Alabama: on May 14 a mob firebombed one bus near Anniston and beat the riders, while another group was brutally attacked in Birmingham with police complicity. When CORE suspended the ride, students from Nashville — including Diane Nash and John Lewis — insisted on continuing, and riders who pressed on to Montgomery were beaten by another mob on May 20.

The violence drew national and international attention. The Kennedy administration, initially seeking to cool the crisis, eventually dispatched federal marshals and pressed the Interstate Commerce Commission, which issued regulations effective November 1, 1961, banning segregation in interstate bus terminals. By the end of the summer more than 400 Freedom Riders, many of them imprisoned at Mississippi's Parchman Farm, had joined rides across the South.

Legacy

The Freedom Rides demonstrated the power of nonviolent direct action to force federal enforcement of constitutional rights, strengthened the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), and set the pattern for later campaigns in Birmingham and Selma.
