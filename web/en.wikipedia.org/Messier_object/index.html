The Messier objects are a set of 110 astronomical objects catalogued by the French astronomer Charles Messier in his Catalogue des Nébuleuses et des Amas d'Étoiles (Catalogue of Nebulae and Star Clusters), published in stages between 1774 and 1781. Because Messier was a comet hunter, he compiled the list of fuzzy, comet-like objects that were not comets, so as to avoid wasting time on them. Ironically, his catalogue of nuisances became one of the most famous lists in astronomy: the Messier objects are among the brightest and most impressive deep-sky objects visible from the Northern Hemisphere, and most can be seen with binoculars or a small telescope.

Contents

The catalogue includes:

  • Galaxies — such as the Andromeda Galaxy (M31), the Whirlpool Galaxy (M51), and the Sombrero Galaxy (M104).
  • Open clusters — such as the Pleiades (M45) and the Wild Duck Cluster (M11).
  • Globular clusters — such as the Great Hercules Cluster (M13) and M22 in Sagittarius.
  • Nebulae — such as the Orion Nebula (M42), the Ring Nebula (M57, a planetary nebula), and the Crab Nebula (M1, a supernova remnant — the first entry, catalogued in 1758 after Messier observed it while following a comet).

A few entries are anomalous: M40 is a double star, M73 is an asterism of four stars, and M102 may be a duplicate observation of M101. The list was extended from 103 to 110 objects in the twentieth century from Messier's and Méchain's unpublished observations.

Observing and the Messier marathon

Amateur astronomers celebrate the catalogue through the Messier marathon, an attempt to observe all 110 objects in a single night, possible around mid-March to early April from mid-northern latitudes. The objects are also popular first targets for astrophotographers.
