Social medicine is a field of medicine and social science that examines how health, disease, and medical care are shaped by social, economic, cultural, and political conditions, and that seeks to improve health by acting on those conditions as well as by treating individuals. The term was introduced in 1848 by the French physician Jules Guérin, in the same year that Rudolf Virchow investigated a typhus epidemic in Upper Silesia and concluded that the epidemic's causes were poverty and political oppression—famously declaring that "medicine is a social science, and politics is nothing else but medicine on a large scale."

History

Nineteenth-century reformers including Edwin Chadwick in England and Virchow in Germany linked disease to sanitation, housing, and working conditions. In the twentieth century the field developed institutional homes in Latin America—where medicina social became a strong academic tradition associated with figures such as Salvador Allende, whose 1939 work analysed Chile's health problems in social terms—and in academic departments in Europe and the United States, where George Rosen wrote its classic histories. In apartheid South Africa, Sidney and Emily Kark developed community-oriented primary care that influenced global primary health care thinking.

Modern practice

Contemporary social medicine overlaps with research on the social determinants of health, health equity, and structural violence—a term Paul Farmer used to describe how social arrangements systematically harm the poor. Training programmes, such as residencies in social medicine in the Bronx and global health equity programmes in Boston and Rwanda, teach clinicians to address housing, food security, and legal needs alongside biomedical care. Social medicine informs debates about universal health coverage, arguing that medical care alone cannot close health gaps produced by inequality.
