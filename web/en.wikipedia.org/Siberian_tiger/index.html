The Siberian tiger, also known as the Amur tiger, is a population of the tiger subspecies Panthera tigris tigris native to the Russian Far East, Northeast China, and possibly North Korea. It once ranged throughout the Korean Peninsula, north China, and eastern Mongolia. The population currently inhabits mainly the Sikhote-Alin mountain region in southwest Primorye Province in the Russian Far East. In 2005, there were 331–393 adult and subadult Siberian tigers in this region, and recent estimates suggest the population has stabilised or grown to around 500 or more.

The Siberian tiger is genetically close to the now-extinct Caspian tiger. Results of a phylogeographic study comparing mitochondrial DNA from Caspian tigers and living tiger populations indicate that the common ancestor of the Siberian and Caspian tigers colonised Central Asia from eastern China via the Gansu−Silk Road corridor.

Characteristics

The Siberian tiger is one of the largest tiger populations, adapted to cold temperate forests. Its coat is paler and less densely striped than other tigers', and it grows a thick winter coat; a layer of fat on the belly and flanks insulates it in sub-zero temperatures. Historical accounts report very large males, though typical adult males weigh around 180–306 kg and measure about 190–230 cm in head-and-body length. It has especially large home ranges: a male's territory may exceed 1,000 km² where prey is scarce.

Ecology and behaviour

The Siberian tiger preys mainly on ungulates, particularly Manchurian wapiti, wild boar, sika deer, and roe deer, and it also takes smaller animals and occasionally livestock. It is largely solitary and nocturnal, marking its territory with scent and scratch marks. Females give birth to litters of two to four cubs, which remain dependent for up to two years. The tiger is the apex predator of its ecosystem, sharing habitat with Amur leopards and, occasionally, competing with brown bears and wolves.

Conservation

By the 1940s, hunting had reduced the Siberian tiger to an estimated 20–30 individuals in the wild. The Soviet Union banned tiger hunting in 1947 — the first country to grant the tiger full protection — and the population slowly recovered. Principal threats today include poaching for the illegal trade in tiger parts, logging and habitat fragmentation, and depletion of prey. Conservation measures include anti-poaching patrols, protected areas such as Land of the Leopard National Park, wildlife corridors across the Chinese border, and monitoring by camera traps. In 2010, Russia hosted the International Tiger Conservation Forum in Saint Petersburg, committing to double wild tiger numbers globally.

Relationship with humans

Attacks on humans are rare and usually provoked; the tiger features prominently in the folklore of the Udege, Nanai, and other indigenous peoples of the Amur region, who traditionally revered it. Captive Siberian tigers are common in zoos worldwide, and the captive population exceeds the wild one.
