Escapology is the practice of escaping from restraints or other traps, performed as entertainment by an escape artist. The term is credited to the Australian escapologist Murray (Norman Murray Walters). Escapes are commonly performed from handcuffs, straitjackets, locked containers, ropes, mail bags, barrels, prison cells, and tanks of water, often in combination and under time pressure or threat of drowning, fire, or falling.

Methods

Escapes rely on a mixture of genuine skill and showmanship. Physical techniques include manipulating locks with concealed picks or keys, exploiting the slack and stretch of ropes and chains, dislocating or compressing the shoulders to slip a straitjacket, holding the breath, and great strength or flexibility. Other escapes are gimmicked: trick cabinets, breakaway panels, dummy locks, and confederates. Performers distinguish "open" escapes, in which the audience sees everything, from escapes performed behind a curtain or screen.

History

Escaping acts appeared in magic shows in the mid-nineteenth century — the Davenport brothers' spirit-cabinet escapes of the 1860s are an early example — but the art was defined by Harry Houdini (1874–1926). Houdini challenged police forces around the world to lock him in their cells, escaped from the Chinese Water Torture Cell, milk cans, and packing crates, and in 1912 began his suspended straitjacket escape, performed upside down from cranes and building ledges. His fame made escapology a headline variety act and inspired generations of successors, including Dorothy Dietrich, the first woman to perform the bullet catch and a noted straitjacket escape artist, and Dean Gunnarson, known for escapes involving alligators, sharks, and being buried alive.

Escapology is dangerous: performers have been injured or killed attempting water, burial, and straitjacket escapes, and Houdini's own death from peritonitis after a punch to the abdomen remains a famous cautionary tale, though it was not sustained during an escape itself.
