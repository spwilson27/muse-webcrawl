Hypophosphatasia (HPP) is a rare inherited disorder characterised by defective mineralisation of bone and teeth, caused by loss-of-function mutations in the ALPL gene, which encodes the tissue-nonspecific form of the enzyme alkaline phosphatase. The name reflects the hallmark biochemical finding of low ("hypo-") phosphatase activity. Severity ranges from lethal perinatal disease to mild adult forms and a dental-only variant (odonto-hypophosphatasia).

Signs and symptoms

Severely affected infants may be born with almost completely unmineralised bones, respiratory failure from a small, soft rib cage, and vitamin-B6-responsive seizures. Children may develop rickets-like deformities, short stature, chronic bone pain, and premature loss of deciduous teeth with intact roots. Adults commonly present with recurrent metatarsal stress fractures, osteomalacia, joint pain, and dental problems. Accumulated substrates of the enzyme, including inorganic pyrophosphate and pyridoxal 5′-phosphate, explain many features.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is suggested by persistently low serum alkaline phosphatase (unusual in other bone diseases, where it is typically high), elevated urinary phosphoethanolamine and plasma pyridoxal 5′-phosphate, and confirmed by ALPL genetic testing. In 2015 the enzyme replacement therapy asfotase alfa — a recombinant form of the enzyme targeted to bone — was approved for perinatal, infantile, and juvenile-onset HPP, transforming the prognosis of the severe forms. Bisphosphonates are generally avoided because they are analogues of pyrophosphate and may worsen the mineralisation defect.
