A peace movement is a social movement which seeks to achieve ideals such as the ending of a particular war (or wars) or minimizing inter-human violence in a particular place or situation. They are often characterised by a belief that humans should not wage war on each other or engage in violent conflicts over language, race, natural resources, religion or ideology. Peace movements include pacifist organisations, anti-war campaigns, disarmament movements, and religious and secular peace activism throughout modern history.

History

Organized peace activism began in the aftermath of the Napoleonic Wars, with peace societies founded in the United States (1815) and Britain (1816) and international peace congresses from the 1840s. Religious groups such as the Quakers, Mennonites and Brethren — the "historic peace churches" — had longer traditions of pacifism and conscientious objection. The Hague Peace Conferences (1899, 1907) and the founding of the International Peace Bureau reflected growing institutional ambitions.

Twentieth century

The First World War generated conscientious-objector movements and organisations such as the Women's International League for Peace and Freedom (1915). After the Second World War, opposition to nuclear weapons drove mass campaigns: the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament (CND) in Britain — whose Gerald Holtom designed the now-universal peace symbol in 1958 — and parallel movements worldwide. The largest mobilizations of the century opposed the Vietnam War in the 1960s–70s, the deployment of new nuclear missiles in Europe in the early 1980s, and the 2003 invasion of Iraq, when coordinated protests on 15 February 2003 drew millions worldwide in what is often called the largest protest event in history.

Methods

Peace movements use marches, vigils, petitions, lobbying, education, boycotts, civil disobedience and nonviolent direct action, drawing on the tradition of Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr. Peace camps, conscientious-objection support networks and citizen diplomacy have also been characteristic.

Criticism

Critics have accused peace movements of naivety, of discouraging necessary defence, or of serving the interests of hostile powers; supporters counter that the movements have advanced arms control treaties, norms against conquest and conscientious objection rights.
