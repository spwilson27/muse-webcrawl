The Order of Assassins (Arabic: الحشَّاشين, romanized: al-Ḥashshāshīyīn; Persian: حشاشین, romanized: Ḥashāshīn) were a Nizari Isma'ili order that existed between 1090 and 1275 AD, based in a network of mountain strongholds in Persia and Syria. Founded by Hassan-i Sabbah, the order formed the military arm of the Nizari Ismaili state and became famous for targeted killings of political and military leaders of the Abbasid Caliphate, the Seljuk Empire, the Fatimid Caliphate and the Crusader states.

Possessing a small territory and facing far larger enemies, the Nizaris resorted to asymmetric warfare, including espionage and the selective assassination of enemy leaders by devotees known as fidā'īs. The word "assassin" in European languages derives from their name.

Origins

After the death of the Fatimid caliph-imam al-Mustansir in 1094, a succession dispute split the Ismaili movement; Hassan-i Sabbah, who had seized Alamut Castle in the Alborz mountains in 1090, led the Nizari branch. From Alamut and fortresses such as Lambsar and Gerdkuh, the Nizaris built a territorial state within hostile Seljuk Persia, while a Syrian branch under Rashid al-Din Sinan ("the Old Man of the Mountain") held castles including Masyaf.

Targets and tactics

Notable victims included the Seljuk vizier Nizam al-Mulk (1092) and, later, Conrad of Montferrat, king-elect of Jerusalem (1192). Fidā'īs typically infiltrated a target's court or public processions and struck with a dagger, often accepting death in the act — a tactic that multiplied the order's psychological effect far beyond its numbers.

Destruction

The Persian strongholds fell to the Mongol campaign of Hülegü Khan; Alamut surrendered in 1256 and its great library was destroyed. The Syrian castles were taken by the Mamluk sultan Baybars in the 1270s. Western legends — hashish use, Marco Polo's "garden of paradise" story — are largely regarded by modern scholars as hostile fabrications, though they shaped the order's enduring fame.
