Indigenous peoples of Mexico

The Indigenous peoples of Mexico (Spanish: pueblos indígenas de México) are those peoples who are the descendants of the original inhabitants of Mexico and who preserve their own cultural, social and political institutions. Mexico recognises 68 national indigenous languages belonging to 11 language families, and the National Institute of Indigenous Peoples (INPI) identifies 68 corresponding peoples. The 2020 census counted about 23.2 million people (roughly 19% of the population) who self-identified as indigenous, and about 7.4 million who spoke an indigenous language.

Major groups

The largest indigenous peoples include the Nahua (descendants of the Aztecs/Mexica), the Maya peoples of Yucatán, Chiapas, Tabasco and Campeche (Yucatec, Tzotzil, Tzeltal, Ch'ol and others), the Zapotec and Mixtec of Oaxaca, the Otomí, Totonac, Mazahua, Mazatec, Huastec, Chinantec, Mixe, Tlapanec, Tarahumara (Rarámuri) and the Yaqui and Mayo of Sonora and Sinaloa.

History

Indigenous Mesoamerican civilisations — Olmec, Maya, Zapotec, Mixtec, Toltec and Aztec — developed agriculture based on maize, cities, calendars and writing before the Spanish conquest (1519–1521). The colonial period reduced the indigenous population drastically through epidemics and exploitation and imposed the caste system, while indigenous communities preserved languages and customs. After independence, 19th- and 20th-century policy often promoted assimilation (mestizaje); the Zapatista uprising of 1994 in Chiapas brought indigenous rights to national attention, and constitutional reforms in 2001 recognised Mexico as a pluricultural nation.
