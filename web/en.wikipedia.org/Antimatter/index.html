Antimatter

Antimatter
Predicted1928, by Paul Dirac
First observed1932 — the positron, by Carl D. Anderson
Key propertyAnnihilates with matter, releasing energy as gamma rays
ExamplesPositrons, antiprotons, antineutrons, antihydrogen

In modern physics, antimatter is defined as matter composed of the antiparticles of the particles that constitute ordinary matter. Every particle has a corresponding antiparticle with the same mass but opposite electric charge and other quantum numbers: the electron's antiparticle is the positively charged positron, the proton's is the antiproton, and so on. When a particle and its antiparticle meet, they annihilate each other, converting their mass entirely into energy, typically as gamma-ray photons, in accordance with E = mc² (see the annus mirabilis papers).

History

Antimatter was predicted in 1928 when Paul Dirac's relativistic equation for the electron produced negative-energy solutions, which he interpreted as a sea filled with electrons, whose holes would appear as positive particles. In 1932 Carl D. Anderson discovered the positron in cosmic-ray cloud-chamber photographs, confirming Dirac's prediction. The antiproton was found at Berkeley in 1955 by Emilio Segrè and Owen Chamberlain, and the antineutron followed in 1956. Physicists at CERN created the first antihydrogen atoms in 1995, and since 2010 experiments at CERN (ALPHA, ATRAP and others) have trapped antihydrogen and measured its spectrum, testing whether antimatter falls up or down in gravity and obeys the same physical laws as matter — so far, it does.

Where antimatter occurs

Antimatter is produced naturally in cosmic-ray collisions, in thunderstorms (positrons and gamma-ray flashes), and in certain radioactive decays; potassium-40 in the human body emits positrons. It is manufactured in minute quantities at particle accelerators. Because antimatter annihilates on contact with ordinary matter, it must be stored in vacuum inside electromagnetic Penning traps, and global production amounts to nanograms — making it by far the most expensive substance known and ruling out near-term use as a fuel or weapon, despite its prominence in science fiction.

The matter–antimatter asymmetry

One of the great open questions in physics is why the observable universe is made almost entirely of matter when the Big Bang should have produced matter and antimatter in nearly equal amounts, which would have annihilated leaving only radiation. This baryon asymmetry requires processes satisfying the Sakharov conditions, including CP violation — which is observed in weak interactions but appears too small to explain the imbalance — and remains an active field of research.

Applications

Antimatter has a vital medical application: positron emission tomography (PET) uses positron-emitting tracer isotopes to image metabolic activity in the body. Antiprotons have also been studied for radiotherapy and for precision tests of fundamental symmetries.
