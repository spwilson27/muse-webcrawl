Turkey as food

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Turkey is the meat of the turkey bird, primarily the domesticated turkey, which is raised on farms worldwide and sold whole or in parts (breast, legs, wings, ground meat and processed products). It is a staple of North American festive cooking and a widely consumed lean poultry meat.

Culinary traditions

Turkey is the centrepiece of Thanksgiving dinner in the United States and Canada, traditionally roasted and served with stuffing, gravy, cranberry sauce and pumpkin pie, and is also common at Christmas in the United States and United Kingdom. Almost 90% of Americans surveyed eat turkey at Thanksgiving. Leftovers are famously used in sandwiches, soups and pot pies.

Preparation methods include roasting, brining, deep-frying (a regional, and hazardous, tradition in the American South), smoking and grilling. Ground turkey is a popular lower-fat substitute for ground beef, and processed turkey products imitate bacon, ham and deli meats.

Nutrition

Turkey meat is high in protein and low in fat, especially the skinless breast, and provides B vitamins (notably niacin and B12), selenium, zinc and phosphorus. Dark meat from the legs contains more fat and iron. Turkey is often cited as a source of the amino acid tryptophan, though its supposed sedative effect after Thanksgiving dinner is largely a myth attributable to the meal's overall size and alcohol.

Production

Commercial turkeys are chiefly broad-breasted white breeds, grown to weights far beyond those of wild turkeys. The United States is the world's largest producer; significant production also occurs in the European Union, Brazil and Canada.
