Interventional radiology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Interventional radiology (IR) is a medical specialty that performs minimally invasive, image-guided procedures to diagnose and treat diseases in nearly every organ system. Using X-ray fluoroscopy, ultrasound, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for real-time guidance, interventional radiologists navigate catheters, guidewires, balloons, stents and other small instruments through blood vessels or other pathways, often avoiding open surgery and general anaesthesia.

History

The field grew out of diagnostic angiography. Key milestones include the Seldinger technique for percutaneous vascular access (1953), Charles Dotter's first transluminal angioplasty in 1964 — Dotter is often called the father of interventional radiology — and the development of balloon catheters by Andreas Gruentzig in the 1970s. In many countries IR is now recognised as a distinct subspecialty of radiology with its own training pathway.

Procedures

  • Vascular interventions: angioplasty and stenting for arterial narrowing, thrombolysis and thrombectomy for clots, embolisation to stop bleeding or block blood flow to tumours and aneurysms, and inferior vena cava filters.
  • Oncological interventions: chemoembolisation (TACE), radioembolisation and percutaneous tumour ablation (radiofrequency, microwave, cryoablation).
  • Non-vascular interventions: image-guided biopsies and drainages, biliary and urinary drainage and stenting, gastrostomy placement, and vertebroplasty.
  • Other: uterine fibroid embolisation, varicocele and pelvic congestion treatment, dialysis access maintenance, and foreign-body retrieval.

Technique and safety

Most procedures use local anaesthesia and conscious sedation, are performed through a needle puncture of a few millimetres, and allow shorter hospital stays than conventional surgery. Risks include bleeding, infection, contrast reactions, kidney injury from iodinated contrast, and radiation exposure to patient and operator, managed through dose-reduction techniques and protective equipment.
