Best interests

Best interests or best interests of the child is a legal standard used by courts to decide issues affecting a child — such as custody, visitation, guardianship, and medical treatment — by reference to the child's welfare rather than the rights or wishes of parents or other adults. It is the paramount consideration in family law proceedings in many jurisdictions and is enshrined in Article 3 of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989), which provides that "in all actions concerning children... the best interests of the child shall be a primary consideration."

Factors

Statutes and case law typically direct courts to consider a non-exhaustive list of factors, which commonly include: the child's physical, emotional, and educational needs; the likely effect of any change in circumstances; the child's age, sex, background, and characteristics; any harm the child has suffered or risks suffering; the capability of each parent (or other carer) to meet the child's needs; the child's own wishes and feelings, weighted by age and understanding; and the importance of preserving relationships with siblings and the wider family. The English Children Act 1989 codified such a "welfare checklist," and similar factor-based approaches are used across the United States, Canada, Australia, and other common law systems.

Applications and criticism

Beyond custody, the standard is applied in adoption, child protection, immigration decisions affecting children, and medical cases — for example where courts authorize or refuse life-sustaining treatment for an incapacitated child, as in the cases of Charlie Gard and Alfie Evans in England. Critics argue the standard is indeterminate and invites judges to substitute their own values, and that it can undervalue parental rights and family autonomy; defenders respond that its flexibility allows fact-sensitive decisions centred on the individual child. The phrase "best interests" is also used more broadly in fiduciary law, medicine, and mental-capacity legislation for decisions made on behalf of adults who lack capacity to decide for themselves.
