In Greek mythology, the Nereids (Greek: Νηρηΐδες, singular Nereid) are sea nymphs, the fifty daughters of the ancient sea god Nereus and the Oceanid Doris. Benevolent and beautiful, they dwell in the depths of the sea and attend Poseidon, aiding sailors and personifying the sea's gentler aspects. Their name survives in modern Greek folklore, where neraida denotes a fairy.

Mythology

Hesiod's Theogony lists fifty Nereids by name; Homer and Apollodorus give overlapping lists of 33–34 names, and Hyginus others, so totals vary between sources. The most famous Nereids include:

  • Thetis — mother of Achilles, who summoned her sisters to mourn with her in the Iliad.
  • Amphitrite — wife of Poseidon and queen of the sea.
  • Galatea — beloved of the Cyclops Polyphemus, who crushed her lover Acis with a rock.
  • Psamathe — mother of Phocus by Aeacus.
  • Calypso and Thoe, Dynamene, Panopea and others named in epic catalogues.

The Nereids escorted Thetis, carried Achilles' new armor from Hephaestus, helped the Argonauts past the Wandering Rocks, and were invoked by sailors for safe passage. Their beauty provoked the boast of Cassiopeia, queen of Aethiopia, whose comparison of herself to them brought Poseidon's wrath and the sea monster sent against her land — the origin of the Andromeda myth.

In art and culture

Nereids riding dolphins, hippocamps, and sea monsters are ubiquitous in ancient Greek and Roman art — on vases, sarcophagi, mosaics, and coins — and remained popular in Renaissance and neoclassical art and in fountains. The 2nd-century AD "Nereid Monument" from Xanthos in Lycia (now largely in the British Museum) took its name from the Nereid figures between its columns. In modern usage, Nereid is also the name of a moon of Neptune, named in keeping with the nymphs' attendance on the sea god.
