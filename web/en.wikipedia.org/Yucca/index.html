Yucca is a genus of perennial shrubs and trees in the family Asparagaceae, comprising about 50 species native to the hot, dry regions of the Americas and the Caribbean. Yuccas are notable for their rosettes of evergreen, tough, sword-shaped leaves and large terminal panicles of white or cream-coloured, bell-shaped flowers.

Description and ecology

Species range from stemless rosettes, such as Yucca filamentosa of the eastern United States, to tree-like forms such as the Joshua tree (Yucca brevifolia) of the Mojave Desert and the soaptree yucca of the Southwest. Yuccas are famous for their obligate mutualism with yucca moths (genus Tegeticula): the female moth deliberately pollinates the flowers while laying eggs in the ovary, and her larvae feed on some of the developing seeds — a classic textbook example of coevolution.

Uses

Indigenous peoples of the Americas used yucca extensively: fibres from the leaves for rope, sandals, and cloth; roots containing saponins as soap and shampoo; and flowers, stalks, and fruits as food. Yuccas remain popular ornamental plants in dry gardens worldwide. The name "yucca" was originally applied by the Spanish to the unrelated cassava (Manihot esculenta), causing occasional confusion; in parts of Latin America cassava is still called yuca. Yucca is the state flower of New Mexico.
