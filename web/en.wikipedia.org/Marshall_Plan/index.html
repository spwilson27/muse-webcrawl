Marshall Plan

The Marshall Plan (officially the European Recovery Program, ERP) was an American initiative enacted in 1948 to provide foreign aid to Western Europe. The United States transferred over $13 billion — roughly $170 billion in modern terms — in economic recovery programs to Western European economies after the end of the Second World War.

Origins

Secretary of State George C. Marshall proposed the program in a speech at Harvard University on 5 June 1947, inviting all European nations, including the Soviet Union, to draw up a joint recovery plan. The Soviet Union forbade its satellite states from participating. Congress passed the Economic Cooperation Act in April 1948.

Implementation

Aid, administered by the Economic Cooperation Administration, funded imports of food, fuel and machinery, stabilised currencies, and encouraged trade liberalisation and European integration through the OEEC, a precursor of the OECD. The largest recipients were the United Kingdom, France and West Germany.

Effects and legacy

By 1952 Western European industrial production had substantially surpassed pre-war levels. Historians debate how much of the recovery the Plan caused versus merely accelerated, but agree on its political importance: it anchored Western Europe to the United States at the start of the Cold War and spurred European cooperation.
