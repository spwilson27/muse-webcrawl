Polyptych

A polyptych (Greek: poly "many" + ptychē "fold") is a painting which is divided into sections, or panels. Specifically, a diptych has two sections, a triptych three, a quadriptych four, a pentaptych five, and so on. Polyptychs were most commonly produced as altarpieces in churches and cathedrals during the late medieval and Renaissance periods.

Structure

Polyptychs typically consist of a large central panel, often depicting the Virgin and Child or a principal saint, flanked by hinged side panels (wings) that could be opened on feast days and closed during penitential seasons. Below the main register there may be a predella, a horizontal band of smaller narrative scenes. The exterior faces of the wings, visible when the altarpiece was closed, were often painted in more muted tones or grisaille.

History

The format developed in Italy and Northern Europe in the 14th century. Famous examples include Duccio's Maestà (1308–1311) for Siena Cathedral and the Ghent Altarpiece (1432) by Hubert and Jan van Eyck, a polyptych of twelve panels. Matthias Grünewald's Isenheim Altarpiece (1512–1516) uses multiple configurations of hinged wings. Although demand declined after the Renaissance, the multi-panel format has been revived by modern artists, and the term is also applied to works divided into multiple physically separate but thematically unified paintings.
