Landfill gas

Landfill gas is a mix of gases created by the action of microorganisms within a landfill. When organic waste decomposes anaerobically (without oxygen), bacteria produce a gas that is roughly half methane and half carbon dioxide, with small amounts of nitrogen, oxygen, hydrogen sulfide, and hundreds of trace non-methane organic compounds.

Production

Decomposition proceeds in phases: aerobic breakdown, acid formation, and finally stable methanogenesis, which may continue for decades after waste is buried. A typical landfill generates gas at significant rates for 10 to 30 years. Moisture content, temperature, waste composition, and pH control the rate. Because methane is a potent greenhouse gas (over 80 times more powerful than CO2 over 20 years), landfills are a major anthropogenic source — the third-largest source of human-related methane emissions in the United States.

Hazards

Landfill gas can migrate underground into nearby buildings, where methane may accumulate to explosive concentrations (between 5% and 15% in air). Incidents such as the 1986 Loscoe explosion in England, which destroyed a house, led to stricter controls. Trace constituents include vinyl chloride, benzene, and other toxic or odorous compounds, and the gas asphyxiates vegetation by displacing oxygen at roots.

Utilisation

Collected gas is burned in flares or used as a renewable fuel: it generates electricity in engines and turbines, is upgraded to pipeline-quality renewable natural gas by removing CO2 and contaminants, and can fuel vehicles or supply industrial heat. Hundreds of landfill-gas energy projects operate worldwide, offsetting both methane emissions and fossil fuel use. Regulations in the US, EU, and elsewhere require large landfills to collect and control gas emissions.
