Mod (subculture)

Mod, from the word modernist, is a subculture that began in late 1950s London and spread throughout Great Britain, eventually influencing fashions and trends in other countries. It continues today on a smaller scale. Focused on music and fashion, the subculture has its roots in a small group of stylish London-based young men and women in the late 1950s who were termed modernists because they listened to modern jazz.

Characteristics

Significant elements of the mod subculture include:

  • Fashion — customised Italian-style suits, slim ties, button-down shirts, fishtail parkas (often worn to protect clothing on scooters), Chelsea boots, and for women, bold geometric styles, miniskirts and heavy eye makeup.
  • Music — modern jazz, rhythm and blues, soul, ska, and later British beat bands such as the Who, the Small Faces, and the Yardbirds.
  • Scooters — Italian motor scooters, especially Lambrettas and Vespas, often decorated with mirrors and lights, as affordable and stylish transport.
  • Amphetamines and all-night clubs — an all-night dance scene centred on clubs in Soho and other cities.

Mods and rockers

During the early-to-mid 1960s, clashes between mods and their rivals, the rockers (associated with leather jackets, motorcycles, and rock and roll), attracted wide media coverage, notably fights at seaside resorts such as Brighton, Margate and Clacton during the 1964 bank holidays. Sociologist Stanley Cohen used the episode as a case study in his theory of "moral panic" in Folk Devils and Moral Panics (1972).

Revivals and legacy

The subculture declined after the mid-1960s as many mods moved into psychedelia and other scenes, but enjoyed a major revival in the late 1970s, influenced by the film Quadrophenia (1979) and bands such as The Jam. Mod fashion and imagery have recurred in Britpop and later popular culture, and the target roundel and parka remain recognisable symbols.
