Container ship

Container ships carry the great majority of the world's non-bulk cargo.

A container ship is a cargo ship that carries all of its load in truck-size intermodal containers, in a technique called containerization. Container ships are a common means of commercial intermodal freight transport and now carry most seagoing non-bulk cargo. Capacity is measured in twenty-foot equivalent units (TEU). The container revolution began in 1956 when Malcom McLean's converted tanker Ideal X carried 58 containers from Newark to Houston.

Size categories

Ships are classed by the waterways they can transit: Panamax (through the original Panama Canal locks), New Panamax, Suezmax, and ultra large container vessels (ULCVs). The largest ships, such as the Ever Alot class, exceed 24,000 TEU, are about 400 metres long, and are powered by single slow-speed two-stroke diesel engines driving one propeller.

Operations and impact

Containerization slashed the cost of international shipping by more than 90 percent compared with break-bulk handling, reshaping world trade and port geography. Major container ports include Shanghai, Singapore and Rotterdam. Incidents such as the 2021 grounding of Ever Given in the Suez Canal, which blocked the waterway for six days, highlight the system's chokepoints.
