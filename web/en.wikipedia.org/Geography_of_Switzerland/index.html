Geography of Switzerland

Switzerland
Area: 41,285 km²
Highest point: Dufourspitze (Monte Rosa), 4,634 m
Lowest point: Lake Maggiore, 193 m
Neighbours: France, Germany, Austria, Italy, Liechtenstein

The geography of Switzerland is dominated by mountains. The landlocked country in west-central Europe covers 41,285 square kilometres and is divided into three main geographic regions: the Alps, which occupy about 60 per cent of the territory in the south and east; the Swiss Plateau (Mittelland), a band of hills and plains running across the centre; and the Jura Mountains along the northwestern border with France. Most of Switzerland's roughly 8.7 million inhabitants live on the Plateau, which contains the largest cities — Zurich, Geneva, Bern, Lausanne, and Basel.

The Alps

The Swiss Alps contain many of Europe's highest peaks, including the Dufourspitze (4,634 m) in the Monte Rosa massif, the Dom, the Weisshorn, and the Matterhorn (4,478 m), one of the world's most recognisable mountains. The range also holds the largest glaciers in western Europe, notably the Aletsch Glacier, about 23 km long, in the Bernese Alps. Important passes such as the Gotthard, Simplon, and Great St Bernard have linked northern and southern Europe for centuries; today the 57 km Gotthard Base Tunnel, opened in 2016, is the world's longest railway tunnel.

Rivers and lakes

Switzerland is sometimes called the "water tower of Europe." The Rhine, Rhône, Inn (a Danube tributary), and Ticino (a Po tributary) all rise in the Gotthard region, giving the country drainage into the North Sea, the Mediterranean, and the Black Sea. The largest lakes lying wholly in Switzerland are Lake Neuchâtel and Lake Lucerne; Lake Geneva (Lac Léman), shared with France, and Lake Constance, shared with Germany and Austria, are the largest overall. The Rhine Falls near Schaffhausen is Europe's largest waterfall by volume.

Climate

The climate is temperate but highly varied over short distances: cold and snowy on the Alpine summits, mild on the Plateau, and almost Mediterranean south of the Alps in Ticino, where palm trees grow beside Lake Lugano and Lake Maggiore. Warm foehn winds can raise temperatures abruptly on the northern Alpine slopes. Nearly a third of the country is forested and about a quarter is used for agriculture; settlement is concentrated below 1,000 metres elevation.
