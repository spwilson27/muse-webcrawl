Aquatic plant

Aquatic plants, also called hydrophytes, are vascular plants that have adapted to living in aquatic environments (fresh water or salt water). They are an important component of wetlands and other water bodies, providing food, oxygen and habitat for aquatic animals. Because living underwater requires special adaptations, aquatic plants are often classified by their growth habit.

Types

  • Emergent plants grow in shallow water with most of the plant above the surface, e.g. cattails (Typha) and many sedges and rushes.
  • Floating-leaved plants are rooted in the sediment with leaves floating on the surface, e.g. water lilies (Nymphaea).
  • Free-floating plants are not rooted and drift at the surface, e.g. duckweed (Lemna) and water hyacinth.
  • Submerged plants grow entirely below the surface, e.g. many pondweeds (Potamogeton) and seagrasses.

Adaptations

Aquatic plants commonly possess aerenchyma—spongy tissue with air spaces that transports oxygen to the roots and provides buoyancy—reduced or absent xylem, thin or finely divided submerged leaves, and stomata on the upper surface of floating leaves. Cuticles are often thin or absent because water loss is not a concern.

Ecological role and uses

Aquatic vegetation oxygenates water, stabilises sediments, cycles nutrients, and shelters fish and invertebrates. Some species, such as the invasive water hyacinth, spread rapidly and clog waterways. Aquatic plants are widely grown in aquaria and garden ponds, and some, like wild rice and lotus, are harvested as food.
