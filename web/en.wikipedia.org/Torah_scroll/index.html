Torah scroll

Hebrew: סֵפֶר תּוֹרָה, Sefer Torah.

A Torah scroll (Hebrew: Sefer Torah) is a handwritten copy of the Torah — the five books of Moses (Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy) — produced on parchment by a specially trained scribe (sofer) according to strict rules of Jewish law. Torah scrolls are the most sacred objects in Judaism and are used for public reading in the synagogue.


A Torah scroll dressed with mantle, breastplate and pointer

Production

A scroll is written on klaf, parchment made from the skin of a kosher animal, ruled into columns of 42 lines. The scribe writes with a quill and specially prepared black ink, forming the 304,805 letters of the Torah in the traditional Ashkenazi or Sephardi script, many letters crowned with decorative tagin (crownlets). No vowel points or cantillation marks are included. Writing a scroll takes about a year; a single error can render it pasul (unfit) until corrected, and proofreading is meticulous, nowadays often computer-assisted for error detection.

Use and adornment

The scroll's parchment sheets are sewn together and attached to wooden rollers called atzei chaim ("trees of life"). When not in use it is kept in the Torah ark (aron ha-kodesh) of the synagogue, dressed — in Ashkenazi practice — with a cloth mantle, a breastplate and a crown or finials (rimmonim); Sephardi scrolls are usually housed in a rigid cylindrical case (tik). A pointer (yad) is used to follow the text during reading so that hands do not touch the parchment. Portions are read publicly on Sabbaths, festivals and Mondays and Thursdays, completing the whole Torah over an annual cycle celebrated at Simchat Torah.

Completion and disposal

Commissioning a scroll is considered a major mitzvah; at its completion ceremony the final letters are outlined and filled in by honoured guests. A scroll too worn to repair is retired to a genizah (storage) and ultimately buried in a Jewish cemetery with honours befitting its sanctity.
