Penrose tiling

A Penrose tiling is an example of an aperiodic tiling: a pattern of shapes that covers the plane without gaps or overlaps but never repeats periodically. Named after the mathematician and physicist Roger Penrose, who investigated such tilings in the 1970s, Penrose tilings display fivefold symmetry and are related to the golden ratio.

Tile sets

Several prototile sets generate Penrose tilings. The best known use two rhombi — a "thin" rhombus with angles of 36° and 144°, and a "thick" rhombus with angles of 72° and 108° — assembled according to matching rules that force aperiodicity. Other sets use a "kite" and "dart", or the original six-tile set. Matching rules can be represented by coloured arcs on the tiles that must align across edges.

Properties

Penrose tilings are self-similar: through a process called inflation and deflation, tiles can be subdivided or grouped into larger copies of themselves, scaling by the golden ratio φ. Any finite patch of a Penrose tiling appears infinitely often in every Penrose tiling (the local isomorphism property), so the tilings are not distinguishable by examining any bounded region. Although the pattern never repeats by translation, it has statistical fivefold rotational symmetry.

Applications and influence

Penrose tilings provided a mathematical model for quasicrystals, discovered in 1982 by Dan Shechtman, whose diffraction patterns show the same forbidden fivefold symmetry; the discovery eventually earned the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The tilings appear in art and architecture, including the floor of the Oxford Mathematics Institute and designs influenced by Islamic girih tiles, which exhibit similar quasiperiodic geometry. Penrose also pursued litigation over the use of the design on consumer products, asserting intellectual property rights in the pattern.
