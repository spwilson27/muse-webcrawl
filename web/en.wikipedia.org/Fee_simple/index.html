Fee simple

In English common law, a fee simple or fee simple absolute is an estate in land, a form of freehold ownership. It is the most complete ownership interest that can be held in real property, comprising the right to use, possess and dispose of the land during the holder's lifetime and to pass it to heirs on death. The word "fee" derives from the feudal concept of a fief, while "simple" means unrestricted — the estate is not limited to a particular class of heirs or subject to conditions.

A deed granting land in fee simple.

History

Under the feudal system that followed the Norman Conquest of England, all land was held ultimately of the Crown. The statute Quia Emptores of 1290 allowed tenants in fee simple to alienate their land freely by substitution. Over time the fee simple became the standard estate of ownership in England and Wales, in the United States (where it is sometimes called fee simple absolute in possession), and in other common-law jurisdictions. Even a fee simple owner holds subject to the sovereign powers of the state: taxation, escheat, eminent domain and police power.

Defeasible fees

A fee simple may be absolute or defeasible. Defeasible estates end on the occurrence of a stated event and include the fee simple determinable (created with durational language such as "so long as," followed by a possibility of reverter), the fee simple subject to condition subsequent (followed by a right of entry), and the fee simple subject to executory limitation, in which the interest shifts to a third party. The fee tail, which restricted inheritance to lineal descendants, historically competed with the fee simple but has been abolished or fallen into disuse in most jurisdictions.
