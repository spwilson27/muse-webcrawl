Graphics pipeline

Stages of a typical 3D rendering pipeline, from vertex data to final pixels

The computer graphics pipeline, also known as the rendering pipeline, is a conceptual model that describes the sequence of steps used to create a two-dimensional raster representation of a three-dimensional scene. It is implemented in graphics hardware and in APIs such as OpenGL, Direct3D, and Vulkan, and is the basis of real-time rendering in video games and interactive applications.

Stages

In the standard pipeline, vertex processing transforms the positions of vertices from object space through model, view, and projection transformations into clip space; since the late 1990s this stage is programmable through vertex shaders. Primitive assembly groups vertices into points, lines, and triangles, followed by clipping against the view volume and culling of back faces. Rasterization converts the primitives into fragments — candidate pixels — interpolating attributes such as color, depth, and texture coordinates. Fragment processing, programmable through fragment (pixel) shaders, computes the final color of each fragment using texturing, lighting, and other effects. Finally, per-sample operations test depth and stencil values and blend fragments into the framebuffer.

Programmability

Early 3D accelerators implemented a fixed-function pipeline in which lighting and transformation parameters could only be configured. The introduction of programmable shaders in 2001 began a transition to the modern pipeline, later extended with geometry, tessellation, mesh, and compute stages, as well as hardware-accelerated ray tracing stages. The pipeline metaphor survives because the flow remains essentially feed-forward: geometry enters one end and pixels come out the other, with each stage operating in massive parallel on the GPU.
