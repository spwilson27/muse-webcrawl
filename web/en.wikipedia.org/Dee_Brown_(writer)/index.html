Dorris Alexander "Dee" Brown (February 28, 1908 – December 12, 2002) was an American novelist, historian, and librarian, best known for his 1970 book Bury My Heart at Wounded Knee, which recounts the history of the American West from the perspective of Native Americans and became an international bestseller that reshaped popular understanding of westward expansion.

Life and career

Brown grew up in Arkansas and Louisiana, where friendships with Native American children and time spent around oil fields and lumber camps fed a lifelong interest in the frontier. He worked as a reporter, teacher, and librarian — including at the U.S. Department of Agriculture library and, for many years, at the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign, where he was a professor of library science — writing history and fiction in parallel with his library career.

Works

Brown wrote more than twenty-five books. Early works of history include The Gentle Tamers: Women of the Old Wild West (1958) and The Fetterman Massacre (1962), and he also wrote novels such as Wave High the Banner (1942) and Yellowhorse (1956). Bury My Heart at Wounded Knee: An Indian History of the American West (1970) drew heavily on council records, treaties, and first-person Native accounts to narrate the years 1860–1890 — from the Long Walk of the Navajo to the massacre at Wounded Knee. It has been translated into many languages, sold millions of copies, and was adapted as an HBO film in 2007. Later works include Creek Mary's Blood (1980) and Hear That Lonesome Whistle Blow (1977), on the transcontinental railroads.

Legacy

Brown's work is credited with bringing a Native-centred narrative of the West to a mass readership and influencing a generation of historians and writers. He died in Little Rock, Arkansas, in 2002, aged 94.
