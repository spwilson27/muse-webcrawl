Science communication

Public lectures are a traditional form of science communication.

Science communication is the communication of science-related topics to non-experts. It encompasses science journalism, popular science books and documentaries, science museums and festivals, public lectures, and the direct engagement of scientists with the public through media and online platforms. Its goals include informing, educating, raising awareness of scientific developments, and supporting democratic decision-making on issues such as health, climate, and technology.

Models

Early approaches followed a "deficit model", assuming public scepticism arose from a lack of knowledge that communication should fill. Later scholarship emphasised dialogue and engagement models, in which publics are active participants whose local knowledge and values shape how science is received—an approach reflected in fields such as citizen science.

Practice

Science communicators include journalists, press officers at universities and institutes, museum educators, and researchers themselves. Training programmes and postgraduate degrees in science communication have expanded since the late twentieth century. Challenges include misinformation, the distortion of findings in news coverage, and communicating uncertainty—for example during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Research

As an academic field, the public communication of science and technology (PCST) studies how scientific knowledge travels between experts and publics and how audiences interpret it. Journals such as Public Understanding of Science and the Journal of Science Communication publish this research.
