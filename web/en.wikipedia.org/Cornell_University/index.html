Cornell University

Cornell University is a private Ivy League research university in Ithaca, New York, United States. Founded in 1865 by Ezra Cornell and Andrew Dickson White, it was New York's land-grant university and was intended from the start to teach and make contributions in all fields of knowledge — summed up in Ezra Cornell's aspiration for an institution "where any person can find instruction in any study".

Cornell enrols roughly 26,000 students across its main campus in Ithaca, a medical campus (Weill Cornell Medicine) in New York City, and a campus in Education City, Qatar. It is organised into seven undergraduate and seven graduate colleges and is consistently ranked among the world's leading universities.

History

The university opened in 1868 as a strikingly egalitarian institution for its era, admitting students regardless of religion or race and women from 1870. It pioneered degree programmes in fields such as hotel administration, industrial and labor relations, and veterinary medicine, and its faculty and alumni include dozens of Nobel laureates, as well as figures such as Pearl S. Buck, Carl Sagan and Toni Morrison, who taught or studied there.

Campus

The Ithaca campus, some 2,300 acres on East Hill above Cayuga Lake, is noted for its gorges, waterfalls and botanical gardens, blending collegiate Gothic, Victorian and modernist architecture around landmarks such as McGraw Tower, whose chimes ring daily. The university's mascot is the Big Red Bear and its colors are carnelian red and white.

Academics and research

Cornell is a major research centre in engineering, agriculture and life sciences, computer science and the physical sciences, operating facilities including the Cornell Lab of Ornithology and a synchrotron light source, and it manages national laboratories and institutes in partnership with the US government.
