Constanța is the oldest continuously inhabited city in Romania and its largest sea port, located on the western coast of the Black Sea in the region of Dobruja. Founded by Greek colonists around the 7th–6th century BC as Tomis, it was later renamed in honour of Constantia, half-sister of the emperor Constantine the Great.

History

Tomis is famously the place of exile of the Roman poet Ovid, sent there by Augustus in AD 8, where he wrote the Tristia and died in AD 17/18. The city prospered as a trading port under Roman and Byzantine rule, declined in the medieval period, and passed to the Ottoman Empire in the 15th century. It became Romanian in 1878, after which the modern port was developed and the city grew rapidly as the country's principal maritime gateway.

Port and economy

The Port of Constanța is one of the largest on the Black Sea and the fourth largest in Europe, handling grain, oil, containers and bulk cargo, and is linked to the Danube by the Danube–Black Sea Canal. Tourism is also important: the city is the gateway to the Romanian Black Sea resorts, including Mamaia just to the north.

Landmarks

Sights include the Art Nouveau Constanța Casino on the seafront promenade, the Genoese Lighthouse, Ovid's statue in the old town square, Roman mosaics, the Great Mahmudiye Mosque and the city's aquarium and museums.
