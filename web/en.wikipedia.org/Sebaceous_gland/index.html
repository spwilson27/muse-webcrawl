Sebaceous gland

A sebaceous gland is a small exocrine gland in the skin of mammals that secretes an oily substance called sebum into hair follicles (or sometimes directly onto the skin surface) to lubricate and waterproof the skin and hair. In humans they are found in greatest numbers on the face and scalp, and are absent from the palms of the hands and soles of the feet.

Structure and secretion

Sebaceous glands are branched acinar glands: clusters of sac-like alveoli filled with lipid-laden cells (sebocytes) opening into a short duct. They are holocrine glands — the secretion is released by the rupture and disintegration of the cells themselves. Sebum consists of triglycerides, wax esters, squalene and free fatty acids; it helps maintain the skin's acid mantle, reduces water loss and has mild antimicrobial action. Specialised free sebaceous glands include the meibomian glands of the eyelids and Fordyce spots on the lips and genitals.

Clinical significance

At puberty, androgens enlarge the glands and increase sebum production. Blockage and inflammation of the pilosebaceous unit, involving the bacterium Cutibacterium acnes, produces acne vulgaris. Other conditions include seborrhoeic dermatitis, sebaceous hyperplasia and, rarely, sebaceous carcinoma. The glands also contribute to vernix caseosa coating newborn infants.
