Kenneth MacAlpin

Kenneth MacAlpin (Medieval Gaelic: Cináed mac Ailpin; Scottish Gaelic: Coinneach mac Ailpein; c. 810 – 13 February 858), known in most modern regnal lists as Kenneth I, was a king of the Picts and, according to later national myth, the first king of Scotland. He thus became the progenitor of the House of Alpin, which ruled the early kingdom of Alba.

Life and reign

Kenneth was the son of Alpín, a king of Dál Riata killed fighting the Picts in 834. After the annihilation of the Pictish aristocracy by Vikings at the Battle of Fortriu in 839, Kenneth pressed claims to the Pictish kingship — probably through the Pictish practice of matrilineal succession — and by about 843 had established himself as king of the Picts. Later tradition credits him with conquering Pictland outright and uniting Picts and Gaels in a single kingdom, the "union of 843", but modern historians regard the merging of the two peoples as a gradual Gaelicisation over the following century.

Tradition associates Kenneth with the translation of the relics of St Columba from Iona to Dunkeld, which he made the centre of the Scottish church, and with making Scone a royal seat. He is said to have campaigned repeatedly into Lothian and to have raided as far south as Dunbar and Melrose. Two of his sons, Constantine I and Áed, became kings; his brother Donald succeeded him in 858. He was buried on Iona.

Historiography

No contemporary account of Kenneth survives; his career is reconstructed from later king lists, the Chronicle of the Kings of Alba, and Irish annals, which record his death in 858. The story of "MacAlpin's treason" — that he massacred the Pictish nobility at a banquet — is a later folk tale without historical support. Modern scholarship debates whether Kenneth was Gael, Pict, or of mixed heritage, and whether he ruled a Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata that absorbed Pictland or a Pictish kingdom undergoing Gaelic acculturation.
