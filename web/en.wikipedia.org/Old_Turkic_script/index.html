The Old Turkic script (also known as the Göktürk script, Orkhon script, or Turkic runes) was the alphabet used by the Göktürks and other early Turkic khanates during the 7th to 10th centuries to record the Old Turkic language. It was later used by successor states including the Uyghur Khaganate and the Yenisei Kyrgyz. The script is named after the Orkhon Valley in Mongolia, where the most famous inscriptions were discovered in 1889 by the expedition of Nikolai Yadrintsev.

Decipherment

The inscriptions were deciphered in 1893 by the Danish linguist Vilhelm Thomsen, who recognized the frequent word täŋri ("heaven/God") and the name Kül Tigin. The texts proved to be Old Turkic, making them among the oldest written records of any Turkic language.

Characteristics

The script is written from right to left and uses separate glyphs for consonant pairs depending on vowel harmony: most consonants have two forms, one used with front vowels and one with back vowels. Vowels are often omitted in the middle of words. The alphabet typically has 38–40 characters, several of which resemble Scandinavian runes in shape, though the two systems are unrelated. Word dividers (usually a colon-like mark ׃) separate words.

Corpus

The most important monuments are the Khushoo Tsaidam inscriptions erected in honour of Kül Tigin (732 CE) and his brother Bilge Khagan (735 CE), which recount the history of the Second Turkic Khaganate. Other inscriptions include the Tonyukuk inscription (c. 720s) and numerous smaller texts from the Yenisei valley, the Talas valley, and Xinjiang. A related variant was used for the Irk Bitig, a 9th-century book of omens written on paper.
