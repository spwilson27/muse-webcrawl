Dodo

Dodo (Raphus cucullatus)
Status: Extinct (c. 1662–1690)
Order: Columbiformes
Family: Columbidae
Native to: Mauritius, Indian Ocean
Height: about 1 m (3 ft 3 in)
Weight: 10.6–17.5 kg (23–39 lb)

The dodo (Raphus cucullatus) is an extinct flightless bird that was endemic to the island of Mauritius, which is east of Madagascar in the Indian Ocean. The dodo's closest relative was the also-extinct Rodrigues solitaire. The two formed the subfamily Raphinae, a clade of extinct flightless birds that are a part of the family which includes pigeons and doves (Columbidae). Subfossil remains show the dodo measured about 1 metre (3 ft 3 in) in height and may have weighed 10.6–17.5 kg (23–39 lb) in the wild.

Description and ecology

The dodo had greyish and brownish plumage, a large hooked beak with a green and yellow tip, tufted feathers on its rear, and small, useless wings. It lived in forests, feeding on fruit, seeds, and fallen nuts, and nested on the ground. Having evolved in isolation without mammalian predators, it was fearless of humans.

Extinction

Dutch sailors first recorded the dodo in 1598. In the following years, the bird was hunted by sailors and invasive species such as rats, pigs, and monkeys destroyed its nests and competed for food, while its habitat was being destroyed. The last widely accepted sighting of a dodo was in 1662; statistical estimates place its extinction around 1690. The dodo's disappearance within less than a century of its discovery called into question the previously held belief that species could not become extinct, and it foreshadowed humanity's ability to drive species to extinction — a concern now studied under disease and introduced-species ecology as well as direct hunting.

Cultural significance

The dodo achieved widespread recognition from its role in Lewis Carroll's Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, and it has become a symbol of extinction and obsolescence — as in the phrase "dead as a dodo". It is the national animal emblem of Mauritius and appears on its coat of arms.
