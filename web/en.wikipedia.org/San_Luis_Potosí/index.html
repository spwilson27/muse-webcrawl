San Luis Potosí is the capital and largest city of the Mexican state of the same name. Founded in 1592 after the discovery of rich gold and silver deposits in the surrounding hills, it was named in honour of Saint Louis IX of France and in reference to the fabulous silver mines of Potosí in Bolivia, to which its discoverers hoped its wealth would compare. The city stands on the high central plateau of Mexico (the Altiplano) at about 1,850 metres above sea level, astride the Camino Real de Tierra Adentro trade route.

History

Before the Spanish conquest the area was contested frontier land of the Chichimeca peoples. Silver strikes at Cerro de San Pedro in 1592 drew miners, missionaries and merchants, and the settlement was formally established on 3 November 1592. Mining wealth built the churches, palaces and plazas of the colonial centre, including the baroque cathedral and the pink-quarrystone churches of El Carmen and San Francisco. In the 19th century the city was occupied successively by American forces in the Mexican–American War and by the French during the intervention; in 1910 the imprisoned Francisco I. Madero drafted the Plan de San Luis Potosí, calling for revolution against Porfirio Díaz, which ignited the Mexican Revolution.

Modern city

Today San Luis Potosí is a major industrial and logistics hub of the Bajío region, with large automotive, steel, mining and food-processing plants and a metropolitan population of well over a million. Its historic centre, part of the Camino Real de Tierra Adentro UNESCO World Heritage Site (inscribed 2010), preserves a grid of colonial streets, notable museums such as the National Mask Museum and the Leonora Carrington Museum, and the surrealist legacy of Edward James, whose garden Las Pozas lies in Xilitla elsewhere in the state. The city’s Holy Week Silent Procession is one of Mexico’s most famous Semana Santa observances.
