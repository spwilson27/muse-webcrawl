Hagiography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hagiography (from Greek ἅγιος hagios, "holy", and -γραφία -graphia, "writing") is a biography of a saint or an ecclesiastical leader, as well as, by extension, an adulatory and idealized biography of any person. Christian hagiographies focus on the lives, and particularly the miracles, ascribed to men and women canonized by the Roman Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox Church, and other Christian traditions.

Development

The genre grew out of the acta — records of the trials and martyrdoms of early Christian martyrs — and the lives of the Desert Fathers, such as Athanasius's Life of Antony. In the medieval West, collections such as Jacobus de Voragine's Golden Legend (c. 1260) became among the most widely read books in Europe. Lives of saints were read aloud in monasteries, shaped liturgical calendars, and provided models of Christian conduct for the laity.

Hagiography in other traditions

Analogous literatures exist in many religions: the manaqib literature of Islam celebrating Sufi saints and companions of the Prophet; Hindu accounts of poet-saints such as the Tamil Periya Puranam; Buddhist lives of the Buddha and of arhats; and Sikh janamsakhi traditions about Guru Nanak. Scholars increasingly use "hagiography" for this whole family of sacred biography.

Critical study

Because hagiographies aim to edify rather than to document, they pose challenges as historical sources: miracles, stock motifs and anachronisms abound. The Bollandists, a Jesuit scholarly society founded in the seventeenth century, pioneered the critical editing of saints' lives in the Acta Sanctorum. Modern historians read hagiography for evidence of social values, religious practice and mentalities, even where the biographical details cannot be verified.

Modern usage

In contemporary usage, "hagiography" is often pejorative, denoting an uncritical, flattering biography of a political leader, celebrity or other public figure that suppresses faults and controversy.
