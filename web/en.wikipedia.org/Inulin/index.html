Inulin

TypePolysaccharide (fructan)
Composed ofFructose units, usually with terminal glucose
SourcesChicory root, Jerusalem artichoke, agave
EnergyAbout 1.5 kcal/g (via colonic fermentation)
UsesDietary fibre, prebiotic, fat/sugar replacer

Inulin is a naturally occurring polysaccharide produced by many types of plants, industrially most often extracted from chicory root. It belongs to a class of dietary fibres known as fructans. Inulin is used by some plants as a means of storing energy and is typically found in roots or rhizomes.

Chemistry

Inulins are polymers composed mainly of fructose units (fructofuranose) linked by β(2→1) glycosidic bonds, usually terminated by a single glucose unit. Chain lengths vary from a few units to about sixty. Because human digestive enzymes cannot hydrolyse the β(2→1) bonds, inulin passes to the colon largely intact, where gut bacteria ferment it.

Sources

Plants that store inulin include chicory, Jerusalem artichoke, dahlia tubers, yacon, garlic, onion, leeks, asparagus, bananas and agave. Commercial inulin is extracted chiefly from chicory root, of which it forms roughly 15–20% of the fresh weight.

Uses

In the food industry inulin is used as a soluble fibre supplement, a prebiotic, a sugar and fat replacer, and a texture modifier in products such as yoghurt and low-fat spreads. Fermentation by colonic bacteria yields short-chain fatty acids, especially butyrate, which nourish the gut lining; excessive doses can cause flatulence and bloating.

Medical use

Because inulin is freely filtered by the glomerulus and neither secreted nor reabsorbed by the kidney tubules, the inulin clearance test was historically the gold standard for measuring the glomerular filtration rate (GFR), a key index of kidney function. It has largely been replaced in practice by creatinine-based estimates.
