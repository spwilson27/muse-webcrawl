Definition

Charm (symbol C) is a flavour quantum number representing the difference between the number of charm quarks (nc) and the number of charm antiquarks (nc̄) present in a particle:

C = nc − nc̄

By convention the sign of flavour quantum numbers agrees with the sign of the electric charge carried by the quark of that flavour. The charm quark, which carries an electric charge of +2/3 e, therefore has charm +1, and the charm antiquark has charm −1. All other quarks have charm 0.

Conservation

Like all flavour quantum numbers, charm is preserved under strong and electromagnetic interactions but not under the weak interaction (see the CKM matrix). For first-order weak decays — those involving only one quark decay — charm can vary only by one unit (ΔC = ±1 or 0). Because first-order processes are far more common than higher-order ones, this can act as an approximate selection rule.

History

The charm quantum number was introduced in the 1960s–1970s with the postulation of the charm quark by Sheldon Glashow, John Iliopoulos and Luciano Maiani (the GIM mechanism, 1970), which explained the suppression of flavour-changing neutral currents. The 1974 discovery of the J/ψ meson — a bound state of a charm quark and antiquark — in the "November Revolution" confirmed the quark's existence and earned Burton Richter and Samuel Ting the 1976 Nobel Prize in Physics.
