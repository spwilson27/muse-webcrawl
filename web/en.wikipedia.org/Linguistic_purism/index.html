Linguistic purism

Linguistic purism is the practice of defining, or recognising, one linguistic variety as purer or of intrinsically greater quality than others, and the associated efforts to protect a language from "foreign" or supposedly corrupting influences — typically loanwords, neologisms formed from foreign elements, slang, or changes in grammar and pronunciation. Purism is a form of prescriptivism and is often bound up with national identity, cultural politics and standardisation.

Forms

Scholars such as George Thomas have classified purism by its targets and orientation: purism may be directed against foreign words (xenophobic purism), against dialectal or social varieties (democratic or elitist purism), against archaisms, or in favour of reviving older native forms. A common technique is the promotion of loan translations (calques) or newly coined native compounds in place of imported words — as with German Fernsprecher once promoted for "telephone", or Icelandic coinages such as tölva ("computer", from "prophetess of numbers").

Examples

Examples abound historically. The Académie française has regulated French since 1635 and France's Toubon Law (1994) mandates French in official contexts; the Accademia della Crusca did similar work for Italian. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, purist movements accompanied nation-building: the Turkish language reform of the 1930s replaced many Arabic and Persian loans with Turkic coinages; the Croatian and Serbian standards diverged partly through purist choices; Tamil purism (the Tanittamil Iyakkam) sought to purge Sanskrit loans. In English, which has no academy, purism has appeared in campaigns for "Anglish" — a register avoiding Latinate words — famously prefigured by writers who preferred "inborn" vocabulary, and in periodic complaints about Americanisms in British English.

Criticism

Descriptive linguists generally regard purism as a social and ideological phenomenon rather than a linguistic necessity: all languages borrow, and attempts to freeze a language's vocabulary often fail or succeed only in official registers, while sometimes stigmatising minority dialects and their speakers. Nevertheless purist movements have shaped the standard forms of many modern languages and remain politically potent.
