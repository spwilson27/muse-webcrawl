Martin Heidegger

Martin Heidegger (26 September 1889 – 26 May 1976) was a German philosopher who is best known for contributions to phenomenology, hermeneutics, and existentialism. His best-known work, Being and Time (1927, Sein und Zeit), is among the most significant and influential philosophical works of the twentieth century, centring on Dasein — the being for whom Being is a question — and analysing temporality, care, authenticity and being-toward-death.

Life

Heidegger was born in Messkirch, Baden, studied theology and philosophy at Freiburg, and became assistant to Edmund Husserl, succeeding him as professor in 1928 after teaching at Marburg, where his students included Hannah Arendt and Hans-Georg Gadamer. In 1933 he became rector of Freiburg University under the Nazi regime, joined the Nazi Party, and delivered an infamous rectoral address aligning the university with National Socialism; he resigned the rectorship in 1934 but remained a party member until 1945. After the war he was barred from teaching until 1951. The posthumous publication of his Black Notebooks (from 2014) renewed controversy over his antisemitism and the relation of his politics to his philosophy.

Thought

Being and Time sought to recover the "question of Being" (Seinsfrage), forgotten, he argued, since Plato; its analysis moves from everyday being-in-the-world, tools (Zuhandenheit), and thrownness to death, conscience and time as the horizon of Being. Later work — the "turn" (Kehre) — turned to language ("language is the house of Being"), poetry (especially Hölderlin), art, and the critique of technology as Gestell (enframing). His destruction of the metaphysical tradition influenced existentialism, hermeneutics, deconstruction, and thinkers from Sartre and Merleau-Ponty to Foucault and Derrida, even as analytic philosophers such as Carnap attacked his style as meaningless.
