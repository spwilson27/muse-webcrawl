Aristocracy

Aristocracy (from Greek aristokratia, "rule of the best") is a form of government in which power is held by a small, privileged ruling class — the aristocrats — usually on the basis of hereditary status, wealth, and land ownership. In common usage, "the aristocracy" refers to the hereditary nobility of a society.

Concept

Aristotle classified aristocracy as one of the "true" forms of government, in which the best citizens rule in the common interest; its degenerate form, rule by the wealthy few for their own benefit, he called oligarchy. Later thinkers, including Polybius and Machiavelli, analysed the role of aristocratic bodies such as senates within mixed constitutions.

History

Aristocracies emerged in the city-states of ancient Greece, where noble families monopolized office after the fall of kingship; Sparta's gerousia and Rome's patricians and senate are classic examples. In medieval and early modern Europe, landed nobilities held political, military, and judicial privileges, often tempered by monarchy. The Venetian Republic after 1297 (the Serrata) entrenched a hereditary merchant aristocracy.

From the eighteenth century, aristocratic privilege was challenged by Enlightenment thought, the American and French Revolutions, and the rise of representative institutions. In Britain aristocrats retained power deep into the nineteenth century, while in France the Revolution abolished noble privilege outright. Most remaining monarchies are now constitutional, and titles are largely honorific.

Modern usage

The word is also applied metaphorically to elites in other fields ("the aristocracy of labour", "an intellectual aristocracy"), and sociologists continue to study hereditary elites as a class.
