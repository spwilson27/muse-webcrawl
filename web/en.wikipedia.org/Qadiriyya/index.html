Qadiriyya

The tomb complex of Abdul Qadir Gilani, Baghdad

The Qadiriyya (Arabic: القادرية) is one of the oldest Sufi orders (tariqas) in Islam. It derives its name from Abdul Qadir Gilani (1077–1166), a Hanbali scholar and preacher of Baghdad, who is regarded by his followers as the order's founder and patron saint, though the order was formally organised by his sons after his death.

History and spread

From Baghdad the Qadiriyya spread widely: to Syria, Egypt and Anatolia, across North and West Africa (where it became one of the most influential orders, notably in Senegal, Mali and northern Nigeria), to the Balkans, and eastward to Central Asia, Persia, India and Indonesia. In West Africa, Qadiri reformist movements were associated with figures such as Uthman dan Fodio of the Sokoto Caliphate. The order's branches are numerous and loosely connected; allegiance to Gilani and his tomb in Baghdad give them a common identity.

Practices

The Qadiriyya is generally characterised by strict adherence to the sharia, generosity and service, and the communal recitation of dhikr (remembrance of God), in many branches performed aloud and accompanied by litanies attributed to Gilani. His most famous work, Al-Ghunya ("Sufficient Provision for the Seekers of the Path of Truth"), collects sermons and teachings. The order remains one of the largest Sufi orders in the world.
