Battery cages are a housing system used for laying hens in intensive egg production. Hens are kept in rows and columns of identical wire cages connected together — like a battery of cells — in large sheds that may hold tens of thousands of birds. The system maximizes stocking density and automates feeding, watering, egg collection, and manure removal.

Description

A conventional ("barren") battery cage typically houses 3–8 hens, each allotted less space than a sheet of A4 paper (about 550–750 cm² in many standards). The sloped wire floor lets eggs roll onto a collection belt. Hens cannot perform natural behaviours such as nesting, perching, dust-bathing, or spreading their wings, and their beaks are commonly trimmed to reduce injurious pecking.

Welfare concerns and regulation

Animal-welfare scientists and advocacy groups argue that battery cages cause frustration, feather loss, and weakened bones (osteoporosis) from lack of exercise. The European Union's Laying Hens Directive banned barren battery cages from 1 January 2012, permitting only "enriched" or "furnished" cages with more space (750 cm²), a nest box, litter, and perches. Switzerland, and several other countries, banned cages earlier or have gone further.

In the United States there is no federal ban, but several states (including California, under Proposition 12, and Massachusetts) have enacted cage-free requirements, and many food companies have pledged to source cage-free eggs. Cage-free, free-range, and barn systems are the main alternatives, though each involves trade-offs in cost, land use, mortality, and disease control.

History

Wire cages for poultry were described in the 1930s; large-scale battery systems became dominant from the 1950s–60s with the industrialization of egg production and the use of antibiotics and automated equipment that made high-density housing viable.
