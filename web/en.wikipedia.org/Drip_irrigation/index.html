Drip irrigation or trickle irrigation is a type of micro-irrigation system that saves water and nutrients by allowing water to drip slowly to the roots of plants, either from above the soil surface or buried below it. The goal is to place water directly into the root zone and minimise evaporation and run-off. In a typical system, water is delivered under low pressure through a network of pipes, tubing, valves, and emitters (drippers) positioned along each row of plants.

History

Primitive forms of drip watering — buried clay pots and perforated pipes — are ancient, but the modern technology dates from experiments in Israel in the 1960s, where engineer Simcha Blass and Kibbutz Hatzerim developed and patented the first practical surface drip emitter and founded the company Netafim in 1965. Plastic tubing made the method economical, and it spread worldwide, becoming one of the most important water-saving technologies in arid agriculture.

Advantages and disadvantages

Properly managed drip systems can reach application efficiencies above 90%, compared with 60–70% for sprinklers and less for flood irrigation. Benefits include lower water and fertiliser use (via fertigation), reduced weed growth and foliar disease because foliage stays dry, higher yields, and the ability to irrigate irregularly shaped or sloping fields. Drawbacks include higher capital cost, clogging of narrow emitters by minerals, algae, or sediment, damage to tubing by rodents and sunlight, and the accumulation of salts at the edges of the wetted zone.

Subsurface drip irrigation buries the lines below the plough layer, further cutting evaporation, and is widely used for high-value vegetables, orchards, vineyards, cotton, and greenhouse crops.
