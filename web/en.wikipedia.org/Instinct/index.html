Instinct

Instinct is the inherent inclination of a living organism towards a particular complex behaviour, containing innate (inborn) elements. The simplest example of an instinctive behaviour is a fixed action pattern (FAP), in which a sequence of behaviours is triggered by a specific stimulus and carried through to completion without learning.

Examples

Classic examples include nest building in birds, web spinning in spiders, migration, the suckling reflex in newborn mammals, and salmon returning to their natal streams to spawn. Ethologists such as Konrad Lorenz and Nikolaas Tinbergen studied instinctive behaviour extensively, identifying innate releasing mechanisms — stimulus filters that trigger fixed action patterns — in phenomena such as the greylag goose's egg-rolling behaviour.

Instinct versus learning

Modern biology treats the instinct–learning dichotomy cautiously. Many behaviours once labelled purely instinctive involve developmental interactions with the environment, and instinctive predispositions often shape what an animal learns (as in birdsong, where an innate template guides imitation). Neuroscientific research associates instinctive behaviours with subcortical circuits — for example, hypothalamic and brainstem systems governing feeding, aggression, mating, and fear.

In psychology

Early psychologists such as William James treated instincts as central to human motivation, and Freud made drives (such as Eros and the death drive) foundational to psychoanalysis. Behaviourists largely rejected instinct talk for humans, but evolutionary psychology revived interest in evolved behavioural predispositions. The term is also used loosely in everyday language for intuition or "gut feeling".
