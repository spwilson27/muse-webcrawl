Tercio

A tercio was a military unit of the Spanish Army in the early modern era. Formed of professional soldiers, tercios pioneered combined-arms tactics — pikemen, swordsmen, and arquebusiers (later musketeers) fighting in disciplined squares — and were the dominant infantry formations of Europe for over a century, central to the military power of Habsburg Spain.

Organization

The first tercios were raised in Italy in 1534. A tercio nominally numbered about 3,000 men, organized into companies (compañías) of roughly 250–300, comprising pikemen in deep blocks (escuadrones), sleeves of shot deployed around the pike block, and officers including the maestre de campo and sergeant major. In practice field strengths varied widely.

Dominance and decline

Tercios proved formidable from the Italian Wars through the Eighty Years' War, winning a reputation for near-invincibility at battles such as Pavia (1525) and Nieuwpoort (1600). Their aura faded after the Battle of Rocroi in 1643, where French forces shattered several Spanish tercios, though the units served on until reforms of the early 18th century replaced them with regiments modeled on French lines.

Legacy

The tercio symbolized Spanish military prestige during the Siglo de Oro. The modern Spanish Legion's regimental units are still called tercios.
