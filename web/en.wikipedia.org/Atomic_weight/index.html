Atomic weight

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Standard atomic weights of the elements

Atomic weight (also called relative atomic mass) is a dimensionless physical quantity defined as the ratio of the average mass of atoms of a chemical element in a given sample to the atomic mass constant. Because both quantities are ratios, the value is dimensionless and is therefore often described simply as the "atomic weight".

Since 1961 the atomic mass constant has been defined as one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Atomic weights are thus relative to carbon-12 and are expressed as numbers very close to the mass number of the most common isotope of each element.

History

The concept of relative atomic weights dates to John Dalton in the early 19th century, who assigned hydrogen a weight of 1 and estimated the weights of other elements relative to it. Later work by Berzelius and others refined the measurements. The modern standard, based on carbon-12, was adopted by IUPAC and IUPAP in 1961.

Standard atomic weights

The Commission on Isotopic Abundances and Atomic Weights (CIAAW) reviews and publishes standard atomic weights roughly every two years. Because isotopic composition varies between natural samples, the standard atomic weights of some elements are expressed as intervals rather than single values; this convention was introduced in 2009 and applies to elements such as hydrogen, lithium, boron, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, silicon, sulfur, chlorine, and thallium.

Terminology

IUPAC recommends the term "relative atomic mass" over "atomic weight", although the latter remains in wide use. The atomic weight of an element differs from the atomic mass of a specific isotope and from the mass number, which is a count of protons and neutrons.
