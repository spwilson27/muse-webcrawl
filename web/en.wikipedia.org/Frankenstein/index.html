Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus is an 1818 novel written by English author Mary Shelley. Frankenstein tells the story of Victor Frankenstein, a young scientist who creates a sapient creature in an unorthodox scientific experiment, and then recoils from and abandons it. Shelley started writing the story when she was 18, and the first edition was published anonymously in London on 1 January 1818, when she was 20. Her name first appeared in the second edition, published in Paris in 1821.

Origins

The novel grew out of a famous ghost-story contest at the Villa Diodati on Lake Geneva during the "Year Without a Summer", 1816, when Shelley, her future husband Percy Bysshe Shelley, Lord Byron, and John Polidori amused themselves during days of incessant rain by reading German ghost stories and then each attempting to write one. Mary's tale, expanded into a novel, became Frankenstein. She later wrote that the idea came to her in a waking dream, in which she saw "the pale student of unhallowed arts kneeling beside the thing he had put together".

Plot

The novel is framed as a series of letters from the Arctic explorer Robert Walton to his sister. Walton rescues Victor Frankenstein, who is pursuing his creature across the ice, and hears his story. As a young student at Ingolstadt, Victor becomes obsessed with discovering the secret of life and assembles a humanoid creature from stolen body parts, animating it with electricity or an unnamed process. Horrified by its appearance, he abandons it. The creature, intelligent and eloquent but rejected by all who see it, learns language and demands that Victor create a female companion. Victor begins, then destroys the second creature; in revenge the monster murders Victor's friend Henry Clerval and Victor's bride Elizabeth on her wedding night. Victor pursues the creature to the Arctic, where he dies aboard Walton's ship; the creature, remorseful, vows to destroy itself on a funeral pyre at the North Pole.

Themes and influence

The subtitle, The Modern Prometheus, links the novel to the Greek myth of the Titan who stole fire from the gods. Its themes — the dangers of unchecked scientific ambition, the responsibilities of creators, prejudice against the different, and the making of monsters by rejection — have made it a founding work of science fiction as well as a classic of Gothic literature. "Frankenstein" is often, in popular usage, taken to be the monster's name rather than the creator's. The creature has become an enduring cultural icon through countless stage and film adaptations, notably the 1931 Universal film starring Boris Karloff.
