Colour organ

A colour organ is an instrument that translates musical sounds into visual displays of coloured light, embodying the idea that music and colour are analogous forms of art. Colour organs range from eighteenth-century keyboard instruments that projected coloured lights through glass panels to twentieth-century electronic devices used in light shows and experimental performances.

The concept rests on a long tradition of speculation about correspondences between musical tones and colours. Isaac Newton, in his Opticks (1704), divided the spectrum into seven colours by analogy with the seven notes of the musical scale, a scheme that has influenced colour-music systems ever since.

History

The French Jesuit Louis-Bertrand Castel proposed the clavecin oculaire (ocular harpsichord) in 1725, a keyboard instrument that would display coloured tapes or panels as notes were played. Although Castel never completed a working instrument, his idea provoked widespread discussion across Europe.

In the nineteenth century, inventors such as Bainbridge Bishop and Alexander Wallace Rimington built practical colour organs. Rimington's instrument, patented in 1893 and demonstrated in London in 1895, used a keyboard controlling coloured lights projected through stained glass. The composer Alexander Scriabin incorporated a part for a colour keyboard (the clavier à lumières) into his symphony Prometheus: The Poem of Fire (1910), one of the most famous artistic uses of the idea.

Twentieth century and beyond

Thomas Wilfred's Clavilux instruments of the 1920s treated projected colour as an art form in itself, which Wilfred called lumia. Later colour organs became popular in 1960s and 1970s psychedelic light shows, and simple electronic "colour organ" kits that flashed lights in time with music were widely sold for home discos.
