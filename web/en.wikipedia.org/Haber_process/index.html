The Haber process, also called the Haber–Bosch process, is an industrial method for synthesising ammonia directly from nitrogen and hydrogen gases: N2 + 3 H2 ⇌ 2 NH3. Developed by the German chemist Fritz Haber in 1909 and scaled up by Carl Bosch at BASF, the process made possible the mass production of nitrogen fertilisers and is often credited with sustaining the food supply of a large fraction of the world's population.

Chemistry and conditions

The reaction is exothermic and reversible. High pressure (typically 150–250 bar) favours ammonia formation, while moderately high temperature (400–500 °C) is required for a practical rate over a promoted iron catalyst — a compromise governed by Le Chatelier's principle. Only about 15% of the gas mixture converts per pass, so unreacted gases are recycled after the ammonia is condensed out. Hydrogen is usually obtained from steam reforming of natural gas, and nitrogen from air.

History and impact

The first industrial plant began operating at Oppau, Germany in 1913. During the First World War the process also supplied Germany with nitrates for explosives after access to Chilean saltpetre was cut off. Haber received the 1918 Nobel Prize in Chemistry; Bosch shared the 1931 prize for high-pressure industrial chemistry. Artificial nitrogen fixation now rivals natural fixation in scale, with environmental consequences including eutrophication and nitrous oxide emissions; the process consumes roughly 1–2% of world energy production.
