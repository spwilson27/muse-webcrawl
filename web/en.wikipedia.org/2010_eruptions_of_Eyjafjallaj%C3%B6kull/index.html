2010 eruptions of Eyjafjallajökull

The 2010 eruptions of Eyjafjallajökull were a series of volcanic events at the Eyjafjallajökull ice cap in southern Iceland in the spring of 2010. A first, largely effusive flank eruption began on 20 March at the Fimmvörðuháls pass; a second, explosive eruption beneath the summit glacier began on 14 April and produced a huge ash cloud that shut down most of European airspace for a week, causing the largest disruption to air travel since the Second World War.

Fimmvörðuháls eruption

After months of earthquakes and ground deformation, a fissure about 500 metres long opened late on 20 March 2010 on the ice-free Fimmvörðuháls pass between Eyjafjallajökull and Mýrdalsjökull. Spectacular lava fountains and two new craters (later named Magni and Móði, after the sons of Thor) drew thousands of sightseers. This phase ended on 12 April and caused little disruption beyond local evacuations.

Summit eruption and ash cloud

On 14 April a new eruption began beneath the summit caldera ice. Meltwater flooded from the glacier (a jökulhlaup), destroying roads, and the interaction of magma with ice produced fine, glassy ash ejected up to 9 kilometres into the atmosphere. Prevailing winds carried the plume southeast over Europe. Because volcanic ash can melt inside jet engines and re-solidify on turbine blades, regulators closed airspace across northern and central Europe from 15 to 20 April; over 100,000 flights were cancelled and more than ten million passengers affected, with airlines losing an estimated US$200 million per day. Restrictions lingered into May, including brief closures around a Champions League semi-final travel scramble and the funeral-period travel of European leaders.

Aftermath

Explosive activity declined through May and the eruption was declared over in October 2010. The crisis led to reforms of ash-tolerance thresholds for aircraft engines and of European airspace management. In Iceland, floods and ashfall damaged farms south of the volcano, but the eruption also boosted tourism: the Fimmvörðuháls hiking route past the new craters became a popular trail, and the volcano's difficult name (roughly "AY-yah-fyat-lah-yer-kootl") became a global talking point.
