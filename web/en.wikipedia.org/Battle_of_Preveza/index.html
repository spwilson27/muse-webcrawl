The Battle of Preveza was a naval battle fought on 28 September 1538 off Preveza in northwestern Greece between the Ottoman fleet commanded by Hayreddin Barbarossa and the fleet of the Holy League — a Christian alliance assembled by Pope Paul III from papal, Venetian, Spanish, Genoese, Maltese and other contingents. Although the League's fleet was larger, its divided command and hesitant tactics allowed Barbarossa to defeat it, capturing or sinking some thirteen ships. The victory secured Ottoman naval supremacy in the Mediterranean for a generation, until the Battle of Lepanto in 1571.

Background

In 1537 Barbarossa, kapudan pasha (grand admiral) of Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent, had ravaged the Venetian possessions in the Aegean and Ionian seas, capturing numerous islands. In response, Pope Paul III organised a Holy League in February 1538. The combined fleet, commanded nominally by the papal admiral Marco Grimani with the imperial viceroy Ferrante Gonzaga and the Genoese admiral Andrea Doria commanding his own squadron in Spanish service, assembled at Corfu and moved against the Ottoman base at Preveza, at the mouth of the Ambracian Gulf, where Barbarossa lay with a smaller fleet.

The battle

After a failed landing attempt and weeks of delay, the League's fleet put to sea southward on 28 September; Barbarossa sallied out of the gulf and formed his galleys in a Y-shaped formation, wings forward. When the wind dropped, the League's great sailing ships and heavily armed Venetian galleys at the van were left isolated and were pounded by Ottoman galleys using their superior mobility, though the giant Venetian galleon of Alessandro Condalmiero repelled repeated attacks single-handed. Andrea Doria, commanding the main body, held off from the action for hours — whether from unfavourable wind, reluctance to hazard the Emperor's galleys, or unwillingness to aid Venice, then Genoa's rival, has been debated ever since. By the time he moved, the League's van was shattered and the fleet withdrew in disorder toward Corfu at nightfall, leaving Barbarossa master of the field.

Consequences

The Holy League dissolved amid recriminations. Venice, abandoned by its allies, made a separate peace in 1540, ceding its last Aegean and Peloponnesian possessions (including Monemvasia and Nauplia) and paying an indemnity. Ottoman fleets subsequently operated as far west as the French coast — Barbarossa wintered at Toulon in 1543–44 — and the balance of naval power in the Mediterranean remained Ottoman until Lepanto.
