Malabar Coast

The Malabar Coast runs along the southwestern shore of peninsular India.

The Malabar Coast is the southwestern coastline of the Indian subcontinent, running along the Arabian Sea through the present-day states of Karnataka and Kerala. Historically the term applied to the coast between the Western Ghats and the Arabian Sea from south of Goa to Kanyakumari at the peninsula's tip. The region is characterised by a narrow coastal plain, a chain of lagoons and backwaters, heavy monsoon rainfall and, historically, a flourishing trade in spices — above all black pepper.

Geography

The coast is a low-lying strip backed by the escarpment of the Western Ghats. Numerous short, fast-flowing rivers descend from the mountains, forming estuaries and the famous Kerala backwaters, a network of brackish lagoons and lakes linked by canals. The climate is tropical and humid, dominated by the southwest monsoon, which arrives first on this coast in early June.

History

The Malabar Coast has traded with the wider world since antiquity. Roman sources record ships sailing from the Red Sea to ports such as Muziris to buy pepper, and Pliny complained of the drain of Roman gold to India. Arab merchants later dominated the trade, and communities of Jews, Christians (the Saint Thomas Christians) and Muslims (the Mappila) took root in the port cities. In 1498 the Portuguese navigator Vasco da Gama reached Kozhikode (Calicut), opening the sea route from Europe to India and inaugurating centuries of European rivalry on the coast, involving the Portuguese, Dutch, French and British East India Companies. The Zamorin of Calicut, the rajas of Cochin and Travancore, and later Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan of Mysore contested control of the region before it came under British rule as the Malabar District of the Madras Presidency.

Economy and culture

Spices, coir, cashew, coconut products, handloom textiles and fishing remain important to the coastal economy, supplemented today by tourism centred on the backwaters and by remittances from workers in the Persian Gulf states. The coast's cosmopolitan history is reflected in its synagogues, mosques, churches and temples, and in cuisines blending local, Arab and European influences.
