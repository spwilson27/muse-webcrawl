Late Bronze Age collapse
Periodc. 1200–1150 BCE (with recovery extending into the 8th century BCE)
RegionEastern Mediterranean: Aegean, Anatolia, Levant, Cyprus, Egypt (survived, weakened)
Fallen powersHittite Empire, Mycenaean Greece, Kassite Babylonia (later), Ugarit, Cypriot kingdoms

The Late Bronze Age collapse was a period of societal collapse in the eastern Mediterranean between about 1200 and 1150 BCE, in which most of the great palace economies and empires of the Bronze Age — the Hittites, Mycenaean Greece, the kingdoms of Cyprus and Ugarit — disappeared within a few decades. Cities were burned and abandoned, long-distance trade and literacy collapsed across the Aegean and Anatolia, and the region entered a "dark age" from which new peoples and states — Phoenicians, Arameans, Philistines, Israelites, and eventually the Neo-Assyrian Empire — emerged.

Evidence

Destruction layers mark sites from Pylos and Mycenae to Hattusa and Ugarit; Ugarit's final letters, baked in the kiln-like heat of the burning city, speak of enemy ships and famine. Egypt under Merneptah and Ramesses III fought off invasions by coalitions remembered as the "Sea Peoples" — groups named in Egyptian inscriptions as the Sherden, Shekelesh, Peleset (generally identified with the Philistines) and others. Some of these raiders were probably as much symptom as cause: drought, attested in pollen and isotope records and in famine letters from Egypt's allies, internal revolt, and the disruption of the tin trade needed for bronze all appear in modern explanations.

Theories

Older accounts blamed single causes — invasion, earthquake storms, or iron technology undermining the bronze monopoly. The modern consensus, articulated by historians such as Robert Drews and Eric Cline (1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed), is one of "systems collapse": a tightly interconnected network of palace states, dependent on one another for grain, tin and copper, suffered a cascade of stresses — climate change and drought, earthquakes, migrations and internal rebellion — that no single state could absorb. Egypt survived but lost its empire; Assyria contracted but endured.

Aftermath

The collapse cleared the way for the Iron Age world: alphabetic writing spread through Phoenician trade, small territorial kingdoms replaced the great palaces, and ironworking — no longer dependent on imported tin — democratised metal tools and weapons.
