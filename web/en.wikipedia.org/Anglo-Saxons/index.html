Anglo-Saxons

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Anglo-Saxons were a cultural group who inhabited England in the Early Middle Ages, tracing their origins to Germanic settlers who arrived in Britain after the fall of the Western Roman Empire in the 5th century. They established the kingdoms of Anglo-Saxon England, spoke the language now called Old English, and formed the ethnic and political foundation of England before the Norman Conquest of 1066.

Origins

Written sources — Gildas, Bede and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle — describe the settlers as Angles, Saxons and Jutes from northern Germany and Denmark, invited or invading after Roman authority collapsed around 410. Archaeology shows furnished burials, cremation cemeteries and distinctive brooches spreading across eastern and southern Britain. Ancient DNA studies indicate substantial migration from the continental North Sea zone, mixed with intermarriage with the native British population, whose descendants contributed a significant share of the ancestry of the modern English.

Society and culture

Anglo-Saxon society was organised around kinship, lordship and the war-band (comitatus), celebrated in heroic poetry such as Beowulf. Settlement was rural, in villages of timber halls and sunken-featured buildings; kingship grew in power as kingdoms consolidated. Early Anglo-Saxon paganism worshipped gods such as Woden and Thunor, but from 597 the Gregorian mission and Irish monks converted the kingdoms to Christianity, bringing literacy, stone churches and monastic learning. Their material culture is famed for garnet jewellery, the Sutton Hoo ship burial, illuminated manuscripts and carved stone crosses; their writing used both the Latin alphabet and runes.

Legacy

From the Anglo-Saxons come the name of England ("land of the Angles"), the shire system, common law roots, many place names, and the core vocabulary and grammar of the English language. The unified kingdom they created — forged in resistance to Viking invasion under Alfred the Great and his heirs — endured until William the Conqueror's victory at Hastings transformed its ruling culture.
