Tammany Hall

Tammany Hall, also known as the Society of St. Tammany or the Columbian Order, was the Democratic Party political machine that dominated New York City (and influenced New York State) politics from the late 18th century into the 20th century. Named for Tamanend, a Lenape chief, the organization began in 1786 as a social and patriotic society and became the archetype of the urban political machine.

Rise to power

Tammany's power rested on its control of Democratic nominations, its network of district leaders and ward heelers, and its ability to mobilize immigrant voters — especially the Irish — by providing jobs, naturalization assistance, food, and other favours in exchange for loyalty. Aaron Burr used the society to support his political ambitions in the election of 1800, and by the mid-19th century Tammany effectively controlled city government.

The Tweed era

Under William M. "Boss" Tweed in the 1860s and early 1870s, Tammany's corruption reached legendary scale: the Tweed Ring embezzled sums estimated in the tens of millions of dollars, largely through inflated construction contracts such as the New York County Courthouse. Exposure by The New York Times and cartoons by Thomas Nast in Harper's Weekly led to Tweed's downfall and imprisonment.

Later years and decline

Later bosses such as Richard Croker and Charles Francis Murphy kept the machine powerful into the early 20th century, backing figures including governor and presidential nominee Al Smith. Tammany's influence waned with the rise of Fiorello La Guardia's reform administration, New Deal welfare programmes that undercut the machine's patronage role, and internal factionalism. It effectively ceased to be a major force by the 1960s.
