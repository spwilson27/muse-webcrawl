Dutch roll

Dutch roll is a type of aircraft motion consisting of an out-of-phase combination of "tail-wagging" (yaw) and rocking from side to side (roll). This yaw-roll coupling is one of the basic flight dynamic modes (others include phugoid, short period, and spiral divergence). The motion is typically damped well in most light aircraft, though some aircraft with well-damped Dutch roll modes can experience a degradation in damping as airspeed decreases and altitude increases.

Description

In Dutch roll, the aircraft's nose moves left and right while the wings rock in the opposite rhythm, so that the nose traces a roughly elliptical path as seen by the pilot. The instability is caused by the interaction between the dihedral effect (rolling moment due to sideslip) and directional (weathercock) stability. If the dihedral effect is strong relative to directional stability, a sideslip generates a large rolling moment, and the resulting bank then generates further sideslip, sustaining the oscillation.

Origin of the name

The name is said to derive from the motion's resemblance to the side-to-side swaying of a Dutch ice skater. The term has been used in aeronautics since at least the 1910s.

Yaw dampers

In swept-wing jet aircraft, Dutch roll can be lightly damped and uncomfortable or even hazardous, so such aircraft are fitted with yaw dampers: automatic systems that sense yaw rate and apply corrective rudder inputs. A failed yaw damper can restrict an airliner's operating envelope, as was relevant in accidents and incidents involving early jet transports.

Flight technique

Pilots are trained not to chase a Dutch roll with ailerons, which can aggravate the oscillation; the standard recovery is to allow the yaw damper to work or, in manual flight, to apply smooth rudder inputs in phase with the yaw to damp the motion.
