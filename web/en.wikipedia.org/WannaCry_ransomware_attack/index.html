WannaCry ransomware attack

The WannaCry ransomware attack was a worldwide cyberattack in May 2017 by the WannaCry ransomware cryptoworm, which targeted computers running the Microsoft Windows operating system by encrypting data and demanding ransom payments in the Bitcoin cryptocurrency. It is estimated to have infected more than 300,000 computers across 150 countries, with total damages ranging from hundreds of millions to billions of dollars.

Background

The worm propagated using EternalBlue, an exploit of the SMBv1 protocol that was developed by the United States National Security Agency and later leaked by the hacker group The Shadow Brokers. Microsoft had released a patch (MS17-010) in March 2017, but many systems remained unpatched. The attack began on Friday, 12 May 2017, and spread rapidly through networks of organisations that had not applied the fix or were running unsupported systems such as Windows XP.

Impact

Among the hardest hit was England’s National Health Service, where thousands of appointments were cancelled and some hospitals diverted ambulances. Other victims included Telefónica in Spain, FedEx, Deutsche Bahn, Renault and numerous universities and government agencies. The malware displayed a ransom note demanding $300 worth of Bitcoin per machine, doubling after three days.

Kill switch

The spread was dramatically slowed when British security researcher Marcus Hutchins registered an unregistered nonsense domain hard-coded into the malware; contacting that domain acted as a kill switch that halted the worm’s propagation on newly infected machines.

Attribution

The United States, United Kingdom and other governments attributed the attack to North Korea, linking the code to the Lazarus Group, a hacking organisation associated with the North Korean state. In 2018 the US Department of Justice charged a North Korean programmer, Park Jin-hyok, in connection with the attack.

Aftermath

The attack spurred renewed debate about stockpiling software vulnerabilities, the security of legacy systems in critical infrastructure, and the patching practices of large organisations. A related exploit, EternalBlue, was used again weeks later in the NotPetya attack.
