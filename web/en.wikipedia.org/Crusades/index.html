Crusades
Date1095–1291 (major expeditions to the Holy Land); crusading in other theatres continued into the 16th–17th centuries
LocationLevant and Anatolia; also Iberia, the Baltic, Egypt, and southern France
ResultInitial crusader states established in the Levant; eventual Muslim reconquest, completed with the fall of Acre (1291)
Key figuresPope Urban II; Godfrey of Bouillon; Richard I of England; Saladin; Frederick II; Louis IX

The Crusades were a series of religious wars initiated, supported, and sometimes directed by the Christian Church in the medieval period. The best known of these military expeditions are those to the Holy Land aimed at reclaiming Jerusalem and its surrounding territories from Muslim rule, between 1095 and 1291. Beginning with Pope Urban II's appeal at the Council of Clermont (1095) — answering a Byzantine request for aid against the Seljuk Turks — tens of thousands of western Europeans "took the cross", motivated by faith, the promise of indulgences, land, and adventure. Crusading was later extended to other fronts: the Iberian Reconquista, the Baltic (Northern Crusades), campaigns against heretics such as the Albigensians, and even against Christian political opponents of the papacy.

The major crusades to the Holy Land

  • First Crusade (1096–1099): after the disastrous "People's Crusade", the main armies captured Nicaea, won at Dorylaeum, endured the siege of Antioch, and stormed Jerusalem on 15 July 1099 amid a notorious massacre. Crusader states were founded: the Kingdom of Jerusalem, the County of Tripoli, the Principality of Antioch, and the County of Edessa.
  • Second Crusade (1147–1149): preached after the fall of Edessa (1144); the kings of France and Germany led expeditions that failed, notably at Damascus.
  • Third Crusade (1189–1192): responding to Saladin's victory at Hattin and capture of Jerusalem (1187). Frederick Barbarossa drowned en route; Richard I of England and Philip II of France recovered Acre and the coast but not Jerusalem; the Treaty of Jaffa secured Christian access for pilgrims.
  • Fourth Crusade (1202–1204): diverted first to Zara and then to Constantinople, which the crusaders sacked in 1204, establishing the Latin Empire — a scandal that deepened the schism between Latin and Greek Christianity.
  • Fifth Crusade (1217–1221): campaign in Egypt ending in defeat at Mansurah.
  • Sixth Crusade (1228–1229): Frederick II regained Jerusalem by diplomacy (Treaty of Jaffa, 1229); the city was lost again in 1244.
  • Seventh (1248–1254) and Eighth (1270) Crusades: led by Louis IX of France against Egypt and Tunis; both failed, Louis dying at Tunis.
  • Final years: the Mamluks under Baibars and his successors dismantled the remaining crusader strongholds; Acre, the last major outpost, fell in 1291.

Military orders and society

The crusades produced the military orders — the Knights Templar (founded c. 1119), the Knights Hospitaller, and the Teutonic Knights — warrior-monks who garrisoned castles such as Krak des Chevaliers and provided banking and logistics across Europe. The crusader states ("Outremer") were feudal societies in which a Latin Christian elite ruled Muslim, Jewish, and Eastern Christian majorities, with Italian maritime republics dominating trade. Contact between cultures transmitted technology, foods, learning, and artistic motifs, even amid violence that included massacres of Muslims, Jews (notably the Rhineland massacres of 1096), and Eastern Christians.

Legacy

The Crusades entrenched religious antagonisms and remain a potent — and often mythologized — reference point in both Western and Middle Eastern political memory. Historians debate their long-term economic and cultural effects, from the growth of Mediterranean trade and taxation systems in Europe to their role in the development of chivalric literature and the concept of holy war. Later "crusades" were preached into the early modern era, including the campaigns associated with the Ottoman advance in Europe.
