Rus' people

The Rus' were a people in early medieval Eastern Europe, generally identified with Norse (Varangian) traders and warriors from Scandinavia who settled among the East Slavs and gave their name to the polity of Kievan Rus', and ultimately to Russia and Belarus. The name appears in sources as Rus' in Slavonic, Rhos in Byzantine Greek, and Ruotsi in Finnish — the modern Finnish word for Sweden.

Origins

From the 8th century, Scandinavian merchants travelled the river routes of Eastern Europe — the route "from the Varangians to the Greeks" down the Dnieper to Constantinople, and the Volga route to the Caspian — trading furs, slaves, wax and amber for silver dirhams. The Russian Primary Chronicle recounts that in 862 the Slavic and Finnic tribes invited three Varangian brothers, led by Rurik, to rule over them, an account whose details are debated but which reflects the Scandinavian role in the region's early states.

Evidence and debate

Evidence for a Scandinavian element includes the Norse names of the first Rus' princes (Rurik, Oleg/Helgi, Igor/Ingvar, Olga/Helga), the names of the Dnieper rapids recorded by Constantine VII, Scandinavian-style burials and artefacts at sites such as Staraya Ladoga, Gnezdovo and Kiev, and the description of the "Rusiyyah" by the Arab traveller Ibn Fadlan. The extent of the Norse contribution to the Rus' state was the subject of the long-running "Normanist controversy" in Russian and Soviet historiography.

Legacy

By the 11th century the Rus' elite had largely Slavicised, adopting Orthodox Christianity after the conversion of Vladimir the Great in 988. The term Rus' came to denote the East Slavic lands and their people collectively, and survives in the names Russia, Belarus ("White Rus'") and in the adjective "Ruthenian".
