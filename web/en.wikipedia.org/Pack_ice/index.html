Pack ice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pack ice, also known as drift ice, consists of large pieces of sea ice — called floes — that are not attached to land and that drift with winds and ocean currents. It contrasts with fast ice, which is anchored to a shoreline, shoal, or grounded iceberg. Pack ice forms when individual floes freeze together into a continuous mass that may be moved as a whole by storms and tides, fracturing into open lanes of water (leads) and piling up into pressure ridges where floes collide.

Formation and structure

Sea ice begins as frazil and nilas in freezing seawater and thickens into floes over one or more winters. In the Arctic, multi-year ice that survives the summer melt was historically several metres thick, though the proportion of such old ice has declined markedly in recent decades. Floes range from metres to kilometres across; where they are driven together, broken slabs raft and ridge, some ridges extending many metres above and below the waterline. Gaps between floes — leads in winter, and melt ponds on the surface in summer — strongly affect how much sunlight the ice absorbs and how quickly it melts.

Distribution and movement

Pack ice covers much of the Arctic Ocean in winter and fringes Antarctica, where it is mostly seasonal first-year ice. Arctic pack circulates principally in the clockwise Beaufort Gyre and the Transpolar Drift, which carries ice from the Siberian coast across the pole toward Fram Strait — the route famously exploited by Nansen's Fram expedition (1893–1896) and later drifting stations. Satellite monitoring since 1979 has documented a long-term decline in Arctic ice extent and thickness, especially at the September minimum, with consequences for weather, ecosystems, and shipping.

Ecology and navigation

Pack ice is habitat: algae growing on its underside support krill, fish, seals, and in the Arctic, polar bears that hunt from the floes. Its seasonal advance and retreat structure the lives of Indigenous peoples of the circumpolar north. For shipping, pack ice historically blocked the Northwest and Northeast Passages; icebreakers can force channels through moderate pack, but converging, ridging ice under pressure remains capable of crushing or besetting vessels, as happened to Shackleton's Endurance in the Weddell Sea in 1915.
