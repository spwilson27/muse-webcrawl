Buddenbrooks, subtitled The Decline of a Family (Verfall einer Familie), is the first novel by German author Thomas Mann, published in 1901. It chronicles the fortunes of the Buddenbrooks, a wealthy merchant family of Lübeck, across four generations from 1835 to 1877, as their commercial vigour and social standing gradually give way to aesthetic sensitivity and decline.

Plot summary

The novel opens with a housewarming at the family's grand new home on Mengstrasse. Consul Johann Buddenbrook junior leads the grain-trading firm with bourgeois discipline; his son Thomas, capable but increasingly weary, sustains the business through force of will, while his brother Christian drifts into hypochondria and bohemian life, and their sister Tony endures two unhappy marriages contracted for the family's advantage. Thomas's son Hanno, a frail, musical child, embodies the family's turn from commerce to art; his early death from typhoid ends the line, and the great house is sold.

Background and reception

Mann drew heavily on his own family, the Manns of Lübeck, and on details of Hanseatic merchant life. The novel was a commercial and critical success and was the principal work cited when Mann received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1929. The Buddenbrook House in Lübeck is now a museum devoted to Mann and his family.
