Ivan Alexandrovich Serov (Russian: Ива́н Алекса́ндрович Серо́в; 13 August 1905 – 1 July 1990) was a Soviet intelligence officer who became the first chairman of the KGB (1954–1958) and later head of the GRU military intelligence (1958–1963). A close associate of Nikita Khrushchev, he had earlier been a senior NKVD officer deeply implicated in Stalin-era repression, including mass deportations and the Katyn massacre's aftermath.

Early career and wartime record

Serov joined the Red Army in 1923, trained as an artillery officer, and transferred to the NKVD in 1939. As deputy people's commissar of internal affairs of Ukraine under Khrushchev, and then of the USSR under Lavrentiy Beria, he organized the deportations of peoples from territories annexed in 1939–40 — Poles, and later the Baltic populations — and directed the deportation of entire nationalities from the North Caucasus and Crimea in 1944, operations in which hundreds of thousands were exiled and many died. After the German invasion he ran rear-security operations, and in 1945 he was the NKVD's chief representative in Germany, supervising the arrest and deportation of German specialists and the building of the East German security apparatus.

Chairman of the KGB

After Stalin's death and Beria's fall in 1953, Serov sided with Khrushchev and in March 1954 became the first chairman of the newly created KGB. In that role he oversaw the security side of de-Stalinization while protecting the institution's interests, and he directed the suppression of the Hungarian Revolution of 1956 on the ground in Budapest. His abrasive style and wartime record made him enemies, and in December 1958 Khrushchev moved him sideways to head the GRU.

Downfall

Serov's career collapsed with the Penkovsky affair: Oleg Penkovsky, a GRU colonel who spied for Britain and the United States, was arrested in October 1962, and Serov — his patron — was held responsible for the catastrophic failure of vetting. In 1963 he was dismissed, expelled from the Communist Party, stripped of his Hero of the Soviet Union title, and demoted in rank. He lived in obscurity in Moscow until his death in 1990; his memoirs, published posthumously decades later, attracted attention for their claims about wartime secrets.
