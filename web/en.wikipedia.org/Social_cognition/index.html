Social cognition

Social cognition is a topic within psychology that focuses on how people process, store, and apply information about other people and social situations. It focuses on the role that cognitive processes play in social interactions.

More technically, social cognition refers to how individuals think about and process information in social contexts. This includes how people perceive themselves and others, how they form impressions, and how they use schemas and heuristics to make judgments about the social world. The field emerged in the late 1970s and early 1980s and overlaps heavily with cognitive psychology and social psychology.

Historical development

Social cognition came to prominence with the rise of cognitive psychology in the late 1960s and early 1970s. Researchers such as Fritz Heider, who proposed balance theory and attribution theory, laid early groundwork. The publication of influential works on person perception and causal attribution in the 1970s helped establish social cognition as a distinct approach.

Key concepts

Schemas are cognitive structures that organise knowledge about social concepts, roles, and events, and guide the processing of new information. Heuristics, such as the availability heuristic and representativeness heuristic, are mental shortcuts people use when making social judgments. Attribution concerns how people explain the causes of behaviour, distinguishing internal (dispositional) from external (situational) causes; the fundamental attribution error describes the tendency to overemphasise dispositional explanations. Other central topics include attitudes, stereotyping, prejudice, self-concept, and theory of mind.

Culture and social cognition

Research comparing individualistic and collectivistic cultures has shown that cultural context shapes basic social-cognitive processes, including attention, causal attribution, and self-construal. Analytic thinking, more common in Western cultures, tends to focus on objects and their attributes, while holistic thinking, more common in East Asian cultures, attends more to context and relationships.
