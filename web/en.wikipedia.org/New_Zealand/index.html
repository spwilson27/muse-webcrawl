New Zealand (Māori: Aotearoa [aɔˈtɛaɾɔa]) is an island country in the southwestern Pacific Ocean. It consists of two main landmasses — the North Island (Te Ika-a-Māui) and the South Island (Te Waipounamu) — and over 700 smaller islands. It is the sixth-largest island country by area and lies east of Australia across the Tasman Sea and south of the islands of New Caledonia, Fiji, and Tonga. Its varied topography and sharp mountain peaks, including the Southern Alps, owe much to tectonic uplift and volcanic eruptions.

New Zealand was the last major habitable landmass to be settled by humans: Polynesian voyagers arrived between about 1280 and 1350 CE and developed the distinctive Māori culture. The first European to sight the country was Abel Tasman in 1642, and extensive contact followed James Cook's voyages from 1769. The Treaty of Waitangi in 1840 made New Zealand a British colony. It became a dominion in 1907 and adopted the Statute of Westminster in 1947.

History

After colonisation, Māori suffered land loss through purchase, confiscation during the New Zealand Wars, and the Native Land Court, while the settler population grew through immigration. New Zealand became the first country to grant women the vote (1893), pioneered the welfare state under the first Labour government (1935), and contributed forces to both world wars. From the 1970s, Māori protest and the Waitangi Tribunal process drove Treaty settlements and a Māori cultural renaissance. The country declared itself nuclear-free in 1987.

Geography and environment

The two main islands are separated by Cook Strait. The South Island is dominated by the Southern Alps, including Aoraki / Mount Cook (3,724 m); the North Island is more volcanic, with the Taupō Volcanic Zone, Lake Taupō, and active volcanoes such as Ruapehu and Whakaari / White Island. Long isolation produced unique biota: most native birds evolved without mammalian predators, and many are flightless (kiwi, kākāpō, moa — the last now extinct). Introduced predators remain the chief conservation threat, prompting the Predator Free 2050 programme.

Government and society

New Zealand is a unitary parliamentary constitutional monarchy with a unicameral House of Representatives elected by mixed-member proportional representation since 1996. It consistently ranks among the world's least corrupt and most democratic countries. The population of about 5.3 million is concentrated in the north; roughly 70% identify as European (Pākehā) and about 17–19% as Māori, with growing Asian and Pacific communities. The economy rests on agriculture (especially dairy), tourism, wine, film production, and services.
