Hawaiki

In Polynesian tradition, great voyaging canoes (waka) sailed from Hawaiki to new islands.

Hawaiki is the traditional ancestral homeland of many Polynesian peoples, from which — according to oral traditions — their ancestors voyaged in great double-hulled canoes to settle the islands of the Pacific, including New Zealand (Aotearoa), Hawaii, Rarotonga, and Tahiti. In Māori tradition, Hawaiki is also the place to which spirits of the dead return, departing from Te Rerenga Wairua (Cape Reinga) at the northern tip of New Zealand.

Traditions

Māori whakapapa (genealogies) recount the journeys of named waka (canoes) such as Tainui, Te Arawa, Mataatua, Kurahaupō, and Aotea from Hawaiki to Aotearoa. Hawaiian tradition similarly names Hawai'iloa and Mo'ikeha as voyagers from a southern homeland (Kahiki, i.e. Tahiti). In Samoan and Tongan belief, related concepts include Pulotu, the spirit world and ancestral home.

Identification

Scholars have long debated whether Hawaiki corresponds to a real place. Its name is cognate with Hawai'i, Savai'i (Samoa), and Havai'i, an old name for Ra'iātea in the Society Islands, which was a major centre of Polynesian religion with the great marae of Taputapuātea. Modern archaeology and linguistics trace the Polynesian homeland to the Tonga–Samoa region, from which voyagers expanded eastward within the last 3,000 years; "Hawaiki" is generally understood as a symbolic, ancestral name preserving memories of earlier homelands rather than a single island.

In culture

Hawaiki figures prominently in Māori proverb, song (waiata), and place names (for example Hawaiki in the Bay of Plenty). The phrase "Hawaiki nui, Hawaiki roa, Hawaiki pāmamao" — "great Hawaiki, long Hawaiki, distant Hawaiki" — expresses its mythic remoteness.
