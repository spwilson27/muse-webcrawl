Screen printing

Screen printing is a printing technique in which ink is forced through a mesh screen onto a substrate, except in areas made impermeable by a blocking stencil. It is also known as silk screening or serigraphy. Because the mesh can be laid on almost any surface, the process is used for posters, textiles and T-shirts, signage, labels, printed electronics and fine-art prints.

Process

A screen is made of fine mesh — historically silk, now usually polyester or nylon — stretched over a frame. A stencil is created on the mesh, commonly by coating it with a light-sensitive emulsion, exposing it through a film positive and washing away the unexposed areas, leaving open mesh in the shape of the image. The screen is placed on the substrate, ink is spread across it, and a rubber squeegee is drawn over the screen, pressing ink through the open areas. Each colour in a design requires its own screen, so multi-colour work proceeds in successive passes. A related tool, the rotary screen, allows continuous printing on fabric.

History

Stencil printing through mesh has roots in Song-dynasty China and was refined in Japan. The modern process developed in England and the United States in the early twentieth century, with key patents around 1907–1915, and spread into commercial graphics, wartime posters and the garment industry. In the 1960s, Pop artists such as Andy Warhol made screen printing a celebrated fine-art medium — his Marilyn Monroe and Campbell's Soup images were produced as silkscreens — giving the term "serigraph" currency for artistic editions.

Applications

Screen printing deposits a thicker layer of ink than most other processes, yielding vivid, durable colour, and it works on paper, fabric, glass, metal, plastic and wood. Industrial uses include printing circuit boards and solar cells, decorating bottles and packaging, and producing membrane switches. Its low setup cost for short runs keeps it popular for posters, merchandise and community print studios.
