Massive open online course

A massive open online course (MOOC) is an online course aimed at unlimited participation and open access via the Web. In addition to traditional course materials, such as filmed lectures, readings and problem sets, many MOOCs provide interactive courses with user forums or social media discussions to support community interactions among students, professors and teaching assistants, as well as immediate feedback to quick quizzes and assignments.

History

The term was coined in 2008 for a connectivist course run by Stephen Downes and George Siemens. MOOCs gained worldwide attention in 2012 — dubbed "the year of the MOOC" — when Stanford-linked ventures launched Coursera and Udacity, and Harvard and MIT founded edX. Early courses attracted enrolments in the hundreds of thousands; Stanford's 2011 artificial intelligence course drew some 160,000 students.

Models and pedagogy

Two broad types are distinguished: cMOOCs, based on connectivist, networked learning, and xMOOCs, based on recorded lectures, automated assessment and a more traditional instructional design. Courses typically run for several weeks with video segments, quizzes, peer-graded assignments and discussion boards. Certificates are usually available for a fee, and providers later developed paid sequences, microcredentials and full online degrees.

Criticism and outcomes

Completion rates are characteristically low — often under 10 per cent of enrolments — and participants are disproportionately already well educated. Critics questioned the pedagogy of recorded lectures and the business models of providers, while supporters point to unprecedented free access to university-level material for learners worldwide and to the role of MOOCs in professional development.
