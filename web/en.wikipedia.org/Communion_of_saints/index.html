Communion of saints

The communion of saints (Latin: communio sanctorum), when referred to persons, is the spiritual union of the members of the Christian Church, living and the dead, but excluding the damned. They are all part of a single "mystical body", with Christ as the head, in which each member contributes to the good of all and shares in the welfare of all. The phrase appears in the Apostles' Creed and is a core doctrine in Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Lutheran and Anglican theology, though interpreted differently across traditions.

Origins

The term communio sanctorum is first attested in the creeds of the fourth and fifth centuries. Interpretations of the Latin sanctorum vary: it may mean "the holy persons" (saints) or "the holy things" (the sacraments), and early commentators debated both readings. The doctrine expresses the belief that all Christians, living and dead, form one body in Christ and share in one another's spiritual goods.

Three states of the Church

Catholic theology distinguishes three states of the Church: the Church militant (the faithful on earth), the Church penitent or suffering (souls in purgatory), and the Church triumphant (the saints in heaven). The communion of saints links these states through prayer, intercession and the sharing of merits.

Views in various traditions

Roman Catholicism

The Catechism of the Catholic Church teaches that the communion of saints is precisely the Church, encompassing the faithful on earth, the souls in purgatory, and the blessed in heaven, all united in charity. Practices such as indulgences and prayers for the dead rest on this doctrine.

Eastern Orthodoxy

Orthodox theology affirms the unity of the Church across death, expressed in prayers for the departed and the veneration of saints, while generally avoiding scholastic definitions.

Protestantism

Lutherans and Anglicans retain the phrase in the Apostles' Creed, generally understanding it as the fellowship of believers united in one Church. Many other Protestants interpret it primarily as the community of Christians living and dead, without invocation of saints or purgatory.
