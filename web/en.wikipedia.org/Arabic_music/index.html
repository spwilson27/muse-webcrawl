Arabic music

Arabic music (Arabic: الموسيقى العربية, romanized: al-mūsīqā al-ʿarabīyya) is the music of the Arab world with all its diverse music styles and genres. Arabic music has a long history of interaction with many other regional musical styles and genres. It represents the music of all the peoples that make up the Arab world today, and of peoples who once lived in or influenced the region.

An oud, the central instrument of Arabic music

Characteristics

The melodic framework of Arabic music is the maqam system: modes built from scales containing characteristic intervals, including quarter tones, with customary melodic phrases and modulation practices. Rhythm is organized into rhythmic cycles called iqa'at (singular iqa'), patterns of beats sounded on frame drums and goblet drums. Vocal music predominates and melodic improvisation (taqsim instrumental, mawwal vocal) is prized. The typical ensemble, the takht, combines oud, qanun, nay, violin, and riq.

History

Pre-Islamic poetry was sung at courts and fairs; under the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates, music flourished in Baghdad and Córdoba, and theorists such as al-Kindi, al-Farabi, and Safi al-Din systematized the modal system, absorbing Persian and Greek influences. In the 19th and 20th centuries, Egypt became a center of recording and film music, producing stars such as Umm Kulthum, Mohammed Abdel Wahab, Abdel Halim Hafez, and Fairuz in Lebanon. Modern Arabic pop blends maqam melody with electronic production and Western harmony.

Instruments

Core instruments include the oud (short-neck lute), qanun (zither), nay (end-blown flute), rababa, violin (adopted and retuned), buzuq, and percussion such as the riq, daf, tabla (goblet drum), and katem. The accordion and electric organ were adapted in the 20th century to play quarter tones.
