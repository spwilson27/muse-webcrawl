Receptor tyrosine kinase

Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) are the high-affinity cell surface receptors for many polypeptide growth factors, cytokines, and hormones. Of the 90 unique tyrosine kinase genes identified in the human genome, 58 encode RTK proteins, divided into 20 subfamilies (classes). RTKs mediate essential processes including cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, and metabolism, and their dysregulation is strongly implicated in cancer.

Structure and mechanism

A typical RTK consists of an extracellular ligand-binding domain, a single transmembrane helix, and a cytoplasmic portion containing the tyrosine kinase domain flanked by regulatory regions. Ligand binding stabilises receptor dimers (or oligomers), enabling the kinase domains to trans-phosphorylate tyrosine residues in each other's activation loops and C-terminal tails. The phosphorylated tyrosines then serve as docking sites for SH2- and PTB-domain-containing signalling proteins such as Grb2, Shc, and PI3K, launching intracellular cascades.

Clinical significance

Mutations, amplifications, and chromosomal translocations of RTKs drive many cancers: HER2 amplification in breast cancer, EGFR mutations in lung cancer, and BCR-Abl-like fusions involving RTKs in leukaemias. RTKs are among the most successful drug targets in oncology, inhibited by monoclonal antibodies (e.g., trastuzumab) and small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g., imatinib for the related non-receptor kinase Abl, erlotinib and osimertinib for EGFR).
