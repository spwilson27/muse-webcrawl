CPT theorem

The CPT theorem states that any Lorentz invariant local quantum field theory with a Hermitian Hamiltonian must respect CPT symmetry: the combined symmetry of charge conjugation (C), parity transformation (P), and time reversal (T). The theorem was first derived by Julian Schwinger in 1951 and proved in greater generality by Wolfgang Pauli and others in the mid-1950s.

Content

Charge conjugation replaces particles with their antiparticles; parity reflects spatial coordinates through the origin; time reversal reverses the direction of time. Individually, each of these symmetries can be violated — parity violation was discovered in weak interactions in 1956, and CP violation was observed in 1964 — but the CPT theorem requires the combination of all three to be an exact symmetry of any theory satisfying its assumptions.

Consequences

A direct consequence of CPT invariance is that particles and antiparticles have exactly the same mass and lifetime, equal and opposite electric charge, and equal and opposite magnetic moments. Experimental tests of these predictions, for example comparisons of the proton and antiproton, are among the most precise tests of fundamental symmetries in physics.

CPT violation

A violation of CPT symmetry would imply a violation of Lorentz invariance, by a result known as the CPT theorem's converse (Greenberg's theorem). Searches for CPT violation are carried out in systems such as neutral kaon oscillations, antihydrogen spectroscopy, and neutrino experiments, and form part of broader tests of Lorentz symmetry in extensions of the Standard Model.
