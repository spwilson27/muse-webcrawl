Floater

Floaters are deposits of various sizes, shapes and consistency within the eye's vitreous humour. Known medically as muscae volitantes ("flying flies"), they appear as spots, threads, cobweb-like shapes or rings drifting in the field of vision, especially against bright uniform backgrounds such as a clear sky or a white screen. Floaters cast shadows on the retina and are perceived most clearly when the pupil is constricted and illumination is bright.

Causes

The vitreous is a clear gel of collagen and hyaluronic acid. With age it liquefies and shrinks (syneresis), and collagen fibres aggregate into visible strands. Often the vitreous detaches from the retina — a posterior vitreous detachment — which can release a ring-shaped floater (a Weiss ring) around the optic disc attachment. Floaters are more common and appear earlier in people with short sight (myopia). A sudden shower of floaters accompanied by flashes or a shadow in the peripheral vision can signal a retinal tear or detachment and needs urgent examination.

Management

Most floaters are harmless and the brain learns to ignore them; no treatment is recommended. For the minority whose vision is substantially impaired, options include pars plana vitrectomy to remove the vitreous, and laser vitreolysis (YAG laser), though both carry risks and their use for ordinary floaters remains debated among ophthalmologists.
