World federalism

World federalism is a political ideology advocating a democratic federal world government. Proponents argue that a system of global governance structured along federal lines — with authority divided between a world-level government and its constituent states, and powers limited to matters of genuinely global concern — is necessary to prevent war, manage global problems such as climate change and pandemics, and protect human rights.

History

The idea of a federated world has intellectual roots in Immanuel Kant's essay Perpetual Peace (1795) and, in antiquity, in Stoic cosmopolitanism and Dante's De Monarchia. Modern world federalism arose in the aftermath of the Second World War, when the devastation of war and the advent of nuclear weapons spurred movements such as the United World Federalists in the United States and parallel campaigns in Europe. The World Federalist Movement was founded in 1947 at Montreux, Switzerland.

Principles

World federalists typically support a written world constitution, a directly elected world parliament, an independent world judiciary with compulsory jurisdiction, and enforcement authority over individuals. The principle of subsidiarity reserves all other matters to national and local governments.

Criticism

Critics question the feasibility of world federation, point to the risk of global tyranny, and argue that cultural and political diversity makes democratic world government impractical. Others advocate functionalist or confederal alternatives, building global cooperation issue by issue rather than through a single constitutional settlement.
