Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976)

Rudolf Karl Bultmann (20 August 1884 – 30 July 1976) was a German Lutheran theologian and professor of the New Testament at the University of Marburg. He was one of the major figures of early-20th-century biblical studies. A prominent critic of liberal theology, Bultmann instead argued for an existentialist interpretation of the New Testament, developing a programme known as demythologization.

Life and career

Bultmann was born in Wiefelstede, the son of a Lutheran pastor. He studied theology at Tübingen, Berlin, and Marburg, where he came under the influence of liberal theologians such as Wilhelm Herrmann. After teaching at Breslau and Giessen, he returned to Marburg in 1921 as professor of New Testament studies, remaining there until his retirement in 1951. Among his students were Hans Jonas, Heinrich Schlier, Günther Bornkamm, Ernst Käsemann, and Ernst Fuchs, who together formed the so-called Bultmann school.

Form criticism

In The History of the Synoptic Tradition (1921), Bultmann applied form criticism to the Gospels, analysing the individual units of tradition — pronouncement stories, miracle stories, legends, and sayings — in terms of their function (Sitz im Leben) within the early Christian community. The work reshaped the study of the Synoptic Gospels for a generation.

Demythologization

In his famous 1941 essay "New Testament and Mythology", Bultmann argued that the mythical world picture of the New Testament — with its three-storied universe, miracles, and demonic powers — is no longer credible to modern people who use electric light and modern medicine. He proposed not eliminating the myths but interpreting them existentially, in terms of human self-understanding. His programme provoked intense controversy and helped set the agenda for post-war theology.

Later work

Bultmann's Theology of the New Testament (1948–53) and his commentary on the Gospel of John (1941) remain influential. Influenced by his Marburg colleague Martin Heidegger, Bultmann read the New Testament through categories of existential philosophy. His correspondence with Karl Barth, another giant of the era, documents their deep theological disagreements.
