Grand duchy

Luxembourg, the world's only remaining grand duchy

A grand duchy is a country or territory whose official head of state or ruler is a monarch bearing the title of grand duke or grand duchess. The title ranks below emperor and king but above a sovereign duke, and grand duchies were especially numerous in the German Confederation and the Russian Empire in the 19th century.

History

The term first appeared in western Europe in the late 16th century for Tuscany, elevated to a grand duchy in 1569. Napoleon created several grand duchies as client states, and the Congress of Vienna (1815) recognised a number of them — Baden, Hesse, Mecklenburg-Schwerin, Mecklenburg-Strelitz, Oldenburg, Saxe-Weimar, and Luxembourg — as members of the German Confederation. In eastern Europe, the Grand Duchy of Lithuania had been a major medieval state, while Finland and various Russian principalities (such as Tver and Moscow in its earlier period) also bore the grand ducal style.

Present day

Today the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg is the only remaining sovereign grand duchy in the world, ruled by Grand Duke Henri. It received the status in 1815, in personal union with the Netherlands until 1890, and is now an independent constitutional monarchy and a member state of the European Union.
