Consumerism

Consumerism is a social and economic order that encourages the acquisition of goods and services in ever-increasing amounts. The term is also used, in a second sense, for the movement seeking to protect and inform consumers — consumer protection. In the critical sense, consumerism describes the tendency of people to identify strongly with the products they consume, and the cultural ideology that consumption is central to happiness and social status.

History

Consumer societies emerged first in northwestern Europe in the 17th and 18th centuries alongside industrialization. Mass production, department stores, advertising and consumer credit expanded dramatically in the 20th century, particularly in the United States after the Second World War, when rising incomes and suburbanization transformed patterns of spending. Thorstein Veblen's 1899 concept of "conspicuous consumption" supplied an early critical analysis.

Criticism

Critics argue that consumerism promotes materialism, planned obsolescence, waste and environmental degradation, and that advertising manufactures desires rather than satisfying needs. Economists and sociologists such as John Kenneth Galbraith and Zygmunt Bauman examined the dependence of modern economies on sustained private consumption. Anti-consumerist responses include voluntary simplicity, the slow movement, Buy Nothing Day and degrowth.

Defences and alternative views

Defenders respond that consumption reflects individual freedom, rising living standards and the satisfaction of real preferences, and that markets respond to consumer demand for ethical and sustainable products. The second, movement sense of consumerism — exemplified by Ralph Nader's advocacy — treats informed, organized consumers as a counterweight to corporate power.
