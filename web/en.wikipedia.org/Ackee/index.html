The ackee (Blighia sapida), also known as the ankye, akee or akee apple, is a fruit of the Sapindaceae (soapberry) family, related to the lychee and longan. Native to tropical West Africa, it was brought to Jamaica in the late 18th century and became the island's national fruit. Cooked with saltfish, it forms ackee and saltfish, the national dish of Jamaica.

Ripe ackee fruit, opened naturally on the tree to reveal the edible yellow arils and glossy black seeds.

Description

The ackee is an evergreen tree growing about 10 metres tall with a short trunk and dense crown. Its pear-shaped fruit turns from green to bright red to yellow-orange as it matures and splits open to expose three large, shiny black seeds, each partly surrounded by soft, creamy yellow flesh — the aril, which is the edible portion.

History and cultivation

The species was described scientifically by Charles Konig in 1806 and named in honour of Captain William Bligh, who carried a specimen from Jamaica to the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, in 1793; the plant itself had reached Jamaica from West Africa, probably on a slave ship, around 1778. Ackee is now grown in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide, including West Africa, the Caribbean, Central America, Florida and Hawaii, and is canned commercially for export.

Toxicity

The arils are safe to eat only when the fruit has ripened fully and opened naturally on the tree. Unripe or overripe arils, and the seeds and membranes, contain the toxic amino acids hypoglycin A and hypoglycin B, which cause Jamaican vomiting sickness — characterised by vomiting, hypoglycaemia, seizures and, in severe cases, death. Because of the risk, imports of raw ackee into the United States were long restricted by the Food and Drug Administration, with only approved manufacturers permitted to import properly processed canned ackee.

Culinary use

In Jamaica the boiled arils are most famously sautéed with salted cod, onions, tomatoes and Scotch bonnet peppers as ackee and saltfish, traditionally served at breakfast with breadfruit, dumplings or festival. In West Africa the fruit is also eaten raw or in soups, and the seeds and pods have local uses in soap-making and fishing poisons.
