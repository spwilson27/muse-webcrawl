The Colossus of Rhodes (Ancient Greek: ὁ Κολοσσὸς Ῥόδιος) was a statue of the Greek sun god Helios, erected in the city of Rhodes, on the Greek island of the same name, by Chares of Lindos in 280 BC. One of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, it was constructed to celebrate the successful defence of Rhodes against an attack by Demetrius I of Macedon, who had besieged the city for a year with a large army and navy in 305 BC.

According to most contemporary descriptions, the statue itself was about 33 metres (108 feet) high — approximately the height of the modern Statue of Liberty from feet to crown — making it the tallest statue of the ancient world. It was built of bronze plates over an iron framework, funded by selling the equipment Demetrius's army had abandoned.

Destruction

The Colossus stood for only 56 years. In 226 BC an earthquake snapped the statue at the knees and toppled it. The Rhodians, following an oracle, declined to rebuild it, and the fallen sections lay on the ground for more than eight centuries; travellers reported that even lying broken, the thumb was so large that few men could encircle it with both arms. After the Arab conquest of Rhodes in the 7th century AD, the remains were reportedly sold as scrap bronze.

Legend and legacy

The familiar image of the statue bestriding the harbour entrance, with ships passing between its legs, is a medieval invention unsupported by ancient sources; the statue most likely stood on a breakwater or near the harbour on a single pedestal. The Colossus inspired Emma Lazarus's sonnet "The New Colossus" (1883), inscribed at the Statue of Liberty, and remains a symbol of Rhodes, appearing on Greek postage stamps and in popular depictions of the ancient wonders.
