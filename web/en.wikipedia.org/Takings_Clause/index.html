Takings Clause

The Takings Clause is the final clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution:

"…nor shall private property be taken for public use, without just compensation."

It limits the power of eminent domain by requiring that government may appropriate private property only for a public use and must pay just compensation. Originally applicable only to the federal government, the clause was held incorporated against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment in Chicago, Burlington & Quincy Railroad Co. v. Chicago (1897).

Physical takings

Where government physically appropriates or permanently occupies property — however minor the intrusion — compensation is generally required as a categorical ("per se") rule. In Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp. (1982), a cable box and wires installed under state law were held a taking; in Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid (2021) the Court extended the per se rule to regulations granting third parties a right of access for limited periods.

Regulatory takings

A regulation may also effect a taking even though title remains with the owner. The modern framework derives from Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City (1978), which weighs (1) the economic impact on the claimant, (2) the extent of interference with distinct investment-backed expectations, and (3) the character of the governmental action. In Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council (1992), the Court held that a regulation depriving an owner of all economically beneficial use is a categorical taking unless the restriction inheres in background principles of state property or nuisance law.

Public use

The "public use" requirement has been read broadly as "public purpose". In Berman v. Parker (1954) and Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff (1984) the Court deferred strongly to legislatures, and in Kelo v. City of New London (2005) it upheld the taking of homes for a private economic-development project — a controversial 5–4 decision that prompted many states to tighten their own eminent-domain laws.

Just compensation

Just compensation is generally the fair market value of the property taken at the time of the taking. Owners may sue for compensation through an inverse condemnation action; in Knick v. Township of Scott (2019) the Supreme Court held that plaintiffs need not exhaust state remedies before bringing a federal takings claim.
