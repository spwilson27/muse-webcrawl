Anchoring

Anchoring (or anchoring bias, and the related focalism) is a cognitive bias in which an individual's decisions are influenced by a particular reference point, the "anchor". When making estimates, people start from an initial anchor value and adjust from it, but adjustments are usually insufficient, so final estimates remain biased toward the anchor — even when the anchor is arbitrary or obviously irrelevant.

Original research

The effect was demonstrated by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in 1974. In one experiment, participants watched a rigged wheel of fortune land on either 10 or 65, then estimated the percentage of African nations in the United Nations. Median estimates were 25% and 45% respectively — participants' answers were dragged toward the random anchor.

Robustness

Anchoring persists among experts (judges sentencing after rolling dice, experienced negotiators, real-estate agents shown a listing price), resists monetary incentives for accuracy, and survives explicit warnings. Explanations include insufficient adjustment, selective accessibility of anchor-consistent information, and numeric priming.

Applications

Anchoring is exploited in marketing (strikethrough "list prices"), negotiation (first offers pull settlements toward them), and law (damages requests anchor awards). Awareness is the main defence: deliberately considering the opposite value or independently generating an estimate before hearing one can reduce the effect.
