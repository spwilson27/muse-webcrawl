In aviation, a transponder (short for transmitter-responder) is an electronic device that produces a response when it receives a radio-frequency interrogation. Aircraft have transponders to assist in identifying them on air traffic control radar. Collision avoidance systems have been developed to use transponder transmissions as a means of detecting aircraft at risk of colliding with each other.

Operation

Air traffic control (ATC) units use the term "squawk" when they assign an aircraft a transponder code, for example "Squawk 7421". The pilot selects the four-digit octal code on the transponder panel. Ground-based secondary surveillance radar (SSR) interrogates the transponder on 1030 MHz, and the transponder replies on 1090 MHz with the selected code and, depending on mode, additional data.

Modes

  • Mode A transmits only the four-digit squawk code identifying the aircraft.
  • Mode C additionally transmits the aircraft's pressure altitude, encoded from an encoding altimeter or air data computer.
  • Mode S provides selective interrogation using a unique 24-bit ICAO address, data-link capability, and transmission of a wider range of flight parameters. Mode S transponders also support ADS-B extended squitter transmissions.

Special codes

Certain squawk codes have standard meanings worldwide: 7500 indicates unlawful interference (hijacking), 7600 indicates radio communication failure, and 7700 indicates a general emergency. Code 2000 is commonly used for aircraft that have not received a specific code in some regions, and 1200 is the standard visual flight rules code in the United States.

Collision avoidance

The Traffic Collision Avoidance System (TCAS) interrogates the transponders of nearby aircraft to determine their range, altitude, and bearing, and issues traffic advisories and resolution advisories to flight crews. TCAS II requires Mode C or Mode S transponders and is mandated for most commercial airliners.

History

Aircraft transponders developed from the World War II identification friend or foe (IFF) systems. Civil secondary radar and standardized modes were adopted internationally through ICAO in the postwar decades, with Mode S introduced to overcome the limitations of garbling and limited code capacity in dense airspace.
