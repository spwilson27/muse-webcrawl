Chandra X-ray Observatory

The Chandra X-ray Observatory (CXO), previously known as the Advanced X-ray Astrophysics Facility (AXAF), is a Flagship-class space telescope launched aboard the Space Shuttle Columbia during STS-93 by NASA on July 23, 1999. Chandra is sensitive to X-ray sources 100 times fainter than any previous X-ray telescope, enabled by the highest angular resolution of any X-ray telescope to date (about 0.5 arcseconds). Since the Earth's atmosphere absorbs the vast majority of X-rays, they are not detectable from Earth-based telescopes; therefore space-based telescopes are required to make these observations. Chandra is named after the Nobel Prize-winning Indian-American astrophysicist Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar.

Mission and design

Chandra was proposed in 1976 by Riccardo Giacconi and Harvey Tananbaum; developed over two decades, it is the third of NASA's Great Observatories, after Hubble and the Compton Gamma Ray Observatory. Its mirror assembly — four pairs of nested paraboloid/hyperboloid mirrors polished to extraordinary smoothness — focuses X-rays by grazing incidence onto two focal-plane cameras: the Advanced CCD Imaging Spectrometer (ACIS) and the High Resolution Camera (HRC), with two transmission grating spectrometers (HETGS and LETGS) for high-resolution spectroscopy. A solid-rocket Inertial Upper Stage placed Chandra in a highly elliptical orbit taking it a third of the way to the Moon, allowing long uninterrupted observations of up to ~55 hours. Operations are run by the Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory's Chandra X-ray Center in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Designed for a five-year life, Chandra remains productive after more than 25 years, its lifetime extended by careful management of radiation damage and contamination on ACIS.

Scientific results

Chandra's sharp images have transformed high-energy astrophysics: resolving the X-ray glow of the Crab Nebula's pulsar wind; imaging shock fronts and "cold fronts" in merging galaxy clusters and the Bullet Cluster, whose offset between hot gas and gravitational lensing mass is key evidence for dark matter; resolving thousands of point sources in deep fields (the Chandra Deep Fields) dominated by distant active galactic nuclei; measuring the expansion of supernova remnants such as Cassiopeia A and Eta Carinae's colliding winds; showing that the supermassive black hole Sagittarius A* is remarkably faint, and mapping hot gas in galaxies, clusters and the cosmic web. In 2016 Chandra data supported the detection of a possible X-ray signature of dark-matter particle decay (the debated 3.5 keV line).

Name

The observatory was renamed in 1998 after a contest: "Chandra" honours Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar (1910–1995), famed for the Chandrasekhar limit for white dwarfs; the word also means "moon" or "luminous" in Sanskrit — see Chandra, the Hindu moon god.
