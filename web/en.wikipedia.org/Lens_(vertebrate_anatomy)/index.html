Lens (vertebrate anatomy)

The lens, or crystalline lens, is a transparent biconvex structure in the eye that, along with the cornea, helps to refract light to be focused on the retina. In humans, by changing shape, it functions to change the focal length of the eye so that it can focus on objects at various distances, thus allowing a sharp real image of the object of interest to be formed on the retina. This adjustment of the lens is known as accommodation. Accommodation is similar to the focusing of a photographic camera via movement of its lenses. The lens is more flat on its anterior side than on its posterior side.

Structure

The lens is part of the anterior segment of the human eye. In front of the lens is the iris, which regulates the amount of light entering into the eye. The lens is suspended in place by the suspensory ligaments of the lens (zonule of Zinn), which attach to the lens near its equatorial line and connect the lens to the ciliary body. Behind the lens is the vitreous body. The lens has three main parts: the lens capsule, the lens epithelium, and the lens fibres. The lens capsule forms the outermost layer, and the lens fibres form the bulk of the interior of the lens. The cells of the lens epithelium, located between the lens capsule and the outermost layer of lens fibres, are found only on the anterior side of the lens.

Development and growth

The lens develops from surface ectoderm during embryonic development, forming first the lens placode and then the lens vesicle. Uniquely among organs, the lens never sheds its cells: new fibres are added at the equator throughout life, laid down in concentric layers over the old, so that the oldest fibres, formed before birth, persist at the centre (the lens nucleus). Because the lens has no blood supply or innervation after development, it must obtain nutrients from the surrounding aqueous humour.

Function

The lens provides roughly one-third of the eye's refractive power (about 20 dioptres in humans; the cornea provides the rest). Its most important dynamic role is accommodation: contraction of the ciliary muscle releases zonular tension, allowing the elastic lens to round up, increasing its optical power for near vision. With age the lens hardens and accommodation is lost, a condition called presbyopia.

Clinical significance

Opacification of the lens is called a cataract, the leading cause of blindness worldwide; it is treated by surgical removal of the lens and replacement with an artificial intraocular lens. Dislocation of the lens (ectopia lentis) can occur in trauma and in connective-tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome. Aphakia is the absence of the lens, and pseudophakia denotes an eye bearing an artificial lens.
