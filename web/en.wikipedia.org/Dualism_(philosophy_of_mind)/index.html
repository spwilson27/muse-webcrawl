Dualism (philosophy of mind)

Dualism in the philosophy of mind is the family of views which hold that the mental and the physical are fundamentally different kinds of things, and that mental phenomena are, in some respects, non-physical. It is contrasted with monism, which holds that reality is ultimately of one kind, and with physicalism, which holds that everything is physical.

Cartesian substance dualism

The most famous formulation is René Descartes' substance dualism: reality is divided into res cogitans (thinking substance, the mind or soul) and res extensa (extended substance, matter). For Descartes, the mind is indivisible and defined by thought, while body is divisible and defined by extension. He proposed that the two interact in the pineal gland. Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia pressed the classic objection: it is unclear how an immaterial substance could cause changes in a material one — the interaction problem.

Varieties

Property dualism holds that there is only one kind of substance but that mental properties are irreducible to physical ones; David Chalmers has defended a form of this view. Epiphenomenalism accepts property dualism but denies that mental states cause physical effects. Parallelism and occasionalism (associated with Leibniz and Malebranche) avoid interaction by treating mental and physical sequences as coordinated without direct causal contact.

Arguments and criticism

Arguments for dualism appeal to the conceivability of minds without bodies, the privacy and qualia of conscious experience, and the apparent gap between physical description and subjective awareness. Critics respond with the interaction problem, the causal closure of physics, neuroscience's evidence of dependence of mind on brain, and parsimony. Gilbert Ryle attacked Cartesian dualism as a "category mistake" — the myth of the "ghost in the machine".
