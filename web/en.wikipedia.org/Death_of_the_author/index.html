Death of the author

"The Death of the Author" (French: La mort de l'auteur) is a 1967 essay by the French literary critic and theorist Roland Barthes. It argues against the practice of incorporating the intentions and biographical context of an author in an interpretation of a text, asserting instead that meaning is produced by the reader: "the birth of the reader must be at the cost of the death of the Author."

Argument

Barthes attacked the traditional view — descending from positivist literary history — that the author is the origin and final authority on a text's meaning. Drawing on Mallarmé, surrealism, and linguistics, he contended that writing is a "tissue of quotations" drawn from innumerable cultural sources, and that the text's unity lies not in its origin but in its destination, the reader. The essay became a landmark of post-structuralism and is frequently paired with Michel Foucault's 1969 response, "What Is an Author?"

Criticism

Critics have argued that anti-authorial theory ignores the social and material conditions of writing, erases minority voices for whom claiming authorship is politically significant, and is self-refuting — Barthes's own name and authority anchor readings of the essay. Others note that in fields such as law, history, and fan studies, the phrase is invoked loosely for the idea that texts escape their creators' control.
