Masonry

Masonry is the craft of building structures from individual units — brick, stone, concrete block, or glass block — laid in courses and bound together with mortar. It is among the oldest construction methods in the world, used for the pyramids of Egypt, the Great Wall of China, Roman aqueducts, Gothic cathedrals, and countless ordinary walls and buildings.

Materials and technique

Masonry units are strong in compression but weak in tension, so traditional masonry relies on mass, arches, vaults, and domes to carry loads. Modern practice adds steel reinforcement, cavity walls, and veneer construction over timber or steel frames. Mortar, typically a mixture of cement, lime, sand, and water, beds the units and accommodates minor movement; joints are tooled for weather resistance.

Types

Common forms include brickwork (in patterns or bonds such as stretcher, Flemish, and English bond), ashlar and rubble stonework, and concrete masonry units (CMU). Skilled masons also build fireplaces, chimneys, and retaining walls. Properly built masonry is durable, fire-resistant, and thermally massive, but labor-intensive compared with frame construction.
