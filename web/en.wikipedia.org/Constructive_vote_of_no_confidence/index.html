Constructive vote of no confidence

A constructive vote of no confidence (German: konstruktives Misstrauensvotum) is a no-confidence vote in a parliamentary system in which the legislature can dismiss the head of government only by simultaneously electing a successor. The mechanism aims to prevent the instability that arises when a majority can agree only on removing a government but not on replacing it.

Origin

The device was introduced in the West German Basic Law of 1949 (Article 67), a reaction to the experience of the Weimar Republic, where negative majorities repeatedly toppled chancellors without forming stable replacements. The Bundestag may express a lack of confidence in the Federal Chancellor only by electing a successor with an absolute majority.

Use in practice

Only two constructive votes have been attempted against German chancellors: a failed attempt against Willy Brandt in 1972 and the successful 1982 vote that replaced Helmut Schmidt with Helmut Kohl. Spain adopted the same mechanism in its 1978 constitution; it was used successfully in 2018, when Pedro Sánchez replaced Mariano Rajoy. Variants exist in Belgium, Hungary, Poland, Slovenia, Albania, Lesotho and several German Länder.

Assessment

Political scientists credit the constructive vote with much of post-war German governmental stability, since opposition parties must unite behind an alternative candidate before they can bring a government down.
