Scrimshaw

Scrimshaw is scrollwork, engravings and carvings done in bone or ivory, most famously by whalers on the teeth and bones of sperm whales. The practice flourished on American and British whaling ships in the nineteenth century, when sailors passed long voyages by engraving pictures — ships, whales, portraits and scenes of home — into whale teeth, rubbing ink or soot into the incised lines to make the image visible.

Technique and objects

The surface of the tooth or bone was polished smooth, the design drawn or transferred, then scratched in with a sail needle or knife, and pigment applied. Besides decorated teeth, scrimshanders produced practical objects such as pie crimpers, corset busks, swifts for winding yarn, and boxes, often as gifts for family at home.

Modern status

Because of restrictions on trade in ivory and marine-mammal products under laws such as the US Marine Mammal Protection Act and CITES, modern scrimshaw is done on legal materials or substitutes, while antique scrimshaw is a valuable collectible field documented by museums such as the New Bedford Whaling Museum.
