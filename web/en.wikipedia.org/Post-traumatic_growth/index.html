Post-traumatic growth

Post-traumatic growth (PTG) is positive psychological change experienced as a result of struggling with highly challenging, highly stressful life circumstances. The concept was developed in the mid-1990s by psychologists Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun at the University of North Carolina at Charlotte. It is distinct from resilience: growth involves transformation beyond the previous level of functioning, not merely recovery.

Domains

Tedeschi and Calhoun measure growth with the Posttraumatic Growth Inventory across five domains: greater appreciation of life, improved relationships with others, increased personal strength, recognition of new possibilities, and spiritual or existential change.

Process

Growth is thought to arise from the cognitive work of rebuilding assumptions about the world shattered by trauma — through deliberate rumination, disclosure and narrative reconstruction, often supported by others. PTG can coexist with continuing distress and post-traumatic stress symptoms; it does not imply that trauma is desirable.

Criticism

Researchers debate whether self-reported growth reflects genuine change or positive illusion and coping appraisal, and whether retrospective self-report measures are valid. The field continues to develop behavioural and longitudinal methods.
