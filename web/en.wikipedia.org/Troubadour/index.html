Troubadour

A troubadour (Occitan: trobador) was a composer and performer of Old Occitan lyric poetry during the High Middle Ages (1100–1350). The tradition flourished in Occitania (southern France) and spread to northern Italy, Catalonia, and northern France, where its counterparts were the trouvères (writing in Old French) and the German Minnesänger. Troubadour song was generally monophonic and secular, performed to instrumental accompaniment, and most famously celebrated fin'amor — courtly love.

History

The earliest known troubadour was William IX, Duke of Aquitaine (1071–1126). The tradition reached its height in the 12th and early 13th centuries with figures such as Bernart de Ventadorn, Arnaut Daniel (praised by Dante), Bertran de Born, and the Comtessa de Dia, one of about twenty known female troubadours (trobairitz). The Albigensian Crusade (from 1209) devastated the Occitan courts that patronized the poets, dispersing the tradition into Italy and Iberia.

Genres and themes

Troubadour genres include the canso (love song), the sirventes (political or moral song), the tenso (debate poem), the alba (dawn song of parting lovers), and the planh (lament). Their verse used intricate rhyme schemes and stanza forms; later manuscripts (chansonniers) preserve some 2,500 poems and about 250 melodies. Imagined biographies of the poets, called vidas, accompany many songs.

Influence

Troubadour lyric shaped the entire European love-poetry tradition, influencing Dante and Petrarch, the Sicilian School, and concepts of romantic love in Western culture. Their work survives through chansonniers compiled mostly in Italy and Catalonia.
