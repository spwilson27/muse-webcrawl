Immunoglobulin

An immunoglobulin is an antibody: a large, Y-shaped protein produced mainly by plasma cells of the immune system to identify and neutralise foreign objects such as bacteria and viruses. "Immunoglobulin" and "antibody" are generally used interchangeably, with immunoglobulin often denoting the molecule as a structural and genetic class, including the membrane-bound antigen receptors of B cells.

Classes

Humans have five classes (isotypes) of immunoglobulin, named for their heavy chains: IgG, the most abundant in blood and the main antibody of secondary immune responses; IgM, the first antibody produced and a pentamer in its secreted form; IgA, dominant in mucosal secretions and breast milk; IgE, involved in allergy and defence against parasites; and IgD, which functions mainly as a B-cell receptor.

Structure

Each immunoglobulin consists of two heavy chains and two light chains linked by disulfide bonds. The tips of the Y (the Fab regions) contain variable domains that bind a specific antigen, while the stem (the Fc region) interacts with immune cells and complement. Enormous diversity of antigen binding is generated by V(D)J recombination and somatic hypermutation of the immunoglobulin genes.
