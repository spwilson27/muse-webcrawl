Bible translations into English

The Bible has been translated into English many times, beginning in the Middle Ages. Early partial translations into Old English were followed by John Wycliffe's complete Middle English version in the 1380s. The invention of printing transformed distribution of the English Bible, and translations such as those of William Tyndale and the King James Version profoundly shaped the English language.

Medieval translations

Portions of Scripture were rendered into Old English by figures such as Bede and in the Lindisfarne Gospels' interlinear gloss. Wycliffe's Bible, produced by John Wycliffe and his followers, was the first complete English translation, made from the Latin Vulgate, and circulated in manuscript despite official opposition.

Early modern translations

William Tyndale's New Testament (1526) was the first printed English Bible translated from Greek; Tyndale was executed in 1536. Miles Coverdale produced the first complete printed English Bible in 1535. The Great Bible (1539) was authorised for use in churches, followed by the Geneva Bible (1560), popular with Puritans, and the Bishops' Bible (1568). The King James Version (1611), commissioned by James I, became the dominant English Bible for centuries and a landmark of English literature.

Modern translations

Discoveries of older manuscripts and advances in scholarship prompted the Revised Version (1881–85) and many twentieth-century translations, including the Revised Standard Version, the New International Version, the New Revised Standard Version, and the English Standard Version. Modern versions differ in philosophy, from formal equivalence ("word for word") to dynamic equivalence ("thought for thought").
