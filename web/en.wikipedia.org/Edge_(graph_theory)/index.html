Edge (graph theory)

[Image: images/Edge_(graph_theory).png — A graph with six vertices and seven edges]

In mathematics, and more specifically in graph theory, an edge is a connection between two vertices of a graph. A graph is defined as an ordered pair G = (V, E) comprising a set V of vertices (or nodes) together with a set E of edges, which are 2-element subsets of V (in an undirected graph) or ordered pairs of vertices (in a directed graph, where edges are often called arcs or arrows).

Definitions

An edge {x, y} is said to join x and y; the vertices x and y are its endpoints (or end vertices) and are said to be incident with the edge and adjacent to one another. An edge joining a vertex to itself is a loop. Two or more edges with the same endpoints are multiple edges (or parallel edges); a graph with neither loops nor multiple edges is a simple graph. The number of edges incident to a vertex is its degree.

Types of edges

In a directed graph, an edge (x, y) is directed from x to y; x is its tail and y its head. Edges may carry additional structure: a weighted graph assigns a number (weight) to each edge, a common model for distances, costs or capacities in network problems. In a hypergraph, edges (hyperedges) may join any number of vertices. Special classes include bridges, whose removal disconnects the graph, and the edges of trees and spanning subgraphs.

Representations

Edges are stored computationally in adjacency lists, adjacency matrices or incidence matrices. The set of edges determines connectivity, paths and cycles — central objects in network analysis, computer science and combinatorics.
