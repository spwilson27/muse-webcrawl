Nonviolent resistance

Other namesNonviolent action, civil resistance
Key figuresMohandas Gandhi, Martin Luther King Jr., Gene Sharp
MethodsProtests, strikes, boycotts, civil disobedience, non-cooperation
Notable campaignsIndian independence movement, U.S. civil rights movement, Solidarity (Poland)

Nonviolent resistance (NVR), also called nonviolent action or civil resistance, is the practice of achieving goals — such as social or political change — through symbolic protests, civil disobedience, economic or political non-cooperation, satyagraha, constructive program, or other methods, while refraining from violence and the threat of violence. This type of action highlights the desires of an individual or group that feel something needs to change to improve the current condition of the resisting person or group.

Nonviolent resistance is often associated with the movements led by Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi in India, by Martin Luther King Jr. and James Bevel in the United States, and by Lech Wałęsa's Solidarity movement in Poland. Research by political scientists such as Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan has suggested that, in the twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, nonviolent campaigns seeking regime change succeeded more often than violent ones.

History

Although the term and systematic theory are modern, examples of nonviolent action are ancient. In the fifth century BCE, the Roman plebeians repeatedly withdrew from the city in the secessio plebis, securing political concessions without bloodshed. In the nineteenth century, labour movements adopted the strike and the boycott — the latter named after Captain Charles Boycott, ostracised by Irish tenants in 1880.

Gandhi developed the philosophy of satyagraha ("truth force") in South Africa and India, culminating in campaigns such as the Salt March of 1930. Gandhi in turn drew on Leo Tolstoy, Henry David Thoreau's essay "Civil Disobedience", and religious traditions including Jainism and Hinduism. In the United States, the civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s used sit-ins, freedom rides, and mass marches to dismantle legal segregation.

Methods

The political scientist Gene Sharp catalogued 198 methods of nonviolent action, grouped into three classes: protest and persuasion (marches, vigils, leafleting), non-cooperation (strikes, boycotts, civil disobedience), and nonviolent intervention (sit-ins, blockades, alternative institutions). Sharp argued that political power ultimately rests on the consent and obedience of subjects, which can be withdrawn.

Effectiveness

Chenoweth and Stephan's study of 323 campaigns between 1900 and 2006 found nonviolent campaigns achieved their objectives in roughly 53 percent of cases, compared with about 26 percent for violent campaigns. Chenoweth also popularised the observation that every campaign in the dataset that mobilised at least 3.5 percent of the population in sustained participation succeeded, though this is a descriptive finding rather than a rule.
