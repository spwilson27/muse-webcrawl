The Egyptian pyramids are ancient masonry structures located in Egypt. Sources cite at least 118 identified pyramids. Most were built as tombs for the pharaohs and their consorts during the Old and Middle Kingdom periods. The best known are those at Giza, on the outskirts of Cairo, including the Great Pyramid — the only surviving wonder of the ancient world.

Historical development

Pyramid building evolved from flat-roofed mastaba tombs. During the Third Dynasty, the Step Pyramid of Djoser at Saqqara (c. 2670 BC), designed by the architect Imhotep, stacked six mastabas into a stepped structure. Sneferu's experiments in the Fourth Dynasty — the Meidum, Bent, and Red pyramids — led to the first true smooth-sided pyramid, a form perfected at Giza by Khufu, Khafre, and Menkaure.

Construction

Pyramids were built of limestone and granite blocks quarried locally or transported along the Nile, encased in fine white Tura limestone. Modern research favors construction by paid Egyptian laborers working in rotating crews rather than slaves, aided by ramps, levers, and sledges lubricated with water. The recently discovered diary of an official named Merer records the transport of casing stones to Khufu's pyramid by boat.

Symbolism and later history

The pyramid shape is thought to evoke the primeval mound of creation and the rays of the sun, linking the deceased king with the sun god Ra. Pyramid building declined after the Old Kingdom but revived in the Middle Kingdom and later in Nubia, where Sudanese pyramids outnumber Egypt's. The Giza necropolis is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the world's most famous archaeological landmarks. See also Egyptology.
