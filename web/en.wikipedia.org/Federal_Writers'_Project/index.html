Federal Writers' Project

The Federal Writers' Project (FWP) was a United States federal government project created in 1935 under the Works Progress Administration's Federal Project Number One to employ unemployed writers, editors, historians, and researchers during the Great Depression. Under national director Henry Alsberg, it employed more than 6,600 people at its height and produced hundreds of publications.

The American Guide Series

The project's best-known undertaking was the American Guide Series: guidebooks for every state, plus many cities and regions, combining driving tours with essays on history, geology, industry, and local culture. Guides to states such as Idaho, Washington, and Massachusetts were among the first published; the series is still regarded as a landmark of American regional writing and a rich snapshot of the country in the 1930s.

Slave narratives and folklore

The FWP also conducted thousands of oral-history interviews. Its most famous legacy is the Slave Narrative Collection: more than 2,300 interviews with formerly enslaved people, conducted mainly in 1936–1938, now held by the Library of Congress as the "Born in Slavery" collection. Project workers also collected "life histories" of ordinary Americans and folklore from ethnic communities across the country.

People

Among the writers who worked for the project were Richard Wright, Zora Neale Hurston, Ralph Ellison, Saul Bellow, John Cheever, Studs Terkel, May Swenson, and Kenneth Rexroth — some as field interviewers and folklorists, others as editors in state offices.

End and legacy

Like other Federal One projects, the FWP came under attack in Congress for allegedly left-wing sympathies. Federal funding ended in 1939 and the project continued in reduced, state-sponsored form until 1943. Its guidebooks, interviews, and folklore collections endure as foundational documentation of American life during the Depression era.
