Cuprate superconductor

Cuprate superconductors are a family of high-temperature superconducting materials made of layers of copper oxide (CuO2) planes separated by layers containing other elements such as lanthanum, barium, strontium, or bismuth. They hold the record critical temperatures of any superconductor at ambient pressure, and their discovery in 1986 revolutionized condensed-matter physics.

Discovery

In 1986, J. Georg Bednorz and K. Alex Müller at IBM Zürich reported superconductivity near 35 K in a lanthanum barium copper oxide, earning the 1987 Nobel Prize in Physics. In early 1987, a team at the University of Houston led by Paul Chu found that yttrium barium copper oxide (YBCO, YBa2Cu3O7) superconducts at 93 K — above the boiling point of liquid nitrogen (77 K) — sparking an explosion of research. Later cuprates such as BSCCO and a mercury-based compound reached 133 K, and 164 K under high pressure.

Structure and physics

Cuprates derive from antiferromagnetic Mott insulators; adding charge carriers ("doping") suppresses magnetism and produces superconductivity over a dome-shaped region of the phase diagram, alongside a mysterious "pseudogap" regime. The superconducting order parameter has d-wave symmetry, and pairing is generally believed to be mediated by magnetic interactions rather than the lattice vibrations of conventional BCS theory — a mechanism still not fully settled.

Applications

YBCO and BSCCO are manufactured as wires and tapes for high-field magnets, power cables, fault-current limiters, and research magnets, although they remain expensive and brittle ceramics requiring careful engineering. Coated-conductor cables have been deployed in demonstration power grids.
