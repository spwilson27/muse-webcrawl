Antiseptic

An antiseptic is a substance that stops or slows down the growth of microorganisms. It is applied to living tissue or skin to reduce the possibility of sepsis, infection, or putrefaction. Antiseptics are generally distinguished from antibiotics, which destroy bacteria within the body, and from disinfectants, which destroy microorganisms found on inanimate objects.

History

The systematic use of antiseptics in surgery began with Joseph Lister in the 1860s, who used carbolic acid (phenol) dressings and sprays, dramatically reducing postoperative infection. Ignaz Semmelweis had earlier shown that hand washing with chlorinated lime reduced childbed fever. Antiseptic technique later evolved into aseptic technique, aiming to exclude microorganisms entirely.

Common antiseptics

  • Alcohols (ethanol, isopropanol) — used in hand rubs and skin preparation.
  • Chlorhexidine — used in mouthwashes, surgical scrubs, and skin antisepsis.
  • Povidone-iodine — a broad-spectrum iodophor.
  • Hydrogen peroxide and boric acid — older agents with narrower modern roles.
