Serpico is a 1973 American biographical crime drama film directed by Sidney Lumet and starring Al Pacino as Frank Serpico, the New York City police officer who went undercover to expose corruption in the NYPD. The screenplay by Waldo Salt and Norman Wexler is based on Peter Maas's biography of Serpico. The film was a critical and commercial success and earned Pacino an Academy Award nomination for Best Actor and a Golden Globe win.

Plot

Frank Serpico joins the NYPD in 1960 as an idealistic patrolman and later works plainclothes. He discovers that many of his fellow officers routinely accept bribes and payoffs from criminals — gamblers, drug dealers and others. Refusing to take money himself, Serpico reports the corruption through departmental channels but finds his complaints ignored or buried, while his colleagues come to distrust him. He eventually goes outside the department, cooperating with investigators and the press. In 1971, during a drug raid in Brooklyn, Serpico is shot in the face; the film leaves open whether fellow officers deliberately failed to protect him. He testifies before the Knapp Commission, which investigated police corruption.

Production and reception

Lumet shot the film on location in New York City in the summer of 1972, filming scenes in reverse chronological order so that Pacino's hair and beard could simply be trimmed progressively. Reviewers praised Pacino's intense performance and the film's gritty realism; it is frequently ranked among the finest crime films of the 1970s and helped consolidate Lumet's reputation for New York–set dramas of institutional failure, alongside Dog Day Afternoon (1975) and Prince of the City (1981).

Legacy

The real Frank Serpico became an emblem of the honest whistleblower; the film inspired a 1976 television series of the same name and remains a reference point in discussions of police corruption. Serpico retired to Europe and later returned to the United States, continuing to speak on police ethics.
