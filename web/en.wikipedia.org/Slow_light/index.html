Slow light


Schematic of a light pulse propagating slowly through a medium with steep normal dispersion

Slow light is the propagation of an optical pulse or other modulation of an optical carrier at a very low group velocity. Slow light occurs when a propagating pulse is substantially slowed by the interaction with the medium in which the propagation takes place.

Group velocities depend on the frequency and the dispersion characteristics of the medium. In 1999, a team of physicists led by Lene Hau was able to slow a pulse of light to about 17 metres per second, and in 2001 they were able to momentarily stop a beam. In 2003, at the Rowland Institute for Science, researchers slowed light to 38 mph in a Bose–Einstein condensate and demonstrated a memory effect for light, which is considered a step toward optical data storage.

Background

The group velocity of light in a material is given by the derivative of the angular frequency with respect to the wave vector. When a material exhibits steep normal dispersion—meaning the refractive index changes rapidly with frequency—the group velocity can become far smaller than the speed of light in vacuum, c, even though the phase velocity remains close to c. Slow light therefore does not violate special relativity: information still cannot travel faster than c.

Methods

Several techniques produce slow light. Electromagnetically induced transparency (EIT) makes an otherwise opaque medium transparent in a narrow spectral window with steep dispersion, slowing pulses dramatically in ultracold atomic gases and in room-temperature vapours. Coherent population oscillation, stimulated Brillouin scattering, and photonic crystal waveguides can likewise produce large group delays on a chip scale.

Applications

Proposed applications include optical buffers and temporary memories for telecommunications and quantum information processing, enhanced interferometers and spectrometers, and tunable optical delays. Storing light as an atomic excitation and releasing it on demand is a key element of quantum repeaters for long-distance quantum networks.

Stopped light

By dynamically reducing the group velocity to zero—for example, by switching off the control beam in an EIT system while the pulse is inside the medium—the pulse can be mapped onto a stationary atomic coherence ("stopped light") and later retrieved. Storage times of up to a minute for images have been demonstrated in rare-earth-doped crystals.
