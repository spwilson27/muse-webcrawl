Irish Republican Army

Active: 1917–present (various incarnations)

Ideology: Irish republicanism, Irish nationalism

Area of operations: Ireland, Northern Ireland, England

The Irish Republican Army (IRA) is a name used by various paramilitary organisations in Ireland throughout the 20th and 21st centuries, all claiming to represent the legacy of the original IRA, the army of the Irish Republic declared in 1916 and active during the Irish War of Independence (1919–1921).

The original IRA

The original IRA grew out of the Irish Volunteers, who fought in the Easter Rising of 1916. Under political leadership of Sinn Féin and Dáil Éireann, and military leaders such as Michael Collins, it waged a guerrilla war against British rule that ended with the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921. The Treaty split the movement: pro-Treaty members formed the National Army of the Irish Free State, while anti-Treaty members fought them in the Irish Civil War (1922–1923) and afterwards refused to recognise the legitimacy of the Irish state.

The Troubles

In 1969 the IRA split into the Marxist-leaning Official IRA and the Provisional IRA. The Provisional IRA became the principal republican paramilitary group during the Troubles in Northern Ireland, waging a campaign of bombings and shootings against British security forces, loyalists, and civilian targets for nearly thirty years. More than 1,700 people were killed by the Provisional IRA. Following the 1994 and 1997 ceasefires and the Good Friday Agreement of 1998, the Provisional IRA formally ended its armed campaign in 2005 and decommissioned its weapons under international supervision.

Dissident groups

Splinter groups rejecting the peace process — including the Continuity IRA and the Real IRA (later part of the "New IRA") — have continued sporadic violence, most notoriously the Real IRA's 1998 Omagh bombing, which killed 29 people. The IRA is designated a terrorist organisation in the United Kingdom and an unlawful organisation in the Republic of Ireland.
