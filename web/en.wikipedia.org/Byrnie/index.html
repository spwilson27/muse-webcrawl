Byrnie

Byrnie
Type of armour
Material: Interlocking metal rings (mail)
Period: Early medieval, especially Norse and Anglo-Saxon

A byrnie (Old Norse: brynja) is a shirt of mail armour, typically extending to about the waist or hips, worn by warriors in early medieval northern Europe.

Description

The byrnie consists of thousands of interlocking iron rings, each riveted or welded closed, forming a flexible defence for the torso. Early medieval examples were generally shorter than the later hauberk, often reaching only the waist, with short sleeves.

In literature and history

Mail shirts appear frequently in Norse sagas and Old English poetry; in Beowulf the hero's war-shirt is described as the work of Weland the smith, and byrnies are prized gifts and heirlooms. Archaeologically mail is rare in graves — a famous byrnie was found in the Gjermundbu burial in Norway.

Related armour

The byrnie is closely related to the hauberk (a longer mail coat), the haubergeon, and Roman lorica hamata, from which northern European mail ultimately derived.
