Epipaleolithic

The Epipaleolithic (or Epipalaeolithic) is a period in the development of human technology at the end of the Ice Age, between the Upper Paleolithic and the Neolithic. It is generally dated from roughly 20,000 to 10,000 years ago in the Near East, though usage varies by region; in Europe the broadly equivalent period is usually called the Mesolithic.

Microliths — small geometric stone tools — are characteristic of Epipaleolithic cultures

Characteristics

Epipaleolithic hunter-gatherers adapted to the warming climate and changing environments that followed the Last Glacial Maximum. The period is marked by microlithic tool industries, intensified exploitation of a broad spectrum of food resources — including wild cereals, fish, and small game — and in some regions the first semi-sedentary settlements.

The Levant

In the Levant, Epipaleolithic cultures such as the Kebaran and the later Natufian are central to understanding the origins of agriculture. The Natufians built permanent settlements with stone architecture and harvested wild grains, representing a transitional stage before full farming in the Neolithic.

Terminology

Scholars prefer "Epipaleolithic" for regions, particularly the Near East, where hunting and gathering continued with little change in tool traditions from the Paleolithic, reserving "Mesolithic" for post-glacial adaptations elsewhere.
