George Berkeley (12 March 1685 – 14 January 1753), known as Bishop Berkeley, was an Anglo-Irish philosopher whose primary achievement was the advancement of a theory he called immaterialism (later referred to as subjective idealism). His best-known dictum is esse est percipi (aut percipere) — "to be is to be perceived (or to perceive)".

Life

Berkeley was educated at Trinity College Dublin, where he spent much of his career. He travelled to America in 1728 hoping to found a college in Bermuda for training clergy and missionaries; the funding failed and he returned in 1731. He became Bishop of Cloyne in 1734. The city of Berkeley, California, and the University of California, Berkeley are named after him, in honour of his poem On the Prospect of Planting Arts and Learning in America, which contains the line "Westward the course of empire takes its way".

Philosophy

In A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge (1710) and Three Dialogues Between Hylas and Philonous (1713), Berkeley argued that physical objects consist merely of collections of ideas perceived by minds, and cannot exist unperceived. He attacked Locke's distinction between primary and secondary qualities, holding that all qualities are equally mind-dependent. Objects persist when no human perceives them, Berkeley held, because they are continually perceived by God. His An Essay Towards a New Theory of Vision (1709) reshaped the study of visual perception and depth.

Legacy

Berkeley is, with Locke and Hume, one of the three great British empiricists. His immaterialism remains one of the most discussed positions in metaphysics, and his critique of abstract ideas influenced later philosophy of language.
