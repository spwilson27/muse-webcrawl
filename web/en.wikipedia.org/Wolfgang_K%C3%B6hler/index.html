Wolfgang Köhler

Wolfgang Köhler (21 January 1887 – 11 June 1967) was a German psychologist and phenomenologist who, like Max Wertheimer and Kurt Koffka, contributed to the creation of Gestalt psychology. His experiments on insight learning in chimpanzees and his defence of the idea that perception is organised into wholes remain influential.

Early life and Tenerife experiments

Köhler was born in Reval (now Tallinn, Estonia) and raised in Germany, studying at Tübingen, Bonn, and Berlin, where he earned his PhD in 1909 under Carl Stumpf. From 1913 to 1920 he directed the Prussian Academy of Sciences' anthropoid research station on Tenerife in the Canary Islands — stranded there through the First World War. His studies of the chimpanzee Sultan and others, published as The Mentality of Apes (1917), showed animals solving problems suddenly, by insight — for example stacking boxes or joining sticks to reach bananas — rather than by gradual trial and error, challenging behaviourist accounts of learning.

Gestalt theory and exile

In 1922 Köhler succeeded Stumpf as head of the Psychology Institute at the University of Berlin, making it a centre of Gestalt research. His books Gestalt Psychology (1929) introduced the school to English-speaking readers. An outspoken critic of the Nazi regime — he wrote the last anti-Nazi article published in a German newspaper in 1933 — Köhler emigrated to the United States in 1935, teaching at Swarthmore College. He served as president of the American Psychological Association in 1959 and died in New Hampshire in 1967.
