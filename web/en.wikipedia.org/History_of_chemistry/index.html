History of chemistry

The history of chemistry runs from ancient practical arts — metallurgy, dyeing, brewing and medicine — through alchemy's search for transmutation and the elixir of life, to the scientific revolution of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the atomic theory and periodic table of the nineteenth, and the quantum and molecular chemistry of the modern era.

Ancient and alchemical chemistry

Early humans mastered fire, pottery, metals and fermentation. Greek philosophers proposed theories of elements (earth, water, air, fire) and atoms (Democritus). Alchemy, arising in Hellenistic Egypt and developed in the Islamic world by figures such as Jabir ibn Hayyan (Geber) and al-Razi, and later in medieval and Renaissance Europe (Paracelsus), contributed laboratory techniques — distillation, sublimation, acids and apparatus — while pursuing the philosopher's stone.

The scientific revolution

In the seventeenth century, Robert Boyle's The Sceptical Chymist (1661) criticised alchemical theory and defined elements operationally. Georg Ernst Stahl's phlogiston theory organised combustion and calcination for a century, even though it was mistaken. Pneumatic chemists such as Joseph Black, Henry Cavendish and Joseph Priestley isolated "airs" (gases) including carbon dioxide, hydrogen and oxygen.

The chemical revolution

Antoine Lavoisier, using precise measurement and the conservation of mass, explained combustion by oxygen, overthrew phlogiston, redefined the element, and with collaborators reformed chemical nomenclature in the 1780s; his Traité élémentaire de chimie (1789) founded modern chemistry. John Dalton's atomic theory (1803–08) and Avogadro's hypothesis (1811) set the stage for determining atomic weights.

Nineteenth-century expansion

Organic chemistry grew from Friedrich Wöhler's urea synthesis (1828) through structural theory (Kekulé, Couper) to a vast synthetic industry (dyes, pharmaceuticals). Dmitri Mendeleev's periodic table (1869) organised the elements and predicted new ones. Physical chemistry emerged with thermodynamics, electrochemistry and kinetics (Gibbs, Arrhenius, Ostwald). Marie Curie and others opened radioactivity at the century's end.

Twentieth century and beyond

Quantum mechanics explained the chemical bond (Heitler, London, Pauling); spectroscopy, chromatography and X-ray crystallography revealed molecular structures, including DNA (1953). Polymer chemistry, organometallics, biochemistry and computational chemistry transformed industry and medicine, while green chemistry now seeks to reduce the field's environmental footprint.
