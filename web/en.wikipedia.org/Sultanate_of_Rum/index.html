The Sultanate of Rum (also the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum or the Anatolian Seljuk Sultanate) was a Turco-Persian Sunni Muslim state in Anatolia from 1077 to 1308. It was established by Suleiman ibn Qutulmish, a member of the Seljuk dynasty, following the Battle of Manzikert (1071), in which the Seljuks defeated the Byzantine Empire and opened Anatolia to Turkish settlement. The name "Rum" derives from the Arabic and Persian word for Rome, referring to the formerly Byzantine lands the sultanate ruled.

History

The sultanate's first capital was Nicaea (İznik), lost to the Crusaders in 1097, after which the capital moved to Konya (Iconium). Under sultans such as Kilij Arslan II and Kayqubad I the state reached its apogee in the late 12th and early 13th centuries, controlling ports on both the Black Sea and the Mediterranean and building a dense network of caravanserais that made Anatolia a hub of international trade.

The decisive blow came with the Mongol victory at the Battle of Köse Dağ in 1243, after which the sultanate became a vassal of the Ilkhanate. In the following decades central authority withered; Anatolia fragmented into a patchwork of Turkish principalities (beyliks), one of which — the beylik of Osman I — grew into the Ottoman Empire. The last Seljuk sultan, Masud II, died in 1308.

Culture

The Rumi court was famously cosmopolitan. Persian was the language of administration and literature, and Konya became home to the Sufi poet Jalal al-Din Rumi (Mawlana), from whom the sultanate takes its common name, and to the Mevlevi order of whirling dervishes. Seljuk architecture — madrasas, mosques, and caravanserais built of finely carved stone, such as the İnce Minareli Medrese in Konya — left a lasting mark on Anatolia.
