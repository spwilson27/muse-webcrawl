Non-homologous end joining

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) is a pathway that repairs double-strand breaks in DNA. It is called "non-homologous" because the broken ends are directly ligated without the need for a homologous template, in contrast to homologous recombination, which requires a sister chromatid to guide repair. NHEJ is efficient but error-prone, frequently introducing small insertions or deletions (indels) at the break site.

Mechanism

The Ku heterodimer (Ku70/Ku80) first binds the DNA ends and protects them, recruiting the DNA-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA-PKcs). End-processing enzymes, including the Artemis nuclease and polymerases mu and lambda, prepare the ends, and the XRCC4–DNA ligase IV complex, stabilised by XLF, seals the break.

Biological roles

NHEJ operates throughout the cell cycle and is the dominant double-strand-break repair pathway in most mammalian cells, especially outside S and G2 phases. It is essential for V(D)J recombination in the developing immune system, where programmed breaks in immunoglobulin and T-cell receptor genes are joined to generate antibody diversity. Defects in NHEJ factors cause immunodeficiency and radiosensitivity syndromes.

Applications

The error-prone nature of NHEJ is exploited in CRISPR–Cas9 genome editing, where targeted double-strand breaks are repaired by NHEJ to knock out genes through frameshift indels.
