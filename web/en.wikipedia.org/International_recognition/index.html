International recognition

International recognition is the acknowledgment by an existing state, or group of states, of the status of a new entity as a state under international law. Recognition is usually expressed through a formal act or statement of a government, and it generally implies a willingness to enter into diplomatic relations with the recognized entity.

Statehood and recognition

Under the declarative theory of statehood, reflected in the 1933 Montevideo Convention, a state exists when it possesses a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states; recognition by others merely declares a pre-existing fact. Under the constitutive theory, by contrast, it is recognition by existing states that creates statehood.

De facto and de jure recognition

Recognition may be de jure — the full legal acknowledgment of an entity as sovereign — or de facto, a more limited, provisional acknowledgment of effective control, often used when a government has come to power by unconstitutional means or when the recognizer has reservations about permanence.

Partially recognized states

A number of entities exercise effective control over territory but enjoy recognition from only some states, or none. Examples include Kosovo, Palestine, Taiwan (the Republic of China), Western Sahara, Northern Cyprus, Abkhazia, South Ossetia, Transnistria and Somaliland. Their status is a recurring subject of international dispute, and admission to organizations such as the United Nations typically depends on broad recognition.

Recognition of governments

Distinct from recognizing states, governments may recognize a particular regime as the legitimate government of a state, particularly after coups or civil wars. Some states, following the British practice adopted in 1980, no longer formally recognize governments at all, dealing instead with whatever authority is in effective control.
