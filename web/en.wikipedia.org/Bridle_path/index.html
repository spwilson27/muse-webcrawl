Bridle path

A bridle path through woodland, reserved for equestrian use.

A bridle path, also called a bridleway or bridle road, is a path or trail used for riding horses, or a wider multi-use trail on which equestrians have the right to travel. In England and Wales, bridleways are a legal category of public right of way: the public may use them on foot, on horseback and on bicycles, but not in motor vehicles. By contrast, ordinary footpaths carry a right of way on foot only.

Legal status

In English law, public bridleways are shown on definitive maps maintained by local authorities and are typically marked with blue waymark arrows, distinct from the yellow arrows of footpaths and the red or plum arrows of byways open to all traffic. Bridleways may cross private land, where the landowner must maintain gates and access but the surface is the responsibility of the highway authority. Many originated as historic routes — packhorse tracks, drove roads and church paths — later recorded under the rights-of-way system formalised by the National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949 and the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000.

In the United States, bridle paths are designated equestrian trails in parks and forests, such as the extensive bridle path systems around Central Park in New York City and many national and state parks. Surfaces are usually unpaved — natural earth, gravel or woodchip — because smooth hard surfacing can be slippery for shod hooves, and multi-use trails commonly set an etiquette in which pedestrians and cyclists yield to horses.
