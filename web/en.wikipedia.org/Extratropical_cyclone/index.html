Extratropical cyclone

Extratropical cyclones, sometimes called mid-latitude cyclones or wave cyclones, are low-pressure areas which, along with anticyclones, drive the weather over much of the Earth. They are capable of producing anything from cloudiness and mild showers to severe gales, thunderstorms, blizzards, and tornadoes. These types of cyclones are defined as large-scale low-pressure weather systems that occur in the middle latitudes of the Earth, outside the tropics.

Formation

Extratropical cyclones form along weather fronts — boundaries between contrasting air masses — through the process of cyclogenesis. The Norwegian cyclone model, developed by Vilhelm Bjerknes and the Bergen School around 1919, describes their life cycle: a stationary front develops a wave, low pressure deepens as warm and cold fronts form, and the system eventually occludes as the cold front overtakes the warm front, cutting off the supply of warm air.

Structure

A mature extratropical cyclone has a cold front trailing to the southwest (in the Northern Hemisphere) and a warm front extending to the east, with a characteristic comma-shaped cloud pattern. Unlike tropical cyclones, which are warm-core systems powered by latent heat over warm oceans, extratropical cyclones have both warm and cold cores at different levels and draw energy from horizontal temperature contrasts (baroclinic instability).

Effects

Extratropical cyclones dominate the weather of the mid-latitudes in winter. Intense examples include nor'easters on the east coast of North America and European windstorms. Some tropical cyclones transform into extratropical storms as they move poleward, a process called extratropical transition.
