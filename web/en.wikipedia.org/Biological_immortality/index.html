Biological immortality

Biological immortality (sometimes referred to as bio-indefinite mortality) is a state in which the rate of mortality from senescence is stable or decreasing, thus decoupling it from chronological age. Various unicellular and multicellular species may achieve this state either throughout their existence or after living long enough. A biologically immortal living being can still die from means other than senescence, such as disease or predation.

Examples

The jellyfish Turritopsis dohrnii can revert its cells to an earlier developmental stage, potentially cycling indefinitely. Hydra show negligible senescence in laboratory studies, and some long-lived organisms such as bristlecone pines and certain clonal colonies exhibit extremely slow ageing. Lobsters maintain telomerase activity, though they still die, often during moulting.

Cell lines

At the cellular level, immortalised cell lines such as HeLa cells can proliferate indefinitely in culture, a property shared with cancer cells. Normal somatic cells, by contrast, are limited by the Hayflick limit on cell divisions.
