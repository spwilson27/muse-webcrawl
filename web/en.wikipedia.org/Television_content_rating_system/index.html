Television content rating system

A television content rating system classifies television programs according to their suitability for particular audiences, based on content such as sex, violence, language, drug use and other mature themes. Most countries have their own system, administered by broadcasters, industry bodies or government regulators, and ratings are usually displayed at the start of a program and used with parental controls such as the V-chip.

United States

The TV Parental Guidelines, introduced in 1997, rate programs as TV-Y, TV-Y7, TV-G, TV-PG, TV-14 and TV-MA, with content descriptors (D, L, S, V, FV) added for suggestive dialogue, language, sexual content, violence and fantasy violence. Broadcasters assign ratings voluntarily, and the system is overseen by the TV Parental Guidelines Monitoring Board.

Other countries

Canada uses the Canadian TV Classification System (E, C, C8+, G, PG, 14+, 18+). Australia classifies free-to-air programs as P, C, G, PG, M, MA15+ and AV15+. The United Kingdom relies on scheduling (the 9 pm watershed) rather than a full ratings scheme for broadcast television, while many European countries use age-based icons such as Germany's FSK system adapted for television.

Criticism

Rating systems are criticized for inconsistency between networks, "ratings creep", and the difficulty of comparing systems between countries. Streaming services increasingly use their own maturity ratings, adapted per territory.
