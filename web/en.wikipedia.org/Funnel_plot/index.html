Funnel plot

A funnel plot is a scatter diagram used in meta-analysis to investigate publication bias and other small-study effects. It plots each study's estimated treatment effect (or other result) against a measure of the study's size or precision, such as the standard error or sample size.

Interpretation

In the absence of bias, results from smaller studies scatter widely at the bottom of the plot while larger studies cluster near the true effect at the top, forming a symmetric inverted funnel around the summary estimate. Asymmetry — typically a gap where small studies with negative or null results would fall — suggests that such studies may have gone unpublished, or that other systematic differences between small and large studies exist.

Statistical tests

Formal tests for funnel plot asymmetry include Egger's regression test and the rank-correlation test of Begg and Mazumdar. Related methods include trim-and-fill, which imputes "missing" studies, and contour-enhanced funnel plots, which overlay regions of statistical significance to help distinguish publication bias from other causes of asymmetry.

Limitations

Funnel plot asymmetry is not proof of publication bias: genuine heterogeneity, poor methodological quality in small studies, and chance can produce the same appearance. Guidelines generally advise against relying on funnel plots when a meta-analysis contains fewer than about ten studies.

History

The technique was developed in the 1980s by Richard Light and David Pillemer and popularised for medical meta-analysis by Matthias Egger and colleagues in the 1990s.
