The Arctic is the polar region at the northernmost part of Earth. It consists of the Arctic Ocean, adjacent seas, and parts of Canada (Northern Canada), Danish Greenland, Finland, Iceland, Norway, Russia, Sweden and the United States (Alaska). Land within the Arctic region has seasonally varying snow and ice cover, with predominantly treeless permafrost under the soil.

Etymology

The word Arctic comes from the Greek ἀρκτικός (arktikos), "near the Bear", referring to the northern constellations Ursa Major and Ursa Minor. Its opposite is the Antarctic.

Climate

The region is characterised by cold winters and cool summers, long periods of continuous daylight (the midnight sun) and darkness (polar night), and precipitation mostly in the form of snow. The Arctic is warming at roughly four times the global average rate, a phenomenon called Arctic amplification, with major consequences for sea ice, permafrost and ecosystems.

Ecology

Arctic life includes organisms adapted to the ice, zooplankton, fish, marine mammals such as seals, walruses and whales, birds, and terrestrial animals including polar bears, Arctic foxes, caribou and musk oxen. Vegetation is dominated by tundra — dwarf shrubs, sedges, lichens and mosses.

Indigenous peoples

Indigenous peoples of the Arctic include the Inuit of Canada, Alaska and Greenland, the Sámi of northern Europe, and numerous peoples of the Russian North such as the Nenets, Chukchi and Evenks. Their rights and interests are represented internationally through organisations such as the Inuit Circumpolar Council and the Arctic Council, founded in 1996, which coordinates cooperation among the eight Arctic states.

Resources and geopolitics

The Arctic is believed to hold substantial undiscovered petroleum and mineral resources, and retreating sea ice is opening shipping routes such as the Northern Sea Route and the Northwest Passage, increasing strategic interest in the region.
