Liver transplantation

Liver transplantation
Specialty: transplant surgery, hepatology
First successful: 1967 (Thomas Starzl)
Indications: end-stage liver disease, acute liver failure, hepatocellular carcinoma
Allocation (US): MELD score
1-year survival: ~85–90%

Liver transplantation or hepatic transplantation is the replacement of a diseased liver with a healthy liver from another person (an allograft). It is the treatment of choice for end-stage liver disease and acute liver failure when no other therapy is effective. Most transplants use the whole liver from a deceased donor (orthotopic transplantation), but living-donor transplantation — in which a segment of a healthy person's liver is transplanted and both portions regenerate — is common, particularly in Asia and for paediatric recipients.

Liver transplantation surgery

History

Thomas Starzl performed the first human liver transplant in Denver in 1963; the patient did not survive long-term. The first successful transplant with survival beyond one year followed in 1967. Sir Roy Calne established the Cambridge programme in 1968. The introduction of the immunosuppressant ciclosporin in the early 1980s transformed outcomes, and in 1983 the US National Institutes of Health declared liver transplantation a therapeutic modality rather than an experimental procedure. Rudolf Pichlmayr pioneered split-liver transplantation (one organ for two recipients) and Silvano Raia in Brazil performed the first living-donor transplant in 1988–89.

Indications and allocation

Indications include decompensated cirrhosis (from viral hepatitis, alcohol-associated liver disease, or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease), fulminant hepatic failure, hepatocellular carcinoma within the Milan criteria, and certain metabolic disorders such as primary biliary cholangitis and Wilson disease. In the United States and many other countries, organs are allocated by the MELD (Model for End-Stage Liver Disease) score, calculated from bilirubin, INR, creatinine and sodium, prioritising the sickest candidates.

Technique and outcomes

The recipient's diseased liver is removed and the donor organ implanted with anastomoses of the vena cava, portal vein, hepatic artery and bile duct; a "piggyback" technique preserving the recipient's vena cava is widely used. Lifelong immunosuppression, usually with tacrolimus, is required. One-year survival now exceeds 85–90% and five-year survival is around 70–80%, though recurrent disease and the side effects of immunosuppression remain major concerns. Globally, roughly 25,000–30,000 liver transplants are performed annually, with demand far exceeding donor supply.
