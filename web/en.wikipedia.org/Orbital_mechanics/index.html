Orbital mechanics or astrodynamics is the application of ballistics and celestial mechanics to the practical problems concerning the motion of rockets, satellites and other spacecraft. The motion of these objects is usually calculated from Newton's laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation. Orbital mechanics is a core discipline within space-mission design and control.

Celestial mechanics treats more broadly the orbital dynamics of systems under the influence of gravity, including both spacecraft and natural astronomical bodies such as star systems, planets, moons, and comets.

Kepler's laws

Johannes Kepler, analysing Tycho Brahe's observations, formulated three laws: (1) the orbit of a planet is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus; (2) a line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times; (3) the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis. Isaac Newton later derived these laws from his universal gravitation.

Orbital energy and manoeuvres

The specific orbital energy of an orbiting body is constant and negative for bound (elliptical) orbits. The vis-viva equation relates speed to position:

v² = μ ( 2/r − 1/a )

where μ is the standard gravitational parameter, r the current distance, and a the semi-major axis. Manoeuvres such as Hohmann transfers, bi-elliptic transfers, plane changes and gravitational assists are used to move spacecraft between orbits with minimal propellant expenditure (delta-v).

Applications

Orbital mechanics underlies launch vehicle guidance, satellite station-keeping, rendezvous and docking, interplanetary trajectory design and mission analysis for crewed and robotic spaceflight.
