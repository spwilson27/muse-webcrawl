Venetian Gothic is a term given to a distinct variety of Gothic architecture that flourished in Venice from the 13th to the 15th century. Combining the pointed arch and traceried windows of northern Gothic with Byzantine domes and horseshoe arches and Islamic decorative motifs absorbed through Venice's maritime trade, it produced a light, ornate, and highly recognisable style adapted to the city's lagoon setting and palace architecture.

Characteristics

Because Venetian buildings rest on wooden piles in soft lagoon mud, walls could not bear heavy loads; façades were therefore pierced with large grouped windows and loggias, often lavishly decorated with quatrefoil tracery, ogee arches, carved capitals, and patterned brickwork or Istrian stone. The typical Venetian palace (such as the Ca' d'Oro, begun 1421) centres on a water-level entrance hall rising to a piano nobile with an arcaded balcony overlooking a canal.

Major monuments

The outstanding public example is the Doge's Palace, rebuilt in stages from 1340, whose pink-and-white patterned façade and open arcades epitomise the style. Great mendicant churches — the Frari (Franciscan) and Santi Giovanni e Paolo (Dominican) — apply Gothic forms in brick on a monumental scale. Venetian Gothic spread to the republic's overseas possessions in Dalmatia, Crete, and Cyprus.

Later reception

The style was famously championed by John Ruskin in The Stones of Venice (1851–53), whose analysis influenced the Gothic Revival far beyond Italy.
