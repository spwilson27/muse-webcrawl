Graves' disease

Proptosis and lid retraction in Graves' ophthalmopathy

Graves' disease, also known as toxic diffuse goiter, is an autoimmune disease that affects the thyroid gland. It is the most common cause of hyperthyroidism, accounting for the majority of cases. The disease is named after Irish physician Robert Graves, who described it in 1835; it was also described by Karl Adolph von Basedow in Germany and is known there as Basedow's disease.

Cause

In Graves' disease, the immune system produces thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins (TSI) — autoantibodies against the thyrotropin (TSH) receptor — that mimic TSH and continuously stimulate the thyroid to grow and to overproduce the hormones thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). Genetic susceptibility, female sex, stress, smoking, and possibly infection are risk factors; the disease is about five to ten times more common in women.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms of hyperthyroidism include weight loss, heat intolerance, tremor, palpitations, anxiety, and increased bowel frequency, together with a diffusely enlarged thyroid (goiter). Distinctive extrathyroidal manifestations include Graves' ophthalmopathy — inflammation and swelling of eye tissues causing bulging eyes (proptosis), double vision, and in severe cases vision loss — in roughly a quarter to a half of patients, and pretibial myxedema, a thickening of skin over the shins. Untreated disease can lead to atrial fibrillation, osteoporosis, and the life-threatening emergency thyroid storm.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on suppressed TSH with elevated T4/T3 and the presence of TSH-receptor antibodies. Treatments include antithyroid drugs such as methimazole, radioactive iodine ablation, and surgical thyroidectomy, followed where necessary by lifelong levothyroxine replacement. Eye disease may require steroids, other immunosuppression, or orbital surgery.
