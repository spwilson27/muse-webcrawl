Border Reivers

Region: Anglo-Scottish border
Period: Late 13th century – early 17th century
Activity: Raiding, cattle rustling
Suppressed: After 1603 Union of the Crowns

The Border Reivers were raiders along the Anglo-Scottish border from the late 13th century to the beginning of the 17th century. They included both English and Scottish people, and they raided the entire border country without regard for their victims' nationality.

Background

During the period of Anglo-Scottish wars, the border lands were repeatedly devastated, making farming precarious. Local families — the riding surnames such as Armstrong, Elliott, Graham, Kerr, Nixon and Robson — turned to livestock raiding as a way of life, defended by fortified bastle houses and peel towers.

Way of life

Reivers were skilled horsemen who rode sturdy hobblers across the moss trods, raiding by moonlight. Feuds between families could last generations, and institutions such as the "hot trod" and the March wardens attempted, often unsuccessfully, to keep order. Words such as "blackmail" and "bereaved" entered English from the reiving world.

Decline

After James VI of Scotland became James I of England in 1603, a determined campaign pacified the "Middle Shires": many reivers were executed or exiled, and the border was brought under central control.
