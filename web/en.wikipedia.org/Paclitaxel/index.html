Paclitaxel, sold under the brand name Taxol among others, is a chemotherapy medication used to treat several types of cancer, including ovarian cancer, breast cancer, lung cancer, Kaposi sarcoma, cervical cancer and pancreatic cancer. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

History

Paclitaxel was discovered through a National Cancer Institute screening programme: bark of the Pacific yew (Taxus brevifolia) collected in 1962 showed cytotoxic activity, and Monroe Wall and Mansukh Wani isolated the active compound in 1971, naming it taxol. Susan Horwitz determined its unusual mechanism in 1979 — it stabilises microtubules rather than destroying them, freezing the mitotic spindle and arresting cell division. Because stripping bark killed the slow-growing yew trees and yields were tiny, supply became a major problem; it was solved by semisynthesis from 10-deacetylbaccatin III, a precursor abundant in yew needles, and later by plant cell fermentation. Bristol-Myers Squibb commercialised it as Taxol, and the FDA approved it in 1992.

Mechanism of action

Paclitaxel binds to the β-tubulin subunit of microtubules and prevents their depolymerisation. Normal dynamic instability of microtubules is essential for mitotic spindle function; by locking microtubules in a stabilised state, the drug blocks cells in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle, leading to apoptosis. This is the opposite of vinca alkaloids, which prevent microtubule assembly.

Medical use and side effects

The drug is given by intravenous infusion, often in an albumin-bound nanoparticle form (nab-paclitaxel, Abraxane) that avoids the Cremophor EL solvent responsible for hypersensitivity reactions. Common side effects include hair loss, numbness and tingling (peripheral neuropathy), low blood counts (myelosuppression), muscle and joint pain, nausea and diarrhoea. Premedication with corticosteroids and antihistamines is routine to reduce allergic reactions.
