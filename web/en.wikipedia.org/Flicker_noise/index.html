Flicker noise

Flicker noise is a type of electronic noise with a 1/f power spectral density. It is therefore often referred to as 1/f noise or pink noise, though these terms have wider definitions. It occurs in almost all electronic devices and can appear as a consequence of a wide variety of effects, such as impurities in a conductive channel, generation and recombination noise in a transistor due to base current, and so on. Flicker noise can be reduced by the techniques of autozeroing and chopper stabilization.

Properties

Flicker noise has the form of pink noise: its spectral density is inversely proportional to frequency, so its power per decade of frequency is constant. The total noise power in a band from f1 to f2 is proportional to ln(f2/f1), which means flicker noise dominates at low frequencies while white noise, whose spectral density is flat, dominates at high frequencies. The frequency at which the two contributions are equal is called the corner frequency or flicker noise corner.

Occurrence

In electronic devices flicker noise is predominantly a low-frequency phenomenon, prominent in MOSFETs, bipolar junction transistors, and resistors. In carbon-composition resistors it is associated with fluctuations in resistance under a steady current. Flicker noise is also observed in many other systems beyond electronics, including the loudness fluctuations of music, the flow of the Nile river, and numerous biological and geophysical processes, a ubiquity sometimes grouped under the study of 1/f phenomena.

Measurement

The measurement of the voltage noise spectral density in the region where flicker noise dominates requires care, since thermal drift and long measurement times can corrupt the result. Cross-correlation techniques between two measurement channels are commonly used to reject the uncorrelated noise of the measuring instruments themselves.
