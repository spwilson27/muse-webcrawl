John Locke

Born: 29 August 1632, Wrington, Somerset, England
Died: 28 October 1704 (aged 72), High Laver, Essex, England
Alma mater: Christ Church, Oxford
Notable works: An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689); Two Treatises of Government (1689)
Era: 17th-century philosophy; the Enlightenment

John Locke (29 August 1632 – 28 October 1704) was an English philosopher and physician, widely regarded as one of the most influential of Enlightenment thinkers and commonly known as the "father of liberalism". Considered one of the first of the British empiricists, following the tradition of Francis Bacon, Locke is equally important to social contract theory. His work greatly affected the development of epistemology and political philosophy, and influenced writers such as Voltaire and Rousseau, and the American revolutionaries.

Life

Locke was born in Wrington, Somerset, and educated at Westminster School and Christ Church, Oxford, where he studied medicine and the new experimental philosophy. He became physician and adviser to Anthony Ashley Cooper, Earl of Shaftesbury, whose political fortunes shaped Locke's early career. Suspected of involvement in plots against Charles II, Locke fled to the Netherlands in 1683, returning to England only after the Glorious Revolution of 1688. He spent his final years at Oates in Essex, the home of Sir Francis and Lady Masham.

Philosophy of mind

In An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689), Locke argued against the existence of innate ideas: the mind at birth is a tabula rasa, a blank slate, and all knowledge derives from experience — from sensation and reflection. He distinguished primary qualities (such as solidity and motion, which resemble qualities in objects themselves) from secondary qualities (such as colour and taste, which exist only as powers to produce sensations in observers). His treatment of personal identity, grounded in continuity of consciousness, founded the modern debate on the subject.

Political philosophy

The Two Treatises of Government (1689) refuted the divine right of kings defended by Sir Robert Filmer and set out a theory of government by consent: in the state of nature men are free and equal, and legitimate government rests on a social contract to protect natural rights to life, liberty and property. If a government violates that trust, the people may resist and alter it. Locke's ideas underpin modern liberal democracy and were echoed in the American Declaration of Independence. His Letter Concerning Toleration argued for religious toleration and a separation between church and state.
