Gymnosperm

The gymnosperms (from Greek gymnos, "naked", and sperma, "seed") are a group of seed-producing plants that includes conifers, cycads, Ginkgo, and gnetophytes. They form the clade Gymnospermae, and the living members are also known as Acrogymnospermae. Their seeds are "naked"—not enclosed in an ovary as in the flowering plants (angiosperms)—but typically borne on the surface of cone-like structures known as strobili.

Characteristics

Gymnosperms are mostly trees and shrubs with secondary growth (wood). Their reproductive structures are usually cones: pollen-bearing male cones and larger ovule-bearing female cones. Pollination is typically by wind. Unlike angiosperms, they lack flowers and fruits, and their xylem consists largely of tracheids rather than vessels, except in the gnetophytes.

Classification

Living gymnosperms comprise about 1,000 species in four divisions: Pinophyta (conifers, about 600 species including pines, spruces, and firs), Cycadophyta (cycads), Ginkgophyta (the single living species Ginkgo biloba), and Gnetophyta (gnetophytes such as Ephedra and Welwitschia). The conifers dominate large parts of the Northern Hemisphere's boreal forests.

Evolutionary history

Gymnosperms originated in the Carboniferous period, more than 300 million years ago, and were the dominant land plants through much of the Mesozoic era, forming the forests in which the dinosaurs lived. Angiosperms rose to dominance in the Cretaceous, but gymnosperms remain ecologically and economically vital, supplying most of the world's softwood timber and paper pulp.
