Hairy tongue

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Other namesLingua villosa; black hairy tongue (lingua villosa nigra)
SpecialtyDentistry, oral medicine
CauseDefective desquamation of filiform papillae
Risk factorsSmoking, poor oral hygiene, antibiotics, dry mouth, soft diet
TreatmentTongue brushing/scraping, removing triggers; usually self-limiting

Hairy tongue is a medical condition in which the filiform papillae on the surface of the tongue elongate, giving the tongue a discoloured, furry or "hairy" appearance. The condition is benign and is usually painless, although it may be accompanied by altered taste, halitosis or a gagging sensation.

Cause and mechanism

Normally the filiform papillae are worn down by friction from food and the palate at roughly the rate at which they grow. In hairy tongue, desquamation fails and the keratinised papillae lengthen — sometimes to more than a centimetre — trapping bacteria, fungi, food debris and pigments from coffee, tea and tobacco. When chromogenic bacteria colonise the elongated papillae the tongue appears black ("black hairy tongue"), though brown, yellow, green and white variants occur.

Risk factors

Reported precipitants include heavy smoking, poor oral hygiene, xerostomia (dry mouth), prolonged antibiotic therapy, oxidising mouthwashes, excessive coffee or black tea consumption, a soft or liquid diet lacking roughage, and head and neck radiotherapy. It is more common in older adults and in people who are systemically unwell.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical, based on the appearance of the posterior dorsal tongue; biopsy is rarely needed but helps distinguish the condition from oral hairy leukoplakia, a different entity associated with Epstein–Barr virus in immunocompromised patients. Management consists of reassurance, discontinuing or modifying triggers, good oral hygiene, and gentle mechanical debridement by brushing or scraping the tongue. The condition typically resolves within days to weeks once the cause is addressed.
