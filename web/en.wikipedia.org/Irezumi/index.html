Irezumi

Japanese: 入れ墨

Literally: "inserting ink"

Irezumi (入れ墨, literally "inserting ink") are traditional Japanese tattoos. Irezumi is tattooing typically reserved for the Japanese style, and the word refers to the insertion of ink under the skin to leave a permanent, usually decorative mark. The word also carries strong associations with the large, full-body designs worn historically by members of the yakuza.

History

Tattooing in Japan dates back to the Jōmon period, and by the Edo period (1603–1867) decorative tattooing had become widespread among labourers, firemen, and gamblers. Woodblock artists such as Utagawa Kuniyoshi popularised elaborate designs with his prints of the heroes of the Suikoden, featuring dragons, flowers, and religious imagery. Because tattooing was banned for much of the Meiji era and associated with criminality, it became linked with the yakuza, who used full-body tattoos as displays of pain tolerance and permanent commitment.

Methods

Traditionally, irezumi are applied by hand using the tebori technique, in which ink is pushed into the skin with a hand-held tool of needles bound to a bamboo or metal handle. The process is slow and painful, and a full body suit can take years of weekly visits and cost tens of thousands of dollars. Distinctive shading (bokashi) and the traditional limited palette — black, red, and later other colours — distinguish the style.

Motifs and social status

Common motifs include koi fish, dragons, phoenixes, cherry blossoms, chrysanthemums, and Buddhist deities such as Fudō Myōō. Because of the yakuza association, tattooed people have historically been barred from many onsen (hot springs), sentō (bathhouses), and gyms in Japan, though attitudes have softened, especially with the rise of tattooing among foreign visitors and young Japanese.
