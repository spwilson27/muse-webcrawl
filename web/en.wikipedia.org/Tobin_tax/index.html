Tobin tax

A Tobin tax is a tax on spot conversions of one currency into another, proposed by the Nobel laureate economist James Tobin in 1972, shortly after the collapse of the Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates. Tobin suggested a small, uniform international tax — he later spoke of around 0.5%, and later still of 0.1% — on foreign-exchange transactions to "throw some sand in the wheels" of short-term currency speculation, discouraging rapid round-trip trades while barely affecting trade and long-term investment tied to real economic fundamentals.

Rationale

Tobin argued that excessive short-run capital mobility limited the ability of national governments to run independent monetary policies and amplified financial crises. Because the tax would be paid on every conversion, it falls most heavily on very short-term speculators — a trader making daily round trips would face the tax hundreds of times a year — while adding a negligible cost to trade and foreign direct investment. Variants include the two-tier "Spahn tax" proposed by Paul Bernd Spahn, with a low normal rate and a punitive rate that activates during speculative attacks.

Debate

Supporters — including anti-globalisation movements, some development economists and, at various times, European governments — argue the tax could reduce volatility and raise very large revenues for public goods or development. Critics reply that currency trading would migrate to untaxed jurisdictions and untaxed instruments, that without universal adoption the tax is unenforceable, that much foreign-exchange turnover is hedging and market-making rather than speculation, and that reduced liquidity might increase rather than dampen volatility. Empirical studies of smaller financial transaction taxes give mixed evidence.

Adoption and related taxes

No global Tobin tax has been implemented. Sweden's financial transaction tax of 1984–1991 is often cited as a cautionary example: trading relocated to London and revenues disappointed. The idea influenced later proposals for broader financial transaction taxes; the European Union debated an FTT from 2011 onward and a subset of member states pursued one under enhanced cooperation, while individual countries including France and Italy introduced narrower taxes on share trading. Stamp duty on shares in the United Kingdom is a longer-lived relative.
