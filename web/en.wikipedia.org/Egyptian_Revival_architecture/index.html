Egyptian Revival is an architectural style that uses the motifs and imagery of ancient Egypt. It is attributed generally to the public awareness of ancient Egyptian monuments generated by Napoleon's conquest of Egypt in 1798, and later by the discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb in 1922. The style expressed itself in battered (sloping) walls, pylon-like entrances, cavetto cornices, lotus-bud columns, obelisks, sphinxes and hieroglyphic ornament.

First wave: after Napoleon

Napoleon's Egyptian campaign (1798–1801) and the lavish Description de l'Égypte sparked European "Egyptomania." Early examples include the Egyptian Hall in Piccadilly, London (1812), and various cemetery gates, prisons and synagogues in Britain, France and the United States, where the massive, solemn forms suited funerary and carceral architecture.

American examples

In the United States, the style was applied to prison design (The Tombs in New York, 1838), medical schools, Masonic temples and rural cemeteries following the rural cemetery movement. The Washington Monument (1848–1884) revived the obelisk form on a monumental scale.

Second wave: Tutankhamun

Howard Carter's 1922 discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb triggered a second wave, blending with Art Deco. It produced exuberant cinemas such as Grauman's Egyptian Theatre in Hollywood (1922) and factories and warehouses with Egyptian detailing during the 1920s.

Legacy

Though never as widespread as the Greek or Gothic revivals, Egyptian Revival left durable monuments and influenced later architecture, including the Luxor Hotel in Las Vegas (1993), a glass pyramid resort that is the style's most conspicuous late echo.
