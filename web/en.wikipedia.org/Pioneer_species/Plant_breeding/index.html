Plant breeding

Plant breeding is the science of changing the traits of plants in order to produce desired characteristics. It has been practiced for thousands of years, since the earliest domestication of crops, and in its modern form applies genetics, molecular biology and statistics to develop varieties with higher yields, better quality, resistance to pests and disease, and tolerance of drought, salinity and other stresses.

History

Farmers have selected seed from their best plants since the Neolithic, gradually transforming wild grasses and legumes into wheat, maize, rice and other staples. Scientific breeding began in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries with the rediscovery of Mendel's laws of inheritance, which allowed breeders to plan crosses deliberately. In the twentieth century, hybrid maize, mutation breeding and the semi-dwarf wheat and rice varieties of the Green Revolution — associated with breeders such as Norman Borlaug — dramatically raised global food production.

Methods

Classical breeding relies on controlled cross-pollination between parent lines, followed by selection over many generations; techniques include pedigree selection, backcrossing to transfer a single trait, and recurrent selection. Modern tools include marker-assisted selection, which uses DNA markers linked to desired genes; tissue culture and doubled haploids to speed up the production of uniform lines; mutation breeding using radiation or chemicals; and genetic engineering and genome editing, which introduce or alter specific genes directly.

Goals and issues

Breeding programs aim to improve yield potential and stability, nutritional quality, shelf life and suitability for mechanized harvest. Plant breeding also raises debates about genetic diversity — modern uniform varieties can displace landraces — and about intellectual property, since commercial varieties are protected by plant breeders' rights and patents in many countries.
