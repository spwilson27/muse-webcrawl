Plant hormone

Plant hormones (or phytohormones) are signal molecules produced within plants that occur in extremely low concentrations and regulate growth, development and responses to the environment. Unlike animal hormones, they are not produced in specialized glands; instead, many plant cells can synthesize them, and they may act near their site of production or be transported through the plant.

Major classes

The principal classes of plant hormones are:

  • Auxins (such as indole-3-acetic acid) promote cell elongation, mediate bending toward light (phototropism) and away from or toward gravity, and maintain apical dominance.
  • Gibberellins stimulate stem elongation, seed germination and flowering; dwarf crop varieties often result from reduced gibberellin production or response.
  • Cytokinins promote cell division and shoot growth and delay leaf senescence.
  • Abscisic acid promotes seed dormancy and closes stomata during drought.
  • Ethylene, a gas, drives fruit ripening and leaf abscission.
  • Brassinosteroids, jasmonates and salicylic acid regulate growth and defense against herbivores and pathogens; strigolactones control shoot branching and root interactions with fungi.

Signaling

Plant hormones act through receptors and signal-transduction pathways that change gene expression. Their effects depend heavily on concentration and on interactions between hormones: for example, the balance of auxin and cytokinin determines whether cultured plant tissue forms roots or shoots.

Applications

Synthetic plant hormones and growth regulators are widely used in agriculture and horticulture — auxin analogues as herbicides and rooting powders, gibberellins to enlarge seedless grapes, and ethylene-releasing or ethylene-blocking treatments to control fruit ripening in storage.
