Plant hunting

Plant hunting is the practice of traveling — often to remote and dangerous regions — to collect seeds, bulbs, cuttings and specimens of plants for gardens, herbaria, nurseries and scientific study. Plant hunters reshaped gardens and agriculture worldwide by introducing thousands of species new to cultivation.

Early collectors

Botanical exploration accompanied European voyages of discovery from the sixteenth century onward. In the eighteenth century, collectors such as Joseph Banks, who sailed with Captain Cook, and the Linnaean apostles carried plants around the globe. The invention of the Wardian case in the 1830s — a sealed glass container — greatly increased the survival of living plants on long sea voyages and made possible the transfer of crops such as tea and rubber between continents.

The golden age

The nineteenth and early twentieth centuries were the golden age of plant hunting. Commercial nurseries, botanical gardens and wealthy patrons sponsored collectors including Robert Fortune, who smuggled tea plants and knowledge out of China; David Douglas, after whom the Douglas-fir is named; Ernest Henry "Chinese" Wilson, who introduced hundreds of Chinese shrubs and trees; George Forrest in Yunnan; and the flamboyant Frank Kingdon-Ward. Many endured shipwreck, disease, robbery and war; some, like Douglas, died in the field.

Modern practice

Today collecting is governed by laws on biodiversity and access, including the Convention on Biological Diversity and national permit systems, and is conducted mainly by botanical institutions for conservation, research and seed banking, such as the Millennium Seed Bank. The ornamental gardens of Europe and North America remain living monuments to the earlier hunters: many familiar rhododendrons, magnolias, primulas and blue poppies arrived through their expeditions.
