Selective breeding

Selective breeding (also called artificial selection) is the process by which humans breed plants and animals for particular traits, choosing parents with desirable characteristics to reproduce. It is the mechanism behind the domestication of crops and livestock over thousands of years.

History

Farmers have selected seed and breeding stock since the Neolithic. Charles Darwin used artificial selection as an analogy for natural selection in On the Origin of Species (1859), citing the dramatic varieties produced in pigeons, dogs and cabbages. Systematic scientific plant breeding began in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and underlies modern agriculture.

Effects and concerns

Selective breeding has produced high-yielding cereals, dairy cattle and countless horticultural varieties, but intense selection can reduce genetic diversity and fix harmful traits alongside desired ones — a concern in several pedigree dog breeds and in genetically uniform crops vulnerable to epidemics.
