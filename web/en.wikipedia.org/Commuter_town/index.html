Commuter town

A commuter town is a populated place that is primarily residential, from which a large proportion of residents travel daily to work in a nearby larger city or employment centre. Such towns are known in North America as bedroom communities or dormitory suburbs, and in British English a settlement of this kind may also be called a dormitory town.

Characteristics

Commuter towns typically have little industry or commerce of their own beyond local services, and house prices and growth are strongly influenced by transport links — railways, motorways, or transit lines — to the employment centre. Their populations may fall sharply during working hours. Many grew from villages absorbed into the commuter belt of a metropolis during 19th- and 20th-century suburbanisation.

Examples

Around London, towns such as Guildford, Reading, and St Albans function substantially as commuter towns. In the United States, many suburbs of New York City, Boston, and Chicago in counties such as Westchester and DuPage are classic bedroom communities. Japanese cities such as Funabashi serve a similar role for Tokyo.

Issues

Dependence on commuting raises concerns about transport congestion, carbon emissions, the hollowing-out of local retail, and social life oriented elsewhere. Countervailing trends include the growth of home working, which has reduced daily commuting for some residents since 2020.
