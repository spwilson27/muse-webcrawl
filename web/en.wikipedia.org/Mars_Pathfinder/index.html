Mars Pathfinder

Mars Pathfinder (MESUR Pathfinder) was an American robotic spacecraft that landed a base station with a roving probe on Mars in 1997. Launched on December 4, 1996, it landed in Ares Vallis on July 4, 1997, and deployed Sojourner, the first rover to operate on another planet. The mission demonstrated a low-cost method of delivering a lander and rover to the Martian surface as part of NASA's Discovery Program.

Mission overview

Pathfinder was the second mission of NASA's Discovery Program, conceived under the "faster, better, cheaper" philosophy of the 1990s. Rather than a soft touchdown using retrorockets alone, the lander descended under a parachute and was cushioned by a cluster of giant airbags, which inflated seconds before impact; after bouncing and rolling to a stop, the airbags deflated and the lander's three triangular panels opened like petals.

The Sojourner rover

The 10.6 kg Sojourner rover — named for abolitionist and women's rights advocate Sojourner Truth — was a six-wheeled vehicle with a rocker-bogie suspension. It carried cameras and an Alpha Proton X-ray Spectrometer (APXS) to analyse the composition of rocks and soil. Although designed to last seven sols, it operated for 83 sols, travelling about 100 metres and analysing numerous rocks near the lander, which was renamed the Carl Sagan Memorial Station.

Scientific results

Pathfinder returned more than 16,500 images from the lander and 550 from the rover, along with chemical analyses of rocks and extensive weather data. The rounded pebbles and conglomerates found at the site suggested a warmer, wetter past with liquid water, and analyses showed Martian soil similar to that found by the Viking landers. Dust devils and morning water-ice frost were observed.

End of mission

The last successful data transmission was on September 27, 1997; after further unsuccessful attempts to re-establish contact, the mission was declared over on March 10, 1998, having outlived its design life many times over. The mission's landing technique was reused for the Mars Exploration Rovers Spirit and Opportunity in 2004.
