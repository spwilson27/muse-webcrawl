Embankment dam

An embankment dam is a large artificial dam made by placing and compacting a complex semi-plastic mound of various compositions of soil, sand, clay, or rock. It has a semi-permeable waterproof natural covering for its surface and a dense, impervious core. This makes such a dam resistant to surface or seepage erosion.

Types

Embankment dams are commonly divided into earthfill dams, made mostly of compacted soil, and rock-fill dams, made mainly of dumped and heavy rock fill, often with a clay core or an asphalt or concrete facing. A zoned embankment contains distinct zones of selected materials, with an impermeable core at its centre. Embankment dams are the most common type of dam worldwide because they can be built from locally available materials and adapt to a wide range of foundation conditions.

Failure modes

Potential failures include overtopping, internal erosion (piping), slope instability and liquefaction during earthquakes. The 1976 failure of the Teton Dam in Idaho, an earthfill dam, is a well-known example attributed to internal erosion.
