J. Stephen Lansing

Born: 1944 (age 80–81)
Nationality: American
Fields: Anthropology, complex systems
Institutions: University of Arizona; Santa Fe Institute
Known for: Balinese water temple networks and subak irrigation

John Stephen Lansing (born 1944) is an American anthropologist best known for his long-term research into the water temple networks of Bali, Indonesia, which coordinate the island's cooperative rice irrigation (subak) system. His work became a landmark case study in the self-organisation of complex adaptive systems.

Balinese irrigation research

Beginning with fieldwork in the 1970s and 1980s, Lansing documented how Balinese farmers' irrigation — the sharing of water and the synchronised timing of planting and fallow periods across whole watersheds — was coordinated not by kings or bureaucracies but by a nested hierarchy of water temples, whose priests set calendars of meetings and rituals. Such coordination controls both water scarcity and pests: synchronised fallows starve rice pests of habitat. During the Green Revolution, development planners bypassed the temples and imposed continuous cropping, producing pest outbreaks and water chaos — after which the temple system was substantially restored.

Modelling and theory

With ecologist James Kremer, Lansing built computer models showing that the temple networks' pattern of coordination could emerge from simple local interactions, as groups of farmers adjusted planting schedules in response to neighbours — an early and influential demonstration of emergent self-organisation in a human institution. His books include Priests and Programmers: Technologies of Power in the Engineered Landscape of Bali (1991), The Balinese (1995) and Perfect Order: Recognizing Complexity in Bali (2006). His research helped underpin the 2012 UNESCO World Heritage listing of the Balinese subak cultural landscape.

Career and later work

Lansing spent most of his career at the University of Arizona, where he is professor emeritus of anthropology, and has been closely associated with the Santa Fe Institute as an external professor and co-director of research projects on coupled human-and-natural systems. His later work extends the Bali approach to co-evolution of language, kinship and genetics in island Southeast Asia and the Pacific, and to sustainability questions in complex social-ecological systems.
