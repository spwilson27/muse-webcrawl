Werther effect

The Werther effect is an increase in suicides following a widely publicized suicide, a form of copycat behaviour. It is named after the wave of imitative suicides reported across Europe after the publication of Goethe's novel The Sorrows of Young Werther (1774), whose protagonist shoots himself after a hopeless love; young men dressed in Werther's yellow waistcoat and blue coat were found dead with copies of the book. Sociologist David Phillips coined the term in 1974 and documented statistical spikes in suicides after front-page suicide stories.

Evidence and mechanism

Research links the effect to social learning and identification: risk increases when reporting is sensational, gives explicit detail about methods, glamorizes the deceased, or presents suicide as an understandable response to adversity. Celebrity suicides produce the largest measured effects. The opposite phenomenon, the Papageno effect, describes the protective influence of stories of people who overcame suicidal crises.

Prevention

Media guidelines issued by the World Health Organization and national bodies advise against detailed descriptions of methods, prominent placement, and simplistic explanations, and encourage including help-line information. Responsible reporting has been shown to reduce imitative suicides.
