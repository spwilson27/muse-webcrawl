Seeing

In astronomy, seeing is the degradation of the image of an astronomical object caused by turbulence in the Earth's atmosphere. The same turbulence that makes stars twinkle blurs telescopic images: instead of a point, a star appears as a restless, dancing disc whose size — the "seeing disc" — is measured in arcseconds. Excellent sites achieve seeing below 0.5 arcseconds, while typical sites experience 1–2 arcseconds or worse.

Causes

Air of differing temperature has differing refractive index. Cells of turbulent air crossing the telescope's line of sight continually distort incoming wavefronts, so the image shimmers, swells and jumps. The effect is parameterised by the Fried parameter, r0, the aperture over which wavefronts remain roughly coherent — often only 10–20 cm at visible wavelengths at mediocre sites. Ground-level turbulence near the telescope, including heat rising from the dome and ground, contributes local or "dome" seeing.

Measurement and mitigation

Seeing is monitored with instruments such as the Differential Image Motion Monitor (DIMM), which measures the relative motion of two star images. Its impact can be mitigated by choosing high, dry, laminar-flow sites such as Mauna Kea, the Atacama Desert and Antarctica; by lucky imaging, which selects the sharpest frames from a high-speed sequence; and most powerfully by adaptive optics, in which deformable mirrors correct the wavefront hundreds of times per second using a guide star. Space telescopes avoid the problem altogether. Seeing improves at longer wavelengths, which is why infrared and radio observations suffer less.
