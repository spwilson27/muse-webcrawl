Misericordia

Misericordia (Latin for "mercy," from miser, wretched, and cor, heart) is a Latin term that gives its name to several charitable institutions, artworks, and devotional traditions in the Roman Catholic world.

The Arciconfraternita della Misericordia

The most famous bearer of the name is the Venerable Archconfraternity of Mercy (Arciconfraternita della Misericordia) of Florence, founded in 1244 and traditionally associated with the porter Piero di Luca Borsi. Its members—originally volunteers who carried the sick to hospital on stretchers—provided burial of the dead, care of the ill, and assistance to the poor, famously continuing their work during plagues while wearing black robes and hoods that concealed their identity so that charity was performed anonymously. The Florentine model spread across Italy and the Portuguese and Spanish worlds, where lay brotherhoods called Misericórdias ran hospitals, orphanages, and welfare services; the Santa Casa da Misericórdia of Lisbon, founded in 1498 under Queen Leonor, is the prototype in Portugal and its former colonies.

Other uses

In church art, the Madonna della Misericordia (Our Lady of Mercy) is an iconographic type showing the Virgin sheltering supplicants beneath her outspread cloak, as in Piero della Francesca's polyptych in Sansepolcro. "Misericordia" also denotes the small ledge on the underside of a choir stall seat—the misericord—that supported a standing worshipper during long services, and a short medieval dagger used to deliver a coup de grâce, the misericorde.
