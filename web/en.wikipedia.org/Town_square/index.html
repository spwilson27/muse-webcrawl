Town square

A market square in a historic town

A town square (also a public square, urban square, or simply the square) is an open public space commonly found in the heart of a traditional town or city, used for community gatherings. Most squares are hardscapes suitable for open markets, concerts, political rallies, and other events requiring a firm flat surface, and they are typically framed by the town's most important buildings — the town hall, church, law courts, and shops.

History

The town square descends from ancient gathering spaces such as the Greek agora and the Roman forum, where commerce, politics, and ceremony converged. In medieval Europe, market squares were chartered centres of trade, often dominated by a market cross and surrounded by guildhalls. Planned squares became set pieces of Renaissance and Baroque urban design (the plaza mayor in Spain, the royal squares of Paris and London), and colonial towns across the Americas were laid out around central plazas under ordinances such as the Laws of the Indies. In New England, the equivalent space is the town green or common.

Functions

Squares remain focal points of civic life: sites of markets and festivals, everyday socializing, protest and celebration alike. Famous examples — from St Mark's Square in Venice and the Grand-Place in Brussels to Red Square in Moscow and Mexico City's Zócalo — demonstrate the square's enduring role as a symbol of the city itself. Urbanists such as William H. Whyte have studied what makes squares succeed: seating, food, water, trees, and "triangulation" that draws strangers into conversation.
