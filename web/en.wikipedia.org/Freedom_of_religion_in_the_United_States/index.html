Freedom of religion in the United States

Freedom of religion in the United States is constitutionally protected by the religion clauses of the First Amendment to the U.S. Constitution: the Establishment Clause prohibits the government from establishing an official religion, and the Free Exercise Clause prohibits the government from interfering with the free exercise of religion. The United States thus has no state church, and religious liberty — historically championed by dissenting Protestants, Thomas Jefferson, and James Madison — is among the nation's founding principles.

Historical development

Many English colonies in America were founded by religious dissenters, yet most still established official churches and restricted dissent; Rhode Island, founded by Roger Williams, was an early exception. After independence, Virginia's Statute for Religious Freedom (1786), written by Jefferson, disestablished the Anglican Church and became a model for the First Amendment (1791). State establishments lingered — Massachusetts did not disestablish until 1833 — and in the 19th century religious minorities such as Catholics, Mormons, and Jehovah's Witnesses faced discrimination despite constitutional guarantees.

Modern doctrine

In the 20th century the Supreme Court applied the religion clauses to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment (Cantwell v. Connecticut, 1940; Everson v. Board of Education, 1947). Landmark rulings have barred state-sponsored prayer and Bible reading in public schools (Engel v. Vitale, 1962; Abington School District v. Schempp, 1963), protected religious exemptions in some contexts (Wisconsin v. Yoder, 1972), and, more recently, held that religious organizations may not be excluded from generally available public benefits (Trinity Lutheran v. Comer, 2017; Carson v. Makin, 2022) and that a public school coach's private prayer was protected (Kennedy v. Bremerton, 2022). The Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 and state counterparts add statutory protections.
