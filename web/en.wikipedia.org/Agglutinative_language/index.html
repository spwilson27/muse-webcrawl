Agglutinative language

An agglutinative language is a type of synthetic language with systems of affixation that allow a great number of morphemes to be combined, each corresponding to a single syntactic feature. In agglutinative languages, words are formed by joining morphemes together in strings, and the boundaries between morphemes remain clear — unlike fusional languages, where affixes combine several meanings and fuse with the stem.

Characteristics

Agglutinative languages tend to have a high rate of affixes per word and transparent, regular morphology with little allomorphy beyond regular phonological processes such as vowel harmony. Verbs and nouns can become extremely long: the Turkish word Çekoslovakyalılaştıramadıklarımızdanmışsınızcasına is a famous illustrative example built entirely from a stem plus a chain of suffixes.

Examples

Typical agglutinative languages include Turkish, Finnish, Hungarian, Estonian, Japanese, Korean, Swahili, Quechua, and many languages of the Caucasus and Siberia. The Uralic and Turkic families are strongly agglutinative, as are Basque and Georgian to a large degree.

Typology

Agglutination is one pole of morphological typology alongside isolating and fusional types; most languages mix strategies, and agglutination is a matter of degree rather than an absolute classification.
