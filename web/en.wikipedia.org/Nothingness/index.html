Nothingness

Nothingness is the state of being nothing: the absence of anything, or the complete lack of existence. It is a central topic in philosophy, where it raises the question — posed by Leibniz and taken up by Heidegger — of "why there is something rather than nothing." The concept is notoriously difficult, since speaking of nothing seems to treat it as a thing.

Philosophy

In ancient Greek thought, Parmenides argued that "what is not" cannot be thought or spoken of, and that non-being is impossible; the atomists Leucippus and Democritus instead admitted the void as real. In modern philosophy, Sartre's Being and Nothingness (1943) treats nothingness (le néant) as introduced into the world by consciousness. Heidegger, in What Is Metaphysics? (1929), held that anxiety reveals the Nothing. Logical positivists such as Carnap dismissed such talk as meaningless metaphysics, while in mathematics and set theory related ideas appear as the empty set and zero.

In Eastern thought and religion

Buddhist philosophy speaks of śūnyatā (emptiness), the doctrine that phenomena lack independent, inherent existence — not sheer non-existence but interdependence, a distinction Madhyamaka philosophers insist upon. Daoism values the usefulness of emptiness, as in the hollow of a vessel. Some theologians, conversely, describe creation ex nihilo — God creating the world out of nothing.
