Imperial China

Imperial China refers to the period of Chinese history from the unification of China under the Qin dynasty in 221 BC until the abdication of the last emperor, Puyi, in 1912, following the Xinhai Revolution. For over two millennia, China was ruled by a succession of imperial dynasties headed by an emperor regarded as the Son of Heaven and holder of the Mandate of Heaven.

Dynasties

The major dynasties of imperial China, in sequence, include:

  • Qin (221–206 BC) — first unification under Qin Shi Huang; standardized script, weights, and measures.
  • Han (206 BC – AD 220) — consolidated imperial government; Confucianism became state orthodoxy.
  • Periods of division, followed by the Sui (581–618) reunification and the Tang (618–907), a golden age of culture and expansion.
  • Song (960–1279) — era of economic and technological flourishing.
  • Yuan (1271–1368) — Mongol rule founded by Kublai Khan.
  • Ming (1368–1644) — restored native rule; maritime expeditions of Zheng He.
  • Qing (1644–1912) — Manchu rule; China reached its greatest territorial extent before decline in the 19th century.

Government and society

Imperial government was administered by a scholar-official bureaucracy recruited largely through the imperial examination system, which tested candidates on Confucian classics. Society was organized hierarchically, with the emperor at its apex, and Confucian ideals emphasized order, filial piety, and meritocratic administration in principle.

End of the empire

Weakened by internal rebellions, foreign encroachment, and the Opium Wars, the Qing dynasty fell in the Xinhai Revolution of 1911. The Republic of China was proclaimed on January 1, 1912, and the last emperor abdicated on February 12, 1912, ending more than 2,000 years of imperial rule.
