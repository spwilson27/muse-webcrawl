Upanishads

The Upanishads are Sanskrit texts that form the philosophical core of Hindu thought.

The Upanishads (/ʊˈpʌnɪʃədz/; Sanskrit: उपनिषद्, Upaniṣad) are late Vedic and post-Vedic Sanskrit texts that "document the transition from the archaic ritualism of the Veda into new religious ideas and institutions" and the emergence of central religious concepts of Hinduism. They are the most recent addition to the Vedas, the oldest scriptures of Hinduism, and deal with meditation, philosophy, consciousness, and ontological knowledge; earlier parts of the Vedas deal with mantras, benedictions, rituals, ceremonies, and sacrifices.

Composed in the first millennium BCE, the Upanishads are among the first texts to explore the ideas of ātman (self) and Brahman (ultimate reality), the doctrine of rebirth (saṃsāra), and liberation (mokṣa). Together with the Bhagavad Gita and the Brahma Sutras, the principal Upanishads provide a foundation for the later schools of Vedānta philosophy. The word Upaniṣad is traditionally interpreted as "sitting down near" — a student seated near a teacher to receive secret instruction.

Development

The oldest Upanishads, such as the Bṛhadāraṇyaka and Chāndogya, date to roughly the mid-first millennium BCE and are attached to Vedic schools (śākhās). Of more than 200 extant Upanishads, a group of about thirteen "principal" (mukhya) Upanishads are regarded as the most ancient and authoritative. Later texts continued to be composed under the name Upanishad well into the medieval and early modern periods, including texts associated with Yoga, Sannyasa (renunciation), and the worship of deities such as Vishnu and Shiva.

Principal Upanishads

  • Bṛhadāraṇyaka — among the oldest, featuring the sage Yājñavalkya and the teaching neti neti ("not this, not this").
  • Chāndogya — source of the famous dictum tat tvam asi ("that thou art").
  • Taittirīya, Aitareya, Kauṣītaki — early prose Upanishads.
  • Kena, Kaṭha, Īśa, Śvetāśvatara, Muṇḍaka — verse and prose texts exploring the self and the absolute.
  • Praśna, Māṇḍūkya — the latter, the shortest, expounds the syllable Om and the states of consciousness.

Philosophy

The Upanishads ask questions the earlier Vedic ritual texts had not: What is the self? What survives death? What is the ground of the world? Their characteristic teaching is the identity of the inner self (ātman) with the universal principle (Brahman): "I am Brahman" (aham brahmāsmi). Knowledge of this identity, rather than ritual performance alone, brings release from the cycle of rebirth driven by karma. The texts also develop early doctrines of meditation, breath control, and renunciation that influenced Buddhism and Jainism as well as later Hindu philosophy.

Influence and translations

The Upanishads were translated into Persian in the 17th century under the Mughal prince Dara Shikoh (Sirr-i-Akbar, "The Great Secret"). A Latin translation of that Persian version by Anquetil Duperron (Oupnek'hat, 1801–1802) reached the German philosopher Arthur Schopenhauer, who called the Upanishads "the consolation of my life." In the 19th and 20th centuries, translations by scholars such as Max Müller and popularisers such as Swami Vivekananda carried their ideas worldwide, shaping modern yoga, the Vedanta movement, and Western interest in Indian spirituality.
