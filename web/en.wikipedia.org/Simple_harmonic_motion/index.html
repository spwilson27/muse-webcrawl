Simple harmonic motion

In mechanics and physics, simple harmonic motion (SHM) is a special type of periodic motion in which the restoring force on a moving object is directly proportional to, and oppositely directed to, the object's displacement from its equilibrium position. Because the resulting motion is sinusoidal in time, a system so described is called a harmonic oscillator.

Definition and mathematics

By Newton's second law and Hooke's law, a mass m attached to a spring of stiffness k obeys m x″ = −k x, whose general solution is x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ), where the angular frequency is ω = √(k/m), A is the amplitude and φ the phase. The period is T = 2π√(m/k) and the frequency f = 1/T. The motion's velocity is greatest at the equilibrium position and zero at the extremes; total mechanical energy is conserved and oscillates between kinetic and potential forms, each proportional to the square of the amplitude.

Examples

Besides the mass on a spring, examples include the simple pendulum (approximately harmonic for small swings, with period T ≈ 2π√(L/g)), the torsion pendulum, a floating object bobbing in water, and the vibrations of molecules about their equilibrium positions. In electrical engineering, the charge in an undamped LC circuit oscillates harmonically, and alternating current is itself sinusoidal.

Dynamics and resonance

Real oscillators experience damping, which gradually reduces the amplitude, and may be driven by an external periodic force, giving rise to resonance when the driving frequency matches the natural frequency. Simple harmonic motion also provides the building block of Fourier analysis: any periodic motion can be decomposed into a sum of harmonic components. The quantum harmonic oscillator, whose energy levels are evenly spaced, is a cornerstone model of quantum mechanics.
