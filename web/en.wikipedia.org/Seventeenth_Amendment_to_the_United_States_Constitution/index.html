Seventeenth Amendment to the United States Constitution

The Seventeenth Amendment to the United States Constitution established the direct election of United States senators in each state by popular vote. It superseded Article I, Section 3 of the Constitution, under which senators were elected by state legislatures. The amendment also outlines the process for filling vacancies in the Senate. It was passed by Congress on May 13, 1912, and ratified by the requisite three-fourths of states on April 8, 1913.

Background

Under the original Constitution, state legislatures chose senators, a design intended to give states a direct role in the federal government. By the late 19th century, problems including deadlocks that left seats empty, corruption, and the influence of corporate interests spurred reform. Many states adopted mechanisms such as advisory popular elections (the "Oregon System") that bound legislators to the voters' choice, effectively achieving direct election before the amendment.

Text

"The Senate of the United States shall be composed of two Senators from each State, elected by the people thereof, for six years; and each Senator shall have one vote. The electors in each State shall have the qualifications requisite for electors of the most numerous branch of the State legislatures."

Effects

The amendment shifted senatorial accountability directly to voters and is a landmark of the Progressive Era. Vacancies are filled by special elections, though state legislatures may empower governors to make temporary appointments until an election is held.
