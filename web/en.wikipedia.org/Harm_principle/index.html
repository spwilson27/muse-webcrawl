Harm principle

The harm principle holds that the actions of individuals should be limited only to prevent harm to other individuals. It was formulated by John Stuart Mill in his essay On Liberty (1859): "the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others."

Statement and scope

Mill argued that a person's own good, whether physical or moral, is not sufficient warrant for coercion: over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign. The principle applies only to adults of sound mind in "civilised" societies, and only to conduct that affects others; purely self-regarding conduct — even if foolish or self-destructive — is beyond the legitimate reach of law and social sanction. Mill grounded the principle in utility "in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of man as a progressive being".

Related ideas

Mill distinguished harm from mere offence, arguing that offence alone does not justify restriction — a distinction later elaborated by legal philosopher Joel Feinberg as the separate "offence principle". The harm principle underlies much of modern liberal thought and debates over paternalism, victimless crimes, drug prohibition, and freedom of speech.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether a clean line can be drawn between self-regarding and other-regarding acts, since most behaviour ripples outward to affect others; whether "harm" can be defined independently of contested moral judgements; and whether the principle gives inadequate weight to communal values and to harms to society as a whole. Communitarian and conservative critics, such as James Fitzjames Stephen and Patrick Devlin, argued that society may legitimately enforce shared morality beyond preventing direct harm.
