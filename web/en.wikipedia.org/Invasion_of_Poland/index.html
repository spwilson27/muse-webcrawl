Invasion of Poland

The invasion of Poland was a joint attack on the Republic of Poland by Nazi Germany, the Slovak Republic, and the Soviet Union, which marked the beginning of the Second World War. The German invasion began on 1 September 1939, one week after the signing of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact between Germany and the Soviet Union. The Soviets invaded Poland on 17 September.

Background

Under the secret protocol of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, Germany and the USSR agreed to partition Poland. Hitler sought to regain territory lost after the First World War, including the Polish Corridor and Danzig, and to secure Lebensraum in the east. A staged provocation, the Gleiwitz incident of 31 August 1939, was used to justify the attack.

The campaign

Germany attacked from the north, west, and south with over 1.5 million men, employing fast-moving armoured and motorised formations supported by the Luftwaffe — tactics later called Blitzkrieg. Polish forces, partially mobilised and deployed along long frontiers, fought delaying actions including the defence of Westerplatte and the Battle of Bzura, the largest Polish counteroffensive.

Britain and France declared war on Germany on 3 September but provided little direct assistance. The Soviet invasion from the east on 17 September doomed the Polish defence plan of withdrawing to the Romanian bridgehead. Warsaw capitulated on 28 September after heavy bombing, and the last large Polish formation surrendered at Kock on 6 October.

Aftermath

Poland was partitioned between Germany and the USSR along the Bug River. Both occupiers carried out mass repression: Germany began the Intelligenzaktion and later the Holocaust on Polish soil, while the Soviets deported hundreds of thousands to Siberia and murdered Polish officers at Katyń. The Polish government-in-exile and underground state continued the struggle; Poland never formally surrendered.
