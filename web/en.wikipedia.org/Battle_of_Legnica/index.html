Battle of Legnica

The Battle of Legnica (Polish: Bitwa pod Legnicą), also known as the Battle of Liegnitz or the Battle of Wahlstatt, was fought on 9 April 1241 near the town of Legnica in Silesia, Poland, during the Mongol invasion of Europe. A combined Polish and allied force under Henry II the Pious, Duke of Silesia, was decisively defeated by a Mongol detachment under Baidar, Kadan and Orda Khan.

Background

The Mongol invasion of 1241 struck Europe on two axes: the main army under Batu Khan and Subutai invaded Hungary, while a northern detachment of perhaps 20,000 men ravaged Poland to prevent Polish and Bohemian forces from aiding the Hungarians. After sacking Kraków and Wrocław, the Mongols encountered Henry II's army—Silesian and Polish knights, infantry, military orders including small contingents of Templars and Hospitallers, and miners from Złotoryja—near Legnica.

The battle

The Mongols employed their characteristic tactics of feigned flight and envelopment. A smoke screen may have been used to conceal their movements—some accounts describe devices producing smoke, occasionally cited as an early instance of chemical warfare. Henry's cavalry charged and was drawn out, enveloped by horse archers, and destroyed. Henry II was killed on the field; legend holds that his head was paraded on a spear before the walls of Legnica. The Mongols are said to have filled nine sacks with ears cut from the dead.

Aftermath

Two days later the main Mongol army destroyed the Hungarians at the Battle of Mohi. However, the Mongols did not press westward into Bohemia in force, where Wenceslaus I had entrenched strong positions; after ravaging Moravia they withdrew to Hungary, and in 1242 the Mongols left Europe altogether following the death of Ögedei Khan. Legnica became a symbol of Polish sacrifice in defence of Christian Europe.
