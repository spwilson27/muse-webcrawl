Magnificat

The Magnificat (Latin for "[My soul] magnifies [the Lord]") is a canticle from the Gospel of Luke (1:46–55), spoken by Mary during her visit to her cousin Elizabeth. Also known as the Song of Mary or the Ode of the Theotokos, it is one of the most ancient Christian hymns and is sung daily at Vespers (Evening Prayer) in the Roman Catholic Church, at Evensong in Anglican worship, and in Eastern Orthodox services.

The Magnificat is Mary's song in response to Elizabeth's greeting at the Visitation.

Text and themes

The canticle begins "My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Saviour." It celebrates God's mercy toward the humble — "He has brought down the powerful from their thrones, and lifted up the lowly; he has filled the hungry with good things, and sent the rich away empty" — and God's faithfulness to Israel in fulfilment of promises to Abraham. Scholars note its strong echoes of Hannah's song in 1 Samuel 2 and of the Psalms; its socially subversive imagery has made it a touchstone for liberation theology, and it was reportedly restricted or banned by some authoritarian regimes (including Guatemala's in the 1980s) because of those verses.

Liturgical use

Since the early medieval period the Magnificat has been the climax of Vespers in the West and is also sung at Lauds' counterpart services elsewhere in the day in some traditions. In the Book of Common Prayer it forms, with the Nunc dimittis, the core of Evensong; congregations traditionally stand for its singing. In Byzantine worship it appears at Matins with the refrain "More honourable than the Cherubim…" interwoven between verses.

Musical settings

Composers have set the Magnificat hundreds of times, from plainsong tones and Renaissance polyphony (Palestrina, Lassus) to Monteverdi's setting in the 1610 Vespers and Vivaldi's RV 610. J. S. Bach's Magnificat in D major, BWV 243 (1723/1733), is among the best known. Mendelssohn, Rachmaninoff (in his All-Night Vigil, as the Song of the Theotokos), Penderecki, Arvo Pärt, and John Rutter have all composed notable versions. In Anglican cathedral music, paired settings of Magnificat and Nunc dimittis ("Mag and Nunc") by Stanford, Howells, and others are staples of the repertoire.
