Candida albicans

Candida albicans is an opportunistic pathogenic yeast and a common member of the human gut flora. It is the most frequent cause of candidiasis in humans and is among the most common fungal infections worldwide, particularly in immunocompromised patients.

Biology

C. albicans is a diploid fungus that grows both as a yeast and as filamentous hyphae or pseudohyphae, a morphological flexibility (dimorphism) closely linked to its virulence. It normally colonizes the gastrointestinal tract, mouth, skin, and genital tract of about half of healthy adults without causing disease. Its genome of roughly 14 million base pairs is carried on eight pairs of chromosomes.

Pathogenicity

When host defences are weakened or the normal bacterial flora is disturbed — for example by antibiotics — C. albicans can overgrow and cause infections ranging from superficial thrush and vaginal yeast infections to life-threatening invasive candidiasis of the bloodstream (candidemia). Virulence factors include adhesins, secreted proteases, biofilm formation, and the ability to switch morphology. The peptide toxin candidalysin damages host epithelial cells.

Treatment

Superficial infections are treated with topical antifungals such as clotrimazole; systemic infections require azoles (e.g., fluconazole), echinocandins, or amphotericin B. Drug resistance, though less common than in some related species, is an increasing clinical concern.

Model organism

C. albicans is an important model organism in fungal biology and medical mycology, though its obligate diploidy and unusual codon usage (CUG translated as serine rather than leucine) complicate genetic studies.
