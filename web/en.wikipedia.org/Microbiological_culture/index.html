Microbiological culture

A microbiological culture, or microbial culture, is a method of multiplying microbial organisms by letting them reproduce in predetermined culture medium under controlled laboratory conditions. Microbial cultures are foundational and basic diagnostic methods used as a research tool in molecular biology. The term culture can also refer to the microorganisms being grown themselves.

Culture media

A growth medium or culture medium is a liquid or gel designed to support the growth of microorganisms. Media differ in the nutrients they supply: general-purpose media such as nutrient broth support many organisms, while selective media contain agents that favour particular species, and differential media contain indicators that distinguish organisms by their metabolic activity. Agar, a polysaccharide derived from seaweed, is the most common solidifying agent; it was introduced into bacteriology in the 1880s through the work of Fanny Hesse in Robert Koch's laboratory.

Types of culture

Pure culture

A pure culture contains only a single species of organism. It is obtained by streaking a diluted sample across an agar plate so that individual cells give rise to separate colonies, a technique developed in the laboratory of Robert Koch. Pure cultures are essential for identifying pathogens and for most experimental work.

Batch and continuous culture

In batch culture, organisms grow in a fixed volume of medium and pass through lag, exponential (log), stationary and death phases. In continuous culture, exemplified by the chemostat, fresh medium is supplied and culture removed at a constant rate, holding the population in a steady state and allowing precise control of growth rate.

Liquid and solid culture

Liquid (broth) cultures allow large quantities of organisms to be grown for biochemical study, while solid media on plates or slants permit isolation of colonies and enumeration of viable cells.

Applications

Microbial cultures are used in clinical diagnosis, where pathogens from patient samples are isolated and tested for antibiotic sensitivity; in the food and fermentation industries; in the production of antibiotics, enzymes and vaccines; and in basic research on genetics and cell biology. Cell culture of animal and plant tissues is a related but distinct technique.

Preservation

Cultures are preserved for long-term storage by refrigeration, freezing, freeze-drying (lyophilisation), or storage in glycerol at −80 °C. National culture collections, such as the American Type Culture Collection, maintain reference strains for research and industry.
