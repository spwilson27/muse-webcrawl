Analog electronics

Analog electronics (or analogue electronics) are electronic systems with a continuously variable signal, in contrast to digital electronics where signals usually take only two discrete levels. The term "analog" describes the proportional relationship between a signal and a voltage or current that represents it.

Signals and circuits

Analog signals can take any value within a range and are used to represent sound, light, temperature, position, and pressure, typically converted into electrical form by transducers such as microphones and sensors. Analog circuits are built from passive components (resistors, capacitors, inductors) and active devices (transistors and operational amplifiers), and perform functions including amplification, filtering, oscillation, modulation, and mixing.

Noise and bandwidth

Because analog signals are continuous, they are inherently susceptible to noise and degradation: every amplification or transmission stage adds distortion and noise that cannot be perfectly removed, unlike digital signals, which can be regenerated. Analog design therefore emphasizes noise performance, linearity, and bandwidth. Historically, radio, television, telephony, and audio equipment were entirely analog; most such systems are now digital, but analog circuits remain essential at the interfaces to the physical world — in radio front ends, power supplies, sensor conditioning, and data converters.
