In vitro diagnostic

In vitro diagnostics (IVD) are medical diagnostic tests that are performed on samples that have been taken from the human body, such as blood, urine, stool, or tissue — typically in a laboratory or at the point of care. The term in vitro ("in glass") reflects that the tests are conducted outside a living organism, in contrast to in vivo tests performed inside the body.

Examples

IVD tests include blood glucose monitoring, blood chemistry and hematology panels, immunoassays (such as pregnancy tests and HIV tests), molecular diagnostics (such as PCR tests for infectious diseases and genetic testing), microbiology cultures, and coagulation tests. Results guide diagnosis, treatment selection, disease monitoring, and screening of asymptomatic individuals.

Regulation

In vitro diagnostics are regulated as medical devices in most jurisdictions. In the United States they are regulated by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), which classes most IVDs by risk. In the European Union, the In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR, Regulation (EU) 2017/746) replaced the earlier directive and introduced a risk-based classification (classes A through D) with stricter requirements for clinical evidence and notified-body review. Companion diagnostics — tests essential for the safe and effective use of a corresponding drug — receive particular regulatory attention.

Market

The global IVD market is one of the largest segments of the medical device industry, driven by demand for infectious disease testing, chronic disease management, and personalized medicine. The COVID-19 pandemic produced a dramatic expansion of molecular and antigen testing capacity.
