Motion picture content rating system

A motion picture content rating system classifies films according to their suitability for audiences, usually based on violent, sexual, or profane content and other mature themes. Most countries have some form of classification, administered by a government body, an industry association, or (in the case of the United States) a voluntary industry system operated by the Motion Picture Association (MPA).

History

In the United States, moral pressure led the industry to adopt the Hays Code (Motion Picture Production Code) in 1930, enforced from 1934, which censored film content directly. The Code collapsed in the 1960s and was replaced in 1968 by the MPA rating system, which classifies rather than censors. Other countries developed their own boards: the British Board of Film Classification (founded 1912) and Australia's Classification Board are prominent examples.

Other systems

The United Kingdom's BBFC uses U, PG, 12A, 12, 15, 18, and R18 categories. Germany's FSK uses age thresholds of 0, 6, 12, 16, and 18. Australia's ACB uses G, PG, M, MA15+, R18+, and X18+. Some systems are advisory, others legally restrictive, and a number of countries ban films outright or require cuts.

Comparison and criticism

Ratings differ sharply between countries: a film rated R in the US may be 15 or 18 in the UK or unrestricted elsewhere. Critics argue that industry-run systems favour violence over sexual content, that NC-17 functions as a commercial stigma, and that rating creep has made categories progressively more permissive over time.
