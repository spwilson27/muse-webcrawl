Prime number

A prime number (or a prime) is a natural number greater than 1 that is not a product of two smaller natural numbers. Its only divisors are 1 and itself: the first few primes are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23 and 29. A natural number greater than 1 that is not prime is called composite. The number 1 is neither prime nor composite, by convention, so that the fundamental theorem of arithmetic holds uniquely.

Fundamental properties

The fundamental theorem of arithmetic states that every integer greater than 1 factors uniquely (up to order) into primes, making primes the "atoms" of the integers. Euclid proved around 300 BCE that there are infinitely many primes. The distribution of primes is governed asymptotically by the prime number theorem: the number of primes less than n is approximately n/ln n. The most famous unsolved problem about primes is the Riemann hypothesis, concerning the zeros of the Riemann zeta function, which encodes detailed information about prime gaps.

Finding and testing primes

The sieve of Eratosthenes efficiently lists all primes up to a bound. For very large numbers, probabilistic primality tests such as Miller–Rabin and the deterministic AKS test are used. The largest known prime is almost always a Mersenne prime of the form 2p − 1, found by distributed projects such as GIMPS; as of the mid-2020s the largest known prime has more than 41 million decimal digits.

Applications

Primes are central to modern cryptography: the security of the RSA cryptosystem rests on the practical difficulty of factoring the product of two large primes, a problem also relevant to number theory more broadly. Primes also appear in hash functions, error-correcting codes and quasi-random number generation, and in nature in the life cycles of periodical cicadas.
