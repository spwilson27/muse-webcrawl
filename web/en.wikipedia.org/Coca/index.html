Coca is a plant in the family Erythroxylaceae, native to western South America, whose leaves contain alkaloids including cocaine. The two cultivated species, Erythroxylum coca and Erythroxylum novogranatense, have been grown in the Andean region for thousands of years. Coca leaves are traditionally chewed or brewed as a tea; they are also the raw material from which cocaine is extracted.

Traditional use

Andean peoples chew coca leaves, held in the cheek with a small amount of an alkaline substance such as lime or ash, for their mild stimulant effect, which suppresses hunger, thirst, pain, and fatigue—valuable at the high altitudes of the Andes, where coca is also used against altitude sickness and in religious and social rituals. Coca tea (mate de coca) is a common beverage in Bolivia and Peru. In this traditional form, the amount of cocaine absorbed is small and slow, and the practice holds deep cultural significance.

Cocaine production and control

Because coca leaf is the source of cocaine, its cultivation is regulated under international drug control treaties; the 1961 Single Convention listed coca leaf as a controlled substance while allowing a temporary exemption for traditional chewing, an arrangement Bolivia later renegotiated to protect domestic traditional use. Colombia, Peru, and Bolivia remain the main producers, and eradication campaigns, alternative development programs, and conflicts with growers (known as cocaleros) have been major political issues in the region for decades.

History

Archaeological evidence shows coca use in the Andes stretching back several millennia. Spanish colonial authorities first condemned and then taxed the trade, since coca was essential to the labor of Indigenous miners in Potosí's silver mines. In the nineteenth century, coca and its derivative cocaine became fashionable in Europe and North America, appearing in wines and in the original formula of Coca-Cola, which still uses a decocainized coca leaf extract for flavoring.
