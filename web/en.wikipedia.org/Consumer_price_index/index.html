Consumer price index

A consumer price index (CPI) is a statistical estimate constructed using the prices of a sample of representative items whose prices are collected periodically. Sub-indices and sub-sub-indices can be computed for different categories and sub-categories of goods and services, being combined to produce the overall index, with weights reflecting their shares in the total of the consumer expenditures covered by the index. The annual percentage change in a CPI is used as a measure of inflation.

Calculation

Statistical agencies track the prices of a fixed "basket" of goods and services — food, housing, apparel, transportation, medical care, recreation and other items — purchased by typical households. The index compares the cost of that basket in a given period with its cost in a base period, conventionally set to 100. Weights are derived from consumer expenditure surveys and updated periodically.

Uses

CPIs are used to adjust wages, pensions, tax brackets and contracts for inflation (indexation), to deflate nominal economic data into real terms, and by central banks as an indicator in setting monetary policy. In the United States, the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the CPI-U (for urban consumers) monthly; the European Union publishes the Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP).

Limitations

Critics note substitution bias (consumers shifting toward cheaper alternatives), quality changes that are difficult to price, the treatment of owner-occupied housing, and the fact that a single average index may poorly represent the inflation experienced by particular groups, such as pensioners or low-income households. Agencies address some issues through hedonic adjustment and chained indices.
