William Caxton (c. 1422 – c. 1491) was an English merchant, diplomat, writer and printer. He is thought to be the first person to introduce a printing press into England in 1476, and as a printer was the first English retailer of printed books. His parentage and date of birth are not known for certain, but he may have been born in the Weald of Kent. In 1471 he went to Cologne and learned the art of printing there; he set up a press in Bruges in association with a Flemish calligrapher, Colard Mansion.

Printing in Bruges and Westminster

At Bruges Caxton printed the first book in the English language, the Recuyell of the Historyes of Troye (c. 1473–74), his own translation of a French romance of the Trojan War, undertaken for Margaret of York, Duchess of Burgundy. Returning to England, he established a press at Westminster in 1476. Among his earliest Westminster publications were Chaucer's Canterbury Tales (1477) and the first dated book printed in England, the Dictes or Sayengis of the Philosophres (1477).

Output and influence

Caxton's press issued more than a hundred books, many of them his own translations from French, Latin and Dutch — including Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur (1485), Aesop's Fables, the Golden Legend and editions of Chaucer, Gower and Lydgate. As a translator and editor he helped standardise the English language through print, choosing the East Midlands–London dialect that became the basis of standard English. His prologues and epilogues are valuable sources for fifteenth-century literary taste. He died around 1491 and was buried at St Margaret's, Westminster; his assistant Wynkyn de Worde succeeded to the business.
