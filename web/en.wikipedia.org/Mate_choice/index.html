Mate choice

Mate choice is one of the primary mechanisms of sexual selection, the other being competition within a sex for access to mates. In mate choice, members of one sex — typically the sex investing more in offspring, often females — preferentially select mates with particular traits, causing those traits to evolve even when they reduce survival. Charles Darwin proposed the idea in The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex (1871).

Mechanisms

Several models explain why choosers prefer particular ornaments or displays. Direct benefits: the chosen mate provides food, territory, protection, or parental care. Good genes: the trait honestly signals health or genetic quality, as in the handicap principle proposed by Amotz Zahavi. Fisherian runaway: a preference and a trait become genetically correlated and exaggerate each other. Sensory bias: preferences arise from pre-existing features of the chooser's sensory system.

Examples

Peahens prefer peacocks with larger, more eyespotted trains; female stalk-eyed flies prefer males with wider eye spans; female guppies prefer brightly coloured males, though predation pressure dampens the preference. In humans, mate choice is mutual and involves cues of health, resources, status, and compatibility, shaped by culture as well as biology.

Mate choice copying

In some species, individuals copy the choices of others: female guppies and sage grouse, for example, are more likely to select a male they have seen mating or being chosen, a form of social information use that can rapidly spread preferences through a population.
