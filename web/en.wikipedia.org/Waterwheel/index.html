A waterwheel is a machine for converting the energy of flowing or falling water into useful forms of power, often in a watermill. A waterwheel consists of a wheel (usually constructed from wood or metal), with a number of blades or buckets arranged on the outside rim forming the driving surface. Water wheels were still in commercial use in the 20th century, but they are no longer in common use. Prior to the advent of electricity and internal-combustion engines, water wheels were widely used to power mills of many types — for grinding grain, sawing timber, fulling cloth, hammering iron, and crushing ore.

History

Water-powered wheels appeared in the ancient world: engineers of the Hellenistic and Roman periods described undershot wheels, and Vitruvius gave an account of the geared watermill. The Roman complex at Barbegal in Gaul, with sixteen overshot wheels, ground flour on an industrial scale. In China, water wheels powered trip hammers and bellows by the Han dynasty. In the medieval Islamic world and in Europe, mills multiplied dramatically — the Domesday Book of 1086 records some 6,500 mills in England alone. John Smeaton's experiments in the 18th century and the work of Benoît Fourneyron and others paved the way for the modern water turbine, which replaced wheels in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Designs

In an undershot wheel, the lower part of the wheel dips into flowing water and is pushed by the current; it is the simplest and oldest design but least efficient. An overshot wheel is turned by water delivered over the top into buckets, using weight as well as impulse, and can achieve efficiencies of 60–85%. A breastshot wheel receives water at about axle height, and a backshot wheel is a variety of overshot wheel rotating in the opposite direction. The pitchback and tub wheels are further variants; the giant Laxey Wheel on the Isle of Man (72 feet in diameter, built 1854 to pump water from mines) is the largest working waterwheel in the world.

Legacy

Though superseded, water wheels remain cultural landmarks, power heritage mills and demonstration sites, and inform small-scale hydro generation designs.
