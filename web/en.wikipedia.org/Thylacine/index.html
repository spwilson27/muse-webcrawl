The thylacine (Thylacinus cynocephalus), also called the Tasmanian tiger or Tasmanian wolf, was a carnivorous marsupial once widespread in Tasmania, mainland Australia and New Guinea. It was the largest known carnivorous marsupial of modern times and the last member of the family Thylacinidae. The last known individual died in captivity at Hobart's Beaumaris Zoo on 7 September 1936.

Description

The thylacine resembled a large, short-haired dog with a stiff tail, an abdominal pouch in females, and a series of 15–20 dark stripes across its lower back that gave it the name "tiger". It was a classic case of convergent evolution with placental canids. Unusually, both sexes had a pouch. The animal could open its jaws remarkably wide, and hunted mainly at night, probably ambushing kangaroos, wallabies and birds.

Extinction

Thylacines disappeared from mainland Australia roughly 2,000–3,000 years ago, likely through competition with the dingo and human pressure. In Tasmania, European settlers blamed the species for sheep losses; a government bounty paid from 1888 to 1909 led to more than 2,000 bounties being claimed. Disease, habitat change and hunting combined to drive the species to extinction; it received legal protection only in July 1936, 59 days before the last captive died. Unconfirmed sightings continue to be reported, and the thylacine has become an icon of extinction and a candidate in de-extinction research; its genome was sequenced from a preserved pup in 2017.

Legacy

The thylacine appears on Tasmania's coat of arms and remains a powerful symbol in Australian culture. 7 September is commemorated in Australia as National Threatened Species Day.
