Automatic transmission

A conventional automatic transmission uses planetary gearsets and a torque converter.

An automatic transmission (sometimes abbreviated AT) is a multi-speed motor vehicle transmission that does not require the driver to change gears manually. The term traditionally refers to hydraulic planetary automatics, but in common usage it also covers continuously variable transmissions (CVTs), dual-clutch transmissions (DCTs) and automated manual transmissions — all of which shift without a clutch pedal.

History

Early self-shifting gearboxes included the 1904 Sturtevant unit and the preselector gearboxes of the 1930s. The first fully automatic transmission to reach mass production was General Motors' Hydra-Matic, introduced on Oldsmobiles for the 1940 model year: it combined a fluid coupling with a hydraulically controlled planetary gearbox. Automatics became dominant in the United States after the Second World War, while remaining a minority choice in Europe and much of Asia until recent decades.

How planetary automatics work

A torque converter transfers engine power through fluid, multiplying torque at low speeds and locking up at cruising speed for efficiency. One or more planetary (epicyclic) gearsets provide the ratios; holding different elements with clutches and brakes selects different gears. Modern units, with eight, nine or ten ratios, are governed by an electronic control unit that adapts shift strategy to driving conditions and driver behaviour.

Other types

CVTs vary ratio continuously using belts or chains and pulleys; DCTs use two clutches to preselect gears for very fast shifts; automated manuals automate a conventional manual gearbox. Electric vehicles generally use single-speed reduction gears, since electric motors deliver torque over a wide speed range, making multi-ratio transmissions largely unnecessary.
