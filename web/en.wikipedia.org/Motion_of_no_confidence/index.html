Motion of no confidence

Used inParliamentary and semi-presidential systems
EffectGovernment must resign or call an election

A motion of no confidence (also called a vote of no confidence or censure motion) is a parliamentary vote by which the legislature declares that it no longer supports a government, a prime minister, or an individual minister. In most parliamentary systems, passage of such a motion obliges the government to resign or to seek a dissolution and fresh elections.

Operation

Typically introduced by the opposition, the motion is debated and put to a vote; the thresholds vary — some countries require an absolute majority of all members rather than a simple majority of those voting. If the motion succeeds against the whole government, the head of state invites a new government to form or dissolves parliament.

Constructive vote of no confidence

Germany's Basic Law (Article 67), followed by Spain and several other constitutions, requires a "constructive" vote: the Bundestag can dismiss the Chancellor only by simultaneously electing a successor with a majority. This device was designed to prevent the negative majorities that destabilised the Weimar Republic. It has succeeded once in Germany, in 1982, when Helmut Kohl replaced Helmut Schmidt.

Notable cases

In the United Kingdom, the 1979 vote of no confidence in James Callaghan's Labour government — lost by a single vote — precipitated the general election that brought Margaret Thatcher to power. In Canada, India, Australia and other Westminster systems, governments have likewise fallen on confidence votes, and the threat of such votes disciplines ruling majorities between elections.
