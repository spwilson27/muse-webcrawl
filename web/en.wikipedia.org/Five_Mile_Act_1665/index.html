Five Mile Act 1665

The Five Mile Act, or the Oxford Act, was an Act of the Parliament of England (17 Charles II c. 2), passed in 1665, which was part of the Clarendon Code — the series of penal laws intended to enforce religious conformity to the Church of England after the Restoration of Charles II.

Provisions

The Act provided that nonconformist ministers who refused to take the oath of non-resistance (a declaration that it was unlawful to take up arms against the king under any pretext) and who would not conform to the Church of England were forbidden to come within five miles of any city, town corporate, or borough, or of any parish where they had previously ministered. Offenders faced a fine of £40 for each offence and imprisonment. The Act also barred ejected ministers from teaching in schools.

Because the Cavalier Parliament was sitting at Oxford in 1665 — having fled London because of the Great Plague — the measure is sometimes called the Oxford Act. It received royal assent in October 1665.

Context and effects

The Act followed the Act of Uniformity 1662, which had ejected some two thousand ministers who would not accept the Book of Common Prayer and episcopal ordination, and the Conventicle Act 1664, which criminalised unauthorised religious assemblies. The Five Mile Act drove nonconformist preaching out of the towns, forcing ministers into rural exile and, in practice, creating hardship for congregations and clergy alike. Enforcement varied over time; the Act ceased to be effective after the Toleration Act 1689 granted freedom of worship to Protestant dissenters, and it was formally repealed in the 19th century.
