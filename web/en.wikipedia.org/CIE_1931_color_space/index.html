CIE 1931 color space

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (recreated from memory)

The CIE 1931 color spaces were the first defined quantitative links between distributions of wavelengths in the electromagnetic visible spectrum and physiologically perceived colors in human color vision. Defined by the International Commission on Illumination (CIE) in 1931 from color-matching experiments by William David Wright and John Guild, the CIE XYZ color space and its derived xy chromaticity diagram remain foundational standards of colorimetry.

The standard observer

Color-matching experiments asked observers to match test colors by mixing red, green, and blue primaries. Averaging the results gave the CIE standard observer, described by three color-matching functions x̄(λ), ȳ(λ), and z̄(λ). Any spectrum can then be reduced to tristimulus values X, Y, and Z; Y was deliberately defined to correspond to luminance. The 1931 observer used a 2° field of view; a supplementary 10° observer followed in 1964.

Chromaticity diagram

Normalizing by X+Y+Z gives chromaticity coordinates x and y, plotted on the famous horseshoe-shaped diagram. The curved boundary (the spectral locus) represents monochromatic light from about 380 to 700 nm, closed by the "line of purples." All colors visible to the standard observer lie within the horseshoe, and the gamut of any real display forms a triangle inside it — demonstrating that no three real primaries can reproduce all visible colors.

Legacy

CIE XYZ is the reference space from which later systems — CIELAB, CIELUV, and the profile connection space of ICC color management — are derived, and it underlies color measurement throughout imaging, lighting, and manufacturing.
