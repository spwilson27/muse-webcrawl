Vigna radiata, commonly known as the mung bean, is a plant species in the legume family Fabaceae. It is widely cultivated across East, South and Southeast Asia for its small, green seeds, which are eaten whole, split, sprouted, or ground into flour. The mung bean is closely related to the urad bean (Vigna mungo) and the adzuki bean (Vigna angularis).

Description

The mung bean plant is an annual vine growing to roughly 15–90 cm in height, with trifoliate leaves and yellow flowers borne in clusters. The seed pods are slender, cylindrical, and contain 10–15 small green seeds each. The plant is well adapted to warm climates and is drought-tolerant, fixing nitrogen in the soil through symbiosis with rhizobia.

Cultivation and uses

Mung beans are believed to have been domesticated in India. Today India, China, Myanmar and Thailand are among the largest producers. The sprouted seeds, known as bean sprouts, are a staple of East and Southeast Asian cuisines. In India, split mung beans form the basis of moong dal; in China, the beans are made into sweet soups, mung bean jelly, and the transparent "cellophane noodles" (also made from starch). Mung bean paste is a common filling for pastries and mooncakes.

Nutrition

Mung beans are a good source of plant protein, folate, manganese, magnesium and dietary fiber, and are low in fat. Sprouting increases their vitamin C content and digestibility.
