Erbium-doped fiber amplifier

Schematic of a simple erbium-doped fiber amplifier

An erbium-doped fiber amplifier (EDFA) is an optical amplifier that uses a length of optical fiber doped with the rare-earth element erbium as the gain medium to amplify an optical signal directly, without converting it to an electrical signal. EDFAs are the most common type of optical amplifier used in long-haul fibre-optic communication systems, operating in the C-band around 1,550 nm, where silica optical fibre has its lowest loss.

Principle of operation

A pump laser, typically operating at 980 nm or 1,480 nm, excites erbium ions (Er3+) in the fibre core to a higher energy state, producing a population inversion. When a signal photon near 1,550 nm passes through, it stimulates the excited ions to emit additional photons of the same wavelength, phase, and direction — stimulated emission — thereby amplifying the signal. Wavelength-division multiplexing couplers combine and separate the pump and signal light, and optical isolators prevent destabilising reflections.

History

The EDFA was demonstrated almost simultaneously in 1987 by two groups: one led by David N. Payne at the University of Southampton in the United Kingdom, and one at AT&T Bell Laboratories in the United States. Combined with wavelength-division multiplexing, EDFAs revolutionised telecommunications in the 1990s by allowing many channels on a single fibre to be amplified simultaneously, greatly reducing the cost of long-distance and transoceanic links.

Advantages and noise

EDFAs offer high gain (often 30–40 dB), low noise (with a noise figure approaching the 3 dB quantum limit), insensitivity to signal polarisation, and compatibility with in-line fibre transmission. Their principal drawback is amplified spontaneous emission noise, which accumulates over cascaded amplifiers and ultimately limits the length of amplifier chains. Gain is not perfectly flat across the band, so gain-flattening filters are used in systems carrying many wavelengths.

Related amplifiers

Other rare-earth-doped amplifiers include thulium-doped amplifiers for the S-band and praseodymium-doped amplifiers once considered for 1,310 nm operation. Raman amplifiers, which use stimulated Raman scattering in ordinary fibre, complement EDFAs in modern systems. For data-centre and access applications, semiconductor optical amplifiers provide a compact alternative.
