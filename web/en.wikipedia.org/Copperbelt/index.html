The Copperbelt is a natural and cultural region of Central Africa known for its extensive deposits of copper and cobalt. It straddles the border between northern Zambia — where it forms Copperbelt Province — and the former Katanga (Shaba) region of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. It is one of the world's richest mining districts.

Geology

The mineralisation is hosted in sedimentary rocks of the Katanga Supergroup, deposited roughly 880 million years ago. Copper and cobalt occur chiefly as sulphide and oxide ores in stratiform bodies, making the belt one of the largest known sediment-hosted copper provinces on Earth.

History

Indigenous peoples smelted copper in the region for centuries before European colonisation. Industrial mining began in the early twentieth century under British and Belgian colonial administrations, drawing in large labour forces and creating new mining towns such as Kitwe, Ndola, and Lubumbashi. After Zambian independence in 1964 the mines were nationalised; they were privatised again in the 1990s and 2000s as global copper prices recovered.

Economy

Copper exports dominate the economies of Zambia and, increasingly, the Congolese mining sector. The region also produces a large share of the world's cobalt, a metal essential for rechargeable batteries. Mining has brought both prosperity and hardship, including boom-and-bust cycles tied to world metal prices, environmental damage from smelting, and labour disputes.
