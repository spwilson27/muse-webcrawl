Food preservation

Food preservation comprises the processes and techniques used to prevent food from spoiling, including methods that inhibit the growth of bacteria, yeasts, and moulds, and that slow the oxidation of fats which cause rancidity. Preservation allows food to be stored safely for extended periods, transported over long distances, and eaten out of season, and it has been practised since ancient times.

Traditional methods

Drying is among the oldest preservation methods, used for grains, fruits, and meats such as jerky. Salting and curing draw out moisture by osmosis; sugar acts similarly in jams and candied fruits. Smoking, pickling in vinegar or brine, and fermentation — which produces lactic acid or alcohol — all create environments hostile to spoilage organisms. Burial, cooling in cellars, and storage in oil or honey were also historically important.

Industrial methods

Canning, developed by Nicolas Appert in the early 19th century, seals heat-sterilised food in airtight containers. Pasteurisation, pioneered by Louis Pasteur, uses milder heat to reduce pathogens in milk, wine, and juices. Refrigeration and freezing slow microbial growth enormously, while vacuum packing and modified-atmosphere packaging limit oxygen. Irradiation, high-pressure processing, and chemical preservatives extend shelf life further.

Health and safety

Preservation prevents foodborne illness and food waste, but methods must be applied correctly: improperly canned low-acid foods can harbour Clostridium botulinum, and excessive nitrites in cured meats have raised health concerns. Modern food science studies "hurdle technology", combining several mild preservation factors to maintain quality with minimal processing.
