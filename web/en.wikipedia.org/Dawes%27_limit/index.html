Dawes' limit

Dawes' limit is a formula to determine the resolving power of a telescope, formulated by the English astronomer William Rutter Dawes (1799–1868) based on his empirical observations of double stars.

The formula

Dawes' limit is conventionally expressed as R = 4.56 / D, where D is the diameter of the telescope's main lens or mirror in inches and R is the resolving power in arcseconds. Equivalently, R = 11.6 / D when D is measured in centimetres. A telescope with a 10 cm aperture thus resolves about 1.16 arcseconds.

Background

Resolving power is the ability of an optical instrument to distinguish two closely spaced point sources, such as the components of a binary star. Dawes derived his criterion experimentally by observing double stars of equal brightness and noting the separation at which two stellar disks appeared just merged or notched rather than cleanly split.

Relation to other criteria

Dawes' limit is somewhat less strict than the Rayleigh criterion, which for a circular aperture gives R = 1.22λ/D and corresponds to a separation of about 5.46/D inches. In practice, atmospheric seeing, optical quality, and magnification usually limit resolution more than diffraction does for ground-based telescopes.
