Moral universalism

Moral universalism (also called moral objectivism) is the meta-ethical position that a universal ethic — a system of moral principles — applies to all individuals regardless of culture, race, sex, religion, nationality, or other distinguishing features. It holds that moral judgements can be true or false independently of who makes them, and stands opposed to moral relativism and moral nihilism.

Forms of universalism

Universalism need not claim that one rigid code settles every question. Moderate forms allow universal principles to be applied with sensitivity to circumstances, and pluralist versions hold that several objective values — liberty, welfare, fairness — may conflict and require balancing. Classical sources of universalist ethics include:

  • Natural law traditions deriving moral rules from human nature or reason, as in Aquinas.
  • Kantian ethics, whose categorical imperative requires acting only on maxims that can be willed as universal law.
  • Utilitarianism, which counts everyone's welfare impartially.
  • Human rights doctrine, expressed in the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights, asserting rights held by all persons everywhere.

Criticisms

Critics argue that apparent universal values often reflect the particular culture that proclaims them, that moral diversity across societies undermines claims of objectivity, and that universalist rhetoric has historically been used to justify imperialism. Universalists reply that disagreement does not entail relativism, that some norms — prohibitions of slavery and torture — command near-global assent, and that relativism itself struggles to condemn oppression within a culture.
