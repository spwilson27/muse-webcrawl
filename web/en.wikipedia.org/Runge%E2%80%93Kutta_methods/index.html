Runge–Kutta methods

The Runge–Kutta methods are a family of implicit and explicit iterative methods used in numerical analysis for the approximate solution of ordinary differential equations. They were developed around 1900 by the German mathematicians Carl Runge and Wilhelm Kutta.

Overview

A Runge–Kutta method advances a solution through a time step by combining several evaluations of the derivative within the step, weighted so that the result matches the Taylor series of the exact solution up to a given order. The general s-stage method is summarised by a Butcher tableau of coefficients. Unlike multistep methods, Runge–Kutta methods are self-starting and require only the current value to take the next step.

The classical fourth-order method

The most famous member of the family, often called "the" Runge–Kutta method or RK4, uses four derivative evaluations per step — at the start, twice at the midpoint, and at the end — combined in the weighted average (k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄)/6. Its local truncation error is of order h⁵, giving fourth-order accuracy overall.

Variants

Embedded pairs such as the Runge–Kutta–Fehlberg (RKF45) and Dormand–Prince methods estimate the error at each step and adapt the step size; the Dormand–Prince pair is the default solver in several software packages. Implicit Runge–Kutta methods, including Gauss–Legendre and Radau collocation methods, are suited to stiff equations and can achieve high order with excellent stability.
