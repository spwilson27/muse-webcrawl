Apert syndrome is a form of acrocephalosyndactyly, a congenital disorder characterized by malformations of the skull, face, hands and feet. It is classed as a branchial arch syndrome, affecting the first branchial (or pharyngeal) arch, the precursor of the maxilla and mandible. Disturbances in the development of the branchial arches in fetal development create lasting and widespread effects.

Characteristic cranial and hand features of Apert syndrome

The condition is named after French physician Eugène Apert, who first described it in 1906, although earlier cases had been reported by English surgeon Samuel Wilks and others in the 19th century.

Signs and symptoms

The defining features of Apert syndrome include craniosynostosis — the premature fusion of one or more cranial sutures, most often the coronal sutures — producing an abnormally shaped skull (acrocephaly), a sunken midface (midface hypoplasia), shallow eye sockets with bulging eyes, and complex syndactyly in which the fingers and toes are fused together, often involving the second, third and fourth digits. Some individuals have intellectual disability, though many have normal intelligence.

Cause

Apert syndrome is caused by mutations in the fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2) gene on chromosome 10. Almost all cases arise from one of two specific point mutations (Ser252Trp or Pro253Arg). The condition is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, but most cases result from new (de novo) mutations, which are associated with advanced paternal age.

Treatment

There is no cure; treatment is surgical and supportive. Operations release the fused skull sutures to allow brain growth, separate fused digits, and correct midface and airway problems. Care is usually coordinated by multidisciplinary craniofacial teams from infancy through adolescence.
