Gnostic Gospels

The Gnostic Gospels are a collection of ancient religious texts attributed to early Christian and Gnostic teachers, written mostly in the 2nd to 4th centuries AD. They present alternative accounts of the teachings of Jesus, often framed as secret revelations given to particular apostles, and reflect the doctrines of Gnosticism: that salvation comes through gnosis, direct inner knowledge of the divine, and that the material world was created by a lesser, ignorant deity (the Demiurge) rather than the supreme God.

Discovery

For centuries these writings were known mainly through the hostile summaries of church fathers such as Irenaeus, who wrote Against Heresies around AD 180. In 1945, Egyptian farmers near the town of Nag Hammadi unearthed a sealed jar containing thirteen leather-bound codices — the Nag Hammadi library — with more than fifty texts in Coptic, including complete copies of several gospels. Elaine Pagels's 1979 book The Gnostic Gospels brought them to wide public attention.

Principal texts

  • Gospel of Thomas — a collection of 114 sayings of Jesus without narrative of his life or death.
  • Gospel of Philip — a Valentinian anthology on sacraments and the bridal chamber.
  • Gospel of Truth — attributed to Valentinus, a meditation on ignorance and knowledge.
  • Gospel of Mary — in which Mary Magdalene recounts a private revelation from Jesus.
  • Gospel of Judas — published in 2006, portraying Judas Iscariot as acting at Jesus's own request.
  • Apocryphon of John — a creation account in which the Demiurge Yaldabaoth fashions the material world.

Significance

Unlike the four canonical gospels, the Gnostic texts generally de-emphasise Jesus's crucifixion and bodily resurrection, presenting him instead as a revealer of saving knowledge. Their recovery reshaped scholarly understanding of the diversity of early Christianity and of the process by which some texts became canonical and others were suppressed.
