Prisoner of conscience

A prisoner of conscience (POC) is anyone imprisoned or otherwise physically restricted because of their political, religious or other conscientiously held beliefs, ethnic origin, sex, colour, language, national or social origin, socioeconomic status, birth, sexual orientation or other status, provided they have not used or advocated violence or hatred. The term was popularised by the human rights organisation Amnesty International.

Origin

The phrase was introduced by the British lawyer Peter Benenson in his 1961 article "The Forgotten Prisoners" in The Observer, which launched the campaign that became Amnesty International. Benenson defined a prisoner of conscience as a person physically restrained from expressing any opinion which he honestly holds and which does not advocate or condone personal violence, and Amnesty adopted the concept as the core of its work: campaigning for the release of prisoners of conscience and the fair trial of political prisoners.

Campaigning

Amnesty groups "adopt" individual prisoners of conscience, publicising their cases and writing letters to the authorities detaining them. The organisation's definition excludes those who have used or advocated violence, which has occasionally generated controversy — for example in debates over whether particular high-profile detainees qualify for the designation.

Notable prisoners of conscience

People widely described as prisoners of conscience have included Nelson Mandela (adopted by Amnesty in the 1960s), Aung San Suu Kyi, Václav Havel, Andrei Sakharov, Liu Xiaobo, Narges Mohammadi and many journalists, activists and religious believers detained for peaceful expression.
