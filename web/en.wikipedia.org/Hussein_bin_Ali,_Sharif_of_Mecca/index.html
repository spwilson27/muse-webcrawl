Hussein bin Ali al-Hashimi (c. 1853/54 – 4 June 1931) was the Sharif and Emir of Mecca from 1908 and, from 1916, King of the Hejaz. A Hashemite descendant of the Prophet Muhammad, he launched the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire during the First World War.

Arab Revolt

In the Hussein–McMahon Correspondence (1915–1916), Hussein negotiated with the British High Commissioner in Egypt over Arab independence in exchange for revolt against the Ottomans. The Revolt began in June 1916; his sons Ali, Abdullah, Faisal and Zeid led armies that, aided by British officers including T. E. Lawrence, captured Aqaba and Damascus. Britain and France, however, had secretly agreed in the Sykes–Picot Agreement to divide much of the region, and the Balfour Declaration complicated promises to the Arabs.

Later life

Hussein proclaimed himself king of the Arab lands, recognised only in the Hejaz, and in 1924 claimed the caliphate after its abolition by Turkey. That year Ibn Saud's forces conquered the Hejaz; Hussein abdicated in favour of his son Ali and went into exile in Cyprus. His descendants became kings of Iraq (Faisal I) and of Jordan, where Abdullah's line still reigns. He died in Amman in 1931 and was buried in Jerusalem.
