Siege of Derry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The siege of Derry in 1689 was the first major event in the Williamite War in Ireland, in which the Jacobite forces of the deposed King James II besieged the largely Protestant city of Derry (Londonderry), held for William III. The city's successful resistance over 105 days became one of the most celebrated episodes in Ulster Protestant tradition.

Background

After the Glorious Revolution of 1688, most of Ireland remained loyal to the Catholic James II. In December 1688, thirteen apprentice boys shut the gates of Derry against a Jacobite regiment approaching the city — an act commemorated ever since. Refugees flooded in, swelling the population to perhaps 30,000. The city's governor, Robert Lundy, favoured negotiation and was replaced amid accusations of treachery; command devolved to Major Henry Baker and the clergyman George Walker.

The siege

Jacobite forces under Richard Hamilton, later directed by James II in person, invested the city in April 1689 and built a boom across the River Foyle to starve it into submission. Inside, food ran so low that dogs, cats, rats and hides were eaten; disease and starvation killed thousands. On 28 July 1689, the relief ships Mountjoy and Phoenix, escorted by HMS Dartmouth, broke the boom and reached the city, ending the siege days later as the Jacobites withdrew and burned their camps.

Legacy

The siege — remembered in the defiant slogan "No Surrender" and in the cry of "Remember 1689" — is commemorated annually by the Apprentice Boys of Derry. It preceded the Williamite victories at the Boyne (1690) and Aughrim (1691) that secured Protestant ascendancy in Ireland.
