Prisoner's dilemma

The prisoner's dilemma is a canonical example in game theory showing why two completely rational individuals might not cooperate, even if it appears in their best interest to do so. It was framed by Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher in 1950, and given its name and story by Albert W. Tucker.

The dilemma

Two prisoners are interrogated separately. Each can cooperate (stay silent) or defect (betray the other). If both stay silent, each serves a short sentence. If one betrays and the other stays silent, the betrayer goes free and the silent one gets a long sentence. If both betray, each gets a moderate sentence. Whatever the other does, each prisoner is better off betraying — yet both betraying leaves both worse off than both staying silent.

Analysis

Defection is a strictly dominant strategy, so the unique Nash equilibrium is mutual defection, although mutual cooperation yields a better total outcome. In the iterated prisoner's dilemma, where the game is played repeatedly, cooperative strategies such as tit for tat can thrive, as shown by Robert Axelrod's computer tournaments.

Real-world examples

The dilemma models arms races, price wars, doping in sport, and the tragedy of the commons — situations where individual incentives undermine collective welfare.
