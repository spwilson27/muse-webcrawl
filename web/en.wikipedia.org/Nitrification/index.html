Nitrification

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nitrification is the biological oxidation of ammonia (NH3) to nitrite (NO2−) followed by the oxidation of nitrite to nitrate (NO3−). It is a key step in the nitrogen cycle in soils and aquatic environments, carried out by chemoautotrophic microorganisms that obtain energy from the oxidation reactions and fix carbon dioxide as their carbon source. The process was first described by Sergei Winogradsky in the 1890s.

Steps and organisms

The first step, oxidation of ammonia to nitrite, is performed by ammonia-oxidizing bacteria such as Nitrosomonas and by ammonia-oxidizing archaea such as Nitrosopumilus, using the enzyme ammonia monooxygenase; it proceeds through hydroxylamine as an intermediate. The second step, oxidation of nitrite to nitrate, is performed by nitrite-oxidizing bacteria such as Nitrospira and Nitrobacter. In 2015, researchers discovered "comammox" Nitrospira species capable of complete ammonia oxidation to nitrate within a single organism.

Environmental significance

Nitrification converts relatively immobile ammonium into mobile nitrate, which plants can assimilate but which also leaches readily into groundwater and waterways, contributing to eutrophication. In agriculture, nitrification inhibitors are sometimes applied with ammonium fertilizers to slow the process. Nitrification is also an essential stage in wastewater treatment, where it is followed by denitrification to remove nitrogen. The process requires oxygen, is inhibited at low pH, and produces acidity; it can generate the greenhouse gas nitrous oxide as a by-product.
