Peripheral artery disease

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) is an abnormal narrowing of arteries other than those that supply the heart or brain. It most commonly affects the legs, where narrowing reduces blood flow and typically causes leg pain when walking, known as intermittent claudication, which usually resolves with rest. PAD is usually caused by atherosclerosis — the build-up of fatty plaque in the arterial walls — and shares risk factors with coronary artery disease.

Signs and symptoms

Many people with PAD have no symptoms. The classic symptom is claudication: cramping pain in the calf, thigh or buttock brought on by walking and relieved by rest. Severe disease may cause pain at rest, non-healing sores or ulcers on the feet and legs, coldness and colour changes in the affected limb, weak pulses, and in the worst cases gangrene and amputation. PAD is also a marker of widespread atherosclerosis, and affected people have a markedly increased risk of heart attack and stroke.

Causes and risk factors

The strongest risk factors are cigarette smoking and diabetes mellitus. Others include high blood pressure, high cholesterol, advancing age, chronic kidney disease and a family history of vascular disease.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is commonly made with the ankle–brachial index (ABI), a comparison of blood pressure at the ankle with that in the arm; an ABI of 0.90 or less indicates PAD. Ultrasound, CT angiography and MR angiography can locate and grade blockages.

Treatment

Treatment focuses on stopping smoking, supervised exercise therapy, and medicines such as statins, antiplatelet drugs and cilostazol. Severe or limb-threatening disease may require angioplasty, stenting, bypass surgery, or, as a last resort, amputation. Globally the condition affects more than 200 million people.
