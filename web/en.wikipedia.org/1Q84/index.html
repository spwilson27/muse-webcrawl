1Q84

1Q84 (Japanese: いちきゅうはちよん, Ichi-Kyū-Hachi-Yon) is a novel by Japanese writer Haruki Murakami, first published in Japan in three volumes by Shinchosha in 2009–2010. The title is a play on George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four: in Japanese, the letter Q (キュー, kyū) is pronounced the same as the numeral 9. Set in a fictionalised version of Tokyo in 1984, the novel interweaves the stories of Aomame, a fitness instructor and assassin, and Tengo, a mathematics teacher and aspiring writer, who each slip into a parallel world — marked by two moons in the sky — that Aomame names "1Q84".

Plot

Aomame, descending an emergency stairway from an expressway, finds the world subtly altered: police carry different weapons, historical events differ, and two moons hang in the sky. She works for a wealthy dowager, killing men who have abused women, and is drawn toward the religious cult Sakigake. Tengo, meanwhile, is asked by his editor Komatsu to secretly rewrite Air Chrysalis, a novella by the seventeen-year-old Eriko Fukada ("Fuka-Eri"), whose narrative of the "Little People" begins to manifest in reality. The alternating narratives converge on the two protagonists' shared childhood memory of holding hands, which gives the novel its emotional core.

Publication and reception

The first two volumes were published simultaneously in May 2009 and sold out on release day in Japan; the third volume followed in April 2010. The English translation, by Jay Rubin and Philip Gabriel, was published by Alfred A. Knopf in October 2011 as a single volume. The novel became an international bestseller, though critical opinion was divided: some reviewers praised its ambition and narrative pull, while others found its length and repetitions excessive. Before publication, Murakami said he hoped the work would serve as a counterpart to Orwell's dystopia, exploring the role of belief and cults — informed by the Aum Shinrikyo sarin attack of 1995 — in contemporary Japan.

Themes

Recurring Murakami themes include parallel worlds, loneliness, music (Leoš Janáček's Sinfonietta opens the novel), religious fanaticism, domestic violence, and the permeability of fiction and reality. The novel also engages with Chekhov's Sakhalin Island and the concept of the "Little People" as a counter-system to Orwell's Big Brother.
