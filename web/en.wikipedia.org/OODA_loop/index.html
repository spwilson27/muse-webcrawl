OODA loop

The OODA loop is a decision-making model describing the cycle of observe, orient, decide, and act. It was developed by United States Air Force Colonel John Boyd, drawing on his experience as a fighter pilot and his study of military history and strategy. Boyd argued that individuals and organizations that can cycle through the loop faster than an adversary — getting "inside" the opponent's decision cycle — gain a decisive advantage, because the opponent is forced to react to a situation that has already changed.

Boyd's OODA loop, with orientation as its central, shaping step.

Overview

Observe: collect information about the unfolding situation from the environment and from one's own forces. Orient: interpret that information through the filter of cultural traditions, genetic heritage, previous experience, and new analysis — Boyd considered orientation the most important step, because it shapes how one observes, decides, and acts. Decide: select a course of action from the available options, as a hypothesis to be tested. Act: carry out the decision, then observe the results, feeding them back into the next cycle.

Applications

Although developed for air combat and military strategy, the OODA loop has been widely applied in business strategy, competitive sports, law enforcement, emergency response, litigation, and cybersecurity, and it has influenced the theory of information warfare. Boyd never published a book; his ideas survive in briefing slides such as Patterns of Conflict and in briefings given to military and business audiences.

Criticism

Commentators have noted that the popular simplified version of the loop — emphasizing raw speed alone — omits much of Boyd's deeper argument about orientation, implicit guidance, and mismatching an adversary's perceptions.
