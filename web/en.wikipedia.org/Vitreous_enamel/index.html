Vitreous enamel

Enamelwork on metal jewellery

Also called: Porcelain enamel

Firing temperature: 750–850 °C

Vitreous enamel, also called porcelain enamel, is a material made by fusing powdered glass to a substrate by firing, usually between 750 and 850 °C (1,380 and 1,560 °F). The powder melts, flows, and then hardens into a smooth, durable vitreous coating. The word enamel comes from the Old High German smelzan, "to melt".

History

Enamelling is an ancient art: the Mycenaeans and Egyptians applied enamel to jewellery and pottery, and Celtic, Byzantine, Chinese, and Islamic craftspeople developed techniques including cloisonné, champlevé, plique-à-jour, and painted enamel. Limoges in France became famous for painted enamels in the Renaissance. Industrial porcelain enamel on cast iron and steel developed in the 19th century for cookware, bathtubs, and signage.

Properties and uses

The coating is hard, chemically resistant, heat-stable, and easy to clean, though it can chip on impact. Industrial applications include cookware, major appliances, water heaters, architectural panels, street signs, and the interior of chemical reactors. Artistically, enamel remains a medium for jewellery and decorative objects, applied to gold, silver, and copper.

Application

Ground coats containing cobalt and nickel oxides promote adhesion to steel; cover coats provide colour and gloss. Enamel slip is sprayed, dipped, or electrostatically applied as dry powder before firing. Careful control of the metal and glass thermal expansion is needed to avoid cracking or peeling.
