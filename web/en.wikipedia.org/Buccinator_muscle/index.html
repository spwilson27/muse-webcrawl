The buccinator is a thin quadrilateral muscle occupying the interval between the maxilla and the mandible at the side of the face. It forms the anterior part of the cheek and the lateral wall of the oral cavity, lying deep to the skin and the buccal fat pad.

Structure

The buccinator arises from the outer surfaces of the alveolar processes of the maxilla and mandible opposite the molar teeth and from the pterygomandibular raphe behind. Its fibres converge toward the angle of the mouth, where they blend with the orbicularis oris. The parotid duct (Stensen's duct) pierces the buccinator opposite the second upper molar tooth to open into the mouth. The muscle is supplied by the buccal branch of the facial nerve; it is an embryological muscle of the second pharyngeal arch.

Function

The buccinator compresses the cheek against the teeth, preventing food from accumulating in the vestibule during chewing and pushing food back onto the occlusal surfaces of the molars. It is also active in whistling, sucking, blowing and playing wind instruments — its name derives from the Latin buccina, a trumpet, hence "trumpeter muscle". In infants it assists in suckling.
