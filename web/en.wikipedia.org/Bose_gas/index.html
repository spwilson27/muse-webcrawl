An ideal Bose gas is a quantum-mechanical phase of matter, analogous to a classical ideal gas. It is composed of bosons, particles with integer spin, that follow Bose–Einstein statistics. The statistical mechanics of bosons was developed by Satyendra Nath Bose for photons and generalized to massive particles by Albert Einstein, who predicted that at sufficiently low temperatures the particles would condense into the lowest quantum state — a phenomenon known as Bose–Einstein condensation.

Bose–Einstein condensation

Unlike fermions, any number of bosons can occupy the same quantum state. Below a critical temperature, a macroscopic fraction of the particles in a Bose gas collapses into the ground state, forming a Bose–Einstein condensate (BEC). Einstein predicted this in 1924–25 on the basis of Bose's counting method. The prediction was first realized experimentally in 1995, when Eric Cornell, Carl Wieman, and Wolfgang Ketterle produced condensates of dilute alkali gases cooled to a few hundred nanokelvin, work recognized by the 2001 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Properties

The thermodynamics of the ideal Bose gas differs sharply from that of a classical gas: its pressure depends only on temperature in the condensed phase, and its heat capacity shows a characteristic cusp at the transition temperature. Real Bose gases include liquid helium-4 below the lambda point (2.17 K), whose superfluid behaviour is related to condensation, and photons in a blackbody cavity, described by Planck's law, a limiting case of the Bose gas formalism.
