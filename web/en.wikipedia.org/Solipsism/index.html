Solipsism

Solipsism
FieldPhilosophy (epistemology, metaphysics)
Core claimOnly one's own mind is sure to exist; knowledge of anything outside it is unjustified
EtymologyLatin solus (alone) + ipse (self)
Related positionsSkepticism, idealism, egocentrism, problem of other minds
The solipsist holds that only one's own mind is certain

Solipsism is the philosophical idea that only one's own mind is sure to exist. As an epistemological position, solipsism holds that knowledge of anything outside one's own mind is unsure; the external world and other minds cannot be known and might not exist outside the mind.

Varieties

Metaphysical solipsism asserts that nothing exists but one's own self and its experiences. Epistemological solipsism is the weaker claim that only the contents of one's own mind are directly accessible to knowledge. Methodological solipsism, used in cognitive science and philosophy of mind, is a research strategy that studies mental processes without assuming external reference.

History

Elements of solipsistic reasoning appear in René Descartes' method of doubt: beginning from cogito ergo sum ("I think, therefore I am"), Descartes found the existence of his own mind certain while the external world required proof, which he supplied through God. George Berkeley's idealism — that things exist only as perceived — skirts solipsism but avoids it by placing other minds and God in his system. Few if any philosophers have openly defended full metaphysical solipsism; it functions chiefly as a sceptical challenge that theories of knowledge must answer.

Criticisms

Standard responses point to the coherence and predictive success of belief in an external world, the apparent absurdity of the position in practice, and arguments (notably Wittgenstein's private-language argument) that thought and language themselves presuppose a public world. Critics also note that solipsism is unfalsifiable rather than supported by positive evidence.
