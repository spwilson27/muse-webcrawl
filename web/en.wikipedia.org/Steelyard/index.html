Steelyard

The Steelyard (German: Stalhof) was the main trading base (kontor) of the Hanseatic League in London during the late medieval and early modern periods. Located on the north bank of the River Thames by the present-day Cannon Street station, it occupied its own walled enclave with warehouses, a weigh-house, residences, a church, and gardens, and its merchants enjoyed extensive tax and customs privileges.

History

German merchants from Cologne had traded in London since the twelfth century and received privileges from Henry II. By 1282 the merchants of the various Hanse towns had united in a single "German Hansa," and by the fifteenth century the Steelyard was a formidable commercial colony of several hundred residents, governed by its own alderman under the law of Lübeck. Its privileges were repeatedly contested by London merchants and were revoked and restored several times.

Name

The English name "Steelyard" is probably a mistranslation of Stalhof ("sample yard," from Middle Low German stael, a sample of goods for weighing and inspection); it has also been connected with the steelyard balance, a form of weighing beam. The popular suggestion that the name refers to the weighing of steel is considered folk etymology.

Closure and legacy

Elizabeth I finally expelled the Hanse merchants and closed the Steelyard in 1598, after escalating disputes. The site was largely destroyed in the Great Fire of London in 1666. The warehouse buildings' site is commemorated by a plaque at Cannon Street station, and the term survives in the Steelyard balance.
