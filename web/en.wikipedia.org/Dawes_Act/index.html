Dawes Act

The Dawes Act of 1887 (also known as the General Allotment Act or Dawes Severalty Act) was a United States federal law that authorised the President to break up Native American tribal land into individual allotments for Native families and individuals. Named after Senator Henry L. Dawes of Massachusetts, its stated aim was to assimilate Native Americans into American society as farmers; its effect was a massive transfer of land — some 90 million acres — out of Native hands.

Provisions

The Act provided for each head of family to receive 160 acres, single adults and orphans 80 acres, and minors 40 acres, held in trust by the federal government for 25 years, after which the allottee received full title and US citizenship. Land remaining after allotment was declared "surplus" and could be sold to non-Native settlers. The Act initially exempted the Five Civilized Tribes and a few others, but the Curtis Act of 1898 extended allotment to them. The Burke Act of 1906 allowed the Secretary of the Interior to issue fee patents early to allottees deemed "competent", often triggering immediate land loss through sale, fraud or tax forfeiture.

Effects

Between 1887 and 1934, the Native land base shrank from about 138 million acres to about 48 million acres. Surplus-land sales accounted for roughly 60 million acres, and allotments lost after patenting for another 30 million. Fractionated heirship — land passing to many heirs as undivided interests — created an enduring administrative problem. The 1928 Meriam Report documented the policy's disastrous social and economic results. The Dawes Act era formally ended with the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 under Commissioner John Collier, which stopped allotment and restored some tribal self-government.
