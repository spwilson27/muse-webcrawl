Andrew Smith Hallidie (16 March 1836 – 24 April 1900) was an English-born American engineer and manufacturer who is regarded as the inventor and promoter of the cable car system in San Francisco.

Early life and wire rope

Hallidie emigrated to California during the Gold Rush era and went into business manufacturing wire rope, used in mining and for suspension bridges. His company, A. S. Hallidie & Co., supplied rope for mines throughout the West.

The cable car

Disturbed by the suffering of horses hauling streetcars up San Francisco's steep hills — and, by his own account, after witnessing a fatal accident involving a horse-drawn car — Hallidie adapted wire-rope haulage to urban transport. His Clay Street Hill Railroad began operation in 1873 and is considered the world's first successful cable car line. Hallidie held key patents on the system and promoted cable railways in other cities.

Legacy

Hallidie Plaza in San Francisco, at the Powell Street cable car turnaround, is named for him, and the city's cable cars remain an enduring symbol of his innovation.
