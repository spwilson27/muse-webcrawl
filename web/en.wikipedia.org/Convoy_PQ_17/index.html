Convoy PQ 17

Convoy PQ 17 was an Allied Arctic convoy that sailed in the summer of 1942 carrying supplies from Iceland to the Soviet port of Archangel. It became the most disastrous convoy of the Second World War after the British Admiralty, fearing an attack by the German battleship Tirpitz, ordered the convoy to scatter and its naval escort to withdraw, leaving the merchant ships to be hunted individually by U-boats and aircraft.

Background

The PQ convoys carried Lend-Lease tanks, aircraft, vehicles and raw materials to the USSR via the North Cape route. PQ 17 comprised some 35 merchant ships with a close escort of destroyers, corvettes and anti-aircraft ships, a cruiser covering force, and a distant heavy covering force including battleships. German forces in Norway — Tirpitz, the heavy cruisers Admiral Hipper and Lützow, U-boats and Luftwaffe bombers based around North Cape — posed a serious threat in the continuous daylight of the Arctic summer.

The scatter order

The convoy sailed from Iceland on 27 June 1942 and was shadowed from 1 July. On the evening of 4 July, First Sea Lord Admiral Sir Dudley Pound, acting on reports that Tirpitz had put to sea, ordered the cruiser force to withdraw westward and the convoy to "scatter" — each merchant ship to proceed independently to Soviet ports. In fact Tirpitz's sortie (Operation Rösselsprung) was brief and the German surface force never engaged; it was recalled once the scatter was detected.

Losses

Dispersed and largely defenceless, the merchant ships were picked off over the following days. Of the 35 ships that set out, only 11 reached Archangel; 24 were sunk, with the loss of some 430 tanks, 210 aircraft, 3,350 vehicles and nearly 100,000 tons of other cargo, along with the lives of more than 150 merchant seamen. Arctic convoys were suspended until the autumn.

Legacy

PQ 17 became a byword for the hazards of remote command decisions taken on incomplete intelligence, and the scatter order remains controversial among historians. The novelist Alistair MacLean drew on the episode for HMS Ulysses, and the disaster featured in official inquiries and extensive post-war analysis of the conduct of Admiral Pound and the Admiralty.
