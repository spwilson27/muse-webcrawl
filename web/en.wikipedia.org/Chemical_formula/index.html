Chemical formula

A chemical formula is a way of presenting information about the chemical proportions of atoms that constitute a particular chemical compound or molecule, using chemical element symbols, numbers, and sometimes also other symbols, such as parentheses, dashes, brackets, commas and plus (+) and minus (−) signs. These are limited to a single typographic line of symbols, which may include subscripts and superscripts. A chemical formula is not a chemical name since it does not contain any words.

Types of formulae

The empirical formula gives the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound — for glucose, CH2O. The molecular formula specifies the exact number of each type of atom in a molecule — for glucose, C6H12O6. A structural formula additionally shows how the atoms are arranged and bonded. A condensed formula compresses structural information into one line, as in CH3CH2OH for ethanol.

Reading a formula

Subscripts after element symbols indicate the number of atoms of that element in the molecule: H2O is two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Parentheses group atoms that repeat, as in Ca(OH)2. A leading coefficient multiplies the entire formula in a chemical equation. Superscripts with + or − denote ionic charge.

History

The modern system of chemical symbols was introduced by Jöns Jacob Berzelius in the early nineteenth century, replacing the pictorial symbols of alchemy and the circles used by John Dalton. Formulae built on this notation made chemical composition and reaction stoichiometry compact and unambiguous across languages.
