Thomas Henry Huxley

Thomas Henry Huxley FRS
Born: 4 May 1825, Ealing, London, England
Died: 29 June 1895 (aged 70), Eastbourne, England
Known for: Championing Darwin's theory of evolution; coining "agnosticism"
Fields: Zoology, comparative anatomy, palaeontology

Thomas Henry Huxley (4 May 1825 – 29 June 1895) was an English biologist and anthropologist specialising in comparative anatomy. He earned the nickname "Darwin's Bulldog" for his vigorous public advocacy of Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection. Huxley was largely self-taught and became one of the most influential scientists and science educators of the Victorian era.

Early life and career

Huxley was born in Ealing, London, the second of eight children. Financial difficulties forced him to leave school at ten, but he continued his education through voracious reading. He studied medicine at Charing Cross Hospital and qualified in 1845. In 1846 he joined the Royal Navy survey ship HMS Rattlesnake as assistant surgeon, spending four years surveying the coasts of Australia and New Guinea. His studies of marine invertebrates, particularly jellyfish, published after his return, established his scientific reputation and led to his election as a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1851.

Defence of evolution

After reading On the Origin of Species (1859), Huxley wrote to Darwin that he was "prepared to go to the stake, if requisite" in its defence. His most famous confrontation came at the Oxford meeting of the British Association in June 1860, where he debated Bishop Samuel Wilberforce. According to the famous (and partly legendary) account, when Wilberforce asked whether Huxley claimed descent from an ape through his grandfather or grandmother, Huxley replied that he would rather have an ape for an ancestor than a man who used his gifts to obscure the truth.

Huxley's Evidence as to Man's Place in Nature (1863) was the first book to apply evolutionary ideas explicitly to human beings, arguing from comparative anatomy that humans and apes share a close common ancestry. He also made important contributions to palaeontology, including work on dinosaurs and early birds, and proposed that birds evolved from small carnivorous dinosaurs.

Agnosticism and education

Huxley coined the term "agnostic" in 1869 to describe his view that certain metaphysical and theological claims are unknowable. He was a tireless campaigner for science education in schools, for the professionalisation of science, and for secular public education. He served as president of the Royal Society from 1883 to 1885. His grandsons included the writer Aldous Huxley, the biologist Julian Huxley, and the Nobel laureate Andrew Huxley.
