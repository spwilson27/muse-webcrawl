History of agriculture

Agriculture began independently in different parts of the globe, and included a diverse range of taxa. At least eleven separate regions of the Old and New World were involved as independent centers of origin. The development of agriculture about 12,000 years ago changed the way humans lived. They switched from nomadic hunter-gatherer lifestyles to permanent settlements and farming.

Origins

Wild grains were collected and eaten from at least 104,000 years ago. However, domestication did not occur until much later. The first eight Neolithic founder crops — emmer wheat, einkorn wheat, hulled barley, peas, lentils, bitter vetch, chick peas and flax — arose in the Fertile Crescent around 9500 BCE. Rice was domesticated in China, maize in Mesoamerica, and potatoes and quinoa in the Andes, among many independent domestications worldwide.

Ancient and medieval farming

Irrigation, crop rotation and ploughs were developed over millennia. Sumerian farmers grew barley and dates; Egyptians farmed the Nile floodplain; Roman agronomists such as Columella wrote farming manuals. In the Middle Ages, the heavy plough, horse collar and three-field system raised productivity in northern Europe.

Modern agriculture

The Columbian exchange moved crops and livestock between hemispheres. The British Agricultural Revolution and later mechanisation, synthetic fertilisers (Haber–Bosch), selective breeding and the Green Revolution of the mid-20th century dramatically increased yields, supporting rapid population growth, while raising modern concerns about sustainability, soil health and environmental impact.
