The Thames Barrier is a retractable barrier system built to protect the floodplain of most of Greater London from exceptionally high tides and storm surges moving up from the North Sea. It has been operational since 1982 and is one of the largest movable flood barriers in the world.

History

The concept arose after the devastating North Sea flood of 1953, which killed over 300 people in England. A report by Sir Hermann Bondi in 1966 led to the Thames Barrier and Flood Prevention Act 1972. Construction began in 1974 at a site near Woolwich chosen because of the river's relative straightness and suitable geology. The barrier was officially opened by Queen Elizabeth II on 8 May 1984.

Design

The barrier spans 520 metres across the Thames at Woolwich Reach. Ten separate steel gates sit between nine concrete piers and two abutments. Four main navigation spans are each 61 metres wide and can be raised; the rising sector gates lie flat on the riverbed in a concrete sill when open, allowing ships to pass. When a flood threat is forecast, the gates are rotated upward, creating a continuous steel wall against the incoming tide.

Operation

The barrier is operated by the Environment Agency and is closed on average several times per year; closures have increased with rising sea levels. A notable series of closures occurred during the stormy winter of 2013–14. The barrier is expected to remain effective, with complementary defences, until around 2060–2070 under current sea-level-rise projections, and a successor scheme forms part of the Thames Estuary 2100 plan.
