Elementary particle


The elementary particles of the Standard Model: quarks, leptons, gauge bosons and the Higgs boson

In particle physics, an elementary particle or fundamental particle is a subatomic particle that is not composed of other particles. The Standard Model presently recognises 17 elementary particles (or 61 if colour charge variations of quarks are counted separately): 6 quarks, 6 leptons, 4 vector bosons and 1 scalar boson, together with their antiparticles.

Overview

Elementary particles are classified by spin. Fermions have half-integer spin and obey the Pauli exclusion principle; they comprise the quarks (up, down, charm, strange, top and bottom) and the leptons (electron, muon, tau and their neutrinos). Bosons have integer spin and mediate interactions: the photon (electromagnetism), the W and Z bosons (weak interaction), the gluons (strong interaction) and the Higgs boson, which is associated with the mechanism giving particles mass. The graviton, a hypothetical carrier of gravity, is not part of the Standard Model.

Generations

Fermions are arranged in three generations of increasing mass: ordinary matter is built from first-generation particles (up and down quarks, the electron and the electron neutrino), while the second and third generations appear in high-energy processes and cosmic rays and decay rapidly into lighter particles.

History

The electron (1897) was the first elementary particle identified, followed by the photon concept (1905), the proton and neutron, and the positron (1932). Particle accelerators from the mid-twentieth century revealed a "particle zoo" of hadrons, which Murray Gell-Mann's quark model (1964) explained as composites. The Higgs boson, predicted in 1964, was observed at CERN's Large Hadron Collider in 2012, completing the Standard Model's roster.

Open questions

The Standard Model does not explain gravity, dark matter, dark energy, neutrino masses or the matter–antimatter asymmetry of the universe. Theories such as supersymmetry postulate additional elementary particles that have not yet been observed.
