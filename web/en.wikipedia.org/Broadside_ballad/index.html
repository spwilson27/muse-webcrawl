Broadside ballad

A broadside ballad is a song printed on one side of a single sheet of paper (a broadside or broadsheet) and sold cheaply in streets, markets and fairs. Broadside ballads were a principal medium of popular song, news and entertainment in Britain and later America from the 16th century into the 19th century.

Form and content

A typical broadside was printed on low-quality paper, headed by one or more crude woodcut images (often re-used and only loosely related to the text), and gave the ballad's words with the instruction that it be sung "to the tune of…" a well-known melody, since no musical notation was provided. Ballads covered current events and sensations — murders, executions, battles, disasters, scandals and prodigies — alongside traditional songs of love, comedy, drink and the supernatural. They were hawked by ballad singers and chapmen for a halfpenny or a penny and were pasted on alehouse and cottage walls.

History and study

Broadsides appeared soon after printing reached England; thousands survive from the 17th century in collections such as Samuel Pepys's, the Roxburghe Ballads and the Bodleian Library's collections. Publishers clustered in centres such as Seven Dials in London and later in industrial towns and American cities. "Last dying speeches" of executed criminals and murder ballads were perennial sellers.

Many ballads collected by folklorists from oral tradition — including numbers in Francis James Child's English and Scottish Popular Ballads — also circulated in broadside form, and the interplay between print and oral transmission shaped the traditional repertoire. The trade declined in the later 19th century with the rise of cheap newspapers, music hall song sheets and improved literacy.
