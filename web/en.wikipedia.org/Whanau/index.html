Whanau — written in Māori with macrons as whānau — is a Māori-language word for an extended family group, the smallest and most basic social unit in traditional Māori society. The unmacronised spelling "whanau" is common in New Zealand English print and in names of organisations, where macrons are often omitted. A whanau consists of several generations — grandparents, parents, children and grandchildren — connected by descent from a common ancestor and bound together by obligations of mutual support, hospitality and collective responsibility.

Meaning and structure

The word is related to the verb whānau, "to be born", and to whenua, meaning both land and placenta/afterbirth — a linguistic link expressing the connection between people and the land of their birth. Members of a whanau typically lived together or near one another, worked collectively in food gathering and cultivation, and shared resources. Several whanau linked by a more distant common ancestor formed a hapū, and hapū in turn combined into an iwi.

Modern usage

In contemporary New Zealand, whanau is used broadly: it may mean one's family in the everyday sense, and public institutions have adopted the concept in programmes such as Whānau Ora, a government policy approach that places families at the centre of health and social services. The term is also applied metaphorically to groups bound by a shared purpose rather than kinship, such as a school "whanau class". New Zealand English speakers routinely use the word without macrons, and it appears in that spelling in legislation, media and the names of community organisations.

Values

Concepts associated with the whanau include whanaungatanga (relationship and kinship), manaakitanga (hospitality and care for others) and aroha (love and compassion). Decision-making within a whanau traditionally rests with elders (kaumātua), whose authority derives from their place in the genealogy (whakapapa).
