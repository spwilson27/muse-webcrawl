Opponent process

The opponent process is a theory in psychology and neuroscience that describes how certain systems respond to stimuli in opposing pairs. It is best known in two applications: Ewald Hering's theory of colour vision, in which colours are encoded by antagonistic channels, and Richard Solomon's opponent-process theory of emotion and motivation.

Colour vision

Proposed by Ewald Hering in the 1870s, the opponent-process theory holds that colour is coded in three opposing pairs — red versus green, blue versus yellow, and black versus white. A cell excited by red is inhibited by green, which explains why we perceive reddish-greens and yellowish-blues as impossible colours, and why staring at a coloured field produces an afterimage in the complementary colour. Modern physiology reconciles the theory with the trichromatic theory of the cones: cone signals are combined into opponent channels in retinal ganglion cells and the lateral geniculate nucleus. Leo Hurvich and Dorothea Jameson gave the theory quantitative form in the 1950s.

Emotion and addiction

Richard Solomon and John Corbit's opponent-process theory of acquired motivation (1974) proposes that every emotional state (the a-process) automatically triggers an opposing state (the b-process) that grows stronger with repetition. The model explains phenomena such as the pleasure of a first drug dose giving way to tolerance and craving, the relief after a parachute jump becoming exhilaration, and the dynamics of addiction and withdrawal.
