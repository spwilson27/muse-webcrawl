Deep operation

Deep operation (Russian: glubokaya operatsiya), also known as Soviet deep battle, is a military theory developed by the Soviet Union in the 1920s and 1930s. It holds that an enemy should be defeated not merely at the front line but throughout the entire depth of his deployment, using successive echelons, artillery, aviation, airborne forces, and mobile armoured groups to break through and exploit into the rear.

Development

The theory was developed by theorists including Vladimir Triandafillov and Mikhail Tukhachevsky, drawing on the experiences of the First World War and the Russian Civil War. It envisioned breakthrough operations followed by "development of success" — mobile groups racing into the enemy's depth to seize crossings, encircle reserves, and paralyse command and logistics.

Fate and application

Deep battle was suppressed during the Great Purge, in which Tukhachevsky and many of its advocates were executed, but it was revived as the Red Army rebuilt during the Second World War. It underlay major Soviet offensives such as Operation Bagration in 1944, and later influenced operational-level doctrine in many countries, including aspects of the US Army's AirLand Battle concept.
