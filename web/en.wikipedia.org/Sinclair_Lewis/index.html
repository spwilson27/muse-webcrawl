Harry Sinclair Lewis (February 7, 1885 – January 10, 1951) was an American writer and playwright. In 1930, he became the first writer from the United States to receive the Nobel Prize in Literature, awarded "for his vigorous and graphic art of description and his ability to create, with wit and humor, new types of characters".

Career

Lewis was born and raised in Sauk Centre, Minnesota, the model for the fictional Gopher Prairie of his breakthrough novel Main Street (1920), a satire of small-town conformity. Babbitt (1922) skewered the American middle-class businessman, and Arrowsmith (1925) examined the culture of science and medicine; Lewis declined the Pulitzer Prize awarded to Arrowsmith. Elmer Gantry (1927) attacked religious hypocrisy, while Dodsworth (1929) portrayed a wealthy manufacturer in retirement.

Later work and legacy

It Can't Happen Here (1935), a cautionary tale about a fascist takeover of the United States, has repeatedly returned to prominence. Lewis's later novels were less well received, and he died in Rome in 1951. His satirical portraits of boosterism and conformity — "Babbitt" entered English as a term for a complacent materialist — remain influential depictions of interwar America.
