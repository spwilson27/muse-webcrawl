Radioactivity

Radioactivity
Emission of ionizing radiation or particles from unstable atomic nuclei
SI unit: becquerel (Bq)

Radioactivity is the property of certain atomic nuclei to emit ionizing radiation spontaneously. It results from radioactive decay of unstable isotopes (radionuclides). The emissions — alpha particles, beta particles, gamma rays and neutrons — can ionize matter and are detected with instruments such as Geiger counters and scintillation detectors.

Units

Activity is measured in becquerels (one decay per second); the older unit is the curie. Absorbed dose is measured in grays, and equivalent dose, weighted for biological effect, in sieverts.

Sources

Natural radioactivity comes from primordial nuclides such as uranium-238, thorium-232 and potassium-40, from their decay products (notably radon gas), and from cosmic-ray interactions producing carbon-14 and tritium. Artificial sources include nuclear reactors, particle accelerators, medical isotopes and fallout from weapons testing.

Effects and uses

Ionizing radiation can damage living tissue and DNA, causing acute radiation syndrome at high doses and increasing cancer risk; it is also harnessed in medicine (diagnostic tracers, radiotherapy), industry (gauging, radiography), archaeology (radiocarbon dating) and energy production. Regulation of exposure follows the principle that doses should be kept as low as reasonably achievable.
