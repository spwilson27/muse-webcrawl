Atmospheric thermodynamics
Field: Atmospheric science
Key concepts: adiabatic processes, lapse rate, potential temperature, CAPE, phase changes of water

Atmospheric thermodynamics is the branch of atmospheric science that applies the laws of thermodynamics to air parcels and weather systems. It explains how heat, moisture, and motion interact to produce clouds, precipitation, and storms, and it provides the diagnostic tools—such as lapse rates, stability indices, and thermodynamic diagrams—used daily in weather forecasting.

Core concepts

Rising air expands and cools approximately adiabatically. Unsaturated air cools at the dry adiabatic lapse rate of about 9.8 °C per kilometre; once saturated, condensation releases latent heat and the cooling slows to the smaller moist adiabatic rate. Comparing the environmental lapse rate with these values determines atmospheric stability: whether displaced parcels return to their level, remain neutral, or accelerate away (instability), feeding convection and thunderstorms.

Key quantities include potential temperature, equivalent potential temperature, mixing ratio, and dew point. Convective available potential energy (CAPE) and convective inhibition measure the energy available to storms and the cap resisting them.

Diagrams

Forecasters plot soundings on thermodynamic diagrams such as the skew-T log-P diagram and the tephigram, from which cloud bases (lifting condensation level), equilibrium levels, and instability can be read directly.
