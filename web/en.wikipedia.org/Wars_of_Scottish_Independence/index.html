Wars of Scottish Independence

The Scots resist English invasion during the Wars of Independence

The Wars of Scottish Independence were a series of military campaigns fought between the Kingdom of Scotland and the Kingdom of England in the late 13th and early 14th centuries. The First War (1296–1328) began with the English invasion of Scotland in 1296 and ended with the signing of the Treaty of Edinburgh–Northampton in 1328. The Second War (1332–1357) began with the English-backed invasion by Edward Balliol and ended with the Treaty of Berwick in 1357.

Background

When King Alexander III of Scotland died in 1286, and his heir Margaret, Maid of Norway, died in 1290, Scotland was left without a monarch. Edward I of England was invited to arbitrate between competing claimants in the "Great Cause". He awarded the crown to John Balliol in 1292, but then sought to assert English overlordship, provoking resistance.

The First War

After Edward invaded in 1296 and deposed Balliol, resistance coalesced around William Wallace and Andrew Moray, who defeated an English army at the Battle of Stirling Bridge in 1297. Wallace was defeated at Falkirk in 1298 and executed in 1305. Robert the Bruce seized the throne in 1306 and, after years of guerrilla warfare, decisively defeated Edward II at the Battle of Bannockburn in 1314. The Declaration of Arbroath (1320) asserted Scotland's independence to the Pope, and England finally recognised Scottish independence in 1328.

The Second War

In 1332 Edward Balliol, son of the former king, invaded with English support and briefly took the throne. The young David II was sent to France for safety. Scottish loyalists waged a long guerrilla campaign, and although David was captured at the Battle of Neville's Cross in 1346, he was ransomed in 1357, bringing the wars to an end with Scotland's independence preserved.

Legacy

The wars cemented figures such as Wallace and Bruce as national heroes and profoundly shaped Scottish national identity.
