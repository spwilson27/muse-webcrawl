Epistasis

Epistasis

Field
Genetics
First described
William Bateson, 1909
Related
Dominance, pleiotropy, gene interaction

Epistasis is the phenomenon in genetics in which the effect of a mutation in one gene depends on the presence or absence of mutations in one or more other genes — that is, the interaction between genes that is not simply additive. The word was introduced by William Bateson in 1909, adapting a Greek term meaning "standing upon".

Types

In classical Mendelian terms, epistasis produces modified dihybrid ratios, such as 9:3:4 (recessive epistasis) or 12:3:1 (dominant epistasis). A classic example is coat colour in Labrador retrievers, where a locus controlling pigment deposition (E/e) masks the effect of the black-versus-brown locus (B/b): dogs homozygous recessive at E are yellow whatever their B genotype. In humans, the Bombay blood phenotype results from epistasis of the FUT1 gene over the ABO locus.

Evolutionary significance

In evolutionary biology and quantitative genetics, epistasis shapes fitness landscapes: because the fitness effect of a mutation depends on genetic background, epistasis can create valleys and multiple peaks in adaptive landscapes, contribute to speciation through hybrid incompatibilities (Dobzhansky–Muller incompatibilities), and influence the evolution of sexual reproduction and mutation rates.
