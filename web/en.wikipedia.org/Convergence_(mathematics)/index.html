Convergence (mathematics)

Convergence
FieldMathematical analysis, topology
OppositeDivergence
Key notionsLimit, Cauchy sequence, uniform convergence, absolute convergence

In mathematics, convergence is the property of certain sequences and series of numbers, functions, or other objects to approach a definite limit, becoming arbitrarily close to it as more terms are taken. A sequence or series that does not converge is said to diverge. Convergence is the foundation of the limit concept and hence of calculus and analysis.

Sequences

A sequence of real numbers (an) converges to a limit L if for every ε > 0 there exists a natural number N such that |an − L| < ε for all n ≥ N. This ε-δ style definition was made rigorous in the nineteenth century by Cauchy and Weierstrass. A sequence in which the terms become arbitrarily close to one another is a Cauchy sequence; in complete spaces such as the real numbers, every Cauchy sequence converges.

Series

An infinite series Σan converges if its sequence of partial sums converges. A series converges absolutely if Σ|an| converges, which implies convergence; series that converge but not absolutely are conditionally convergent, and by the Riemann series theorem their terms can be rearranged to sum to any prescribed value. Standard tests for convergence include the comparison, ratio, root, integral and alternating series tests.

Functions

Sequences of functions may converge pointwise — at each point separately — or uniformly, with a single N working for all points in the domain. Uniform convergence, studied by Stokes, Seidel and Weierstrass, is the condition that preserves continuity and permits exchanging limits with integrals and (under additional hypotheses) derivatives. In metric and topological spaces, convergence generalises to nets and filters, and in probability theory one distinguishes convergence in distribution, in probability and almost surely.
