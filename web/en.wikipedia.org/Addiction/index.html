Addiction

Medical information
Specialty:Psychiatry, addiction medicine, clinical psychology
Symptoms:Compulsive engagement in rewarding stimuli despite adverse consequences; craving; loss of control
Risk factors:Genetics, environment, trauma, early exposure, mental disorders

Addiction is a neuropsychological disorder characterised by a persistent and intense urge to use a drug or engage in a behaviour that produces natural reward, despite substantial harm and other negative consequences. Repetitive drug use can alter brain function in synapses similar to natural rewards like food or falling in love, in ways that perpetuate craving and weakens (but does not completely negate) self-control. This phenomenon — drugs reshaping neuronal transmission in the brain — has led addiction to be characterised as a brain disorder with a complex variety of psychosocial as well as neurobiological (and thus involuntary) factors that are implicated in its development.

Substance and behavioural addictions

Classic hallmarks of addiction include impaired control over substances or behaviour, preoccupation with the substance or behaviour, continued use despite consequences, and denial. Habits and patterns associated with addiction are typically characterised by immediate gratification (short-term reward), often coupled with delayed deleterious effects (long-term costs). Examples of drug addictions include alcoholism, cannabis addiction, amphetamine addiction, cocaine addiction, nicotine addiction, opioid addiction, and sedative addiction. Behavioural addictions — such as gambling addiction (recognised in the DSM-5) and gaming disorder (recognised in the ICD-11) — involve compulsions not related to exogenous substances, though the classification of other putative behavioural addictions remains debated.

Mechanisms

Addictive drugs and behaviours act on the brain's reward system, particularly the mesolimbic dopamine pathway centred on the nucleus accumbens. Transcription factors such as ΔFosB, which accumulate with repeated exposure, are implicated in the long-lasting changes underlying compulsive use. Tolerance, dependence, and withdrawal contribute to the maintenance of substance use disorders, but addiction can persist long after withdrawal has resolved, reflecting durable neuroadaptations and conditioned cue responses that drive relapse.

Treatment and recovery

Effective treatments include behavioural therapies (such as cognitive behavioural therapy and contingency management), mutual-help groups (e.g. twelve-step programmes), and medications: opioid agonist therapy (methadone, buprenorphine) for opioid use disorder, nicotine replacement and varenicline for tobacco, and acamprosate or naltrexone for alcohol use disorder, among others. Recovery is often a long-term process with risk of relapse; harm-reduction approaches (needle exchanges, naloxone distribution) reduce mortality and disease transmission even where abstinence is not achieved.
