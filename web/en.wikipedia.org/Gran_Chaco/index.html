Gran Chaco

CountriesParaguay, Bolivia, Argentina, Brazil
AreaApproximately 647,500 km²
ClimateSemi-arid to tropical savanna
Major riversPilcomayo, Bermejo, Paraguay

The Gran Chaco (from Quechua chaqu, "hunting land") is a sparsely populated, hot and semi-arid lowland plain in the Río de la Plata basin, divided among western Paraguay, southeastern Bolivia, northern Argentina, and a small part of Brazil. Covering some 647,500 square kilometres, it is one of South America's major geographical regions and one of its largest remaining areas of dry forest.

Geography and ecology

The Chaco is a flat alluvial plain sloping gently from the Andean foothills in the west toward the Paraguay River in the east. Rainfall decreases from east to west; the Humid Chaco near the river gives way to the semi-arid and arid Central and Dry Chaco, dominated by thorn forest of quebracho and algarrobo trees, cacti, and palm savannas. Wildlife includes jaguars, giant anteaters, peccaries (including the Chacoan peccary, once thought extinct), and the giant armadillo. The region experiences extreme summer heat—among the highest temperatures recorded in South America—and seasonal flooding.

History

Indigenous peoples—including Wichí, Toba (Qom), Guaraní, and Ayoreo groups—have inhabited the Chaco for millennia and long resisted colonial penetration. In the 20th century the region was contested in the Chaco War (1932–1935) between Bolivia and Paraguay, one of the bloodiest South American wars of the century, fought partly over rumoured oil deposits; Paraguay gained most of the disputed territory. Mennonite colonies founded from the 1920s became centres of cattle ranching and dairy production in the Paraguayan Central Chaco.

Modern pressures

Since the 2000s the Gran Chaco has had one of the world's highest deforestation rates, driven by cattle ranching and soybean cultivation, threatening its biodiversity and the land rights of indigenous communities, including groups living in voluntary isolation.
