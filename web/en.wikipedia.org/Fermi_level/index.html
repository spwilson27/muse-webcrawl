Fermi level

Fermi level
Named afterEnrico Fermi
FieldCondensed matter physics, statistical mechanics

The Fermi level of a solid-state body is the thermodynamic work required to add one electron to the body. It is the electrochemical potential of electrons in the solid, usually denoted EF or μ, and it determines the statistical occupation of electron states through the Fermi–Dirac distribution:

f(E) = 1 / ( e(E − μ)/kT + 1 )

At absolute zero, the Fermi level equals the Fermi energy, the energy of the highest occupied quantum state in a system of non-interacting fermions.

In band theory

In a metal, the Fermi level lies inside a partially filled band, so electrons near it can move freely and conduct current. In an insulator, the Fermi level lies within a large band gap between the valence and conduction bands. In an intrinsic semiconductor it lies near the middle of a small gap; doping shifts it toward the conduction band (n-type) or valence band (p-type), a principle exploited in diodes and transistors.

Measurement and significance

The Fermi level can be measured by techniques such as photoelectron spectroscopy and Kelvin probe microscopy (which yields the work function relative to it). Differences in Fermi levels drive the behaviour of p–n junctions, Schottky barriers, and thermoelectric effects. When two materials are brought into contact, electrons flow until their Fermi levels align, creating a contact potential.
