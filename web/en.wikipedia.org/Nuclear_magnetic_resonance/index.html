Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is a physical phenomenon in which atomic nuclei in a magnetic field absorb and re-emit electromagnetic radiation at a characteristic resonance frequency. The effect depends on the magnetic properties of the isotope: only nuclei with non-zero spin, such as 1H and 13C, exhibit NMR.

Principle

In an external magnetic field, nuclear spins align with or against the field, creating a small energy difference. Radiofrequency pulses can flip the spins; as they relax, they emit a signal (the free induction decay) whose frequencies — the chemical shifts — reveal the local chemical environment of each nucleus. Coupling between neighbouring spins splits the peaks, giving detailed structural information.

Applications

NMR spectroscopy is the most powerful routine method for determining the structure of organic molecules in solution, and is also used to study protein structures and dynamics. The same physics underlies magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in medicine. Isidor Rabi first described NMR in molecular beams in 1938; Felix Bloch and Edward Purcell independently demonstrated it in bulk matter in 1946, sharing the 1952 Nobel Prize in Physics.
