Bank War

Andrew Jackson's campaign against the Second Bank of the United States defined the politics of the early 1830s.

The Bank War was a political struggle that developed around the rechartering of the Second Bank of the United States (B.U.S.) during the presidency of Andrew Jackson in the 1830s. The affair resulted in the destruction of the bank and its replacement by various state banks, and it marked a major episode in the development of American presidential power and party politics.

Chartered in 1816 for twenty years, the Second Bank served as the federal government's fiscal agent and regulated state bank notes. Its president from 1823, Nicholas Biddle, ran it as a powerful quasi-central bank. Jackson — who distrusted banks and paper money on principle and regarded the B.U.S. as an unconstitutional monopoly benefiting elites — entered office in 1829 already hostile to recharter.

Course of the struggle

At the urging of Henry Clay and Daniel Webster, Congress passed a recharter bill in 1832, four years early, hoping to make it an election issue; Jackson vetoed it on 10 July 1832, asserting both constitutional objections and a broad claim of independent presidential interpretation of the Constitution. He won re-election decisively that November. In 1833 Jackson struck again, ordering the removal of federal deposits to selected state banks (the "pet banks"); his resistance to Senate pressure — including the censure of 1834, later expunged — entrenched the principle of an assertive presidency. The bank's federal charter expired in 1836.

Consequences

Without a central regulator, state bank lending expanded rapidly, fuelling a speculative boom in western land. Jackson's Specie Circular of 1836 required hard money for public land purchases; the following year the Panic of 1837 plunged the country into depression. The Bank War helped crystallise the second party system: Jacksonian Democrats against the new Whig Party. The United States would have no central bank again until the Federal Reserve in 1913.
