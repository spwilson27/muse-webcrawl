Bile duct

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

SystemDigestive system
FunctionCarries bile from liver and gallbladder to the duodenum
Key partsHepatic ducts, cystic duct, common bile duct

A bile duct is any of the tube-like structures that carry bile, the digestive fluid produced by the liver, to the small intestine. The network of bile ducts inside and outside the liver is known as the biliary tree.

Anatomy

Bile is collected from liver cells into tiny canaliculi, which merge into intrahepatic ducts and then into the right and left hepatic ducts. These join at the porta hepatis to form the common hepatic duct. The cystic duct from the gallbladder joins it, and below that junction the channel is called the common bile duct (ductus choledochus), typically 6–8 cm long. The common bile duct descends behind the duodenum, usually joins the pancreatic duct at the ampulla of Vater, and empties into the second part of the duodenum through the major duodenal papilla, guarded by the sphincter of Oddi.

Function

Between meals, bile is diverted into the gallbladder for storage and concentration. When fatty food enters the duodenum, the hormone cholecystokinin triggers gallbladder contraction and relaxation of the sphincter of Oddi, releasing bile to emulsify dietary fats and aid absorption of fat-soluble vitamins.

Clinical significance

Obstruction of the bile ducts — most often by gallstones (choledocholithiasis), strictures, or tumours of the pancreas or bile duct (cholangiocarcinoma) — causes obstructive jaundice, pale stools, and dark urine. Ascending infection of an obstructed duct causes cholangitis, a medical emergency. Imaging includes ultrasound, MRCP, and ERCP, the last of which can also remove stones or place stents.
