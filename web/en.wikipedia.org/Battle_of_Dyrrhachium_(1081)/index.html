The Battle of Dyrrhachium was fought on 18 October 1081 between the Byzantine Empire, led by the new emperor Alexios I Komnenos, and the Italo-Normans of Robert Guiscard, Duke of Apulia, outside the Adriatic port of Dyrrhachium (modern Durrës). It was the opening battle of the first Norman invasion of the Balkans and ended in a severe Byzantine defeat — though Alexios escaped and ultimately outlasted the invasion.

Background

Guiscard, having completed the Norman conquest of Byzantine southern Italy (Bari, the last Byzantine outpost, fell in 1071), aimed at the empire itself, using as pretext the cause of the deposed emperor Michael VII, whose son had been betrothed to Guiscard's daughter. In spring 1081 the Normans crossed the Adriatic and besieged Dyrrhachium, key to the Via Egnatia and the road to Thessalonica and Constantinople. Alexios, emperor for barely six months, scraped together a polyglot army — Byzantine regulars, the Varangian Guard (largely Anglo-Saxon exiles), Turkish mercenaries and contingents from allied Balkan peoples — and marched to relieve the city.

The battle

Guiscard's army was drawn up with the sea on one flank; his wife Sikelgaita is depicted by Anna Komnene rallying the Norman troops. Alexios planned a pincer movement, but the Varangians and a premature attack by his left wing disrupted the plan. When the Norman heavy cavalry under Guiscard charged, the Byzantine centre collapsed; the Varangians, cut off around the church of St Michael, were massacred almost to a man. Alexios himself was wounded and barely escaped to Ohrid, leaving perhaps 5,000–6,000 dead, including much of the empire's military aristocracy.

Aftermath

Dyrrhachium fell in February 1082 and the Normans advanced inland toward Thessalonica. But Guiscard was recalled to Italy by revolts and papal appeals for help against Emperor Henry IV — whom Alexios's gold had incited — and although Bohemond won further victories, supply, disease and Alexios's diplomacy eroded the invasion. After Guiscard's death at Cephalonia in 1085, the remaining Normans withdrew, and Byzantium recovered Dyrrhachium. The battle remains a classic study of the clash between Norman shock cavalry and the Byzantine combined-arms tradition, told vividly in the Alexiad and in Latin chroniclers such as William of Apulia.
