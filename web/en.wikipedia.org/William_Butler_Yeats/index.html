William Butler Yeats

William Butler Yeats
Born: 13 June 1865, Sandymount, Ireland
Died: 28 January 1939, Menton, France
Occupation: Poet, playwright
Awards: Nobel Prize in Literature (1923)

William Butler Yeats (13 June 1865 – 28 January 1939) was an Irish poet, dramatist, and writer, and one of the foremost figures of 20th-century literature. A pillar of the Irish literary establishment, he helped found the Abbey Theatre and served as a Senator of the Irish Free State. In 1923 he became the first Irishman to receive the Nobel Prize in Literature.

Early life and the Celtic Twilight

Yeats was born in Dublin into an artistic Anglo-Irish family; his father, John Butler Yeats, was a portrait painter. His early poetry, gathered in The Wanderings of Oisin (1889) and The Wind Among the Reeds (1899), drew on Irish mythology and folklore and helped launch the Irish Literary Revival. With Lady Gregory and Edward Martyn he founded the Irish Literary Theatre, which became the Abbey Theatre in 1904.

Personal life and Maud Gonne

Yeats's unrequited love for the nationalist Maud Gonne inspired much of his lyric poetry; he proposed to her repeatedly and was refused. In 1917 he married Georgie Hyde-Lees, whose automatic writing sessions with him produced the esoteric system of A Vision (1925), which underlies many of his later poems' imagery of gyres and historical cycles.

Later work

Yeats's greatest poetry came late: collections such as The Tower (1928) and The Winding Stair (1933) include "Sailing to Byzantium," "Among School Children," "Leda and the Swan," and "The Second Coming" (1919), whose lines — "Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold" — became among the most quoted of the century. He spent his final years partly in France and died at Menton in 1939; his remains were reinterred at Drumcliff, County Sligo, beneath the epitaph he composed: "Cast a cold Eye / On Life, on Death. / Horseman, pass by!"
