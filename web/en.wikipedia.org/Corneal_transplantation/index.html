Corneal transplantation

Corneal transplantation, also known as corneal grafting or keratoplasty, is a surgical procedure in which a damaged or diseased cornea is replaced by donated corneal tissue. When the entire cornea is replaced it is known as penetrating keratoplasty; when only part of the cornea is replaced it is known as lamellar keratoplasty. It is among the most commonly performed and most successful transplant procedures in medicine.

Indications

Transplantation is indicated when the cornea becomes cloudy or distorted so that vision cannot be corrected with glasses or contact lenses. Causes include keratoconus, Fuchs' dystrophy, corneal scarring from infection or trauma, corneal ulcers, and complications of previous eye surgery.

Procedure

In penetrating keratoplasty, a circular disc of the patient's cornea is removed with a trephine and replaced with a matching disc from a donor, held in place by fine sutures. Lamellar techniques replace only the diseased layers: deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK) spares the patient's endothelium, while endothelial keratoplasty procedures such as DSAEK and DMEK replace only the inner layer, allowing faster visual recovery.

Risks and outcomes

The cornea's lack of blood vessels gives grafts a high survival rate compared with other organs, and routine systemic immunosuppression is not usually required. Risks include graft rejection, infection, glaucoma, and irregular astigmatism. Eye banks screen and store donor tissue, and most grafts restore useful vision.
