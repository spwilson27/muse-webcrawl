Tacit knowledge

Tacit knowledge is knowledge that is difficult to extract or articulate, as opposed to explicit knowledge that can be codified in words, numbers or manuals. It includes skills, intuition and know-how acquired through personal experience.

Origins

The philosopher Michael Polanyi introduced the concept in The Tacit Dimension (1966), famously observing that "we can know more than we can tell." Riding a bicycle, recognising a face, or a craftsperson's feel for materials are classic examples.

Knowledge management

Organisations study tacit knowledge because valuable expertise often resides in employees' habits and judgement rather than in documents. Nonaka and Takeuchi's SECI model describes how tacit knowledge can be shared through socialisation and partially converted to explicit knowledge through externalisation.

Criticism and discussion

Scholars debate whether tacit knowledge can truly be converted into explicit form or whether articulation always loses its essential character. The concept remains influential in philosophy of science, education and artificial intelligence, where embodied skill resists simple rule-based capture.
