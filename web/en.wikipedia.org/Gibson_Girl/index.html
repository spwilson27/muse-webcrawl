Gibson Girl

The Gibson Girl was the personification of the feminine ideal of beauty in the United States at the turn of the twentieth century, as portrayed in the satirical pen-and-ink drawings of artist Charles Dana Gibson from the 1890s through the First World War. She is widely considered the first American standard of feminine beauty.

Appearance and character

The Gibson Girl was tall and slender yet voluptuous, with an exaggerated S-curve silhouette created by the corset. Her most distinctive feature was her hair, piled high in a bouffant, pompadour, or loose chignon atop her head. Unlike earlier ideals, she was depicted as confident, independent, and socially assured: she played golf and tennis, rode bicycles, attended college, and regarded the men who courted her with amused detachment. Gibson's drawings, published widely in magazines such as Life, showed her as modern and self-possessed without challenging social conventions outright.

Models and influence

Gibson said the image was a composite of many women rather than a portrait of one model, though his wife Irene Langhorne and her sisters were important inspirations; the actress Camille Clifford became famous as a living embodiment of the type. The Gibson Girl influenced fashion, advertising, and popular culture, appearing on merchandise of every kind, and she paved the way for later ideals such as the flapper of the 1920s.

The image faded during the First World War, as women took on war work and fashions changed, but it remains an iconic representation of the Edwardian-era American beauty ideal and the emerging "New Woman".
