Grassmann's law

Grassmann's law, named after its discoverer Hermann Grassmann (1863), is a dissimilatory sound change in Ancient Greek and Sanskrit. It states that if an aspirated consonant is followed by another aspirated consonant in the next syllable, the first one loses its aspiration. The law operated independently in Greek and in Indo-Aryan after their separation from Proto-Indo-European, and is a classic example of long-distance dissimilation in phonology.

Examples

In Greek, the reduplicated present of the verb títhēmi "I put" (from the root thē-, "put") shows loss of the first aspiration: the expected form *thithēmi became títhēmi. Similarly the Greek word for "hair", nominative thríx, has genitive trikhós, because in the nominative the following aspirate triggered deaspiration of the initial consonant, while in the genitive the aspirate cluster was broken up by other sound changes. In Sanskrit, the root dha- "put" reduplicates as dadhāti rather than *dhadhāti, and the root budh- "awaken" forms bodhati for the same reason.

Interaction with other changes

In Greek, Grassmann's law applied after Grimm-type aspiration of Proto-Indo-European voiced aspirates, and it interacts with the loss of aspiration before fricatives: the alternation between thríx and trikhós is explained by ordering the deaspiration of *kʰ before *s (which bleeds Grassmann's law) or vice versa in different forms. The law is also one of the arguments used in reconstructing the phonology of the Indo-European parent language.
