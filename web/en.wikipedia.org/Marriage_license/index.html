Marriage license

A marriage license (or marriage licence in British English) is a document issued, either by a government or a church authority, authorizing a couple to marry. The procedure for obtaining a license varies between jurisdictions but typically involves the couple applying in advance, providing identification and paying a fee; the license is then signed at or after the ceremony and filed with the registrar, from which a marriage certificate can later be issued.

History

Licences began in the medieval church as an alternative to the reading of banns, allowing a couple to marry without the customary three weeks of public announcement. In England, Lord Hardwicke's Marriage Act 1753 made formal requirements a matter of civil validity. In colonial and later United States practice, the license became a state-issued prerequisite to a lawful ceremony; in the early 20th century many states added medical requirements such as blood tests for syphilis, most of which have since been repealed.

Requirements and variations

Requirements differ widely: minimum ages (generally 18, with exceptions), waiting periods between application and use (from none to several days), expiry periods, residency rules and, in some places, premarital counselling. Many jurisdictions recognise common-law marriage without a license, though the number is declining. The license records the names, ages and signatures of the parties, the officiant and witnesses, and its filing creates the official record of the marriage.
