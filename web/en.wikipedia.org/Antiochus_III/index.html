Antiochus III the Great (Greek: Ἀντίοχος ὁ Μέγας; c. 241 – 3 July 187 BC) was the sixth ruler of the Seleucid Empire, reigning from 222 to 187 BC. Coming to the throne as a teenager amid revolts that had stripped the empire of much of its territory, he spent his reign restoring Seleucid power: he reconquered the Upper Satrapies in the east, subdued Asia Minor, and seized Palestine from Ptolemaic Egypt. His later wars against the Roman Republic ended in defeat at Thermopylae and Magnesia and the humiliating Treaty of Apamea (188 BC), which curtailed Seleucid power in the west.

Early reign and revolts

Antiochus succeeded his murdered brother Seleucus III in 222 BC. The empire was in crisis: Molon, satrap of Media, and his brother Alexander rebelled in the east, while the king's uncle Achaeus seized control of Asia Minor. Antiochus first crushed Molon's revolt in 220 BC, then turned to the Fourth Syrian War against Ptolemy IV of Egypt, which ended with his defeat at the Battle of Raphia (217 BC) and the loss of Coele-Syria.

The eastern anabasis

Between 212 and 205 BC Antiochus undertook a great eastern expedition recalling the campaigns of Alexander the Great and Seleucus I. He forced Artaxias of Armenia into vassalage, reduced the Parthian king Arsaces II, marched into Bactria and besieged Euthydemus I at Bactra, and crossed the Hindu Kush into India, where he renewed treaties with the Indian king Sophagasenus and received elephants and treasure. The expedition earned him the title "the Great" and restored his prestige and revenues.

Wars in the west

Antiochus next recovered Asia Minor from Achaeus's successors and, in alliance with Philip V of Macedon, invaded Egypt's possessions, winning the decisive Battle of Panium (200 BC) and annexing Coele-Syria and Palestine in the Fifth Syrian War. In 196 BC he crossed into Thrace, claiming the heritage of Lysimachus, which brought him into confrontation with Rome.

War with Rome and death

Invited by the Aetolian League, Antiochus landed in Greece in 192 BC, opening the Roman–Seleucid War. Roman forces under Manius Acilius Glabrio defeated him at Thermopylae (191 BC), and in Asia the armies of Lucius Cornelius Scipio and his brother Scipio Africanus crushed his army at the Battle of Magnesia (190/189 BC). By the Treaty of Apamea (188 BC) Antiochus surrendered his territories west of the Taurus Mountains, paid an indemnity of 15,000 talents, gave up his war elephants and most of his fleet, and sent hostages — including his son, the future Antiochus IV — to Rome. In 187 BC, while attempting to seize the treasury of a temple of Bel in Elam to pay the indemnity, he was killed by the local population.
