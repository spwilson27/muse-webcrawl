The Hundred Years' War was a series of armed conflicts fought between the kingdoms of England and France during the Late Middle Ages. It originated from disputed claims to the French throne between the English royal House of Plantagenet and the French House of Valois. Lasting 116 years, from 1337 to 1453, the war was punctuated by long periods of truce and is conventionally divided into three phases: the Edwardian War (1337–1360), the Caroline War (1369–1389), and the Lancastrian War (1415–1453).

Causes

The proximate cause was dynastic. When Charles IV of France died in 1328 without a male heir, Edward III of England—grandson of Philip IV through his mother Isabella—claimed the French crown, but the French nobility chose Philip of Valois as Philip VI, invoking principles later articulated as Salic law. Beneath the claim lay feudal and economic friction: the English king held Gascony as a vassal of the French crown, France supported Scotland against England, and both crowns contested influence over wealthy Flanders and its wool trade. In 1337 Philip confiscated Gascony, and Edward responded by formally asserting his claim to the throne.

Course of the war

Edwardian phase

English armies won striking victories with longbow-centred tactics: at sea at Sluys (1340), on land at Crécy (1346), and at Poitiers (1356), where King John II of France was captured. The Treaty of Brétigny (1360) granted Edward an enlarged Aquitaine in full sovereignty in return for renouncing the French crown. Civil unrest in France—the Jacquerie and the revolt of Étienne Marcel—and the disruption of chevauchée raids devastated the French countryside.

Caroline phase

Under Charles V and his constable Bertrand du Guesclin, France avoided pitched battle and steadily recovered territory in the 1370s, leaving England with little more than coastal enclaves. Both sides, exhausted and distracted by internal crises (the Peasants' Revolt in England, the Armagnac–Burgundian civil war in France), settled into truce from 1389.

Lancastrian phase

Henry V renewed the war in 1415, winning at Agincourt against a much larger French army, then conquered Normandy. Allied with Burgundy after the assassination of Duke John the Fearless, England secured the Treaty of Troyes (1420), which made Henry heir and regent of France. But both Henry and Charles VI died in 1422, leaving the infant Henry VI proclaimed king of both realms while the Dauphin, Charles VII, held the south. The siege of Orléans (1428–29) was lifted by a French relief army inspired by Joan of Arc, whose victories at Orléans and Patay and whose escort of Charles to his coronation at Reims reversed the war's momentum. Joan was captured, tried, and burned at Rouen in 1431, but French recovery continued: Paris was regained (1436), Burgundy defected at the Congress of Arras (1435), and reformed French armies with powerful artillery won the final battles of Formigny (1450) and Castillon (1453). England retained only Calais.

Significance

The war accelerated the decline of chivalric cavalry, elevated longbowmen and then gunpowder artillery, and hastened the growth of professional standing armies and centralised taxation in both kingdoms. It sharpened distinct English and French national identities—notably, English replaced French in English government—and left France devastated but politically consolidated under the Valois monarchy.
