Mesh generation

Mesh generation
FieldComputational geometry / numerical simulation
OutputsStructured, unstructured, or hybrid meshes
ApplicationsFinite element analysis, CFD, computer graphics

Mesh generation is the practice of creating a mesh — a discretization of a geometric domain into small, simple cells (elements) — suitable for solving partial differential equations by numerical methods such as the finite element and finite volume methods. The mesh subdivides a continuous domain into triangles or quadrilaterals in two dimensions, or tetrahedra, hexahedra, prisms, and pyramids in three, on which the equations of physics can be approximated.

Types of meshes

Structured meshes have regular grid connectivity, like a deformed Cartesian grid, and give efficient, accurate solvers but are hard to fit to complex geometry. Unstructured meshes, typically of triangles or tetrahedra, conform to arbitrary shapes and permit local refinement but require more storage and computation. Hybrid and boundary-layer meshes combine prismatic layers near walls — essential for resolving viscous flow gradients in computational fluid dynamics — with unstructured elements elsewhere. Mesh quality is judged by element shape measures such as aspect ratio, skewness, and minimum angle, since poorly shaped elements degrade accuracy and solver stability.

Techniques

Common generation methods include Delaunay triangulation and its dual Voronoi approach; advancing-front methods, which grow elements inward from the boundary; octree (quadtree) methods, which subdivide space and clip cells to the geometry; and sweeping/mapping methods for structured blocks. Commercial packages (ANSYS Meshing, Pointwise) and open-source tools (Gmsh, TetGen, Netgen) automate generation, and mesh adaptation can refine or coarsen elements during a simulation based on error estimates — a central technology in aerospace, automotive, biomedical, and geophysical simulation.

Challenges

Generating high-quality three-dimensional meshes for industrial geometries remains one of the most human-time-consuming steps of the simulation pipeline, and robust automatic hexahedral meshing is still an open research problem. Contemporary research addresses parallel generation, anisotropic adaptation, high-order curved elements, and learning-based approaches.
