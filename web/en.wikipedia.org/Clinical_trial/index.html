Clinical trials are prospective biomedical or behavioural research studies on human participants designed to answer specific questions about biomedical or behavioural interventions, including new treatments (such as novel vaccines, drugs, dietary choices, dietary supplements, and medical devices) and known interventions that warrant further study and comparison.

Clinical trials generate data on dosage, safety, and efficacy. They are conducted only after health-authority and ethics-committee approval. Depending on product type and development stage, investigators initially enrol healthy volunteers and/or patients into small pilot studies, and subsequently conduct larger-scale studies in patients if the data so far suggest safety and efficacy.

Phases

  • Phase 0: exploratory studies in very few subjects, sometimes with microdoses.
  • Phase I: first testing in a small group of healthy volunteers (or patients) to assess safety, dosage, and side effects.
  • Phase II: testing in a larger group of patients to assess efficacy and further evaluate safety.
  • Phase III: large, often randomised controlled trials comparing the intervention with standard care or a placebo, forming the basis for regulatory approval.
  • Phase IV: post-marketing surveillance after approval.

Design

Trials may be randomised, blinded (single- or double-blind), and placebo-controlled to reduce bias. Protocols define eligibility criteria, endpoints, and statistical analysis in advance. Since 2005, the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors has required prospective registration of trials in public registries such as ClinicalTrials.gov.

Ethics and history

Modern trial ethics derive from the Nuremberg Code (1947) and the Declaration of Helsinki (1964), emphasising informed consent and independent review. James Lind's 1747 scurvy experiment aboard HMS Salisbury is often cited as an early controlled trial; the 1948 Medical Research Council trial of streptomycin is considered the first modern randomised controlled trial.
