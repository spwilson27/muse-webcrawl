Soju

Soju (Korean: 소주) is a clear Korean distilled alcoholic beverage. It is traditionally made from rice, although modern producers often use other starches such as wheat, barley, sweet potatoes, or tapioca. Soju typically has an alcohol content ranging from about 12.5% to over 50% ABV, with the most common commercial varieties around 16–20%.

Soju is the best-selling spirit in South Korea and, through the brand Jinro, has frequently been the world's best-selling spirit by volume. It is usually consumed neat in small shot glasses, often accompanying meals and social gatherings.

History

Distillation techniques arrived on the Korean Peninsula during the Mongol invasions of the 13th century, when the Yuan dynasty introduced the Middle Eastern alembic (araq) to garrisons such as those in Andong and Kaesong; Andong soju remains famous for its traditional high-strength style. During the Joseon dynasty, soju was a prized drink, though periodic bans were imposed when rice was scarce.

In 1965, amid rice shortages, the South Korean government prohibited the use of rice for soju, and producers shifted to diluted ethanol made from other starches, flavored and sweetened to resemble the original. Restrictions were relaxed in the 1990s, and both mass-market diluted soju and premium distilled soju have flourished since.

Production

Traditional soju is made by fermenting a grain mash with nuruk, a Korean fermentation starter containing wild yeasts and molds, and then distilling the resulting brew in a pot still. Modern green-bottle soju is mostly produced by diluting high-strength neutral ethanol with water and adding sweeteners and flavorings. Alcohol content of mainstream brands has gradually fallen from around 30% in the 1970s to roughly 16% today.

Consumption and etiquette

Soju drinking has an associated etiquette: pouring for others with two hands or with the right hand supported by the left, receiving with two hands, and turning away from elders when drinking. It is frequently mixed with beer to make somaek, and fruit-flavored varieties have become popular since the mid-2010s. Soju features prominently in Korean dining culture alongside barbecue and other shared dishes.

Brands

Major brands include Jinro's Chamisul, Lotte's Chum-Churum, and regional brands such as Hallasan (Jeju), C1 (Busan), and Ipsaeju (Jeolla). Premium distilled brands such as Hwayo and Andong Soju target consumers seeking traditional methods.
