Microfinance

FocusFinancial services for low-income clients
Key productsMicrocredit, microsavings, microinsurance
PioneersMuhammad Yunus, Grameen Bank

Microfinance is a category of financial services targeting individuals and small businesses that lack access to conventional banking and related services. Microfinance includes microcredit, the provision of small loans to poor entrepreneurs and small businesses lacking collateral, as well as savings accounts, insurance and money transfers.

History

The modern movement traces to experiments in the 1970s, most famously the Grameen Bank founded by Muhammad Yunus in Bangladesh, which lent tiny sums — often to groups of women who guaranteed one another's loans — and achieved high repayment rates. Yunus and Grameen shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006 "for their efforts to create economic and social development from below". Institutions such as ACCION, BRAC and Pro Mujer spread similar models across Asia, Africa and Latin America.

Debate

Microfinance was long promoted as a tool capable of lifting households out of poverty, but randomised evaluations have generally found more modest effects: expanded business activity and consumption smoothing, with limited average impact on income or poverty. Critics point to high interest rates, over-indebtedness crises in some markets, and the commercialisation of once-charitable lenders. Supporters argue that access to financial services is valuable in itself and that microfinance is best understood as one part of broader financial inclusion.
