Powertrain

In a motor vehicle, the powertrain comprises the components that generate power and deliver it to the road surface: the engine or motor, transmission, driveshafts, differentials, and the final drive. The term drivetrain is often used more narrowly for the components after the engine — transmission, driveshaft, and axles — though in common and industry usage the two terms overlap heavily.

Components

  • Engine/motor — converts fuel or electrical energy into mechanical rotation (internal combustion engine, electric motor, or a hybrid combination).
  • Transmission — matches engine speed and torque to road conditions through gear ratios (manual, automatic, CVT, or single-speed reduction in most electric cars).
  • Driveshaft and axles — transmit rotation to the driven wheels, via constant-velocity joints where steering or suspension movement is involved.
  • Differential — splits torque between wheels on an axle while allowing them to rotate at different speeds in corners.

Layouts

Vehicles are arranged as front-, rear-, or all-wheel drive, with engines mounted longitudinally or transversely. The classic layout is front-engine, rear-wheel drive; most modern passenger cars use front-engine, front-wheel drive for packaging efficiency. Hybrid powertrains combine an engine with one or more electric motors and a battery, in series, parallel, or power-split configurations; battery-electric powertrains simplify the drivetrain dramatically, often to a single-speed gearbox and inverter-driven motor.

Control and development

Modern powertrains are governed by electronic control units that coordinate fuel injection, ignition, gear selection, and torque distribution, optimising performance, fuel economy, and emissions. Manufacturers commonly organise engineering around shared "powertrain" divisions and platforms, since the engine–transmission combination largely determines a vehicle's character, efficiency, and regulatory compliance.
