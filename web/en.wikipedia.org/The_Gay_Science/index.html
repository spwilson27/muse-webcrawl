The Gay Science

The Gay Science (German: Die fröhliche Wissenschaft; sometimes translated as The Joyful Wisdom) is a book by the German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche, first published in 1882 and issued in an expanded edition in 1887 with a fifth book, a preface, and an appendix of songs. Composed largely of aphorisms and short essays interspersed with poems, it is among the most personal and stylistically varied of Nietzsche's works.

Key ideas

The book contains the first statement of the idea of the eternal recurrence (aphorism 341, "The Greatest Weight", in which a demon proposes that one's life will repeat forever) and the famous declaration "God is dead" (aphorism 125, "The Madman", who announces that humanity has killed God and must become worthy of the deed). Book Four closes with aphorism 342, Incipit tragoedia ("the tragedy begins"), introducing the figure of Zarathustra, whom Nietzsche's next book, Thus Spoke Zarathustra, would take as its prophet.

Title

The title, drawn from the Provençal notion of the gai saber — the art of poetry practised by the troubadours — signals Nietzsche's ambition to unite art and science, lightness and rigour. Nietzsche considered the book his most intimate work, the record of a convalescence and an affirmation of life after illness.

Editions and translations

The widely read English translation is Walter Kaufmann's (1974). The original 1882 edition contained four books and 342 aphorisms; the 1887 edition added Book Five, opening with an aphorism on the death of God and its consequences for European morality.
