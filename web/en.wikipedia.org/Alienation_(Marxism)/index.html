Alienation (Marxism)

Karl Marx's theory of alienation (German: Entfremdung) describes the social alienation of people from aspects of their human nature consequent to living in a society of stratified social classes. It is a central concept of the early Marx, developed most fully in the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, first published in 1932.

Four forms of alienation

Marx argued that under capitalism the worker is alienated in four ways: from the product of their labour, which belongs to another; from the labour process itself, which is coerced and not self-directed; from their "species-being" (Gattungswesen), their human potential for free, conscious, creative activity; and from other workers, with whom they are placed in competition.

Foundations

Marx adapted the concept from Hegel, for whom alienation was a stage in the self-development of Spirit, and from Feuerbach, who applied it to religion. For Marx it was a material condition rooted in private property and the division of labour, to be overcome not by thought but by transforming the relations of production.

Influence

The theory has shaped sociology, critical theory, and existential Marxism, and the term "alienation" passed into wider social-scientific and popular usage.
