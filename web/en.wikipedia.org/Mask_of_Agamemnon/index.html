Mask of Agamemnon

The Mask of Agamemnon is a gold funeral mask discovered at the Bronze Age site of Mycenae in southern Greece. The discoverer, the archaeologist Heinrich Schliemann, believed it to be the burial mask of the legendary Greek king Agamemnon, leader of the Greeks in the Trojan War.

Discovery

Schliemann excavated Grave Circle A at Mycenae in 1876, uncovering a series of shaft graves rich in gold. The mask was found in Grave V, placed over the face of a male burial. Schliemann famously telegraphed a Greek newspaper that he had "gazed upon the face of Agamemnon". It is now displayed in the National Archaeological Museum of Athens, which calls it the "Mona Lisa of prehistory".

Description and dating

The mask is made of a thick sheet of gold, hammered against a wooden background with details chased in with a sharp tool. It depicts a bearded, mature man with closed eyes, prominent ears and distinctive moustache. The mask is dated to about 1550–1500 BC — some three centuries before the traditional date of the Trojan War — so it cannot in fact depict Agamemnon, though it remains the most famous of the five gold masks found at Mycenae and the name has stuck.

Authenticity debate

Because Schliemann had a reputation for embellishment, the mask's authenticity was questioned in the 20th century, with some scholars suggesting it was fabricated or altered. The mainstream view, however, accepts it as a genuine Mycenaean artefact, while acknowledging that Schliemann may have embellished details such as the moustache.
