Feliformia is a suborder within the order Carnivora consisting of "cat-like" carnivorans, including cats (large and small), hyenas, mongooses, viverrids (civets and genets), and related taxa. In contrast to the other suborder of Carnivora, Caniformia ("dog-like" carnivorans), feliforms generally have shorter snouts and fewer teeth, reflecting a more strictly carnivorous diet.

Characteristics

Feliforms share a distinctive structure of the auditory bulla, the bony chamber enclosing the middle ear, which is double-chambered and formed from two joined bones — a feature used to diagnose the group in the fossil record. Most living feliforms have retractile or semi-retractile claws and tend toward digitigrade stance.

Classification

Living families include the Felidae (cats, about 40 species), Hyaenidae (hyenas and the aardwolf), Herpestidae (mongooses), Viverridae (civets and genets), Eupleridae (Malagasy carnivorans such as the fossa), Prionodontidae (Asiatic linsangs) and Nandiniidae (the African palm civet). Extinct groups include the nimravids, sometimes called "false sabre-tooth cats", and the percrocutids.

Evolution

The carnivoran suborders diverged in the Eocene. Feliforms radiated across Africa, Eurasia and eventually the Americas; Madagascar's euplerids descend from a single colonisation event by a mongoose-like ancestor roughly 20 million years ago.
