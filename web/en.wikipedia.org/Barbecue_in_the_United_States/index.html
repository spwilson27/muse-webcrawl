Barbecue in the United States

Barbecue in the United States is a distinct culinary tradition of cooking meat slowly over indirect heat and wood smoke — "low and slow" — as distinct from grilling over direct high heat, though in everyday American usage "barbecue" may also refer to a casual outdoor cookout of grilled food. American barbecue developed in the South from Indigenous, European, and African techniques and ingredients, and is organised into fiercely defended regional styles differing in preferred meats, woods, rubs, and sauces.

Regional styles

The Carolinas are known for pork — whole hog in eastern North Carolina, dressed with a thin vinegar-and-pepper sauce, and pork shoulder in the west with a tomato-tinged sauce; South Carolina adds a distinctive mustard-based "gold" sauce. Memphis specialises in pork ribs (served "dry" with a spice rub or "wet" with sauce) and pulled pork shoulder. Kansas City barbecue uses many meats, cooked with a rub and finished with a thick, sweet tomato-and-molasses sauce. Central Texas style centres on beef brisket and sausage, seasoned simply with salt and black pepper and smoked over post oak, reflecting German and Czech butcher-shop traditions. Other notable traditions include Alabama white sauce on chicken, Kentucky mutton, and Santa Maria–style tri-tip in California.

Techniques and culture

Classic barbecue cooks tough cuts — brisket, pork shoulder, ribs — at temperatures around 225–275 °F (107–135 °C) for many hours, rendering fat and converting connective tissue. Fuels and smoke woods vary regionally: hickory and oak in the Southeast and Midwest, mesquite in parts of Texas. Barbecue restaurants ('cue joints), competitive cook-off circuits sanctioned by bodies such as the Kansas City Barbeque Society, and festivals anchor a rich folk culture, and the tradition has spread nationally and internationally since the late twentieth century.

Origins

The word derives from barbacoa, the Spanish rendering of a Taíno term for a wooden frame used to cook over fire in the Caribbean. Enslaved Africans and their descendants were central to developing the pit-cooking techniques, seasonings, and sauces of Southern barbecue, a contribution increasingly recognised by food historians.
