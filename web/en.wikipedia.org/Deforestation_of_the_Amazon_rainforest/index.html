Deforestation of the Amazon rainforest

Deforestation of the Amazon rainforest refers to the clearing and conversion of the Amazon biome, the world's largest tropical rainforest, most of which lies within Brazil. Since the 1970s large-scale clearing for agriculture, cattle ranching, logging and infrastructure has removed a substantial fraction of the forest.

Causes

Cattle ranching accounts for the largest share of cleared land, followed by soybean cultivation, much of it grown for animal feed. Logging, mining, hydroelectric dams and road building open remote areas to further settlement. Land speculation and insecure tenure encourage clearing as a way of claiming land.

Environmental impact

The Amazon stores vast quantities of carbon; deforestation releases it as carbon dioxide and reduces the forest's capacity to act as a carbon sink. Scientists warn of a "tipping point" beyond which reduced rainfall recycling could convert parts of the forest to savanna. Deforestation also threatens the region's exceptional biodiversity and the livelihoods of Indigenous peoples.

Policy and trends

Brazilian deforestation rates fell sharply between 2004 and 2012 under stronger enforcement and protected-area expansion, rose again in the late 2010s and early 2020s, and have fluctuated with changes in government policy. Satellite monitoring by Brazil's National Institute for Space Research (INPE) provides near-real-time alerts through the DETER system and annual estimates through PRODES.
