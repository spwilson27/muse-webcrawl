Computus

Computus (Latin for "computation") is the calculation used to determine the calendar date of Easter. At the First Council of Nicaea in AD 325 it was agreed that Easter would be observed on the same day throughout the Christian world, independent of the Jewish calendar. The canonical rule eventually adopted was that Easter falls on the first Sunday after the first full moon occurring on or after the spring equinox (fixed as 21 March).

History

Different regions long used different methods, giving rise to the Easter controversy. In the early Middle Ages, the tables of Dionysius Exiguus (525) and the writings of the Venerable Bede helped standardise computus in the Latin West. Computus was a central subject of early medieval science and education, and many surviving manuscripts are Easter tables.

Algorithms

Because the ecclesiastical full moon is a tabulated approximation rather than the astronomical event, Easter computation is a purely arithmetical calendar problem. Numerous algorithms exist, including the "Anonymous Gregorian algorithm" published in Nature in 1876 and Gauss's Easter algorithm. Carl Friedrich Gauss devised a compact method based on modular arithmetic, later corrected by others. Easter can fall between 22 March and 25 April in the Gregorian calendar.
