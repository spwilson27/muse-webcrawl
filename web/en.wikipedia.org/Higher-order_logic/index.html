Higher-order logic

In mathematics and logic, a higher-order logic (HOL) is a form of logic in which, in addition to quantification over individual variables (as in first-order logic), one may also quantify over predicates, relations, and functions. Higher-order logics are distinguished by the depth of such quantification: second-order logic allows quantification over sets and unary predicates, while third- and higher-order logics extend this to sets of sets, and so on.

Semantics

Under standard (full) semantics, second-order quantifiers range over the entire power set of the domain, making the logic very expressive: second-order arithmetic, for example, is categorical, uniquely characterizing the natural numbers. Leon Henkin's general semantics restricts the range of higher-order quantifiers to a designated collection of sets, under which higher-order logic behaves much like many-sorted first-order logic and regains completeness.

Properties

With standard semantics, higher-order logics fail the completeness and compactness properties of first-order logic: by Gödel's incompleteness theorems, no effective proof system can capture all validities of full second-order logic. Consequently, set theory — often viewed as higher-order logic in disguise — is used as the usual foundation in which higher-order reasoning is carried out.

Applications

Higher-order logic underlies interactive theorem provers such as HOL Light, Isabelle/HOL, and Coq's calculus of constructions, which are used in formal verification of hardware, software, and mathematics. Its type-theoretic formulation, descending from the simple theory of types of Church (1940) and ultimately from the ramified type theory of Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica, is also influential in linguistics and the philosophy of language through Montague grammar.
