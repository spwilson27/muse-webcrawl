Ravelin

A ravelin is a detached outwork in a fortification, consisting of a triangular or V-shaped work placed in the ditch in front of the curtain wall, between two bastions, to protect a gate or curtain from direct fire and assault. Ravelins are a characteristic element of the bastion fort (trace italienne) system developed in Italy in the sixteenth century and refined by engineers such as Vauban in the seventeenth century. A larger ravelin with flanks is sometimes called a demi-lune (half-moon).

Design

The ravelin has two faces meeting at a salient angle pointing toward the attacker, with an open gorge at the rear facing the main wall so that, if captured, it cannot easily be held against fire from the ramparts. It divides the ditch, forcing attackers to take it before reaching the curtain, and its guns can flank the faces of the adjacent bastions. Access from the fortress was by a small bridge, postern or boat across the ditch, which could be removed in a siege. Ravelins were typically lower than the main rampart so the defenders' fire could sweep over them.

Use

Ravelins appear in star forts across Europe and in colonial fortifications worldwide. As siegecraft developed, additional outworks — counterguards, tenailles and crownworks — were placed in front of ravelins, deepening the defensive zone. Well-preserved examples survive at fortresses such as Naarden, Bourtange, Palmanova and the citadels of Lille and Besançon.
