Strange quark

The strange quark or s quark is the third lightest of all quarks, a type of elementary particle. Strange quarks are found in subatomic particles called hadrons. Examples of hadrons containing strange quarks include kaons (K), strange D mesons (Ds), Sigma baryons (Σ), and other strange particles.

The strange quark has an electric charge of −1/3 e and a bare mass on the order of 95 MeV/c². Like all quarks, it is an elementary fermion with spin 1/2, and experiences all four fundamental interactions: gravitation, electromagnetism, weak interactions, and strong interactions.

History

The strangeness quantum number was introduced in the early 1950s by Murray Gell-Mann and others to explain the unexpectedly long lifetimes of certain newly discovered particles such as kaons and hyperons, which were produced strongly in pairs but decayed slowly via the weak interaction. In 1964 Gell-Mann and George Zweig independently proposed the quark model, in which these particles contained a new kind of quark — the strange quark. Strange quarks were indirectly confirmed in deep inelastic scattering experiments at SLAC in the late 1960s.

Strangeness

Particles containing strange quarks were assigned a quantum number called strangeness, defined as S = −(ns − ns̄), so that each strange quark contributes −1. Strangeness is conserved in strong and electromagnetic interactions but can change by one unit in weak decays, which explains why strange particles are always produced in pairs yet decay slowly.
