Coal gas

Coal gas, also known as town gas or manufactured gas, is a flammable gaseous fuel produced by heating coal in the absence of air (destructive distillation) in retorts at a gasworks. A typical coal gas contained roughly 50% hydrogen, 25–35% methane, 5–12% carbon monoxide, and smaller quantities of other hydrocarbons, nitrogen, and impurities. From the early 19th century until the mid-20th century it was the standard piped fuel for street lighting, domestic illumination, cooking, and heating in towns across Europe, North America, and Australia.

History

William Murdoch demonstrated gas lighting at the Boulton & Watt works in Birmingham around 1802, and in 1812 the Gas Light and Coke Company was chartered to light London. Gasworks spread rapidly: Baltimore became the first American city with gas lighting in 1816. The gas industry drove innovations including the gasholder (gasometer), the gas meter, and incandescent mantles (the Welsbach mantle) that greatly improved light output late in the century. By-products — coke, coal tar, and ammonia — became feedstocks for the emerging chemical industry, yielding dyes, pharmaceuticals, and fertilisers.

Production

Coal was carbonised in firebrick retorts at temperatures around 1,000–1,300 °C. The crude gas was cooled, washed to remove tar and ammonia, and purified — traditionally through beds of iron oxide ("purifier boxes") — to remove hydrogen sulfide before storage in telescoping gasholders. Variants included carburetted water gas, made by passing steam over incandescent coke and enriching the gas with oil vapour, which was widely used in the United States.

Decline

Coal gas was displaced after the Second World War by natural gas, which has roughly twice the calorific value and requires no local manufacture. Britain converted its entire network from town gas to natural gas between 1967 and 1977, a programme involving the conversion or replacement of some 40 million appliances. The carbon monoxide content of coal gas had made it highly toxic — a notorious agent in accidental poisoning and suicide — and its disappearance from domestic supply was followed by a marked fall in such deaths, a case often cited in suicide-prevention research.

Legacy

Former gasworks sites are frequently contaminated with tar, phenols, and heavy metals, requiring remediation before redevelopment; surviving gasholders, such as those at Bromley-by-Bow in London and the Gasometer in Vienna, have been preserved as industrial landmarks. Manufactured gas processes remain historically important as precursors of modern coal gasification technology.
