Cub Scouts, originally Wolf Cubs, are the junior section of the Scout Movement for children roughly between seven and eleven years old. Robert Baden-Powell founded the section in 1916 in response to demand from younger boys who were too young for Scouts, drawing the program's theme from Rudyard Kipling's The Jungle Book: the pack leader is called Akela after the wolf pack's leader, and ceremonies echo Mowgli's story.

Program

Cub Scouts meet in packs, usually weekly, and work through a progressive award scheme of badges earned for outdoor skills, creativity, community service and personal challenges. Activities include camping, hiking, games and the Grand Howl opening ceremony. In many countries the section is co-educational; in the United Kingdom girls have been admitted since the early 1990s, and in the United States the Cub Scout program opened fully to girls in 2018.

Around the world

Cub sections exist in most national Scout organisations, with names, uniforms and age ranges varying by country. Cubs who reach the top of the section move on to the Scout section, often in a formal "moving-on" ceremony.
