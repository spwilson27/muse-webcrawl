Rod cells are photoreceptor cells in the retina of the eye that function in low-light conditions and are responsible for scotopic (night) vision and peripheral vision. Named for their cylindrical, rod-like shape, they are far more sensitive to light than the other class of photoreceptors, the cone cells, but they do not distinguish colour and provide relatively poor visual acuity. The average human retina contains roughly 120 million rod cells.

Structure

A rod cell consists of a synaptic terminal, an inner segment containing the nucleus and metabolic machinery, and an outer segment joined by a connecting cilium. The outer segment is a stack of membranous discs — up to a thousand in some species — densely packed with the visual pigment rhodopsin. Rods continually shed the tips of their outer segments, which are phagocytosed by the retinal pigment epithelium, while new discs form at the base.

Rods are concentrated at the outer edges of the retina and are absent from the centre of the fovea, which is populated entirely by cones; this distribution is why faint objects at night are best seen slightly to one side of the centre of gaze.

Phototransduction

When a photon is absorbed by rhodopsin, its retinal chromophore isomerises from 11-cis to all-trans, triggering a G-protein (transducin) cascade that activates phosphodiesterase, lowers cyclic GMP levels, and closes cGMP-gated sodium channels. Unusually, this hyperpolarises the cell and reduces its release of glutamate onto bipolar cells; darkness is therefore the stimulus that keeps rods depolarised and signalling.

A single photon can produce a measurable response in a rod, making rods capable of detecting light at the physical limit, though the brain's circuitry pools signals from many rods to distinguish genuine light from thermal noise.

Sensitivity and adaptation

Rods saturate in bright daylight and take many minutes to recover full sensitivity in darkness — the phenomenon of dark adaptation, reflected in the rod branch of the adaptation curve after about 20–30 minutes. Their spectral sensitivity peaks near 498 nm, producing the Purkinje shift: at dusk, blues and greens appear relatively brighter than reds because rod vision replaces cone vision.

Clinical significance

Diseases affecting rods include retinitis pigmentosa, a group of inherited disorders in which rod degeneration precedes cone loss, causing night blindness and tunnel vision; congenital stationary night blindness; and vitamin A deficiency, which impairs rhodopsin regeneration and causes night blindness. Because rods dominate the peripheral retina, their pathology is often first noticed as loss of side vision.
