A terrier is a type of dog originally bred to hunt vermin and to pursue prey animals into their burrows. Terriers range in size from very small, such as the Russian Toy, to large, such as the Airedale, generally weigh between 5 and 32 kilograms, and are known for their feisty, energetic and tenacious temperaments.

Etymology and history

The word derives through Old French from Latin terra, "earth", reflecting the terrier's role of "going to ground" after foxes, badgers, rats and other burrowing animals. Most terrier breeds were developed in Britain and Ireland during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, when they were used to control vermin around farms and to bolt foxes from their earths during hunts.

Types and breeds

Kennel clubs group terriers variously. The FCI places most in its Group 3, subdivided into large and medium terriers, small terriers, bull-type terriers and toy terriers. Breeds include the Border Terrier, Cairn Terrier, Fox Terrier, Manchester Terrier, Norfolk and Norwich Terriers, Staffordshire Bull Terrier and Yorkshire Terrier. Bull-type terriers descend from crosses between bulldogs and terriers made for blood sports in the early nineteenth century.

Temperament and health

Terriers are characteristically bold, active and strong-willed, with a high prey drive; they can be challenging to train off-lead around small animals. Many wire-coated breeds are relatively low-shedding. Genetic studies group most terriers as a distinct cluster of breeds developed largely in the nineteenth century.
