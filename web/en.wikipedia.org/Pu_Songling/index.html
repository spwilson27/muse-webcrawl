Pu Songling (Chinese: 蒲松齡; pinyin: Pú Sōnglíng; 5 June 1640 – 25 February 1715) was a Chinese writer of the Qing dynasty, best known as the author of Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio (Liaozhai zhiyi), a celebrated collection of supernatural and fantastic short stories written in classical Chinese. Though he failed repeatedly in the imperial civil-service examinations, his tales — of fox spirits, ghosts, scholars, and corrupt officials — made him one of the masters of the Chinese short story, and his collection has been adapted countless times for film, television, and theatre.

Life

Pu was born in Zichuan District, in present-day Zibo, Shandong, into a family of modest means of partly Mongol descent. He distinguished himself early, passing the county-level examinations at the top of his cohort, but despite decades of attempts he never advanced beyond the lowest degree, earning only a senior licentiate's status at the age of 71. For most of his life he earned his living as a private tutor in the household of a wealthy local family, the Bi family, where he worked for some thirty years. Tradition holds that he gathered story material by offering tea to travellers at a roadside stall near his home, inviting them to exchange tales.

Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio

The Liaozhai zhiyi, largely complete by about 1680 but published only posthumously (first printed in 1766), contains nearly five hundred tales. Writing in an elegant classical Chinese, Pu transformed the older zhiguai tradition of anomaly accounts into sophisticated fiction: fox-spirit romances, ghost stories, satires of examination corruption and venal judges, and stories of friendship and love across the boundary between the human and spirit worlds. Many tales carry a closing comment "The Historian of the Strange says…", a device modelled on the historian's judgments in classical historiography. Famous stories include "The Cricket", "Painted Skin", and "Nie Xiaoqian", the last adapted into the film A Chinese Ghost Story (1987).

Other works and legacy

Pu also wrote poetry, songs, popular dramas (liqu), essays on agriculture and medicine, and vernacular tales intended for a wider audience. His former residence in Zichuan is preserved as a museum, and the Lu Xun Literary Prize's short-story award in China is named for him (the Pu Songling Short Story Award being among the honours associated with his name in Shandong). Translations of the Liaozhai tales, including those by Herbert Giles (1880) and John Minford, have made him the most widely read pre-modern Chinese fiction writer in English after the great novelists.
