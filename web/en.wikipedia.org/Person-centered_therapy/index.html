Person-centered therapy

Person-centered therapy, also known as person-centered psychotherapy, client-centered therapy and Rogerian psychotherapy, is a form of psychotherapy developed by psychologist Carl Rogers beginning in the 1940s. It is a major school of humanistic psychology in which the therapist avoids directing the client, instead providing an accepting relationship in which the client can discover their own solutions.

Core conditions

Rogers proposed that three therapist attitudes are necessary and sufficient for therapeutic change: congruence (genuineness), unconditional positive regard (acceptance and care), and empathic understanding of the client's internal frame of reference. When these conditions are present, Rogers held, the client's innate tendency toward growth — the "actualising tendency" — is released and incongruence between self-concept and experience diminishes.

History and influence

Rogers set out his approach in Counseling and Psychotherapy (1942) and Client-Centered Therapy (1951), and renamed it "person-centered" as he applied it beyond therapy to education, groups and conflict resolution. He was a pioneer of psychotherapy research, recording and analysing sessions empirically. The approach influenced motivational interviewing, emotion-focused therapy, and the general emphasis on the therapeutic relationship in modern practice, though critics question whether the core conditions are truly sufficient for all disorders.
