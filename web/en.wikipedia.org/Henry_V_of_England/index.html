Henry V of England

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Henry V (c. 16 September 1386 – 31 August 1422) was King of England from 1413 until his death in 1422. Despite his relatively short reign, his military successes in the Hundred Years' War — above all the astonishing victory at Agincourt in 1415 — made England one of the strongest kingdoms in Europe and culminated in the Treaty of Troyes (1420), under which he was recognised as heir and regent of France and married the French princess Catherine of Valois. He died suddenly two years later, leaving an infant son and an unstable dual monarchy.

Early life

Henry was born at Monmouth Castle, the eldest son of Henry Bolingbroke (later Henry IV) and Mary de Bohun. He was knighted as a boy, fought at the Battle of Shrewsbury in 1403 — where he was struck in the face by an arrow and famously survived its surgical extraction — and campaigned extensively against the Welsh revolt of Owain Glyndŵr. As Prince of Wales he also took a leading role in government during his father's illness, clashing with him over policy and courting a reputation (amplified by Shakespeare) for a wild youth, for which the historical evidence is thin.

Reign and the French war

On succeeding in 1413, Henry suppressed the Lollard rising of Sir John Oldcastle and a plot by the Earl of Cambridge, then revived the English claim to the French crown. In 1415 he invaded Normandy, captured Harfleur, and on the march to Calais, with an army sick and outnumbered perhaps three-to-one or worse, destroyed the flower of the French nobility at Agincourt on 25 October 1415 (St Crispin's Day), where longbowmen behind stakes annihilated repeated French attacks.

From 1417 he conducted a methodical conquest of Normandy — Caen, Falaise, and Rouen all fell — and allied with Burgundy after the murder of Duke John the Fearless. The Treaty of Troyes (1420) disinherited the Dauphin Charles; Henry married Catherine of Valois and entered Paris. Campaigning continued against the Dauphin's forces south of the Loire.

Death and legacy

Henry died at the Château de Vincennes on 31 August 1422, probably of dysentery contracted during the siege of Meaux. He was 35. His son Henry VI, nine months old, succeeded to both crowns. Henry's reputation rested on his piety, discipline, administrative energy — he promoted the use of English in government documents — and generalship. Shakespeare's Henry V (c. 1599), with the St Crispin's Day speech ("We few, we happy few, we band of brothers"), fixed his image as the archetypal warrior-king.
