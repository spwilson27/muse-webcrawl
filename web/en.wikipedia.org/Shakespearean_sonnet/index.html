Shakespearean sonnet

The 1609 quarto of Shakespeare's Sonnets, containing 154 sonnets.

The Shakespearean sonnet, also called the English sonnet, is a sonnet form used by William Shakespeare. It consists of fourteen lines written in iambic pentameter, structured as three quatrains followed by a final rhyming couplet. The rhyme scheme is ABAB CDCD EFEF GG. Shakespeare wrote 154 sonnets, published together in a quarto in 1609.

The typical structure presents a problem, situation, or question in the three quatrains and resolves or comments on it—often with a striking "turn" or volta—in the concluding couplet. Famous examples include Sonnet 18 ("Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?"), Sonnet 29 ("When, in disgrace with fortune and men's eyes"), Sonnet 116 ("Let me not to the marriage of true minds"), and Sonnet 130 ("My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun").

The sequences within the collection

Sonnets 1–126 are conventionally understood as addressed to a young man, the "Fair Youth", urging him to marry and procreate in the opening "procreation sonnets" (1–17) and later exploring the poet's intense devotion and rivalries. Sonnets 127–152 concern the "Dark Lady", a woman with whom the poet has a troubled relationship; sonnets 153 and 154 are mythological treatments of Cupid. The dedication of the 1609 quarto to "Mr. W. H." as the "only begetter" of the sonnets has generated centuries of speculation about the identity of the addressees.

Form and predecessors

The English sonnet form was developed earlier by Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey, and used by Sir Philip Sidney and Edmund Spenser, but Shakespeare's handling of it—particularly his use of the couplet as a summary or ironic reversal—made it the dominant English sonnet form. Unlike the Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet with its octave and sestet, the Shakespearean sonnet allows three parallel developments before the resolution.
