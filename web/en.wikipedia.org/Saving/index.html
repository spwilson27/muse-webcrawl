Saving

Saving is income not spent — the portion of disposable income that is not used for consumption. Saving may involve reducing expenditures, such as recurring costs, or setting resources aside in deposits, pension accounts, investment funds, or cash. In economics, personal saving is defined, variously, as any income that is not used for immediate consumption, and it is a flow concept, whereas savings (with an "s") refers to the accumulated stock of assets.

Methods

Saving can take the form of holding cash, deposit accounts at banks, contributions to retirement plans such as pensions or individual retirement accounts, or investment in securities. Each method differs in liquidity, risk, and return. Interest paid on deposits partially offsets the erosion of purchasing power caused by inflation.

Economic views

Classical economics treated saving as a source of funds for investment, supporting capital accumulation and growth. Keynesian economics emphasised that, in the short run, an increase in the desire to save can reduce aggregate demand — the "paradox of thrift" — and may deepen a recession if investment does not rise to match. Life-cycle and permanent-income theories explain saving as smoothing consumption over a lifetime and over fluctuations in income.

Saving rate

The personal saving rate is saving as a percentage of disposable personal income. It varies widely across countries and over time, influenced by demographics, social insurance, credit availability, and cultural factors.
