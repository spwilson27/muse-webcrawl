Parliament of Great Britain

Parliament of Great Britain Existed: 1707–1800
Seat: Palace of Westminster, London
Chambers: House of Commons; House of Lords
Created by: Acts of Union 1707
Succeeded by: Parliament of the United Kingdom

The Parliament of Great Britain was the legislature of the Kingdom of Great Britain, formed in May 1707 following the ratification of the Acts of Union by both the Parliament of England and the Parliament of Scotland. The two parliaments were replaced by a single body, which met in the former home of the English parliament, the Palace of Westminster in London. It existed until 1 January 1801, when the Acts of Union 1800 merged Great Britain and Ireland into the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland and the Parliament of Great Britain became the Parliament of the United Kingdom.

Composition

The new parliament preserved the institutional forms of the English parliament: a sovereign, a House of Lords composed of Lords Spiritual and Temporal, and an elected House of Commons. Scotland sent 45 members to the Commons — a number far below proportion of population but generous relative to its taxable wealth — and 16 representative peers elected by the Scottish peerage to sit in the Lords for each parliament. The first Parliament of Great Britain was essentially a continuation of the last English parliament, with Scottish representatives added.

The 18th-century parliament

Over the course of the 18th century, this parliament oversaw the emergence of the office of prime minister — first under Robert Walpole, generally regarded as the first holder of the office (1721–1742) — and of organised party conflict between Whigs and Tories. Parliament's authority expanded with the growth of the fiscal-military state, the national debt, and the British Empire; its claim to tax the American colonies without their representation provoked the American Revolution. Although the franchise remained narrow and many seats were controlled by aristocratic patrons — the "unreformed" system — parliament was nonetheless the arena for major reform debates, the abolition of the slave trade in 1807 lying just beyond the body's lifetime under its successor.

Union with Ireland

In the aftermath of the Irish Rebellion of 1798, the government of William Pitt the Younger carried the Acts of Union 1800 through both the British and Irish parliaments. On 1 January 1801 the Parliament of Great Britain ceased to exist, replaced by the Parliament of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland, with 100 Irish MPs and 32 Irish peers joining at Westminster.
