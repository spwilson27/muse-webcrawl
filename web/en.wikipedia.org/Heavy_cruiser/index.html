Heavy cruiser

The heavy cruiser was a type of cruiser developed in the early twentieth century and defined, after the London Naval Treaty of 1930, as a cruiser with guns larger than 6.1 inches (155 mm) in calibre but no greater than 8 inches (203 mm), on a displacement of up to 10,000 tons standard. Cruisers with guns of 6.1 inches or less were classified as light cruisers. The type evolved from the British Hawkins-class cruisers built during the First World War.

Development

The Washington Naval Treaty of 1922 capped cruiser displacement at 10,000 tons and gun calibre at 8 inches, prompting a generation of "treaty cruisers" designed right up to those limits. The London Treaty then split the category into heavy (Type A) and light (Type B) cruisers and limited their numbers. Navies of the United States, Britain, Japan, France, Italy, and Germany built heavy cruisers as scouts for the battle fleet, commerce raiders and protectors, and powerful independent surface combatants.

Second World War service

Heavy cruisers fought extensively in the Second World War: in the Atlantic, the Mediterranean, and above all the Pacific, where ships such as the Japanese Takao class, the American New Orleans and Baltimore classes, and British County-class ships fought in surface actions around Guadalcanal and elsewhere, and provided anti-aircraft and shore-bombardment support. Many were lost, notably at the Battle of Savo Island in 1942.

Decline

After 1945, aircraft and missiles displaced gun-armed cruisers. The U.S. Navy completed the all-gun Des Moines class just after the war; most heavy cruisers were retired by the 1970s, the last, USS Newport News, being decommissioned in 1975. The term survives informally for large surface combatants, though modern "cruisers" are missile-armed.
