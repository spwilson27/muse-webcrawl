Vascular dementia

Vascular dementia
SpecialtyNeurology, psychiatry
CausesStroke, small-vessel disease, reduced cerebral blood flow
Risk factorsHypertension, diabetes, smoking, atrial fibrillation

Vascular dementia is dementia caused by problems in the blood vessels that supply the brain, typically from a series of minor strokes or a single major stroke leading to cognitive decline. It is generally considered the second most common cause of dementia after Alzheimer's disease, and the two often occur together ("mixed dementia").

Signs and subtypes

Symptoms depend on which brain regions are affected and often progress in a stepwise fashion after each vascular event. Subtypes include multi-infarct dementia, strategic infarct dementia (a single stroke in a critical area such as the thalamus), and subcortical vascular dementia from small-vessel disease (Binswanger disease). Early features often include slowed thinking, executive dysfunction, gait disturbance, and mood changes, with memory relatively preserved at first compared with Alzheimer's disease.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis combines cognitive testing, neurological examination, and brain imaging (MRI showing infarcts or extensive white-matter hyperintensities). There is no cure; management focuses on preventing further strokes by controlling blood pressure, diabetes, and cholesterol, antiplatelet therapy where appropriate, and not smoking. Cholinesterase inhibitors may offer modest benefit in some patients.
