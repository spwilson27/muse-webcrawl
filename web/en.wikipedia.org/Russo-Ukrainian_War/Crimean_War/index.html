The Crimean War (October 1853 – February/March 1856) was fought between the Russian Empire and an alliance of the Ottoman Empire, France, the United Kingdom and the Kingdom of Sardinia. The conflict grew out of the "Eastern Question" — the decline of the Ottoman Empire — and a dispute over the rights of Christian minorities and the custody of holy places in Ottoman Palestine, over which Russia claimed a protectorate.

Course of the war

After Russia occupied the Danubian principalities and destroyed an Ottoman squadron at Sinop, Britain and France declared war in March 1854. The allies landed in Crimea in September 1854, won the battles of the Alma, Balaclava — infamous for the Charge of the Light Brigade — and Inkerman, and settled into the year-long Siege of Sevastopol, which fell in September 1855. Secondary operations ranged from the Caucasus, where Kars changed hands, to allied naval expeditions in the Baltic and even the Pacific coast of Siberia.

Modern warfare and medicine

The war is remembered as one of the first "modern" conflicts: rifled muskets, explosive shells, railways and the telegraph were used at scale, and war correspondents and photographers reported it to home audiences in near real time. The appalling death toll from disease — far exceeding battle deaths — prompted Florence Nightingale's reforms of military nursing at Scutari and Mary Seacole's work near the front, and contributed to the birth of professional nursing and modern military medicine.

Outcome

The Treaty of Paris (1856) neutralised the Black Sea, demilitarised its coasts, guaranteed Ottoman territorial integrity and trimmed Russian territory in Bessarabia. Russia's defeat exposed the empire's backwardness and spurred the Great Reforms of Alexander II, including the emancipation of the serfs in 1861.
