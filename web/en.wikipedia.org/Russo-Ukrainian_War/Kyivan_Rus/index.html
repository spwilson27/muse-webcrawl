Kyivan Rus (also spelled Kievan Rus') was a medieval state in Eastern Europe, a federation of East Slavic and Finnic peoples under the Rurik dynasty, which at its height stretched from the Baltic Sea to the Black Sea steppe. Its legacy is claimed by the modern nations of Ukraine, Russia and Belarus, and its history has become politically contested in the context of the present Russo-Ukrainian War.

Origins

According to the Primary Chronicle, the Varangian (Scandinavian) prince Rurik established himself at Novgorod in 862; his successor Oleg seized Kyiv in 882 and made it his capital, uniting the northern and southern centres of the trade route "from the Varangians to the Greeks" along the Dnieper. The Rus signed trade treaties with Constantinople and alternately raided and traded with the Byzantine Empire, the Khazars and the steppe Pechenegs.

Christianisation and golden age

In 988 Volodymyr the Great adopted Orthodox Christianity from Byzantium, an event commemorated as the Baptism of Rus, which tied the land to Byzantine culture and literacy. Under Yaroslav the Wise (1019–1054) Kyiv flourished: the Russkaya Pravda law code was compiled, Saint Sophia Cathedral was built, and dynastic marriages linked the Rurikids to the royal houses of Europe.

Fragmentation and fall

From the mid-eleventh century the succession system of lateral inheritance fragmented Rus into rival principalities — Kyiv, Novgorod, Vladimir-Suzdal, Galicia-Volhynia and others. The sack of Kyiv by Andrei Bogolyubsky (1169) symbolised the shift of power northeastwards. The Mongol invasion of 1237–1240 destroyed Kyiv and most Rus principalities, ending the Kyivan state; its southwestern lands later passed to Lithuania and Poland, while the northeast developed into Muscovy.
