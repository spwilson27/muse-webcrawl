The Holodomor (Ukrainian: Голодомор, "to kill by starvation") was a man-made famine in Soviet Ukraine in 1932–1933 that killed millions of Ukrainians. It occurred during the USSR-wide Soviet famine of 1930–1933, but in Ukraine it was intensified by policies specific to the republic, and it is recognised by Ukraine and more than thirty other countries, as well as the European Parliament, as a genocide committed by Joseph Stalin's regime.

Background and causes

Stalin's forced collectivisation of agriculture from 1929 destroyed peasant farming, while Ukraine — the Soviet Union's breadbasket — was set grain-procurement quotas that officials enforced through confiscation of harvests, seed grain and even household food under the "Law of Five Ears of Grain" (August 1932). Villages that failed to meet quotas were "blacklisted," blockaded and stripped of all foodstuffs. In late 1932 and early 1933 the regime sealed Ukraine's internal borders, and the new passport system prevented starving peasants from leaving to seek food, while the Soviet government concealed the catastrophe and refused foreign aid offers.

Scale and recognition

Demographers estimate excess deaths in Ukraine at roughly 3.9 million, within a broader range of scholarly estimates. Journalists such as Malcolm Muggeridge and Gareth Jones reported the famine abroad, while Walter Duranty of The New York Times publicly downplayed it. The historian Raphael Lemkin, who coined the word "genocide," described the Ukrainian famine as a classic example of Soviet genocide. Debate continues over legal characterisation and exact numbers, but the centrality of state policy to the famine is broadly accepted.

Memory

Holodomor Memorial Day is observed in Ukraine on the fourth Saturday of November, and the National Museum of the Holodomor-Genocide stands in Kyiv. Remembrance of the famine has been a defining element of Ukrainian national identity in the current Russo-Ukrainian conflict.
