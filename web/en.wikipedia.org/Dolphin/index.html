A dolphin is an aquatic mammal in the clade Odontoceti (toothed whales). Dolphins belong to the families Delphinidae (the oceanic dolphins), Platanistidae (the Indian river dolphins), Iniidae (the New World river dolphins), Pontoporiidae (the brackish dolphins), and the possibly extinct Lipotidae (baiji). There are about 40 extant species in 19 genera. Closely related to whales and porpoises, dolphins evolved about 50 million years ago from land-dwelling ancestors.

Biology

Dolphins have streamlined bodies, a horizontal tail fluke, flippers, and a blowhole. Most species have a pronounced beak and conical teeth suited to catching fish and squid. They navigate and hunt by echolocation, generating clicks in nasal passages and listening for echoes with the fat-filled melon. Dolphins are fast swimmers — common dolphins can exceed 55 km/h in bursts — and frequently ride bow waves and surf.

Intelligence and behaviour

Dolphins are among the most intelligent animals: they have large brains relative to body size, complex social structures (pods and fission–fusion societies), individual "signature whistles", and documented behaviours including tool use (sponging in Shark Bay, Australia), cooperative hunting, play, and apparent self-recognition in mirrors. The common bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops truncatus) is the species most often kept in dolphinariums and featured in research.

Threats

Major threats include bycatch in fishing nets, drive hunts, pollution, noise, and habitat loss. Several river dolphins are critically endangered; the baiji of the Yangtze was declared functionally extinct in 2006, the first cetacean extinction attributable to human activity.
