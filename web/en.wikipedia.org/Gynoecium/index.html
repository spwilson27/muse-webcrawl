Gynoecium

The gynoecium: the female reproductive whorl of a flower.

The gynoecium is the innermost whorl of a flower, consisting of one or more pistils and comprising the female reproductive organs of flowering plants. Each pistil is made up of one or more fused or separate carpels; a carpel consists of a stigma (which receives pollen), a style, and an ovary containing ovules.

Structure

The stigma is the receptive tip, often sticky or feathery, where pollen lands and germinates. The style is the stalk through which pollen tubes grow. The ovary at the base encloses one or more chambers (locules) bearing ovules attached to a placenta; after fertilisation the ovules become seeds and the ovary wall (pericarp) typically develops into the fruit.

Types

A gynoecium with a single carpel is monocarpous; with several separate carpels, apocarpous (as in buttercups and strawberries); and with fused carpels, syncarpous (as in lilies and tomatoes). The position of the ovary relative to the other floral parts is a key taxonomic character: ovaries may be superior (hypogynous flowers), inferior (epigynous flowers), or half-inferior (perigynous flowers).

Evolution

Carpels are interpreted as modified leaves (megasporophylls) that folded and fused around the ovules, an innovation that defines the angiosperms ("enclosed seeds") and contrasts with the exposed ovules of gymnosperms. Fossil flowers such as Archaefructus show early forms of carpel closure.
