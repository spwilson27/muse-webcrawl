Traction engine

A preserved steam traction engine

A traction engine is a steam-powered tractor used to move heavy loads on roads, plough ground, or provide power at a chosen location via a belt from its flywheel. The name derives from the Latin tractus, meaning "drawn", since the prime function of early examples was to haul loads. Traction engines were built mainly in the United Kingdom from the 1850s, peaking in the early 20th century before being displaced by internal-combustion tractors in the 1920s–1940s.

Design

A typical agricultural engine carries a horizontal locomotive-type boiler with a single or compound cylinder mounted above it, driving a crankshaft and large flywheel. The rear wheels — large, spoked, and fitted with strakes for grip — are driven through gearing, and steering is by chains winding on a drum turned by a worm gear on the steering wheel. Many engines carried a winch drum for hauling, and a canopy over the footplate.

Types and uses

General-purpose agricultural engines drove threshing machines and saw benches from the belt and hauled implements between farms; ploughing engines worked in pairs, winching a reversible plough back and forth across a field. Heavier road locomotives hauled exceptional loads and fairground equipment, showman's engines added dynamos and ornate decoration to power fairground rides, and smaller steam tractors and steam rollers served haulage and road-making respectively.

Preservation

Thousands of engines survive in preservation, rallied each summer at steam fairs such as the Great Dorset Steam Fair, one of the largest gatherings of its kind.
