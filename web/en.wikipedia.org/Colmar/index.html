Colmar (French: [kɔlmaʁ]; Alsatian: Colmer; German: Kolmar) is a city in the Haut-Rhin department and Grand Est region of north-eastern France, near the German border. It is the prefecture of the department and the third-largest commune in Alsace, after Strasbourg and Mulhouse, with a population of about 67,000.

First mentioned in 823 as Columbaria, Colmar prospered as a market town on the Alsace wine route and joined the Décapole, a league of ten imperial cities, in 1354. Like the rest of Alsace, it was annexed by Germany in 1871, returned to France in 1918, annexed again in 1940, and liberated after the Battle of the Colmar Pocket in early 1945.

Geography and climate

Colmar lies on the Lauch River at the foot of the Vosges mountains, about 20 km west of the Rhine. Sheltered by the Vosges, it is one of the driest cities in France, with roughly 600 mm of annual precipitation — a microclimate favourable to Alsace wine production.

Sights

The old town is renowned for its well-preserved half-timbered houses, the Gothic St Martin's Church (built 1235–1365), and the "Little Venice" quarter of canalside houses along the Lauch. The Unterlinden Museum, housed in a former convent, holds the Isenheim Altarpiece by Matthias Grünewald (c. 1512–1516), one of the masterpieces of German Renaissance art. Colmar was the birthplace of the painter Martin Schongauer and of Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi, sculptor of the Statue of Liberty; a museum and a 12-metre replica of the statue commemorate him.

Economy

Colmar is a centre of the Alsace wine trade (Riesling and Gewürztraminer) and hosts the annual Alsace wine fair. Tourism, drawn by the old town and the Route des Vins, is a major industry.
