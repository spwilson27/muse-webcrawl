Tower Bridge is a Grade I listed combined bascule and suspension bridge in London, built between 1886 and 1894, designed by Horace Jones, city architect of London, and engineered by John Wolfe Barry. The bridge crosses the River Thames close to the Tower of London, from which it takes its name, and is one of five London bridges owned and maintained by the Bridge House Estates. It is sometimes confused with London Bridge, which lies about half a mile upstream.

Design and construction

By the 1870s, development in the East End of London had made an additional Thames crossing essential, but a conventional high bridge would block access for tall-masted ships to the Pool of London. A Special Bridge or Subway Committee was formed in 1876 and more than 50 designs were submitted. Jones's design, approved in 1884, combined two 65-metre towers on piers sunk into the riverbed, side spans built as suspension bridges, and a central span of 61 metres split into two equal bascules that can be raised to an angle of 83 degrees to allow river traffic through. The high-level walkways between the towers, intended for pedestrians when the bascules were raised, were closed in 1910 but reopened in 1982 as part of the Tower Bridge Exhibition.

Operation

The bascules were originally powered by steam-driven hydraulic machinery; in 1974 the system was converted to electro-hydraulic drive, though the original steam engines are preserved as an exhibit. The bridge lifts around 800 times a year, and river traffic takes precedence over road traffic. Painted in its current red, white and blue scheme in 1977 for the Silver Jubilee of Elizabeth II, Tower Bridge has become one of the most recognisable symbols of London.
