Georges Seurat

Georges Pierre Seurat (2 December 1859 – 29 March 1891) was a French post-Impressionist artist. He devised the painting techniques known as chromoluminarism and pointillism and used conté crayon for drawings on paper with a rough surface. Seurat's large-scale work A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte (1884) altered the direction of modern art by initiating Neo-Impressionism, and is one of the icons of late 19th-century painting.

Biography

Seurat was born in Paris to a prosperous family. He studied art first at the École Municipale de Sculpture et Dessin and then at the École des Beaux-Arts (1878–1879), and read scientific treatises on colour — notably Michel Eugène Chevreul's law of simultaneous contrast and Ogden Rood's Modern Chromatics — that underpinned his method. After military service at Brest, he returned to Paris in 1880, sharing a studio and working in black-and-white conté drawings before turning to painting.

Neo-Impressionism

Rejected by the Salon in 1884, Seurat co-founded the Société des Artistes Indépendants, where he exhibited A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte — a huge canvas built from small, regular touches of complementary colour meant to fuse in the viewer's eye. The critic Félix Fénéon coined the term "Neo-Impressionism" for the movement, whose adherents included Paul Signac, Camille Pissarro and Maximilien Luce. Seurat called his method "chromoluminarism"; popular usage settled on "pointillism" (or divisionism). Later major works include Bathers at Asnières (1884), Le Bec du Hoc, Grandcamp (1885), Parade de cirque (1888), Young Woman Powdering Herself (1890), Le Chahut (1890) and the unfinished The Circus (1891).

Death and legacy

Seurat died in Paris on 29 March 1891, aged 31, of an illness variously identified as meningitis, pneumonia or diphtheria; his young son with Madeleine Knobloch died two weeks later of the same condition. Despite his short career, Seurat's systematic approach influenced the Fauves, the Cubists, Futurism and much of twentieth-century abstract painting, and La Grande Jatte later inspired the Stephen Sondheim musical Sunday in the Park with George.
