Domino theory

The domino theory as applied to Southeast Asia during the Cold War.

The domino theory is a geopolitical theory that was prominent in the United States from the 1950s to the 1980s. It held that if one country in a region came under the influence of communism, the surrounding countries would follow, in a domino effect. The theory was used by successive United States administrations to justify American intervention around the world during the Cold War, most notably in Southeast Asia.

Origins

The metaphor is usually dated to a press conference by President Dwight D. Eisenhower on 7 April 1954, when, discussing Indochina, he spoke of the "falling domino" principle: "You have a row of dominoes set up, you knock over the first one, and what will happen to the last one is the certainty that it will go over very quickly." Similar reasoning had circulated in the Truman administration's containment policy.

Application in Southeast Asia

The theory underpinned American escalation in Vietnam: Washington feared that a communist victory in South Vietnam would lead to communism in Laos, Cambodia, Thailand, Burma, Malaysia, and Indonesia. After the fall of Saigon in 1975, Laos and Cambodia did become communist; however, the predicted wider cascade did not occur, and the communist states of the region soon came into conflict with one another.

Criticism

Critics argued that the theory oversimplified local nationalist movements, treated diverse countries as passive pieces, and helped draw the United States into a costly war. Defenders respond that American resistance bought time for non-communist Southeast Asian states to consolidate economically and politically.
