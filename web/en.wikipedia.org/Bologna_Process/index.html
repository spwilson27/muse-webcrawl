Bologna Process

Launched19 June 1999 (Bologna Declaration)
Members49 countries (European Higher Education Area)
GoalComparable, compatible higher education systems across Europe

The Bologna Process is a series of agreements between European countries designed to ensure comparability in the standards and quality of higher-education qualifications. It began with the Bologna Declaration of 19 June 1999, signed by education ministers of 29 countries, and culminated in the launch of the European Higher Education Area (EHEA) in 2010.

Key reforms

  • A system of three cycles — bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees — replacing many long single-tier national degrees;
  • The European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System (ECTS), so that study periods abroad are recognised at home;
  • The Diploma Supplement, a standardised description of qualifications;
  • Cooperation in quality assurance through the European Association for Quality Assurance (ENQA) and national agencies;
  • Promotion of student and staff mobility, notably through the Erasmus programme.

Development and criticism

Ministerial summits every two to three years (Prague 2001, Berlin 2003, Bergen 2005, London 2007, Leuven 2009, and later meetings) extended the process. Membership grew to 49 countries, including states outside the European Union such as Turkey, Ukraine, and (until its suspension in 2022) Russia. Critics have argued that compressing degrees into three- or four-year bachelor's programmes overloaded curricula and subordinated education to employability; students protested implementation in several countries. Nevertheless, the three-cycle structure is now the norm across most of Europe.
