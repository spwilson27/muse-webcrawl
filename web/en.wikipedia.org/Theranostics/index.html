Theranostics

Theranostics is a portmanteau of therapeutics and diagnostics: a field of medicine that combines a specific targeted therapy with a specific diagnostic test, so that the same molecular target is first imaged to select and monitor patients and then treated. The term was popularized in the 1990s by John Funkhouser. In its most developed form, in nuclear medicine, it refers to using one radioactive drug to image tumours and a second, closely related radioactive drug to deliver cell-killing radiation to the same targets.

Radiotheranostics

The oldest theranostic is radioiodine: iodine-123 or a low dose of iodine-131 images thyroid tissue, and iodine-131 then treats hyperthyroidism and differentiated thyroid cancer. Modern practice centres on receptor-targeted pairs, especially in neuroendocrine tumours: gallium-68 DOTATATE (or copper-64 DOTATATE) PET/CT identifies somatostatin-receptor-positive disease, and lutetium-177 DOTATATE (Lutathera, approved in 2018 on the basis of the NETTER-1 trial) delivers beta radiation to those tumours. In prostate cancer, gallium-68 or fluorine-18 PSMA PET selects patients for lutetium-177 PSMA-617 (Pluvicto, approved in 2022 after the VISION trial showed improved survival in metastatic castration-resistant disease).

Other applications

Outside nuclear medicine, "theranostics" describes companion diagnostics generally — tests such as HER2 testing before trastuzumab — and engineered nanoparticles designed to combine imaging contrast and drug delivery in one particle. Dosimetry, estimating the radiation dose each patient's organs and tumours will receive, is a distinctive feature of the radiotheranostic approach and an active research area, as are alpha-emitting agents such as actinium-225.

Outlook

Theranostics is a paradigm case of precision or personalized medicine: treatment is given only when imaging proves the target is present, and response can be monitored with the same tool. Challenges include the cost and logistics of radiopharmaceutical supply, limited production capacity for isotopes, and the need for specialized centres.
