Intensity interferometer

An intensity interferometer is an astronomical instrument that measures the angular diameters of stars by correlating the intensity fluctuations of light recorded by two separated telescopes, rather than combining the light waves themselves. It exploits the Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect: photons from a thermal (chaotic) light source arrive with a statistical tendency to bunch together, and the degree of correlation between intensity fluctuations at two detectors decreases as the separation between them increases, in a way determined by the source's spatial coherence and hence its angular size.

History

Robert Hanbury Brown and Richard Q. Twiss demonstrated the effect in the laboratory in the 1950s, following their development of it to measure the diameter of Sirius. The theory initially drew scepticism from physicists who doubted that photons could behave as correlated, until experiments confirmed the photon-bunching interpretation within quantum optics. Hanbury Brown then built the Narrabri Stellar Intensity Interferometer in New South Wales, Australia, which operated from 1963 to 1974 with two 6.5-metre reflectors on a circular track up to 188 metres across. It measured the diameters of 32 stars with unprecedented precision and determined effective temperatures across the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram.

Advantages and limitations

Because the technique measures intensities rather than phases, it is almost immune to atmospheric turbulence that limits conventional (amplitude) interferometers, and it tolerates rough optics used purely as light buckets. Its weakness is sensitivity: the correlation signal is tiny, requiring very bright stars and large collecting areas, and it yields the modulus of the source's visibility but not its phase, restricting image reconstruction. Interest revived in the 2000s–2010s with modern fast photon-counting detectors and large telescopes such as Cherenkov telescope arrays (VERITAS, MAGIC, HESS and the planned CTA), which can serve as enormous light collectors for intensity interferometry on much fainter targets.
