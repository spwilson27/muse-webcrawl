Glutamine

L-Glutamine, the most abundant free amino acid in human blood.

Glutamine (symbol Gln or Q) is an alpha-amino acid used in the biosynthesis of proteins. Its side chain is an amide formed by replacing the hydroxyl group of glutamic acid with an amine. It is encoded by the codons CAA and CAG. Glutamine is the most abundant free amino acid in the human body and serves as a major carrier of nitrogen between tissues.

Functions

Glutamine is classified as non-essential because healthy people synthesize it from glutamate and ammonia via glutamine synthetase, chiefly in skeletal muscle. It is, however, "conditionally essential": during illness, injury, or intense catabolic stress, demand can exceed synthesis. Glutamine is a preferred fuel of enterocytes lining the gut and of rapidly dividing immune cells; it donates nitrogen for nucleotide synthesis, contributes to acid–base balance through renal ammonia production, and is the precursor from which the brain recycles glutamate and GABA (the glutamate–glutamine cycle).

Sources and supplements

Dietary glutamine is plentiful in both animal and plant protein. It is sold as a nutritional supplement popular among athletes, although evidence for performance benefits in healthy people is limited. In medicine, oral glutamine is approved in some countries to reduce complications of sickle cell disease, and glutamine supplementation is used in certain critical-care and intestinal-failure settings, with ongoing debate about dose and benefit.

Chemistry

Glutamine is unstable in solution over long storage, slowly cyclizing to pyroglutamate with release of ammonia, which is why cell-culture media often use stabilized dipeptide forms. Its amide side chain can be deamidated enzymatically, a reaction exploited by tissue transglutaminase in coeliac disease.
