Rashi

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rashi (Hebrew: רש״י; 22 February 1040 – 13 July 1105), an acronym of Rabbi Shlomo Yitzhaki, was a medieval French rabbi and the most influential commentator on the Hebrew Bible and the Talmud. Born in Troyes, in the Champagne region of northern France, he wrote the first comprehensive commentaries on the Tanakh and on nearly the entire Babylonian Talmud. His clear, concise explanations — written for students at every level — have been printed in virtually every edition of the Bible and Talmud for centuries, and the semi-cursive Hebrew typeface used for his commentaries is still called Rashi script.

Life

Shlomo Yitzhaki was born in Troyes, the only child of Yitzhak and Rivka. Popular legend attributes his birth to his father's miraculous rescue of a precious gem from Christians who sought it for an idol. After early study in Troyes, he travelled to the great yeshivas of the Rhineland — Worms and Mainz — where he studied under leading scholars including Yaakov ben Yakar. Around 1070 he returned to Troyes and founded a yeshiva that drew students from across Europe. By tradition he earned his living as a vintner in the wine country of Champagne. Rashi had three daughters, Yocheved, Miriam and Rachel, whose learned descendants — including his grandsons Rashbam and Rabbeinu Tam — became the core of the Tosafist school. He died in 1105, during the era of the First Crusade, whose massacres of Rhineland Jewish communities form the sorrowful backdrop to his final years.

Commentaries

Rashi's commentary on the Bible, especially the Torah, seeks the plain sense (peshat) of the text while drawing selectively on midrashic interpretation to resolve difficulties, explaining grammar, vocabulary and context in lucid Hebrew peppered with Old French glosses (la'az) that are now a key source for scholars of medieval French. His Talmud commentary explains the Aramaic dialect, supplies missing connective logic, and defines technical terms, making independent study of the Talmud possible for generations of students. Works attributed to him also include responsa, liturgical poems (selichot) reflecting the Crusade persecutions, and commentaries on the prayer book.

Legacy

Rashi's influence on Jewish learning is unmatched: study of the Torah portion of the week with his commentary is itself a religious obligation in traditional practice, hundreds of supercommentaries have been written on his work, and his readings shaped even Christian scholarship through Nicholas of Lyra and, in turn, Martin Luther. His commentaries were among the very first Hebrew books printed.
