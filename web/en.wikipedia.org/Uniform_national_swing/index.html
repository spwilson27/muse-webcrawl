Uniform national swing

Uniform national swing, or UNS, is a method used in psephology — notably in the United Kingdom — to predict the outcome of a general election in individual parliamentary constituencies from national opinion polls. It assumes that the change in each party's share of the vote since the previous election ("swing") will be the same in every constituency, so that a national shift of a given size is applied uniformly to each seat's previous result to determine which seats change hands.

Method

Swing in the British two-party (Butler) sense is the average of one party's gain and the other's loss in vote share. Under UNS, if a party's national vote rises five points while its rival's falls five, every constituency is modelled as moving five points in that party's favour, and seats whose previous majority is smaller than the swing are predicted to flip. Broadcasters use the method for election-night projections and for converting poll leads into seat estimates between elections.

Accuracy and limitations

UNS often performs reasonably because swings in British elections have historically been fairly uniform across regions. However, it fails when change is geographically uneven: the rise of the Scottish National Party in 2015, the Liberal Democrats' concentrated support, differing swings between Leave and Remain areas after the Brexit referendum, and tactical voting all violate the uniformity assumption. Alternatives include proportional swing, regional swing models, and multilevel regression with poststratification (MRP), which estimate seat outcomes from demographic modelling rather than uniform shifts.
