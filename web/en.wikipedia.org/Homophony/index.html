Homophony

HomophonyField: music theory
Texture: melody with chordal accompaniment
Contrasts: monophony, polyphony

In music, homophony is a texture in which a primary melody is supported by an accompaniment of chords, so that all parts move together in essentially the same rhythm. It is one of the principal musical textures, alongside monophony (a single unaccompanied melodic line) and polyphony (multiple independent melodic lines).

Characteristics

Homophonic music foregrounds melody-and-accompaniment: think of a singer with piano chords, a hymn in four-part harmony, or most popular songs. The accompanying voices typically articulate the harmony in block chords or simple broken patterns, subordinate to the tune. Much music of the Classical era — Mozart's piano sonatas, Haydn's quartets — is homophonic, reflecting an aesthetic shift away from Baroque counterpoint.

History

Homophony emerged prominently in the early Baroque period with the Florentine Camerata's monody and the new emphasis on expressive solo singing over a basso continuo. The galant style of the mid-eighteenth century consolidated homophony as the default texture of Western art music.

Related concepts

Homorhythm — all voices moving in identical rhythm — is the extreme form. Heterophony, in which performers simultaneously vary a single melody, is a distinct texture found in many folk traditions.
