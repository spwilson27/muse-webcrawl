Ubiquitin ligase

A ubiquitin ligase (or E3 ubiquitin ligase) is a protein that recruits a ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (E2) loaded with ubiquitin and facilitates the transfer of ubiquitin to a specific target protein. The resulting ubiquitination most commonly marks the target for degradation by the proteasome, but it can also regulate the protein's activity, localisation and interactions. E3 ligases confer substrate specificity on the ubiquitin system, and more than 600 are encoded in the human genome.

Mechanism

Ubiquitination proceeds in three steps: a ubiquitin-activating enzyme (E1) adenylates ubiquitin and forms a thioester with it; the ubiquitin is passed to a conjugating enzyme (E2); and an E3 ligase binds both the charged E2 and the substrate. RING-type E3s act as scaffolds that allow ubiquitin to pass directly from E2 to a lysine on the substrate, whereas HECT and RBR E3s first accept ubiquitin onto their own catalytic cysteine, forming an E3–ubiquitin intermediate before transfer.

Classes and complexes

The largest family is the RING E3s, which include multi-subunit cullin-RING ligases such as the SCF (Skp1–Cullin–F-box) complex and the anaphase-promoting complex, key regulators of the cell cycle. HECT E3s (around 28 in humans) include NEDD4-family enzymes. The RBR family, including Parkin and HOIP, combines features of both mechanisms. E3s often assemble ubiquitin chains linked through specific lysines: K48-linked chains usually signal proteasomal degradation, while K63-linked chains have signalling roles.

Medical relevance

Defects in E3 ligases cause numerous diseases: loss of the von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) ligase causes a cancer syndrome; Parkin mutations cause early-onset Parkinson's disease; and MDM2 amplification promotes cancer by driving degradation of the tumour suppressor p53. E3 ligases are major drug targets — immunomodulatory drugs such as lenalidomide work by redirecting the cereblon E3 to destroy transcription factors, and PROTAC molecules exploit E3s to degrade chosen proteins.
