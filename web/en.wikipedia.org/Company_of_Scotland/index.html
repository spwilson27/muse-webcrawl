Company of Scotland

The Company of Scotland Trading to Africa and the Indies, also called the Scottish Darien Company, was an overseas trading company created by an act of the Parliament of Scotland in 1695. It is best known for the Darien scheme, the disastrous attempt to establish a Scottish colony, "Caledonia," on the Isthmus of Panama in the late 1690s.

Founding

The Act of 26 June 1695 granted the company a monopoly of Scottish trade to Asia, Africa, and the Americas, along with powers to establish colonies in unoccupied or ceded territory. The venture attracted enormous public enthusiasm in Scotland, with investors from all ranks of society subscribing some £400,000 — perhaps a quarter of the country's liquid capital. The banker and merchant William Paterson, a founder of the Bank of England, was a leading promoter.

The Darien expeditions

The first expedition of five ships and about 1,200 settlers sailed in July 1698 and founded New Edinburgh on the Gulf of Darién. The colony suffered from disease, poor supplies, Spanish hostility, and an English refusal — ordered by King William II and III, anxious not to offend Spain — to assist it. The settlement was abandoned in 1699; a second expedition fared no better, surrendering to a Spanish siege in March 1700. Of roughly 2,500 settlers who went to Darien, few returned.

Consequences

The collapse ruined many Scottish investors and became a major grievance in Anglo-Scottish relations. Under the Acts of Union 1707, Scotland's investors were compensated with payments (the "Equivalent") funded partly through the new British national debt, a provision that helped secure support for the Union of the two kingdoms.
