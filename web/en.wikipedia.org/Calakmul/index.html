Calakmul

Calakmul (also spelled Kalakmul) is an ancient Maya city in the present-day state of Campeche, Mexico, deep in the jungles of the Petén Basin region, about 35 km (22 mi) from the Guatemalan border. It was one of the largest and most powerful of all Classic-period Maya cities.

History

Calakmul was rediscovered from the air by biologist Cyrus L. Lundell in 1931 and reported to Sylvanus Morley. The name means "city of the two adjacent pyramids" in Yucatec Maya. During the Classic period (c. 250–900 AD) Calakmul, ruled by the Kaan ("Snake") dynasty, was the capital of a vast regional state and the great rival of Tikal to the south. The two superpowers fought a long series of proxy and direct wars across the Maya lowlands, each heading a network of allied and vassal cities. Under kings such as Yuknoom Ch'een II ("the Great") in the 7th century, Calakmul's influence stretched across much of the lowlands, before defeats by Tikal in 695 and later reversed its fortunes.

The site

The urban core covers about 30 km² and contains roughly 6,750 recorded structures. Structure II, a massive pyramid complex about 45 m (148 ft) high, is among the largest structures of the Maya world. The site has yielded more carved stelae — 117 or more — than any other Maya site, many badly eroded. Causeways (sacbeob) linked Calakmul to allied cities such as El Mirador and Dzibanche.

Conservation

The ruins lie within the Calakmul Biosphere Reserve, Mexico's largest tropical forest reserve. In 2002 the Ancient Maya City of Calakmul was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, extended in 2014 to include the surrounding biosphere reserve as a mixed cultural and natural property.
