Foundations of mathematics

Principia Mathematica (1910–1913) attempted to derive mathematics from logic.

The foundations of mathematics are the study of the logical and philosophical basis of mathematics—of the concepts, axioms, and rules of inference from which mathematical theorems are derived. The field asks what mathematical objects are, what makes mathematical statements true, and whether mathematics can be given a single secure foundation free of paradox and doubt.

Historical development

Euclid's Elements provided the ancient model of an axiomatic system, deriving geometry from a handful of postulates, though its tacit assumptions were only exposed in the 19th century. The invention of calculus raised new questions about infinitesimals and limits, answered by the rigorisation of analysis by Cauchy, Weierstrass, and Dedekind, who reduced analysis to arithmetic. Georg Cantor's set theory then promised a foundation for all of mathematics, until paradoxes—notably Russell's paradox of 1901, the set of all sets that do not contain themselves—shook confidence in the naive theory.

Foundational schools

Three schools emerged in the early 20th century. Logicism, advanced by Frege and by Russell and Whitehead in Principia Mathematica, held that mathematics is reducible to logic. Formalism, led by David Hilbert, treated mathematics as the manipulation of symbols according to rules and sought a finitary proof that a complete formal system for mathematics is consistent—Hilbert's programme. Intuitionism, founded by L. E. J. Brouwer, rejected non-constructive methods such as the law of excluded middle for infinite domains, insisting that mathematical objects are mental constructions.

Gödel and after

In 1931 Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems showed that any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove, and cannot prove its own consistency—defeating Hilbert's programme in its original form. In practice, mathematicians adopted axiomatic set theory, especially Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with the axiom of choice (ZFC), as the standard foundation, while research continued into alternatives such as category theory, type theory, and homotopy type theory. The foundations of mathematics remain a live meeting point of mathematics, logic, and philosophy.
