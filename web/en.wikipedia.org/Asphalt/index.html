Asphalt

Asphalt, also known as bitumen, is a sticky, black, highly viscous liquid or semi-solid form of petroleum. It may be found in natural deposits, such as the Pitch Lake in Trinidad and the La Brea Tar Pits, or obtained as a residue from the distillation of crude oil. The term is also used, especially in North America, for asphalt concrete, the composite material of bitumen binder and mineral aggregate that is the most common surface for roads, airport runways and parking lots worldwide.

History

Natural asphalt was used in the ancient Near East as mortar, waterproofing and adhesive — for example in Mesopotamian construction and in the paving of processional ways in Babylon. Industrial use expanded in the 19th century with patents for asphalt pavements, and surged with the automobile age in the 20th century. The vast majority of modern asphalt is produced from petroleum refining.

Uses

About 70 percent of asphalt production goes into road construction as the binder in asphalt concrete; other uses include roofing shingles and felts, waterproof coatings, and pipeline protection. Asphalt concrete is almost entirely recyclable: reclaimed asphalt pavement is routinely milled and reused in new mixtures, making it one of the most recycled materials by weight in the United States.

Properties

Bitumen is a complex mixture of hydrocarbons whose stiffness varies strongly with temperature: it softens when heated, permitting mixing and compaction, and hardens in service. Performance-graded specifications match binder grades to local climates to resist rutting in heat and cracking in cold.
