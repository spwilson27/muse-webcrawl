Bukhara (Uzbek: Buxoro) is the fifth-largest city in Uzbekistan and the capital of Bukhara Region. Situated on the Silk Road, the city has long served as a centre of trade, scholarship, culture and religion. It has been inhabited for at least five millennia, and its historic centre — containing numerous mosques, madrasas and a royal citadel — is listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site.

History

Bukhara rose to prominence under the Persian Samanid dynasty (9th–10th centuries), becoming one of the great intellectual centres of the Islamic world; the scholar Ibn Sina (Avicenna) was born nearby and studied in its library. The city was devastated by Genghis Khan in 1220, though the Kalyan minaret was spared. In the 16th century, under the Shaybanid dynasty, it became capital of the Khanate (later Emirate) of Bukhara and a major hub of the Central Asian slave and cotton trades. Russia made the emirate a protectorate in 1868; the last emir was overthrown by the Red Army in 1920.

Sights

Major monuments include the Ark fortress, the Samanid Mausoleum (one of the oldest surviving buildings in Central Asia), the Po-i-Kalyan complex with its 47-metre minaret, the Chor Minor, the Lyab-i Hauz ensemble, and domed bazaars (toqi) still used by traders.

Demographics

The population is predominantly Uzbek, with a significant Tajik-speaking population; the city was also home to the Bukharan Jews, a Jewish community dating back centuries, most of whom emigrated after 1991.
