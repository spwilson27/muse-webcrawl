Mid-engine design

In automotive engineering, a mid-engine layout describes the placement of an automobile engine in front of the rear-wheel axles, but behind the front axle, or — in the most common sports-car usage — between the passenger compartment and the rear axle. By mounting the engine close to the car's centre of mass, a mid-engine design achieves favourable weight distribution and low polar moment of inertia, which improve handling response.

Variants

  • Front mid-engine, rear-wheel drive (FMR) — engine behind the front axle but ahead of the cabin, as in many classic sports cars.
  • Rear mid-engine, rear-wheel drive (RMR) — engine between the cabin and the rear axle, the classic supercar layout (e.g. Lamborghini Miura, Ferrari 458).
  • Front mid-engine, front-wheel drive (FMF) — rare; used by a few French cars of the 1930s–1950s.
  • Rear mid-engine, four-wheel drive — used by cars such as the Audi R8 and Lamborghini Huracán.

Advantages and disadvantages

Centralised mass gives neutral handling, strong traction for the driven rear wheels, and excellent braking balance. Drawbacks include limited luggage and passenger space, more difficult engine access and cooling, higher cost, and a tendency toward sudden oversteer at the limit in rear-mid designs.

History

The layout appeared in racing as early as the 1920s Benz Tropfenwagen and was vindicated in Grand Prix racing by Auto Union and later Cooper, whose rear-mid cars revolutionised Formula One in the late 1950s. The Lamborghini Miura (1966) popularised the layout for road-going supercars.
