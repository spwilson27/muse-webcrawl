Bandwidth-delay product

In data communications, the bandwidth-delay product (BDP) is the product of a data link's capacity (in bits per second) and its round-trip delay time (in seconds). The result is the maximum amount of unacknowledged data that can be in flight on the network at any moment — data that has been transmitted but not yet acknowledged.

Examples

A low-speed satellite link with a bit rate of 64 kbit/s and a round-trip delay of 550 ms has a BDP of about 35,200 bits, or 4,400 bytes. By contrast, a 1 Gbit/s link with a 100 ms round-trip time has a BDP of 100 million bits, or 12.5 megabytes. Links with a large bandwidth-delay product are sometimes called long fat networks (LFNs).

TCP implications

TCP's original 16-bit window field limited the receive window to 65,535 bytes, which is too small for high-BDP paths; the window scale option (RFC 1323) was introduced to allow much larger windows so such links can be fully utilized. Protocols and buffer sizing are often designed around the BDP of the path.
