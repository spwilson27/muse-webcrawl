Charles Hardin Holley (September 7, 1936 – February 3, 1959), known as Buddy Holly, was an American singer and songwriter who was a central and pioneering figure of mid-1950s rock and roll. Born in Lubbock, Texas, he learned guitar and sang country and western music as a teenager before opening for Elvis Presley in 1955 and embracing rock and roll.

Career

Working with producer Norman Petty in Clovis, New Mexico, Holly and his band the Crickets recorded a string of hits in 1957–58, including "That'll Be the Day", "Peggy Sue", "Oh, Boy!", "Maybe Baby" and "Rave On". His distinctive vocal hiccup, Fender Stratocaster playing, and use of studio techniques such as double-tracking made him hugely influential; the Crickets' lineup of two guitars, bass and drums also became the template for later rock bands including the Beatles, whose name was partly inspired by the Crickets.

Death and legacy

On February 3, 1959, Holly died in a plane crash near Clear Lake, Iowa, along with fellow performers Ritchie Valens and the Big Bopper — a tragedy later memorialised in Don McLean's song "American Pie" as "the day the music died". Despite a recording career of barely two years, Holly was among the first artists inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame, in 1986, and his songs have been covered by generations of musicians.
