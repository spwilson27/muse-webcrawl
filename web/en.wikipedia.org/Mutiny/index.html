Mutiny

Mutiny is a revolt among a group of people — typically members of a military or the crew of a ship — to oppose, change, or remove their commanding officers. The term is commonly found in military and naval contexts, where collective insubordination against lawful authority has historically been among the most severely punished of offences.

Famous mutinies

  • The Mutiny on the Bounty (1789), in which Fletcher Christian and others seized HMS Bounty from Captain William Bligh.
  • The Indian Rebellion of 1857, begun by sepoy mutinies against the British East India Company.
  • The Spithead and Nore mutinies (1797) in the British Royal Navy, driven largely by grievances over pay and conditions.
  • The mutiny aboard the Russian battleship Potemkin (1905), later immortalized in Eisenstein's film.
  • The Wilhelmshaven and Kiel mutinies (1918) that helped trigger the German Revolution at the end of the First World War.
  • The Invergordon Mutiny (1931) over naval pay cuts.

Legal status

Under most military codes, mutiny is a capital or near-capital offence. The United States Uniform Code of Military Justice (Article 94) defines mutiny and sedition as creating violence or disturbance with intent to usurp or override lawful military authority, punishable by death. British law likewise treated mutiny with extreme severity for centuries, though capital punishment for it was progressively restricted.

Causes

Mutinies typically arise from grievances over pay, food, discipline, brutal officers, or dangerous conditions, and sometimes from political or ideological conviction. Many historical mutinies were effectively strikes for better terms rather than attempts at revolution.
