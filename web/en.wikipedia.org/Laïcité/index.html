Laïcité

Laïcité is the constitutional principle of secularism in France, roughly rendered "secularity". It embodies the strict separation of church and state and the neutrality of the state toward religion, while guaranteeing freedom of conscience. The word derives from the Greek laïkos, "of the people".

Historical development

Laïcité grew out of the French Revolution's challenge to the Catholic Church, the 19th-century "war of the two Frances", and the Ferry laws of the 1880s that made public primary education free, compulsory, and secular. It was codified by the Law of 9 December 1905 concerning the separation of the Churches and the State, which ended state funding of religions (with exceptions, notably Alsace-Moselle) and guaranteed free exercise of worship. The Constitution of the Fifth Republic declares France "an indivisible, secular [laïque], democratic and social Republic".

In public life

Under laïcité, public servants must remain religiously neutral, and public schools exclude religious instruction. Laws of 2004 and 2010 prohibit conspicuous religious symbols in public schools and face coverings in public spaces, measures that remain debated in relation to France's Muslim population and to liberal models of secularism abroad.

Comparison

French laïcité is often contrasted with accommodationist models, such as that of the United States, and with the assertive secularism of Kemalist Turkey, which it influenced.
