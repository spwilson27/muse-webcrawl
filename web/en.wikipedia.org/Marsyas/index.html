Marsyas

In Greek mythology, Marsyas (Ancient Greek: Μαρσύας) was a Phrygian satyr who invented the aulos (a double-reeded wind instrument) — or in another version picked up the instrument after Athena discarded it — and challenged the god Apollo to a musical contest. He lost and was flayed alive for his hubris; his skin was hung in a cave at Celaenae in Phrygia, through which the river Marsyas flowed.

The flaying of Marsyas was a frequent subject of classical and Renaissance art.

The myth

According to the standard account, Athena invented the aulos but threw it away when she saw in a stream's reflection that playing it distorted her face; the satyr Marsyas found it and became so skilled that he boasted he could outplay Apollo, god of the lyre. The Muses judged the contest. In one version the contest ended in a draw until Apollo proposed that both play their instruments upside down — possible for the lyre but impossible for the aulos; in another, Apollo added singing to his lyre-playing, which Marsyas could not match. Marsyas lost, and Apollo had him tied to a pine tree and flayed alive. His blood, or the tears of his mourners, formed the river Marsyas, a tributary of the Maeander.

In ancient thought

The contest was often read allegorically: Apollo's lyre represented order, reason, and moderation, while Marsyas's aulos embodied ecstasy and emotion — the Apollonian and Dionysian contrast later made famous by Nietzsche. Plato, in the Symposium, compares Socrates to Marsyas for the power of his speech to "flay" and move his listeners. The city of Celaenae displayed the satyr's skin, which Herodotus and Xenophon both mention.

In art

The flaying of Marsyas was depicted in Hellenistic sculpture (including the famous hanging Marsyas of the Pergamene school) and later by Titian, whose Flaying of Marsyas is among his last and most harrowing paintings. The myth has also inspired modern works, including Anish Kapoor's monumental sculpture Marsyas at Tate Modern (2002).
