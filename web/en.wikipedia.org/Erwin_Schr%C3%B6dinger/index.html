Erwin Schrödinger

Died4 January 1961, Vienna, Austria
Known forSchrödinger equation, Schrödinger's cat

Erwin Rudolf Josef Alexander Schrödinger (12 August 1887 – 4 January 1961) was an Austrian-Irish physicist who developed fundamental results in quantum theory, in particular the Schrödinger equation, for which he shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1933 with Paul Dirac. He is also known for the "Schrödinger's cat" thought experiment.

Wave mechanics

In 1926, while a professor at the University of Zurich, Schrödinger published a series of papers founding wave mechanics: he proposed that electrons in atoms behave as waves described by a wavefunction ψ, whose evolution is governed by his equation, and showed the equivalence of his approach with Heisenberg's matrix mechanics. The statistical interpretation of |ψ|2 as a probability density, proposed by Max Born, underlies the standard interpretation of quantum mechanics.

Schrödinger's cat

In 1935, dissatisfied with the Copenhagen interpretation, Schrödinger devised his famous paradox: a cat sealed in a box with a quantum-triggered poison would, under a naive reading, be both alive and dead until observed — an absurdity intended to highlight the problem of applying quantum superposition to macroscopic objects and the unresolved measurement problem.

Later life and other work

Schrödinger fled Nazi Germany in 1933, spent time at Oxford and Princeton, and from 1940 worked at the Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies in Ireland, becoming an Irish citizen. His 1944 book What Is Life? influenced the development of molecular biology, inspiring the search for a hereditary "aperiodic crystal" that anticipated DNA.
