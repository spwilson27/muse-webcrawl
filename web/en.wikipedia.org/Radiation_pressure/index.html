Radiation pressure

Radiation pressure (also known as light pressure) is the mechanical pressure exerted upon a surface due to the exchange of momentum between the object and the electromagnetic field. Although photons have no rest mass, they carry momentum, so absorbing a beam of light transfers momentum to the absorber, and reflecting it transfers twice as much. For an absorbed beam the pressure equals the energy flux divided by the speed of light, P = I/c; for a perfectly reflecting surface it is 2I/c.

Discovery and measurement

Johannes Kepler proposed that light pressure explained why comet tails point away from the Sun. James Clerk Maxwell predicted the effect theoretically in 1873 from electromagnetic theory, and it was measured with great delicacy by Pyotr Lebedev (1900) and by Nichols and Hull (1901) using torsion balances.

Applications and effects

Radiation pressure from sunlight is small — about 9 micronewtons per square metre for a reflector at Earth's distance — but it measurably perturbs spacecraft orbits and is harnessed deliberately by solar sails such as IKAROS and LightSail 2. Inside stars, radiation pressure helps support massive stars against gravity and limits their mass; in astronomy it also shapes comet dust tails, sorts interplanetary dust (the Poynting–Robertson effect), and in laser physics enables optical tweezers and laser cooling of atoms.
