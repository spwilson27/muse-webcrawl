Secondary source

In scholarship, a secondary source is a document or recording that relates or discusses information originally presented elsewhere. A secondary source contrasts with a primary source, which is an original object or document — the raw material or first-hand evidence created at the time under study. Secondary sources involve analysis, synthesis, interpretation, or evaluation of primary sources, and are typically at least one step removed from the events they describe.

Classification

Whether a source is primary or secondary depends on context and discipline. A history textbook summarizing research is a secondary source; a scholarly journal article presenting new analysis of documents is also usually secondary, though the same article could serve as a primary source for a study of the historiography of its field. Sources that index, abstract, or compile other sources — such as encyclopedias and bibliographies — are often called tertiary sources.

Examples

Common secondary sources include textbooks, scholarly books and articles, biographies, literary criticism, encyclopedias, and magazine articles that analyze events after the fact. In legal research, secondary sources include treatises, law review articles, and restatements that explain and analyze primary law.

Use in research

Historians and other scholars rely on secondary sources to place primary evidence in context, to understand how interpretations have developed, and to engage with the arguments of other researchers. Wikipedia's own sourcing policies strongly prefer secondary sources because they provide analysis and establish the relative significance of claims.
