The Crimean War was a military conflict fought from October 1853 to February–March 1856 in which the Russian Empire lost to an alliance of the Ottoman Empire, the United Kingdom, France, and the Kingdom of Sardinia. The immediate cause was a dispute over the rights of Christian minorities in the Holy Land, then part of the Ottoman Empire, but the deeper cause was the decline of Ottoman power and the determination of Britain and France to prevent Russian expansion toward the Mediterranean and India — the rivalry later called the "Eastern Question".

Background

In the early 1850s France, under Napoleon III, pressed Catholic claims to guardianship of the holy places in Palestine, while Russia asserted a protectorate over the Ottoman Empire's Orthodox Christian subjects. When the Ottoman sultan conceded to French demands, Tsar Nicholas I demanded terms the Ottomans, encouraged by Britain, rejected. Russian troops occupied the Ottoman Danubian principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia in July 1853, and the Ottomans declared war that October. On 30 November 1853 a Russian fleet destroyed an Ottoman squadron at the Battle of Sinop, which inflamed British and French opinion; Britain and France declared war on Russia in March 1854.

Course of the war

After Russia evacuated the Danubian principalities, the allies resolved to strike the great Russian naval base at Sevastopol in the Crimea. Allied armies landed at Eupatoria in September 1854 and defeated the Russians at the Battle of the Alma, then settled into the long Siege of Sevastopol. Two battles during the siege became famous: Balaclava (25 October 1854), remembered for the Charge of the Light Brigade — a misunderstood order that sent British cavalry against Russian guns "into the valley of Death", as Tennyson's poem put it — and Inkerman (5 November 1854), a confused infantry struggle in fog known as the "soldier's battle".

The siege lasted eleven months. Conditions in the allied camps during the winter of 1854–55 were appalling: storms destroyed supply ships, disease killed far more men than battle, and military administration collapsed. Florence Nightingale's nursing work at Scutari and her statistical campaign for sanitary reform, together with Mary Seacole's care near the front, transformed public expectations of military medicine. Reporters such as William Howard Russell of The Times, sending dispatches by telegraph, made this one of the first wars reported almost in real time, and Roger Fenton's photographs made it the first extensively photographed war. French troops finally stormed the Malakoff redoubt in September 1855, and the Russians evacuated Sevastopol. Fighting also occurred in the Caucasus, the Baltic (where allied fleets bombarded Bomarsund and Sveaborg), the White Sea, and even the Pacific at Petropavlovsk.

Treaty of Paris

The war ended with the Treaty of Paris, signed on 30 March 1856. The Black Sea was neutralised — demilitarised, with no warships or coastal arsenals — Russia surrendered southern Bessarabia, gave up its claim to a protectorate over Ottoman Christians, and the Danubian principalities and Serbia were placed under collective European guarantee. The Ottoman Empire was formally admitted to the Concert of Europe.

Significance

The Crimean War is often called the first "modern" war: it saw early use of railways (including a purpose-built military line at Balaclava), the telegraph, rifled muskets firing Minié balls, trench warfare anticipating the First World War, and explosive naval shells that doomed wooden warships. Politically, it shattered the conservative alliance system of 1815, isolated Russia, and cleared the way for the unifications of Italy and Germany. Defeat convinced Tsar Alexander II that Russia must modernise, prompting the emancipation of the serfs in 1861 and sweeping military reforms.
