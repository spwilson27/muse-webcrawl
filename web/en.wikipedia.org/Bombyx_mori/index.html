Bombyx mori, commonly known as the domestic silk moth, is a moth species belonging to the family Bombycidae. Its larva, the silkworm, is one of the most economically important insects, being the primary producer of silk. The species was domesticated in China from the wild silk moth Bombyx mandarina more than 5,000 years ago.

The silkworm feeds almost exclusively on mulberry leaves. After four moults, the larva spins a cocoon of a single continuous thread of raw silk 300 to 900 metres long. In sericulture the pupae are killed by heat or steam so the filament can be unwound unbroken.

Domestication

Thousands of years of selective breeding have left B. mori entirely dependent on humans: the adult moths are flightless, lack functional mouthparts, and cannot survive in the wild. The genome was sequenced in the 2000s, making the silkworm a model organism for lepidopteran genetics. Two strains, univoltine and bivoltine/polyvoltine, differ in how many generations they produce per year.

Uses

Beyond silk textiles, silkworm pupae are eaten in parts of Asia, and the moth is used in research and education. Legend credits the discovery of silk to the Chinese empress Leizu in the 27th century BCE.
