Pork

Pork is the culinary name for the meat of the domestic pig (Sus scrofa domesticus). It is the most commonly consumed meat worldwide, with evidence of pig husbandry dating back to around 5000 BC. Pork is eaten both freshly cooked and preserved; curing extends the shelf life of pork products, and ham, bacon, gammon, and sausages are all made from pork.

Production and consumption

China is by far the world's largest producer and consumer of pork, followed by the European Union and the United States. Pigs convert feed to meat efficiently and can be raised on varied diets, contributing to pork's global prevalence. Fresh pork cuts include the loin, shoulder (butt), belly, leg (ham), ribs, and chops.

Preserved products

Traditional preservation by salting, smoking, curing, and drying produces ham, bacon, pancetta, prosciutto, chorizo, salami, and a wide range of sausages. These products historically allowed pork slaughtered in autumn to be eaten through the winter.

Religious and cultural restrictions

Pork is prohibited in Islam (as haram) and in Judaism (as non-kosher); the pig is classified as an unclean animal in both traditions. Many Hindus avoid pork, and some Buddhists and Seventh-day Adventists also abstain. These prohibitions significantly shape regional patterns of consumption.

Nutrition and health

Pork is rich in protein, thiamine (vitamin B1), and other B vitamins, as well as minerals such as zinc and selenium. Fat content varies greatly by cut. Undercooked pork historically carried risks of trichinellosis and tapeworm infection, now rare in regulated supply chains.
