Eclipsing binary

An eclipsing binary, also known as an eclipsing variable star, is a binary star system whose orbital plane lies so nearly in the line of sight of the observer that the components periodically eclipse one another as seen from Earth. The total brightness of the system therefore varies periodically, dipping whenever one star passes in front of the other.

Light curves

The brightness of an eclipsing binary is plotted against time as a light curve. A typical light curve shows a nearly constant brightness interrupted by two minima per orbital period: a deep primary minimum, when the hotter, brighter star is eclipsed, and a shallower secondary minimum, when the cooler companion is eclipsed. From the shape, duration and depth of the eclipses, astronomers can determine the relative sizes, temperatures and orbital inclination of the two stars.

Types

Eclipsing binaries are traditionally classified by the shape of their light curves. Algol-type systems (EA) have well-separated components and flat out-of-eclipse sections. Beta Lyrae-type systems (EB) are semi-detached, with continuously varying brightness. W Ursae Majoris-type systems (EW) are contact binaries in which both stars fill their Roche lobes and the two minima are nearly equal in depth.

Scientific importance

Because the geometry of an eclipsing system can be modelled precisely, eclipsing binaries provide some of the most accurate measurements of stellar masses and radii available in astronomy. Combined with spectroscopic radial-velocity measurements, eclipses allow both stars to be "weighed" and measured directly, testing theories of stellar structure and evolution.

Notable examples

  • Algol (Beta Persei), the prototype eclipsing variable, known since antiquity
  • Beta Lyrae, prototype of the EB class
  • Epsilon Aurigae, a long-period system with a mysterious eclipsing companion
