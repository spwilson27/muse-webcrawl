Metanarrative

Related conceptsPostmodernism, grand narrative, master narrative, legitimation
Key thinkerJean-François Lyotard

A metanarrative (also grand narrative or master narrative) is a narrative about narratives of historical meaning, experience, or knowledge, which offers a society legitimation through the anticipated completion of a master idea. The term gained prominence through Jean-François Lyotard's The Postmodern Condition (1979), in which he famously defined the postmodern, "simplifying to the extreme," as "incredulity toward metanarratives."

Lyotard's account

For Lyotard, modern science and political orders justified themselves by appeal to grand stories: the emancipation of humanity through reason and revolution, the triumph of capital, the speculative unfolding of Spirit in Hegel, or the Marxist narrative of class struggle ending in a classless society. Postmodernity, he argued, is marked by the collapse of belief in such overarching frameworks, leaving a plurality of local "little narratives" (petits récits) and language games, none of which can claim universal authority.

Examples

Commonly cited metanarratives include the Enlightenment faith in progress, religious eschatologies that read history as a path toward salvation, nationalist foundation myths, and Whig interpretations of history as steady advance toward liberty. In literary studies, the term is also used more loosely for any story that frames or comments upon other stories.

Criticism

Critics have noted that declaring the end of metanarratives can itself function as a metanarrative, and that movements rejecting grand stories often smuggle in universal claims of their own. Others argue that metanarratives persist — in ideas of inevitable globalization, technological singularity, or climate crisis — and that the analytical task is to examine how they legitimate power rather than to announce their disappearance.
