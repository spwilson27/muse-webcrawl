In physics, a Galilean transformation is used to transform between the coordinates of two reference frames which differ only by constant relative motion within the constructs of Newtonian physics. These transformations, together with spatial rotations and translations in space and time, form the inhomogeneous Galilean group (assumed throughout below). Without the translations in space and time the group is the homogeneous Galilean group. The transformations are named after Galileo Galilei, who articulated the underlying relativity principle in 1632.

Equations

Consider two inertial frames S and S′ in standard configuration, with S′ moving at velocity v along the x-axis of S, origins coinciding at t = t′ = 0. An event with coordinates (t, x, y, z) in S has coordinates in S′ given by:

t′ = t,   x′ = x − vt,   y′ = y,   z′ = z

Differentiating gives the velocity addition rule u′ = u − v, and differentiating again shows that acceleration is invariant: a′ = a. Since mass and force are also taken as frame-independent, Newton's laws are invariant under the transformation.

Properties

The transformation embodies Newtonian assumptions: time intervals and the distance between simultaneous events are absolute; simultaneity itself is absolute; and there is no speed limit. The set of all such transformations parameterised by boost velocity, rotations and translations forms a ten-parameter Lie group, the Galilean group, which is the symmetry group of Newtonian spacetime.

Contrast with Lorentz transformations

Maxwell's equations are not invariant under Galilean transformations, and experiments such as Michelson–Morley failed to reveal the expected ether wind. Einstein's 1905 special relativity replaced the Galilean boost with the Lorentz transformation, in which time mixes with space and simultaneity becomes relative; at velocities small compared with c, the Lorentz transformation reduces to the Galilean one. Formally, the Galilean group is the contraction of the Poincaré group as c → ∞.
