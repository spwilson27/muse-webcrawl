Dallol (hydrothermal system)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dallol is a hydrothermal system and cinder-cone volcano in the Danakil Depression of the Afar Region of northern Ethiopia. Lying about 116 metres below sea level, it is among the lowest and hottest inhabited-adjacent places on Earth, in a remote rift zone where the African continent is slowly splitting apart. Its landscape of acid springs, salt formations, and sulphur terraces in brilliant yellows, greens, and oranges results from magma heating mineral-rich groundwater close to the surface.

Geology

Dallol sits within the Danakil Depression, part of the Afar triple junction where the Red Sea, Gulf of Aden, and East African rifts meet. The Dallol dome formed by intrusion of magma into thick evaporite (salt) sequences; an explosive eruption in 1926 created a maar crater. Groundwater percolating through the salt and volcanic rocks emerges at boiling temperature, extremely acidic (some pools have pH values near or below zero) and saturated with iron, sulphur, and halite, which precipitate into chimneys, terraces, and "salt flowers."

Life in extremes

The springs are a natural laboratory for astrobiology: their combination of hyperacidity, high temperature, and concentrated brine challenges the limits of life. Studies have reported ultra-small microorganisms in some Dallol waters, while the most extreme pools — hot, acidic, and rich in magnesium salts — appear sterile even by extremophile standards, helping define where life cannot persist, with implications for Mars analogues.

Human presence

A now-abandoned potash mining settlement at Dallol and the nearby salt workings of Lake Asal and Lake Karum are served by the Afar salt trade, in which camel caravans carry salt blocks cut from the depression floor to the Ethiopian highlands. The nearby town of Berhale and the semi-permanent camp at Hamed Ela support tourism to the site despite extreme heat and remoteness.
