Roman architecture

Periodc. 509 BC – 4th century AD (Republic and Empire)
Preceded byEtruscan and Greek (Hellenistic) architecture
Key innovationsArch, vault, dome; concrete (opus caementicium); basilica; aqueduct; triumphal arch
Key treatiseVitruvius, De architectura (1st century BC)
Iconic buildingsPantheon, Colosseum, Baths of Caracalla, Basilica of Maxentius

Ancient Roman architecture adopted the external language of classical Greek architecture for its own purposes, which were so different from Greek buildings as to create a new architectural style. The two styles are often considered one body of classical architecture. Roman architecture flourished in the Roman Republic and even more under the Empire, when the great majority of surviving buildings were constructed. It used new materials, particularly Roman concrete, and newer technologies such as the arch and the dome to create buildings that were typically strong and well engineered.

Materials and technology

The Romans' decisive technical advantage was concrete — opus caementicium, a rubble aggregate set in lime-pozzolana mortar that could be cast into almost any shape, hardened underwater, and was then faced with brick, stone, or marble. Combined with the round arch, the barrel and groin vault, and the dome, concrete freed interiors from the span limits of post-and-lintel construction. The Pantheon's dome (completed c. AD 126), 43.3 metres across and still the world's largest unreinforced concrete dome, demonstrates the sophistication of Roman engineering: graded aggregates, coffering, and a central oculus lighten the structure.

Building types

Rome invented or perfected many building types that remain foundational. The basilica — a large covered hall for law courts and business — grew from the Basilica Julia and Basilica Aemilia to the vast vaulted Basilica of Maxentius, and its plan later shaped the Christian church. Imperial fora, triumphal arches, and honorific columns expressed state power. For leisure, Romans built amphitheatres such as the Colosseum (AD 80, seating some 50,000), circuses for chariot racing, theatres, and the immense imperial thermae — the Baths of Caracalla and Diocletian combined bathing with libraries, gardens, and exercise yards.

Infrastructure was equally characteristic: aqueducts (Aqua Claudia, the Pont du Gard), sewers such as the Cloaca Maxima, paved roads and bridges across the empire, lighthouses, warehouses, and standardized town plans (castra) with grid streets around a forum. Housing ranged from the aristocratic domus and country villa to the multi-storey insulae of Rome's crowded districts.

The orders and Vitruvius

Roman designers adapted the Greek orders — Doric, Ionic, Corinthian — adding the Tuscan and Composite orders, but increasingly used columns decoratively, applied to arched concrete structures rather than as free-standing supports, as on the Colosseum's stacked arcades. Our knowledge of Roman design theory rests largely on Vitruvius's De architectura (c. 30–15 BC), the only architectural treatise to survive from antiquity, which codified proportion, planning, and building craft and later became the bible of Renaissance architecture.

Influence

Roman architecture underpins Byzantine, Romanesque, Renaissance, Baroque, and Neoclassical architecture; its vocabulary of arches, domes, and orders was revived from the 15th century onward and applied to churches, palaces, courthouses, and parliaments worldwide. Roman engineering — roads, aqueducts, concrete harbours — also left a permanent mark on the landscape of Europe, North Africa, and the Near East.
