Anise (Pimpinella anisum), also called aniseed or rarely anix, is a flowering plant in the family Apiaceae native to the eastern Mediterranean region and Southwest Asia. Its flavour, similar to liquorice, fennel and star anise, comes chiefly from the aromatic compound anethole. The seeds, whole or ground, are used in a wide variety of regional and ethnic cuisines.

Description

Anise is a herbaceous annual plant growing to about 0.9 m (3 ft) tall. The leaves at the base are simple and lobed, while those higher on the stem are feathery and divided. The flowers are small and white, arranged in dense umbels typical of the carrot family. The fruit is an oblong, pear-shaped schizocarp 3–5 mm long, harvested when it ripens from green to grey-brown.

Cultivation and history

Anise was first cultivated in Egypt and the Middle East and was prized in ancient Rome, where spiced anise cakes were served at feasts as a digestive, as described by Pliny the Elder. It was grown in Europe in the Middle Ages and is now cultivated worldwide, including in Turkey, Egypt, Spain, and India.

Uses

Anise seeds flavour liqueurs and spirits including Greek ouzo, Turkish rakı, French pastis and absinthe, Italian sambuca, and Middle Eastern arak. In cooking, anise is used in Indian spice mixtures, Italian biscotti and pizzelle, Mexican champurrado, and Peruvian picarones. Anise oil and extracts are used in traditional medicine as a carminative and mild expectorant, and as a flavouring in confectionery such as liquorice sweets and British aniseed balls.

Anise should not be confused with star anise (Illicium verum), an unrelated spice from a tree in the family Schisandraceae, which shares the compound anethole.
