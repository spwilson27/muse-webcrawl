Seven Bridges of Königsberg

The Seven Bridges of Königsberg is a historically notable problem in mathematics. The city of Königsberg in Prussia (now Kaliningrad, Russia) was set on both sides of the Pregel River and included two large islands connected to each other and to the mainland by seven bridges. The puzzle asked whether one could walk through the city crossing each bridge exactly once and return to the starting point.

Euler's solution

In 1736, Leonhard Euler proved that no such walk exists. By abstracting the land masses to points (vertices) and the bridges to lines (edges), Euler founded the field of graph theory and prefigured topology. He observed that, except at the start and finish, each time a walk enters a land mass it must also leave it, so every vertex must have an even number of edges (degree) for a closed trail to exist — or exactly zero or two vertices of odd degree for an open one. All four land masses in Königsberg had odd degree, so neither kind of walk is possible.

Legacy

Euler's paper, Solutio problematis ad geometriam situs pertinentis, is regarded as the first theorem of graph theory and the beginning of topology. A walk using each edge exactly once is now called an Eulerian path or circuit. Of the seven bridges, two survive in modern Kaliningrad, and the changed configuration would now permit an Eulerian path (though not a circuit).
