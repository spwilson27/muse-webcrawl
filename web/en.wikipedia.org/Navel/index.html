The navel (clinically known as the umbilicus, commonly known as the belly button or tummy button) is a protruding, flat, or hollowed area on the abdomen at the attachment site of the umbilical cord. All placental mammals have a navel, although it is generally most visible in humans because of the relative lack of body hair.

Structure

The umbilicus is used to visually separate the abdomen into quadrants for clinical purposes. It lies at the level of the L3–L4 vertebrae in adults. After birth, the umbilical cord is clamped and cut, and the remaining stump dries and falls off within a few weeks, leaving the navel. Whether the navel protrudes ("outie", roughly 10% of people) or is recessed ("innie") depends on how the skin and underlying tissue attach, not on how the cord was cut.

Clinical significance

The navel is a site of umbilical hernias, which are common in infants and usually resolve on their own. Infections of the cord stump (omphalitis) are a concern in newborns. In laparoscopic surgery the umbilicus is a common port of entry. Navel lint (omphalolith in larger accumulations) collects in recessed navels, and the navel hosts a diverse bacterial microbiome, as demonstrated by the Belly Button Biodiversity project.

Culture

The navel has carried symbolic meanings — in Greek religion the omphalos at Delphi marked the "navel of the world". In fashion and media, display of the navel has at times been considered immodest (it was famously censored on American television in the 1960s) and at other times fashionable, as with low-rise jeans and navel piercing, one of the most common body piercings.
