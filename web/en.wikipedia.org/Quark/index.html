A quark is a type of elementary particle and a fundamental constituent of matter. Quarks combine to form composite particles called hadrons, the most stable of which are protons and neutrons, the components of atomic nuclei. All commonly observable matter is composed of up quarks, down quarks and electrons. Owing to a phenomenon known as colour confinement, quarks are never found in isolation; they exist only within hadrons or in a quark–gluon plasma.

Flavours

There are six types, or flavours, of quarks: up and down, charm and strange, top and bottom, arranged in three generations of increasing mass. Up and down quarks have the lowest masses and are the most stable; heavier quarks decay rapidly into them. Every quark flavour has a corresponding antiparticle called an antiquark.

Properties

Quarks have electric charge, mass, colour charge and spin. They are the only elementary particles to experience all four fundamental interactions: electromagnetism, gravitation, the strong interaction (mediated by gluons between colour charges, described by quantum chromodynamics) and the weak interaction, which alone can change quark flavour.

History

The quark model was proposed independently by Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig in 1964 to organise the proliferating hadrons; Gell-Mann took the name from a line in James Joyce's Finnegans Wake ("Three quarks for Muster Mark!"). Evidence from deep inelastic scattering at SLAC in 1968 revealed point-like constituents inside the proton, and the remaining flavours were discovered through the 1970s–90s, culminating in the top quark at Fermilab in 1995.
