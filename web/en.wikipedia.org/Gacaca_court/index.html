The gacaca courts were a system of community justice in Rwanda revived and adapted by the post-genocide government to deal with the enormous caseload arising from the 1994 genocide against the Tutsi. The name gacaca refers to the short lawn grass on which traditional village elders once sat to resolve disputes. Between 2005 and their official closure in 2012, the modern gacaca courts tried close to two million cases — far more than the formal courts or the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda could have handled.

Background

After the genocide, more than 100,000 suspects filled Rwanda's prisons, and at ordinary trial rates prosecuting them would have taken over a century. The government therefore adapted the traditional gacaca dispute-resolution mechanism into a nationwide participatory court system. A law was passed in 2001; pilot courts began in 2002, and nationwide trials ran from 2005.

How gacaca worked

Communities elected panels of lay judges of recognised integrity, the inyangamugayo. Accused persons were categorised by the gravity of their alleged acts: planners, leaders and notorious killers (Category 1) were initially reserved to ordinary courts (later partly transferred), while other killings, assaults and property offences went to gacaca at cell and sector level, with appeal courts above. Confession, apology and guilty pleas were strongly incentivised with reduced sentences, much of them converted to community service (travaux d'intérêt général). Proceedings were held in public, in Kinyarwanda, with the whole community expected to attend and testify.

Assessment

Supporters credit gacaca with clearing the caseload, surfacing truth about local killings, locating victims' remains and reintegrating communities. Critics — including Human Rights Watch — raised concerns about due process, the absence of defence counsel, intimidation of witnesses, false accusations, and the exclusion of crimes committed by the victorious Rwandan Patriotic Front. The system's archive of records is regarded as a major historical source on the genocide.
