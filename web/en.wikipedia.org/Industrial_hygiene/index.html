Industrial hygiene

Industrial hygiene, also called occupational hygiene, is the science and practice of anticipating, recognizing, evaluating, and controlling workplace hazards that can cause illness or injury. Industrial hygienists protect workers from chemical agents (gases, vapours, dusts, fumes), physical agents (noise, radiation, heat, vibration), biological hazards, and ergonomic stressors. The profession's classic framework — anticipation, recognition, evaluation, and control — underpins occupational safety and health regulation.

History

The field's roots lie in observations linking work and disease, from Bernardino Ramazzini's De Morbis Artificum Diatriba ("Diseases of Workers", 1700) to studies of miners' lung diseases. In the early 20th century, Alice Hamilton pioneered industrial toxicology in the United States, investigating lead and other poisons in factories. The U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 and the creation of OSHA and NIOSH professionalized the field; the American Industrial Hygiene Association (AIHA), founded in 1939, and certification as a Certified Industrial Hygienist (CIH) through the Board for Global EHS Credentialing are central institutions.

Methods

Evaluation uses exposure monitoring — personal air sampling, noise dosimetry, surface wipes — compared against occupational exposure limits such as OSHA's permissible exposure limits (PELs) and the ACGIH's threshold limit values (TLVs). Controls follow a hierarchy: elimination or substitution of the hazard, engineering controls (ventilation, enclosure), administrative controls (work practices, rotation), and finally personal protective equipment such as respirators.

Applications

Industrial hygienists work in manufacturing, construction, healthcare, mining, laboratories, and government, addressing hazards from asbestos and silica dust to infectious aerosols and indoor air quality. The discipline overlaps with safety engineering, toxicology, and epidemiology within the broader field of occupational health.
