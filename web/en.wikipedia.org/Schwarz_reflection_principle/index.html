Schwarz reflection principle


Image placeholder: Schwarz reflection principle

In complex analysis, the Schwarz reflection principle is a way to extend the domain of definition of a holomorphic function of a complex variable beyond the real axis (or other boundary arcs). Named after Hermann Schwarz, it states, in its simplest form, that if a function is holomorphic in the upper half-plane, continuous up to the real axis, and real-valued on a segment of the real axis, then it can be extended to a holomorphic function on the symmetric region of the lower half-plane by the rule f(z̄) = conjugate of f(z).

Statement and generalizations

More precisely, if f is holomorphic in a domain symmetric about the real line and maps the real points of the domain to real values, then the reflected function agrees with f on the original domain and is holomorphic throughout. The principle generalizes to reflection across analytic arcs and circles via conformal mapping — for example, reflection in a circle corresponds to inversion. It is a prototype of analytic continuation arguments and is closely related to the Morera and Riemann removable singularity theorems.

Applications

The reflection principle is used to construct conformal maps of polygons (Schwarz–Christoffel mappings), to extend solutions of boundary-value problems, and in proving results such as the Phragmén–Lindelöf principle and extension theorems for functions defined in half-planes and strips.
