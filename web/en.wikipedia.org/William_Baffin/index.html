William Baffin (c. 1584 – 23 January 1622) was an English navigator, explorer and cartographer. He is chiefly known for his attempts to discover a Northwest Passage from the Atlantic to the Pacific, and for his precise surveys of the Arctic — Baffin Bay and Baffin Island, the fifth-largest island in the world, bear his name. Remarkably accurate, Baffin's chart of Baffin Bay was so far ahead of his contemporaries that his discoveries were doubted and omitted from maps for two centuries, until confirmed by John Ross in 1818.

Arctic voyages

Of obscure origins, Baffin first appears as chief pilot on James Hall's 1612 voyage to Greenland, during which Hall was killed. He then served in the Spitsbergen whale fishery. In 1615, as pilot to Robert Bylot in the Discovery, he explored Hudson Strait and Hudson Bay, concluding correctly that no navigable Northwest Passage led west from the bay. In 1616 Bylot and Baffin sailed far north through Davis Strait into the great bay now called Baffin Bay, charting its coasts and reaching the entrances of Smith Sound, Jones Sound and Lancaster Sound — the last of which is in fact the true entrance to the Northwest Passage, though Baffin judged it closed by ice.

Later service and death

Baffin afterwards joined the East India Company, making voyages to Surat and the Red Sea, and was engaged in surveying the Persian Gulf. He was mortally wounded by a cannon shot during the Anglo-Persian capture of Qeshm from the Portuguese on 23 January 1622.

Legacy

Besides Baffin Bay and Baffin Island, his name is preserved by a rose variety and by Mount Baffin in Nunavut. His observations of longitude at sea by lunar distances were among the earliest practical attempts at the problem.
