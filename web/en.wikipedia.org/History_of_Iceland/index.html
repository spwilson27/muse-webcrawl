History of Iceland

The history of Iceland begins with the settlement of the island by Norse seafarers in the late 9th century AD. Iceland remained an independent commonwealth until 1262, when it came under Norwegian and later Danish rule. It regained full sovereignty as the Republic of Iceland in 1944.

Norse settlers reached Iceland in the late 9th century.

Settlement era (c. 870–930)

According to the medieval Landnámabók (Book of Settlements), the first permanent Norse settler was Ingólfr Arnarson, who built his farm at Reykjavík around 874. Earlier, Irish monks known as papar may have lived on the island. Settlers came from Norway and from Norse colonies in the British Isles, bringing Celtic thralls and livestock.

The Commonwealth (930–1262)

In 930 the chieftains established the Althing, an annual assembly at Þingvellir, often described as one of the world's oldest parliaments. Around the year 1000 Iceland adopted Christianity by a decision of the lawspeaker Þorgeir Þorkelsson. The Commonwealth was a state without a king or executive, governed by chieftains (goðar). Internal feuding during the Sturlung Age ended with the signing of the Old Covenant in 1262, bringing Iceland under the Norwegian crown.

Foreign rule

Norway and Iceland passed to Denmark under the Kalmar Union and later the Danish monarchy. The Reformation was imposed in the 16th century, and a Danish trade monopoly restricted commerce until the 18th century. The eruption of the Laki fissure in 1783 caused famine and hardship known as the Mist Hardships.

Independence

The nationalist movement led by Jón Sigurðsson won home rule in 1904 and sovereignty in personal union with Denmark in 1918. Occupied by British, then American forces during the Second World War, Iceland voted to sever ties with Denmark and proclaimed the Republic of Iceland on 17 June 1944. It joined NATO in 1949 and the European Economic Area in 1994, and prospered through fishing, aluminium, geothermal energy and tourism.
