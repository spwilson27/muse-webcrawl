Literary theory

Literary theory is the body of ideas and methods used in the practical reading of literature. It offers scholars a range of lenses — historical, linguistic, philosophical, psychological, political — through which texts can be interpreted, and it asks fundamental questions about what literature is, how meaning is produced, and what role readers, authors, and contexts play in that process.

History

Reflection on literature is ancient: Aristotle's Poetics analyzed tragedy, plot, and character, while Horace and Longinus debated decorum and the sublime. In the nineteenth century, criticism was dominated by historical and biographical approaches and by Matthew Arnold's idea of literature as a "criticism of life." The twentieth century transformed the field. Russian Formalism in the 1910s–20s treated literature as a set of devices producing "defamiliarization." The Anglo-American New Criticism of the 1930s–50s insisted on close reading of the autonomous text, rejecting the "intentional fallacy" and "affective fallacy."

Major schools

Structuralism and post-structuralism

Drawing on Ferdinand de Saussure's linguistics, structuralists such as Roland Barthes and Gérard Genette sought the underlying systems — narratology, myth structures, codes — governing texts. Post-structuralism, associated with Jacques Derrida's deconstruction, emphasized the instability of meaning and the play of differences within language.

Reader-response and reception theory

Reader-response critics, including Stanley Fish and Wolfgang Iser, shifted attention to the reader's experience, arguing that meaning arises in the act of reading rather than residing wholly in the text.

Marxist, feminist, and postcolonial theory

Marxist criticism examines literature's relation to ideology and material conditions. Feminist theory, from Virginia Woolf through Elaine Showalter, interrogates gender representation and recovered women's writing. Postcolonial theory — Edward Said's Orientalism (1978), Homi Bhabha, Gayatri Spivak — analyzes literature's entanglement with empire and the representation of colonized peoples.

Other approaches

Psychoanalytic criticism applies Freud and Lacan; new historicism (Stephen Greenblatt) reads texts within their cultural moment; ecocriticism addresses nature and environment; queer theory, narratology, cognitive poetics, and digital humanities continue to extend the field.

The "theory wars"

From the 1960s through the 1990s, literary theory was a site of intense controversy — over the canon, over jargon, and over the political commitments of scholarship. By the 2000s some scholars announced a "post-theory" moment, while others argued theory had simply become the normal background of literary study.
