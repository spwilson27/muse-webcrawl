Stellar classification

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (spectral classification)

In astronomy, stellar classification is the classification of stars based on their spectral characteristics. Electromagnetic radiation from the star is analyzed by splitting it with a prism or diffraction grating into a spectrum exhibiting the rainbow of colors interspersed with spectral lines. Each line indicates a particular chemical element or molecule, with the line strength indicating the abundance of that element. The strengths of the different spectral lines vary mainly due to the temperature of the photosphere, although in some cases the true abundance differences also play a role.

The Morgan–Keenan system

The modern classification scheme is the Morgan–Keenan (MK) system, which assigns each star a spectral type from the sequence O, B, A, F, G, K, M — running from the hottest (O type) to the coolest (M type) — subdivided by Arabic numerals 0–9, plus a luminosity class in Roman numerals (I for supergiants through V for main-sequence dwarfs). The Sun is a G2 V star. The sequence is often remembered by the mnemonic "Oh, Be A Fine Girl/Guy, Kiss Me."

History

The scheme descends from the Harvard classification developed at Harvard College Observatory in the late nineteenth century, based on the strength of hydrogen Balmer lines, and was reorganized into the present temperature sequence largely through the work of Annie Jump Cannon. The Henry Draper Catalogue classified more than 225,000 stars. Antonia Maury and Ejnar Hertzsprung contributed early recognition of luminosity differences within a spectral class.

Additional classes

Cooler objects use classes L, T, and Y for brown dwarfs; carbon stars are classed C; Wolf–Rayet stars are class W. Spectral peculiarities are indicated by suffixes such as "e" for emission lines and "p" for peculiar spectra.
