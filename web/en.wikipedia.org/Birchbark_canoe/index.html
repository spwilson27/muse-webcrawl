Birchbark canoe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A birchbark canoe is a lightweight canoe built by Indigenous peoples of the northern forests of North America, using bark of the paper birch (Betula papyrifera) stretched over a wooden frame of cedar or spruce, sewn with spruce root (watap) and sealed with spruce or pine gum.

Construction

A single large sheet of bark, harvested in spring, forms the hull; it is shaped over a gunwale frame, staked out on a building bed, and laced to ribs and sheathing. The finished craft is light enough to portage — large fur-trade canoes (canots du maître) were about 11 m long and could carry several tonnes with a crew of eight to twelve, while smaller canots du nord served the interior trade routes.

Significance

Birchbark canoes were the principal watercraft of the North American fur trade and of peoples such as the Ojibwe, Algonquin and Mi'kmaq, enabling travel across the continent's lake-and-river networks. Building traditions continue today among Indigenous craftspeople and heritage builders.
