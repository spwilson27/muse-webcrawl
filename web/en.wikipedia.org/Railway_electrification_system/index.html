Railway electrification system

A railway electrification system supplies electrical energy to railway locomotives and multiple units so they can operate without an on-board prime mover. Power is delivered either through an overhead line (catenary) contacted by a pantograph, or through a conductor rail (third rail, and for some guided systems a fourth rail). Systems differ by voltage and by whether the supply is direct current (DC) or alternating current (AC) at mains or special frequencies.

Common standards

Widely used systems include 600–750 V DC third rail (metro and some suburban networks), 1,500 V DC and 3,000 V DC overhead, 15 kV AC at 16.7 Hz (Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Sweden, Norway), and 25 kV AC at 50/60 Hz, which has become the standard for new main-line and high-speed electrification worldwide because higher voltage reduces current and substation spacing. London Underground uses a four-rail system at 630 V DC.

Overhead lines

Overhead equipment consists of a contact wire suspended from a catenary wire, supported by portals or cantilevers, with the running rails normally forming the return path. Auto-tensioned wires keep contact stable at speed. Neutral sections separate feeds from different substations.

Third rail

Conductor-rail systems are cheaper to install and suit confined tunnels, but the exposed live rail is a safety hazard, voltages are limited, and ice can disrupt collection. Top-contact, bottom-contact and side-contact rails are all in use, with cover boards for protection.

Advantages and drawbacks

Electric traction offers high power, rapid acceleration, regenerative braking and no exhaust emissions at point of use, and electric trains are generally cheaper to maintain. The infrastructure, however, is expensive to install, so electrification is most justified on busy routes; elsewhere diesel or battery traction may persist, and some networks use electro-diesel or battery-electric multiple units to bridge gaps.
