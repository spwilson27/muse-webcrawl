Periodc. 7th–5th century BCE

Kosala was an ancient Indian kingdom in the northern Gangetic plain, roughly corresponding to the Awadh region of modern Uttar Pradesh and adjoining parts of Nepal. It was one of the sixteen great states (Mahajanapadas) of the 6th century BCE and is prominent in Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions.

Geography and cities

Kosala lay northeast of the Ganges, bounded by the Himalayan foothills to the north and the kingdom of Kashi to the south. Its early capital was Ayodhya on the Sarayu River, later replaced by Shravasti, a major commercial city where the Buddha spent many rainy-season retreats at the Jetavana monastery.

History

Vedic and epic traditions associate Kosala with the Ikshvaku (Solar) dynasty, to which the hero Rama of the Ramayana belonged. By the Buddha's lifetime the kingdom was ruled by King Pasenadi, a contemporary and patron of the Buddha who nonetheless clashed with rising Magadha. Around the late 5th century BCE, Kosala was absorbed into the expanding Magadhan empire under Ajatashatru's successors.

Religious significance

Kosala was a heartland of early Buddhism and Jainism. Shravasti was among the Buddha's most frequented residences, and many discourses of the Pali Canon are set there. Mahavira, the 24th Jain Tirthankara, also preached in the region, and Ayodhya is revered in Jain tradition as the birthplace of several Tirthankaras.

Legacy

The name Kosala survives in regional memory and in later polities such as the medieval Kosala kingdom of Odisha and Chhattisgarh (Dakshina Kosala). Its cities remain major pilgrimage centres.
