Woodcut
TypeRelief printmaking technique
RegionEurope (art form), from c. 1400
Notable artistsAlbrecht Dürer, Hans Holbein the Younger, Hokusai

Woodcut is a relief printing technique in printmaking. An artist carves an image into the surface of a block of wood — typically with gouges — leaving the printing parts level with the surface while removing the non-printing parts. The block is inked and the image is transferred to paper, usually by hand pressure or a press.

History

As an art form, the woodcut developed in Europe around 1400, following the spread of paper. Single-leaf woodcuts of religious subjects were sold to pilgrims before the technique was adopted for book illustration. Albrecht Dürer raised the medium to a high art in works such as the Apocalypse series (1498) and the Rhinoceros (1515). In Japan the related tradition of woodblock printing culminated in the polychrome ukiyo-e of Hokusai and Hiroshige.

Technique

The woodcut block is cut along the grain of a plank (unlike wood engraving, which uses the end grain). Areas to remain white are cut away; the lines and shapes left standing receive the ink, producing bold contrasts of black and white. Twentieth-century artists such as the German Expressionists revived the technique for its direct, vigorous quality.
