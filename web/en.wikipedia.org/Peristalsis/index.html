Peristalsis

TypeInvoluntary muscle contraction pattern
Occurs inOesophagus, stomach, intestines, ureters, vas deferens, earthworm locomotion
Controlled byEnteric and autonomic nervous systems

Peristalsis is the involuntary, radially symmetrical contraction and relaxation of muscles that propagates in a wave along a tube, propelling its contents forward. It is best known as the mechanism that moves food through the digestive tract, from the oesophagus through the stomach and intestines, but it also occurs in the ureters (moving urine to the bladder), the vas deferens, and in the locomotion of earthworms and other invertebrates.

Mechanism

A peristaltic wave consists of contraction of circular smooth muscle behind a bolus and simultaneous relaxation of the muscle ahead of it, with longitudinal muscle activity coordinating the shortening of the tube. In the gut, the reflex is organised by the enteric nervous system: distension of the wall activates sensory neurons that excite contraction on the oral side and inhibition on the anal side (the "law of the gut" described by Bayliss and Starling). Slow electrical waves generated by interstitial cells of Cajal set the rhythm — about three cycles per minute in the stomach and around twelve in the duodenum.

Digestive role

Peristalsis begins when a swallowed bolus enters the oesophagus, carrying it to the stomach in seconds; it works regardless of gravity, which is why food can be swallowed while upside down. In the small intestine, peristaltic waves mix chyme with digestive secretions and move it toward the colon over several hours, while between meals the migrating motor complex sweeps residual debris onward. In the large intestine, slow haustral movements and periodic mass movements propel faeces toward the rectum.

Disorders

Failure of peristalsis underlies conditions such as achalasia of the oesophagus, gastroparesis, ileus after abdominal surgery, and Hirschsprung's disease, in which a segment of colon lacking enteric ganglia cannot relax. Excessively strong or uncoordinated peristalsis contributes to cramping pain in irritable bowel syndrome.
