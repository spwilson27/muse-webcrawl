Lake Nyos disaster

The Lake Nyos disaster occurred on 21 August 1986 at Lake Nyos in northwestern Cameroon, when a limnic eruption suddenly released a large volume of carbon dioxide (CO2) from the lake's deep waters. The gas cloud suffocated 1,746 people and around 3,500 livestock in nearby villages, making it one of the deadliest limnic eruptions in recorded history.

Background

Lake Nyos is a meromictic crater lake whose bottom waters had become saturated with CO2 seeping in from a magmatic source below. Because the lake's layers rarely mix, the gas accumulated under pressure for many years. A similar but smaller event had occurred at nearby Lake Monoun in 1984, killing 37 people.

The eruption

Late in the evening of 21 August 1986, something — possibly a landslide, small earthquake, or gradual saturation reaching a tipping point — disturbed the lake's stratification. Deep, CO2-laden water rose, and as pressure fell the gas came out of solution explosively, in turn drawing up more water in a self-sustaining fountain reported to have reached tens of metres high. Between 100,000 and 300,000 tonnes of CO2 (some estimates suggest up to 1.6 million tonnes) were released. A wave surged across the lake, stripping vegetation to a height of about 25 m on the far shore.

The gas cloud

Being denser than air, the CO2 cloud — reportedly up to 50 m thick — poured down two valleys branching from the lake at speeds estimated between 20 and 50 km/h, and in places up to 100 km/h. It travelled as far as 25 km from the lake. Villages in its path, including Nyos, Kam, Cha, and Subum, were inundated; nearly every resident of some settlements died in their sleep or while attempting to flee. Survivors described a strange smell and loss of consciousness; clinical findings included respiratory failure and skin lesions initially mistaken for burns, later attributed to pressure and oxygen deprivation.

Aftermath

News reached authorities two days later due to the remoteness of the area. Survivors were resettled in camps away from the lake. Scientific investigation confirmed the limnic eruption mechanism, and from 2001 degassing pipes were installed in Lake Nyos (and later Lake Monoun) to vent CO2 safely. Many displaced residents eventually returned to the area despite the lingering risk and the slow erosion of the lake's natural dam.
