New Horizons is an interplanetary space probe that was launched as part of NASA's New Frontiers program. Engineered by the Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics Laboratory and the Southwest Research Institute, with Alan Stern as principal investigator, the spacecraft was launched on 19 January 2006 from Cape Canaveral on an Atlas V rocket, attaining the highest launch speed of any artificial object from Earth at the time.

After a gravity assist at Jupiter in 2007, New Horizons flew by Pluto on 14 July 2015, becoming the first spacecraft to explore the dwarf planet. On 1 January 2019 it flew by the Kuiper Belt object 486958 Arrokoth, the farthest and most primitive object visited by a spacecraft.

[ Image: Pluto in natural colour, imaged by New Horizons in July 2015 ]

Pluto flyby

The spacecraft approached Pluto's surface to within about 12,500 km, imaging the nitrogen-ice plain Sputnik Planitia, water-ice mountains, and the large moon Charon in detail. Data revealed active geology, a hazy atmosphere, and cryovolcanism. The probe carries some of the ashes of Clyde Tombaugh, Pluto's discoverer, along with a US flag, a Florida state quarter, and other mementos.

Kuiper Belt extended mission

Following the Pluto encounter, NASA approved an extended mission into the Kuiper Belt. The flyby of Arrokoth — a contact binary nicknamed Ultima Thule — returned images of a remarkably pristine planetesimal. New Horizons continues to make distant observations of other Kuiper Belt objects and measurements of the outer heliosphere as it recedes from the Sun at roughly three astronomical units per year.

Instruments

The payload includes the LORRI telescopic imager, the Ralph imaging spectrometer, the Alice ultraviolet spectrometer, the REX radio science experiment, the SWAP and PEPSSI plasma instruments, and a student-built dust counter. Power comes from a single GPHS radioisotope thermoelectric generator fuelled with plutonium-238.
