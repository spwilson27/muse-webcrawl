Rear-wheel drive

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rear-wheel drive (RWD) is a form of engine and transmission layout in automobiles in which the engine drives the rear wheels only, while the front wheels are used for steering. It was the dominant layout of the 20th-century motor industry — most classically in the front-engine, rear-wheel-drive (FR) configuration, where power travels from a longitudinally mounted engine through a gearbox and a propeller shaft to a differential on the rear axle. Other RWD layouts place the engine amidships (MR, as in many sports cars) or at the rear (RR, as in the original Volkswagen Beetle and Porsche 911).

Characteristics

Because driving and steering are split between the axles, an RWD car can offer balanced weight distribution, good steering feel uncorrupted by "torque steer", and strong traction under acceleration, when weight transfers rearward onto the driven wheels. The layout suits powerful vehicles: driveshafts and rear differentials can be made robust, towing and load-carrying are easier, and the front suspension need not package drive shafts. Disadvantages include the intrusion of a transmission tunnel, higher cost and weight than front-wheel drive, and reduced traction on snow and ice, where the undriven front wheels must push through resistance while lightly loaded rear wheels may slip — the driven wheels are also prone to oversteer if power is applied abruptly in a turn, the basis of the technique of drifting.

History and use today

Virtually all early cars were rear-wheel drive, following the pattern of the 1891 Panhard layout. From the 1970s and 1980s front-wheel drive displaced RWD in mass-market passenger cars for packaging and economy reasons. RWD remains standard for pickup trucks, large SUVs built on separate chassis, most premium and sports cars (BMW, Mercedes-Benz, the Ford Mustang, the Mazda MX-5) and many purpose-built racing cars. Several electric vehicles have revived the layout, since an electric motor on the rear axle needs no propshaft; some EVs use dual motors giving all-wheel drive instead.
