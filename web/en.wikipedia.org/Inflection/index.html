Inflection

In linguistic morphology, inflection (less commonly, inflexion) is a process of word formation in which a word is modified to express different grammatical categories such as tense, case, voice, aspect, person, number, gender, mood, animacy, and definiteness. The inflection of verbs is called conjugation, while the inflection of nouns, adjectives, pronouns, and determiners is called declension.

Inflection is distinguished from derivation: inflectional affixes never change the word class of the word they attach to and typically produce forms of the same lexeme (for example, dog → dogs), whereas derivational affixes can change word class and meaning (for example, teach → teacher).

Examples

English

Modern English is a language with relatively little inflection compared with its ancestor Old English or with languages such as Latin or Russian. Its remaining inflectional suffixes include the plural -s (dog/dogs), the possessive -'s, the third-person singular present -s (he runs), the past tense -ed, the present participle -ing, the past participle -en/-ed, and the comparative and superlative -er/-est. Some irregular forms survive, such as man/men (vowel change, or umlaut) and go/went (suppletion).

Latin

Latin nouns fall into five declensions, inflecting for five or more cases (nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, ablative, and sometimes vocative and locative) and two numbers. Latin verbs conjugate for three persons, two numbers, six tenses (in the indicative), three moods, and two voices, producing well over a hundred forms per verb.

Types of inflectional marking

  • Affixation — the most common type: prefixes, suffixes, infixes, and circumfixes added to a stem.
  • Stem change — internal modification such as ablaut (sing/sang/sung) or consonant gradation.
  • Reduplication — repetition of all or part of the stem, common in Austronesian and other languages.
  • Tone or stress change — e.g., English recórd (verb) versus récord (noun).
  • Suppletion — replacement of the stem by an unrelated form, as in good/better/best.

Inflection and language typology

Languages are sometimes classified by how they use morphology. Analytic languages (such as Mandarin Chinese) rely mostly on word order and particles, while synthetic languages use inflection heavily. Among synthetic languages, fusional languages (Latin, Russian) pack several grammatical meanings into single affixes, whereas agglutinative languages (Turkish, Finnish, Swahili) attach a sequence of affixes each carrying one meaning. Languages with rich inflection, such as Sanskrit, Ancient Greek, and Lithuanian, preserve features inherited from Proto-Indo-European.
