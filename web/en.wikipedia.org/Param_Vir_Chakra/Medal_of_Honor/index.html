The Medal of Honor (MOH) is the United States government's highest and most prestigious military decoration, awarded to recognize American soldiers, sailors, marines, airmen, guardians, and coast guardsmen who have distinguished themselves by acts of valor. The medal is normally awarded by the President of the United States and is often referred to as the Congressional Medal of Honor because it is presented "in the name of the Congress."

Criteria and history

The standard for the medal is conspicuous gallantry and intrepidity at the risk of life above and beyond the call of duty. The Army version was established on 12 July 1862, during the Civil War, following the Navy version of December 1861. Since then, more than 3,500 Medals of Honor have been awarded, roughly two-thirds of them for actions in the Civil War era and later campaigns through the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan. Nineteen servicemen have received it twice; five received both the Army and Navy versions for the same action in the First World War.

Design and privileges

There are three current variants — Army, Sea Service (Navy, Marine Corps, Coast Guard), and Air and Space Forces — each suspended from a light-blue ribbon studded with thirteen white stars. Recipients receive special privileges under law, including a monthly pension, eligibility for burial at Arlington National Cemetery, and by tradition the rendering of a salute by all ranks. It is frequently compared with India's Param Vir Chakra and Britain's Victoria Cross.
