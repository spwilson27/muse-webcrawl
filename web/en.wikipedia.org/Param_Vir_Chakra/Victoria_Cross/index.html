The Victoria Cross (VC) is the highest and most prestigious decoration of the British decorations system. It is awarded for valour "in the presence of the enemy" to members of the British Armed Forces and may be awarded posthumously. It was introduced on 29 January 1856 by Queen Victoria to honour acts of valour during the Crimean War and takes precedence over all other British orders and medals.

Design and origin

The cross pattée, made of bronze traditionally said to come from cannons captured at Sevastopol, bears the crown of Saint Edward, a lion, and the inscription "For Valour." It hangs from a crimson ribbon with a laurel-wreath bar. Since 1856, the medal has been made by the London jewellers Hancocks & Co. from gunmetal supplied by the Royal Logistic Corps.

Recipients

Only 1,358 crosses have been awarded to 1,355 individuals, three of whom received bars (a second VC). Just 15 have been awarded since the Second World War, including awards for Korea, the Falklands, Iraq, and Afghanistan. Many Indian soldiers received the VC during the world wars; India's later Param Vir Chakra was consciously modeled on its standing, and the Australian, Canadian, and New Zealand Victoria Crosses continue the tradition within those nations' own honours systems.
