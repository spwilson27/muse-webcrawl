City Beautiful movement

The City Beautiful movement was a reform philosophy of North American architecture and urban planning that flourished during the 1890s and 1900s with the intent of introducing beautification and monumental grandeur in cities. It was a part of the Progressive social reform movement in North America, and held that beautification could thus promote a harmonious social order that would increase the quality of life.

Origins

The movement is usually identified with the World's Columbian Exposition of 1893 in Chicago, whose "White City" of gleaming neoclassical and Beaux-Arts buildings around a central lagoon, designed by a team led by Daniel Burnham and Frederick Law Olmsted, dazzled millions of visitors and offered a vision of orderly, harmonious urbanism. Journalist and critic Charles Mulford Robinson popularised the term "City Beautiful" in his writings on civic aesthetics.

Principles and implementation

City Beautiful plans emphasised civic centres with monumental public buildings grouped around plazas, broad tree-lined boulevards and parkways, classical architecture, and comprehensive planning. Landmark projects included the McMillan Plan for Washington, D.C. (1901–02), which restored L'Enfant's design and created the National Mall ensemble; Burnham's Plan of Chicago (1909), co-authored with Edward H. Bennett; and schemes for Cleveland, Denver, San Francisco, and Manila and Baguio in the Philippines.

Criticism and legacy

Critics charged the movement with privileging aesthetics and ceremony over housing, sanitation, and the practical needs of the poor — "city beautiful" versus "city practical". Nevertheless, it helped establish the professions of urban planning and landscape architecture in the United States, seeded park systems and planning commissions in hundreds of cities, and left a durable legacy of civic buildings and boulevards.
