Dietary fiber

Main typesSoluble fiber; insoluble fiber
SourcesWhole grains, legumes, fruit, vegetables, nuts, seeds
Recommended intakeRoughly 25–38 g per day for adults

Dietary fiber (or roughage) is the portion of plant-derived food that cannot be completely broken down by human digestive enzymes. It consists largely of non-starch polysaccharides such as cellulose, hemicellulose and pectin, together with lignin and resistant starch. Unlike other food components, fiber passes relatively intact through the stomach and small intestine into the colon.

Types

Fiber is conventionally classified by solubility. Soluble fiber dissolves in water to form a gel-like material and is fermented by gut bacteria; it is found in oats, barley, legumes and apples. Insoluble fiber, including cellulose and lignin, adds bulk to stool and is abundant in whole-wheat flour, wheat bran and many vegetables.

Physiology

In the colon, gut microbes ferment fermentable fibers to short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate, which nourish colonocytes. Fiber slows gastric emptying, moderates postprandial blood glucose, binds bile acids and increases stool bulk and transit regularity.

Health effects

Diets rich in fiber are associated with lower risks of colorectal cancer, cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes. Health authorities generally recommend that adults consume at least 25 grams of fiber per day, though average intakes in many Western countries fall below this level.

History of the concept

The "fiber hypothesis" was popularized in the 1970s by Denis Burkitt and Hugh Trowell, who linked low-fiber diets to diseases common in industrialized societies. E. H. Hipsley had earlier coined the term "dietary fibre" in 1953.
