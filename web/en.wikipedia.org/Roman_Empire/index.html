The Roman Empire was the post-republican state of ancient Rome, characterised by autocratic rule under an emperor and vast territorial holdings around the Mediterranean and in Europe, North Africa and the Near East. It began in 27 BCE when the Senate granted Octavian the title Augustus, and in its unified form endured until 395 CE. The western half collapsed in the 5th century, while the eastern half — the Byzantine Empire — survived until 1453.

History

Principate

Augustus preserved republican forms while concentrating military and civil power. His successors of the Julio-Claudian dynasty expanded the empire into Britain (43 CE) and consolidated the Rhine and Danube frontiers. The Flavian dynasty followed the civil war of 69 CE. Under the "Five Good Emperors" (96–180), the empire reached its territorial peak when Trajan conquered Dacia and briefly Mesopotamia (117 CE), and enjoyed the long stability of the Pax Romana.

Crisis and Dominate

The Crisis of the Third Century (235–284) brought invasion, plague, inflation and rapid imperial turnover. Diocletian stabilised the state, dividing administration between East and West and establishing the Tetrarchy; Constantine reunified the empire, legalised Christianity, and founded Constantinople in 330. Theodosius I made Christianity the state religion and was the last emperor to rule both halves.

Fall of the West and survival of the East

During the 5th century, Germanic peoples crossed the frontiers, sacked Rome (410, 455) and carved kingdoms from the western provinces; the last western emperor, Romulus Augustulus, was deposed in 476. The eastern empire, wealthier and more urbanised, endured for another thousand years as the Byzantine Empire, preserving Roman law and Greek learning until the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453.

Government and society

The empire was run through provinces governed by imperial appointees, a professional army of legions and auxiliaries (about 30 legions at its height), and a network of cities with local elites administering daily life. Roman law, citizenship — extended to all free inhabitants by Caracalla in 212 CE — roads, aqueducts, concrete construction and the Latin and Greek languages bound the state together. Slavery was integral to the economy, and trade reached from Britain to India.

Legacy

Rome's law, language (through the Romance languages), engineering, calendar, and the spread of Christianity shaped Europe and the Mediterranean world permanently; its institutions and vocabulary remain reference points for Western political thought.
