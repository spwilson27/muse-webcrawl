Essential fatty acid

Essential in humans: Linoleic acid (omega-6), alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3)

Sources: Seed oils, nuts, oily fish (for long-chain derivatives)

Essential fatty acids, or EFAs, are fatty acids that humans and other animals must ingest because the body requires them for good health, but cannot synthesize them. Only two fatty acids are known to be essential for humans: alpha-linolenic acid (an omega-3 fatty acid) and linoleic acid (an omega-6 fatty acid). The term refers to the fatty acids involved in biological processes, and not those used as fuel.

Functions

From these two precursors the body can synthesize longer-chain fatty acids: arachidonic acid from linoleic acid, and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) from alpha-linolenic acid, though conversion of the omega-3 precursor is limited. These long-chain fatty acids are precursors of eicosanoids — prostaglandins, thromboxanes, and leukotrienes — and structural components of cell membranes, especially in the brain and retina.

Deficiency

Essential fatty acid deficiency is rare in healthy adults but can occur in fat malabsorption or prolonged parenteral nutrition without lipids, causing scaly dermatitis, hair loss, poor wound healing, and impaired growth in infants. George and Mildred Burr's rat experiments in 1929 first demonstrated that certain fats are dietary essentials.

Dietary sources

Linoleic acid is abundant in vegetable oils such as sunflower and soybean oil; alpha-linolenic acid occurs in flaxseed, chia, walnuts, and canola oil. Oily fish provide preformed EPA and DHA.
