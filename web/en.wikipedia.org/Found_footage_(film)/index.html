Found footage (film)

GenreFilm technique / horror subgenre
Key worksCannibal Holocaust (1980), The Blair Witch Project (1999), Paranormal Activity (2007)

Found footage is a cinematic technique and film genre in which all or a substantial part of the film is presented as if it were film or video recordings recorded by the characters of the film, and later "discovered" and presented to the audience. The events are seen through the camera of one or more of the characters involved, often accompanied by their real-time, off-camera commentary. The footage may be presented as "raw" and unedited, adding to its sense of realism.

History

Precursors include Orson Welles's 1938 War of the Worlds radio broadcast (in another medium) and experimental films using archival material. Ruggero Deodato's Cannibal Holocaust (1980) is widely credited as the first feature built around recovered footage. The technique exploded commercially with The Blair Witch Project (1999), made for roughly $60,000 and grossing nearly $250 million worldwide, and Paranormal Activity (2007), which spawned a major franchise. Cloverfield (2008), REC (2007), Chronicle (2012) and Searching (2018) extended the device to monster films, superhero stories and "screenlife" formats.

Characteristics

Found footage films typically feature shaky handheld camerawork, naturalistic lighting, improvisation and unknown actors. The format's low production costs and high returns made it attractive to studios in the 2000s and 2010s, though critics have noted motion sickness among some viewers and the contrivance some plots require to keep the camera running.

Related meanings

The term also describes, in experimental cinema, films assembled from pre-existing footage — collage films by artists such as Bruce Conner — a usage distinct from the narrative horror genre discussed above.
