Shaktism (Sanskrit: शाक्तसम्प्रदाय, Śāktasampradāya) is one of the major denominations of Hinduism, alongside Shaivism, Vaishnavism and Smarta. It centres on the worship of Shakti or Devi — the divine feminine creative power — as the supreme being, the ultimate reality from which all gods and the universe itself arise.

Beliefs

For Shaktas, the Goddess is both transcendent and immanent: she is prakriti (nature), maya (creative illusion) and the energy (shakti) that animates the male deities, who are inert without her. She is worshipped in many forms, gentle and fierce: Parvati, Durga, Kali, Lakshmi, Saraswati, and the ten Mahavidyas. The Devi Mahatmyam (c. 5th–6th century CE), part of the Markandeya Purana, is the foundational scripture of goddess theology, narrating her victories over the demons Madhu-Kaitabha, Mahishasura, and Shumbha-Nishumbha.

Traditions and practice

Two broad families of practice exist: the Srikula (centred on the goddess Lalita Tripurasundari, strong in South India, using the Sri Yantra) and the Kalikula (centred on Kali and Durga, strong in Bengal, Assam and Nepal). Shaktism is closely intertwined with Tantra, employing mantra, yantra, mudra and kundalini yoga. Its theology recognises the Shakti Pithas — sites where body parts of the goddess Sati are said to have fallen.

Festivals

The great festivals are Navaratri (nine nights of the Goddess, twice yearly), culminating in Durga Puja in Bengal and the northeast, and Kali Puja around Diwali. Millions of pilgrims visit goddess shrines such as Kamakhya in Assam, Vaishno Devi in Jammu, and the Kalighat temple in Kolkata.

History

Goddess worship in South Asia has roots reaching to prehistoric figurines of the Indus Valley. The tradition crystallised in the early medieval period with the Puranas and Tantras, flourished under patronage in Bengal and Assam, and was reshaped in the colonial era, when Kali and Durga became symbols of Indian nationalism as Bharat Mata, Mother India.
