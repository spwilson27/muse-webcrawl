George Bernard Shaw (26 July 1856 – 2 November 1950), known at his insistence as Bernard Shaw, was an Irish playwright, critic, polemicist and political activist. His influence on Western theatre, culture and politics extended from the 1880s to his death and beyond. He wrote more than sixty plays, including major works such as Man and Superman (1902), Pygmalion (1913) and Saint Joan (1923).

Early life

Shaw was born in Dublin to a lower-middle-class Protestant family. He moved to London in 1876 and struggled for years as a novelist and critic before finding success as a dramatist. He became a leading figure in the Fabian Society and a prominent socialist pamphleteer.

Plays

Shaw's early plays, collected as Plays Pleasant and Plays Unpleasant, attacked social conventions and Victorian hypocrisy. Pygmalion, his most popular play, was later adapted into the musical and film My Fair Lady. His screenplay for the 1938 film of Pygmalion won an Academy Award, making him the first person to win both a Nobel Prize and an Oscar.

Views and legacy

Shaw was a committed socialist, a campaigner for alphabet reform, and a famously witty public speaker. Some of his political statements, including flirtations with eugenics and admiration for authoritarian leaders, remain controversial. He died at his home, Shaw's Corner in Ayot St Lawrence, aged 94.
