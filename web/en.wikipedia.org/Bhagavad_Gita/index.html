Bhagavad Gita

The Bhagavad Gita (Sanskrit: भगवद्गीता, "The Song of God"), often referred to as the Gita, is a 700-verse Hindu scripture that is part of the epic Mahabharata. It is a sacred text of Hinduism and one of the most important works in Indian philosophy and literature.

Content

The Gita is set in a narrative framework of a dialogue between the Pandava prince Arjuna and his charioteer and guide, Krishna, an avatar of the god Vishnu. At the start of the Kurukshetra War, Arjuna is filled with moral dilemma and despair about fighting his own relatives. Krishna counsels him on his duty (dharma) as a warrior and prince, and expounds a wide range of spiritual and philosophical teachings.

The dialogue covers the paths to liberation: selfless action (karma yoga), devotion (bhakti yoga), and knowledge (jnana yoga), as well as the nature of the self (atman), the supreme reality (Brahman), and the practice of meditation and detachment.

Themes

Central themes of the Gita include dharma (duty), the immortality of the soul, the doctrine of rebirth, and the ideal of action performed without attachment to its fruits (nishkama karma). In chapter 11, Krishna reveals his universal form (vishvarupa) to Arjuna, displaying his cosmic nature.

"You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions." — Bhagavad Gita 2.47

Influence

The Gita has been translated into hundreds of languages and commented upon by philosophers including Adi Shankara, Ramanuja, and Madhvacharya. It profoundly influenced leaders such as Mahatma Gandhi, who called it his spiritual dictionary, and it is widely read both as a religious scripture and as a work of philosophy.
