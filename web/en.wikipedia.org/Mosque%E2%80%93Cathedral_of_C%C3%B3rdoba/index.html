The Mosque–Cathedral of Córdoba (Spanish: Mezquita-Catedral de Córdoba) is a former Islamic mosque in Córdoba, Spain, converted into a Roman Catholic cathedral. It is regarded as one of the most accomplished monuments of Moorish architecture, famous for its vast hypostyle hall of red-and-white double arches.

History

Construction of the Great Mosque began in 786 under Abd al-Rahman I, the Umayyad emir of Córdoba, on a site previously occupied by a Visigothic church. Successive rulers enlarged the mosque over two centuries, adding the innovative ribbed dome of the maqsurah and the richly decorated mihrab under al-Hakam II in the 10th century. At its completion it was one of the largest mosques in the Islamic world, covering about 24,000 square metres with more than 850 columns.

After the Christian conquest of Córdoba in 1236, the building was consecrated as a cathedral. In the 16th century a Renaissance cathedral nave was built in the centre of the prayer hall — a project King Charles V is said to have regretted on seeing the result. The minaret was encased in a Baroque bell tower.

Architecture

The prayer hall's double-tiered horseshoe arches, alternating stone and brick voussoirs, create a seemingly endless forest of columns. The complex was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984.
