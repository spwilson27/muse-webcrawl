Battlement

A battlement, in defensive architecture such as that of castles and city walls, is a parapet at the top of a wall consisting of alternating solid uprights, called merlons, and gaps, called crenels (or embrasures). The pattern — known as crenellation — allowed defenders to fire missiles or arrows at attackers while taking cover behind the merlons.

History

Battlements appear in the fortifications of ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, and Mycenaean Greece, and are characteristic of medieval European and Islamic military architecture. Distinctive regional forms include the swallowtail (Ghibelline) merlons of northern Italy and the stepped merlons found across the Middle East and South Asia.

Later use

With the advent of gunpowder artillery, crenellated parapets lost their defensive value, but battlements continued to be used decoratively, for example in Gothic Revival architecture, collegiate buildings, and romantic castle restorations of the 19th century. In England, a formal "licence to crenellate" was historically required to fortify a manor house.
