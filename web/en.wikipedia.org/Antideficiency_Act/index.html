Antideficiency Act

The United States Capitol, seat of Congress, which controls federal appropriations

The Antideficiency Act (ADA), codified principally at 31 U.S.C. § 1341, is a United States federal statute that prohibits federal agencies from spending or obligating funds in excess of, or in advance of, congressional appropriations. It is a cornerstone of Congress's constitutional power of the purse.

Provisions

The Act bars federal officials from:

  • making or authorising expenditures or obligations exceeding amounts available in an appropriation or fund;
  • incurring obligations before an appropriation is made (unless authorised by law);
  • dividing appropriations in ways that would evade the restriction, and accepting "voluntary services" for the government except in emergencies involving the safety of human life or protection of property.

Agencies must report violations to the President and Congress, with a copy to the Government Accountability Office (GAO), and knowing and wilful violations can be punished by fines or imprisonment.

History

The original Act was passed in 1884 after agencies routinely spent beyond their budgets and presented Congress with accomplished facts ("coercive deficiencies"). It was strengthened in 1905–1906 and recodified and expanded by the Budget and Accounting Act era reforms, with major amendments in 1950 and 1982.

Government shutdowns

Attorney General opinions issued by Benjamin Civiletti in 1980 and 1981 interpreted the Act to mean that, when appropriations lapse, agencies must suspend operations — the legal basis for United States federal government shutdowns. During a lapse, only activities involving the safety of life and property, or otherwise constitutionally required, may continue, and most federal employees in affected agencies are furloughed.
