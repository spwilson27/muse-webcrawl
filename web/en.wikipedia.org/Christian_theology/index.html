Christian theology

Christian theology is the theology — the systematic study of the divine and religion — of Christian belief and practice. It concentrates primarily upon the texts of the Old Testament and of the New Testament, as well as the Christian tradition. Christian theologians use biblical exegesis, rational analysis and argument to attain an understanding of God's nature, Jesus Christ, salvation, and the human condition.

Branches

Theology is conventionally divided into sub-disciplines: biblical theology; historical theology, which studies doctrinal development through the church councils and writers such as Augustine, Aquinas and the Reformers; systematic (or dogmatic) theology, organised by topic; and practical theology, covering preaching, pastoral care and ethics.

Core doctrines

Central topics include the nature of God and the Trinity (one God in three persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit); Christology, the person and work of Jesus Christ, defined at the Council of Chalcedon (451) as fully God and fully human; soteriology, how humans are saved — by grace, faith, and the atonement — a doctrine sharply contested in the Protestant Reformation; pneumatology, the work of the Holy Spirit; ecclesiology, the nature of the church; and eschatology, the "last things": death, judgment, heaven and hell.

Schools and traditions

Distinct theological traditions developed within Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, and the many Protestant churches. Scholasticism in the medieval West, exemplified by Thomas Aquinas's Summa Theologiae, sought to reconcile faith with Aristotelian reason. The 16th-century Reformation produced Lutheran, Reformed and Anglican theologies, and later movements include liberal theology, neo-orthodoxy (Karl Barth), liberation theology and feminist theology.

Sources and method

Christian theologians draw on Scripture, tradition, reason and experience — a combination often summarised in the Wesleyan Quadrilateral. Councils such as Nicaea (325) and creeds such as the Nicene Creed define orthodox doctrine for most churches, while the interpretation of the Bible (hermeneutics) remains a perennially contested discipline.
