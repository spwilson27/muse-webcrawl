Second Isaiah, or Deutero-Isaiah, is the name given by modern biblical scholars to the anonymous author of chapters 40–55 of the Book of Isaiah in the Hebrew Bible. Instead of the 8th-century BC prophet Isaiah of Jerusalem, most scholars hold that these chapters were composed near the end of the Babylonian exile, in the decades before the Persian king Cyrus the Great conquered Babylon in 539 BC and permitted the exiles to return.

Authorship and dating

The theory of multiple authorship of Isaiah goes back to medieval commentators such as Ibn Ezra and was developed systematically by Johann Christoph Döderlein and Johann Gottfried Eichhorn in the 18th century. Chapters 40–55 differ markedly from chapters 1–39 in style, vocabulary, theology, and historical setting: Jerusalem has already been destroyed, the audience is addressed as exiles in Babylon, and Cyrus is named twice (Isaiah 44:28; 45:1) as God's "anointed" — the only place in the Hebrew Bible where a foreign king receives the title of messiah. Chapters 56–66, attributed to a still later "Third Isaiah," are generally dated after the return.

Content and themes

The section opens with the words "Comfort, comfort my people" (Isaiah 40:1) and is characterised by soaring poetry, vigorous argument for monotheism against Babylonian idolatry, and promises of a new exodus through the desert back to Zion. Its author proclaims that Israel's punishment is complete and that the God who created the world will redeem his people "not for your sake... but for my name's sake."

Chapters 40–55 also contain the four Servant Songs (Isaiah 42:1–4; 49:1–6; 50:4–9; 52:13–53:12), passages describing a chosen servant of God who brings justice to the nations and, in the fourth song, suffers and bears the sins of others. The identity of the servant — Israel collectively, an individual prophet, or a future messianic figure — has been debated for two millennia, and the songs became central to early Christian interpretation of the death of Jesus.

Influence

Second Isaiah's universal monotheism, its call to the nations, and phrases such as "a light to the nations" (Isaiah 49:6) profoundly shaped later Judaism and Christianity. Its opening verses are quoted in all four Gospels in connection with John the Baptist ("a voice cries out in the wilderness"), and Handel's Messiah draws heavily on its text.
