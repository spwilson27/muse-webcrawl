Dental implant

A dental implant is a prosthetic device, typically made of titanium, that interfaces with the bone of the jaw or skull to support a dental prosthesis such as a crown, bridge, or denture. The biological process by which the implant fuses with bone is called osseointegration.

Components and procedure

A conventional implant consists of the implant fixture (a screw-like post placed surgically into the jawbone), an abutment, and the visible prosthetic tooth. After placement, the bone is allowed to heal around the fixture over several months before the final restoration is attached. Planning typically uses radiographs and CT scans to assess bone quantity and position; where bone is insufficient, grafting may be required.

History

Modern implantology stems from the work of Swedish orthopedic surgeon Per-Ingvar Brånemark, who discovered in the 1950s that titanium chambers placed in bone became firmly integrated with it. The first patient was treated in 1965, and commercial implants followed in the late 1970s and 1980s.

Uses and outcomes

Implants can replace a single tooth, support bridges spanning several teeth, or anchor removable dentures. Long-term studies report high survival rates, commonly above 90–95% over ten years for individual implants. Risks include infection, failure of osseointegration, nerve injury, and peri-implantitis, an inflammatory condition affecting the tissues around the implant.
