Galleon

A galleon was a large, multi-decked sailing vessel used as an armed cargo carrier primarily by European states from the 16th to the 18th centuries during the Age of Sail and the first to start the great development of future modern battleships. Galleons were constructed from oak (for the hull), pine (for masts), and various hardwoods for hull and decking. Hulls were usually carvel-built.

History

The galleon developed from the carrack in the mid-16th century, combining a lower, longer hull with a distinctive beakhead prow. Spanish galleons carried treasure from the Americas in the famous treasure fleets; the Manila galleons crossed the Pacific between Acapulco and Manila from 1565 to 1815, one of the longest trade routes in history. English galleons, lower and faster "race-built" designs, famously fought the Spanish Armada in 1588.

Design

Distinctive features included a long, prominent beak or head projecting forward, a tall, multi-decked sterncastle, and three or four masts rigged with square sails on the fore and main masts and lateen sails on the mizzen. War galleons could mount dozens of cannon on broadside gun decks.

Decline

By the mid-18th century the galleon had been superseded in naval warfare by the ship of the line and in commerce by faster vessels such as the East Indiaman and later the clipper.
