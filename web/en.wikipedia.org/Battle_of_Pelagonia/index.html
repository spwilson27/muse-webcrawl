Battle of Pelagonia

DateSeptember 1259
LocationPlain of Pelagonia, near modern Bitola, North Macedonia
ResultDecisive Nicaean victory
BelligerentsEmpire of Nicaea vs. Principality of Achaea, Despotate of Epirus, Kingdom of Sicily, Duchy of Athens

The Battle of Pelagonia took place in September 1259 between forces of the Empire of Nicaea and an anti-Nicaean alliance led by the Despotate of Epirus, the Principality of Achaea and the Kingdom of Sicily. It is widely regarded as a decisive event in the history of the late medieval Balkans: the Nicaean victory destroyed the coalition arrayed against Emperor Michael VIII Palaiologos and cleared the way for the Byzantine recovery of Constantinople from the Latins two years later.

Background

After the Fourth Crusade shattered the Byzantine Empire in 1204, the Empire of Nicaea emerged in western Asia Minor as the leading Greek successor state. Under John III Vatatzes and Theodore II Laskaris, Nicaea expanded into Thrace and Macedonia. Alarmed, Michael II Komnenos Doukas of Epirus formed an alliance cemented by marriages with Manfred of Sicily and William II of Villehardouin, Prince of Achaea, who invaded Nicaean Macedonia in 1259. Michael VIII, who had just seized power in Nicaea as regent (and soon usurper), sent an army under his brother, the sebastokrator John Palaiologos, stiffened with Turkish, Cuman, German, Hungarian and other mercenaries.

Battle

The allied coalition was riven by mutual suspicion: the Epirotes distrusted their Latin allies, while John I Doukas, the illegitimate son of Michael II, quarrelled with William of Achaea over an insult to his wife. Nicaean agents sowed further discord with false rumours. On the eve of battle, Michael II and the Epirote troops slipped away, and John Doukas went over to the Nicaeans. Left nearly alone, the Achaean knights and Manfred's German men-at-arms fought bravely but were overwhelmed by the Nicaean army, whose horse-archers and cavalry routed them. William of Villehardouin was captured — legend says he was found hiding under a haystack and recognized by his protruding teeth — along with many Frankish nobles.

Consequences

The victory opened Greece to Nicaean invasion; William was released only after surrendering the great fortresses of Monemvasia, Mistra and Maina in the Morea — the seed of the future Byzantine Despotate of the Morea. In July 1261 Nicaean troops retook Constantinople almost by accident, restoring the Byzantine Empire. Pelagonia is thus often described as one of the most consequential battles of the medieval Greek world.
