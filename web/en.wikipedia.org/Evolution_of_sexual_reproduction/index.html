Evolution of sexual reproduction

Evolution of sexual reproduction
FieldEvolutionary biology
OriginEarly eukaryotes, over 1 billion years ago

The evolution of sexual reproduction describes how sexually reproducing animals, plants, fungi and protists evolved from a common ancestor that was itself a single-celled eukaryote. Sexual reproduction is widespread in eukaryotes despite its substantial costs, a puzzle sometimes called the "queen of problems in evolutionary biology".

The cost of sex

Sexual reproduction carries a twofold cost: a sexual population grows only half as fast as an asexual one because males do not directly produce offspring, and recombination breaks up favourable gene combinations. Yet sex persists in the great majority of eukaryotic species.

Proposed advantages

Leading explanations hold that genetic recombination generates variation that allows populations to adapt to changing environments and coevolving parasites (the Red Queen hypothesis), and that sex helps purge deleterious mutations (Muller's ratchet). DNA repair during meiosis may also have been an original driver of the process.

Origins

The machinery of meiosis is shared by all eukaryotes, suggesting sex arose in their common ancestor, probably more than a billion years ago. Evidence from fossils of red algae suggests sexual reproduction was established by about 1.2 billion years ago.
