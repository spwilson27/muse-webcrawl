Anglo-Irish

Anglo-Irish (Irish: Angla-Éireannach) is a term used historically for a privileged social and ethnic class in Ireland: the mostly Protestant descendants of English and Welsh settlers whose ancestors arrived from the Anglo-Norman invasion of the 12th century through the plantations of the 17th century. As the "Protestant Ascendancy", this class dominated Irish political, economic and social life for centuries, supplying the members of the Parliament of Ireland, the senior ranks of the professions, the officer corps of the British Army in disproportionate numbers, and a remarkable share of English-language literature — including Jonathan Swift, Edmund Burke, Oscar Wilde, W. B. Yeats, George Bernard Shaw and Bram Stoker. Since the independence of most of Ireland in 1922 the term has largely become a historical one.

Terminology and identity

The term entered common use in the late 18th and 19th centuries to describe Irish-born Protestants of English descent who saw themselves as simultaneously Irish and loyal to the British connection — "an English interest in Ireland", in one classic formulation, yet fiercely proud of Irish distinctiveness, as in Henry Grattan's parliament of 1782. Their identity was cultural and class-based as much as ethnic: Gaelicised "Old English" families, many of them Catholic, occupied an ambiguous middle position, and intermarriage blurred boundaries over the centuries.

History

Following the Tudor conquest, the Williamite war (1689–91) and the Penal Laws, Anglican landowners held the overwhelming majority of Irish land by the 18th century, an ascendancy sustained by a parliament from which Catholics were excluded. The Act of Union 1800 abolished that parliament and tied Ireland directly to Westminster. Catholic emancipation (1829), disestablishment of the Church of Ireland (1869) and the Land Acts of 1870–1903 — which transferred land to tenant farmers — progressively dismantled Ascendancy power, and the Irish War of Independence, partition and the creation of the Irish Free State (1922) ended it in the south, where many "big houses" were burned during the revolutionary period and many families emigrated. In Northern Ireland, unionist politics retained an Anglo-Irish dimension within the United Kingdom.

Culture

The Anglo-Irish produced a distinctive literary tradition, from Swift's satire and Burke's political philosophy through the Irish Literary Revival — led by Yeats, Lady Gregory and J. M. Synge — to Elizabeth Bowen and Samuel Beckett. The Anglo-Irish "big house" novel became a genre in itself, chronicling the decline of the class. Anglo-Irish families were also prominent in horse racing, hunting, the British and Indian civil and military services, and scholarship.
