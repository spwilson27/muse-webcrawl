Coercivity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Coercivity, also called the magnetic coercivity, coercive field or coercive force, is a measure of the ability of a ferromagnetic material to withstand an external magnetic field without becoming demagnetized. Coercivity is usually measured in oersted or ampere/meter units and is denoted HC. It is the intensity of the applied magnetic field required to reduce the magnetization of a material to zero after it has been driven to saturation — that is, the field at which the hysteresis loop crosses zero magnetization (or, in the related definition, zero flux density, giving the "normal" coercivity).

Hard and soft magnets

Materials with high coercivity are called magnetically hard and are used to make permanent magnets; examples include neodymium and samarium–cobalt magnets, with coercivities of hundreds of thousands to millions of amperes per meter. Materials with low coercivity are magnetically soft — such as electrical steel and soft ferrites — and are used in transformer cores and inductors, where magnetization must reverse easily with little energy loss.

Measurement and mechanisms

Coercivity is measured with magnetometers and hysteresigraphs that trace the material's hysteresis loop. Physically, coercivity is determined by how strongly magnetic domain walls are pinned by defects, grain boundaries, and precipitates, and by the magnetocrystalline anisotropy of the material. It generally decreases with increasing temperature. The concept has an analogue in ferroelectric materials, where the coercive field is the electric field needed to reverse polarization, and coercivity-like terms are used in fields from geology (paleomagnetic recording in rocks) to data storage engineering, where recording media require a balance between coercivity high enough for stability and low enough to be written.
