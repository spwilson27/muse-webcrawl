The Tragedy of Hamlet, Prince of Denmark, often shortened to Hamlet, is a tragedy written by William Shakespeare sometime between 1599 and 1601. It is Shakespeare's longest play and is widely considered among the most powerful and influential works of world literature.

Plot

Prince Hamlet of Denmark is summoned home after his father's death to find his mother Gertrude married to his uncle Claudius, now king. The ghost of the old king reveals that Claudius murdered him, and Hamlet swears revenge. Feigning madness, Hamlet stages a play re-enacting the murder to test Claudius's guilt, then confronts his mother and kills the eavesdropping Polonius. His daughter Ophelia, Hamlet's beloved, goes mad and drowns. In the final scene, Hamlet is fatally wounded in a rigged fencing match with Ophelia's brother Laertes; before dying he kills Claudius, and the Norwegian prince Fortinbras claims the throne.

Themes and sources

The play explores madness — real and feigned — revenge, mortality and the difficulty of certain knowledge; the famous soliloquy "To be, or not to be" contemplates death and suicide. Shakespeare's sources include the legend of Amleth in Saxo Grammaticus's Gesta Danorum and an earlier, lost Elizabethan play known as the Ur-Hamlet.

Texts and legacy

Hamlet survives in three early versions — the "bad" First Quarto (1603), the Second Quarto (1604/05) and the First Folio text (1623) — which modern editions usually conflate. It has been performed more than any other Shakespeare play and adapted countless times in theatre, film, opera and literature.
