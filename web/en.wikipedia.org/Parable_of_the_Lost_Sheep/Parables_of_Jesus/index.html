Parables of Jesus

Parables of Jesus
SourcesSynoptic Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke)
NumberRoughly 30–40, depending on definition
Common themesKingdom of God, mercy, repentance, readiness for judgment

The parables of Jesus are short illustrative stories told by Jesus of Nazareth in the canonical Gospels, making up a substantial fraction of his recorded teaching. Drawn from everyday life — farmers, shepherds, merchants, servants and debtors — they typically carry a single striking point about the Kingdom of God and often end in a surprising reversal.

The lost trilogy

Luke 15 groups three parables about the lost being found: the Lost Sheep, in which a shepherd leaves ninety-nine sheep to recover one, rejoicing like the Good Shepherd of John's Gospel; the Lost Coin; and the Prodigal Son. Luke frames them as Jesus's answer to criticism for eating with tax collectors and sinners.

Other well-known parables

Among the most influential are the Good Samaritan, the Sower, the Mustard Seed, the Talents, the Wise and Foolish Virgins, the Pharisee and the Tax Collector, and the Sheep and the Goats. John's Gospel contains no narrative parables in the synoptic sense but uses extended metaphors such as the Good Shepherd and the True Vine.

Interpretation

Early and medieval readers often read the parables allegorically, finding a coded meaning in every detail. Since Adolf Jülicher's work in the late 19th century, scholars have more often sought a single main point, though allegorical readings persist in debate. The parables remain central to preaching and have deeply influenced Western literature and art.
