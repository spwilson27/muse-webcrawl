High-performance liquid chromatography

Acronym: HPLC
Classification: Chromatography
Analytes: Organic and inorganic compounds
Other techniques: UHPLC, LC–MS

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), formerly called high-pressure liquid chromatography, is a technique in analytical chemistry used to separate, identify, and quantify each component in a mixture. It relies on pumps to pass a pressurised liquid solvent (the mobile phase) containing the sample through a column packed with a solid adsorbent material (the stationary phase). Each component interacts slightly differently with the adsorbent, so the components flow out of the column at different rates and are separated.

Operation

A typical instrument comprises a solvent reservoir, a high-pressure pump (operating at up to about 400 bar for HPLC, and over 1000 bar in modern UHPLC systems), an injector or autosampler, a column packed with particles of 2–5 micrometres, and a detector — commonly UV–visible absorbance, fluorescence, or a mass spectrometer (LC–MS). Separations may be isocratic (constant solvent composition) or gradient (composition changing over time).

Types

The most common mode is reversed-phase chromatography, in which the stationary phase is nonpolar (e.g. C18-bonded silica) and the mobile phase is a polar water/organic mixture. Other modes include normal-phase, ion-exchange, size-exclusion, affinity, and chiral chromatography for separating enantiomers.

Applications

HPLC is ubiquitous in pharmaceutical quality control and pharmacokinetics, clinical diagnostics (e.g. glycated haemoglobin measurement), food safety testing for pesticides and additives, environmental analysis, forensics, and the purification of proteins and synthetic chemicals at preparative scale.
