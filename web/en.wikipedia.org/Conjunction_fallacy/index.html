Conjunction fallacy

The conjunction fallacy (also known as the Linda problem) is an inference that a conjoint set of two specific events is more likely than one specific event from the set. It is a type of formal fallacy that occurs in decision-making research and in probability judgments.

The most cited example of the fallacy originated with Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman. Subjects were told about a woman named Linda, described as a 31-year-old, single, outspoken philosophy graduate concerned with discrimination and social justice who had participated in anti-nuclear demonstrations. Most respondents judged it more probable that Linda is "a bank teller and is active in the feminist movement" than that Linda is simply "a bank teller" — even though a conjunction can never be more probable than one of its constituents.

Explanation

Tversky and Kahneman explained the effect through the representativeness heuristic: the combined description resembles a stereotype more strongly, so it feels more representative, and hence is judged more probable. Critics have argued that some respondents interpret the simple option as excluding the other property, or that frequency formats reduce the error.

Probability rules

The probability of two events occurring together (in "conjunction") is always less than or equal to the probability of either one occurring alone: P(A and B) ≤ P(A) and P(A and B) ≤ P(B). This follows because the conjunction is logically a subset of each event.
