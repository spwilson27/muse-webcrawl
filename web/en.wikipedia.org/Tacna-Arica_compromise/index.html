Tacna–Arica compromise

Instrument: Treaty of Lima (1929)
Parties: Chile and Peru
Signed: 3 June 1929
Outcome: Tacna to Peru; Arica to Chile

The Tacna–Arica compromise, settled by the Treaty of Lima in 1929, was the diplomatic resolution of the territorial dispute between Chile and Peru over the provinces of Tacna and Arica, left unresolved since the War of the Pacific.

Background

Under the Treaty of Ancón (1883), which ended the war, Chile occupied Tacna and Arica for ten years, after which a plebiscite would decide their fate. The plebiscite was never held, and the question festered for decades, with Peru pressing for the provinces' return and Chile consolidating its control. US-mediated efforts in the 1920s, including a failed plebiscite attempt under President Calvin Coolidge's commission, gave way to direct negotiation.

The settlement

With the good offices of US President Herbert Hoover, the two countries agreed in the Treaty of Lima (3 June 1929) to divide the territory: Tacna was returned to Peru, while Arica remained Chilean. Chile paid Peru compensation of six million dollars, and Chile undertook to build port facilities at Arica for Peru's use. A boundary line was drawn from the Pacific coast to the Bolivian frontier, finally fixing the border.

Legacy

The compromise ended a half-century dispute and normalised relations, though the lack of a sovereign Pacific port for Bolivia — a separate grievance arising from the same war — continued to shape regional diplomacy.
