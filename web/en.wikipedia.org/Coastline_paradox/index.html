Coastline paradox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The coastline paradox is the counterintuitive observation that the coastline of a landmass does not have a well-defined length. This results from the fractal-like properties of coastlines: as the scale of measurement decreases, the measured length increases, because ever-smaller bays, inlets and irregularities are captured. The phenomenon was first investigated empirically by the English scientist Lewis Fry Richardson and later popularised by Benoit Mandelbrot as a motivation for the mathematics of fractals.

Richardson's research

Richardson, studying the relationship between the length of international borders and the probability of war, noticed that published lengths of the same border varied wildly between sources — for example, the border between Spain and Portugal was reported by one country as 987 km and by the other as 1,214 km. He discovered that the measured length L of a curve depends on the length G of the measuring ruler, roughly as L ∝ G1−D, where D is a constant (now called the fractal dimension) greater than 1 for a coastline.

Mandelbrot and fractals

In a famous 1967 paper, "How Long Is the Coast of Britain? Statistical Self-Similarity and Fractional Dimension," Mandelbrot argued that coastlines exhibit statistical self-similarity: their jaggedness looks similar at many scales. The west coast of Britain has a fractal dimension of about 1.25, while smoother coasts such as South Africa's are closer to 1.02, and Norway's fjord-indented coast is higher still. In the ideal mathematical limit, an infinitely detailed coastline would have infinite length within a finite area.

Implications

Because a coastline's length depends on the measurement method and scale, official figures from different agencies are not comparable. Geographers therefore quote coastline lengths only with a stated method and scale. The paradox illustrates why fractal geometry is needed to describe natural forms that Euclidean geometry handles poorly.
