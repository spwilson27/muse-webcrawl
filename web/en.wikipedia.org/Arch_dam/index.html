Arch dam

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


An arch dam transfers water pressure to the valley sides

An arch dam is a concrete dam that is curved upstream in plan. The arch dam is designed so that the force of the water against it, known as hydrostatic pressure, presses against the arch, compressing and strengthening the structure as the arch transfers the load to the foundation and abutments at the sides of the gorge. An arch dam is most suitable for narrow gorges or canyons with steep walls of stable rock to support the structure and stresses.

Design

Because they are thinner than gravity dams, arch dams require much less concrete, making them economical where good rock exists. Types include the constant-radius arch, the variable-radius arch, and the double-curvature (cupola) arch, which curves in both plan and section. Many large dams combine arch and gravity action (arch-gravity dams).

History and examples

Early arch dams were built by the Romans, notably the Monte Novo dam in Portugal. The modern era of arch dam construction began in the late 19th and early 20th centuries in Australia, Europe and the American West. Famous examples include Hoover Dam (United States, an arch-gravity dam, 1935), the Kariba Dam (Zambia/Zimbabwe), the Inguri Dam (Georgia, among the tallest arch dams at 271.5 m), the Deriner Dam (Turkey), and the El Atazar and Almendra dams in Spain. The Vajont Dam in Italy remains notorious for the 1963 landslide disaster in its reservoir, though the dam structure itself survived.

Safety

Arch dams depend critically on the strength of their abutments. Their failure modes, loads from earthquakes, temperature and sediment, and long-term concrete behaviour are studied using finite-element models and instrumented monitoring.
