Crust (geology)

In geology, the crust is the outermost solid shell of a rocky planet, dwarf planet or natural satellite. On Earth it is the thin, rigid layer above the mantle, chemically distinct from the mantle below; the boundary between them is the Mohorovičić discontinuity (the Moho). Earth's crust makes up less than one per cent of the planet's volume and, together with the uppermost mantle, forms the lithosphere that is broken into tectonic plates.

Two types

Continental crust is thick (typically 30–50 km, up to about 70 km beneath mountain ranges), relatively light, and composed largely of granitic rocks rich in silica and alumina. It is ancient, with some rocks nearly four billion years old. Oceanic crust is thin (about 5–10 km), dense, and basaltic; it is continually created at mid-ocean ridges and recycled into the mantle at subduction zones, so none is older than about 200 million years.

Composition

By mass, Earth's crust is dominated by oxygen (about 47%) and silicon (about 28%), followed by aluminium, iron, calcium, sodium, potassium and magnesium. This abundance underlies the extraction of metals and the formation of soils on which terrestrial life depends.
