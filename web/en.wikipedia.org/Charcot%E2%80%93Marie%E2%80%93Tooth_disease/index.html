Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease

Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (CMT) is a group of inherited disorders that damage the peripheral nerves. It is one of the most common inherited neurological disorders, affecting roughly 1 in 2,500 people. The disease is named after its three discoverers, Jean-Martin Charcot, Pierre Marie, and Howard Henry Tooth, who described it in 1886.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually begin in the legs and feet in adolescence or early adulthood and progress slowly. They include muscle weakness and wasting in the feet and lower legs (sometimes giving an "inverted champagne bottle" appearance), foot deformities such as high arches and hammertoes, foot drop, loss of touch sensation, and diminished reflexes. Hand weakness may develop later.

Cause

CMT is caused by mutations in genes involved in the structure and function of peripheral nerve axons or their myelin sheaths. The most common form, CMT1A, results from a duplication of the PMP22 gene on chromosome 17. Dozens of causative genes are known, with autosomal dominant, recessive, and X-linked inheritance patterns.

Management

There is no cure; treatment focuses on physiotherapy, occupational therapy, orthotic devices such as ankle braces, and sometimes orthopaedic surgery. Life expectancy is generally normal.
