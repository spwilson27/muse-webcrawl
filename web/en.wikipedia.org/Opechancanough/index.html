Opechancanough

Opechancanough (c. 1554 – 1646) was a tribal chief of the Powhatan Confederacy of what is now Virginia in the United States. He was the younger brother (or possibly half-brother or cousin) of the paramount chief Powhatan (Wahunsenacawh), and became paramount chief himself after Powhatan's death in 1618, exercising power effectively from that time. Opechancanough led the two great coordinated attacks on the English colony of Virginia in 1622 and 1644, known as the Indian Massacres, which together killed hundreds of colonists but ultimately failed to expel the English.

Early life and background

Details of Opechancanough's origins are uncertain; some Spanish records suggest he may have been the same person as a young man taken to Spain in the 1560s and baptised as Don Luís de Velasco, though historians debate the identification. As chief of the Pamunkey, one of the principal tribes of the confederacy, he was a formidable and respected leader, and he captured Captain John Smith in 1607 during the famous encounter that preceded Smith's audience with Powhatan.

The attacks of 1622 and 1644

On 22 March 1622, Opechancanough's warriors attacked English settlements along the James River in a surprise offensive that killed about 347 colonists — nearly a third of the colony's population — including the destruction of settlements such as Henricus and Martin's Hundred. The colony retaliated with years of war and reprisals. Two decades later, in April 1644, the aged chief, by then so frail he had to be carried on a litter and have his eyelids lifted to see, launched a second great offensive that killed around 400–500 colonists. This attack also failed, and the war ended with the colony's counteroffensive.

Capture and death

In 1646 Opechancanough was captured by colonists under Governor William Berkeley and taken to Jamestown, where crowds came to view the elderly chief; he reportedly responded with dignity and scorn. He was killed while a prisoner, shot in the back by a soldier assigned to guard him, despite Berkeley's wish that he be kept alive. His death and the subsequent treaty of 1646, which confined the Powhatan peoples to reservations north of the York River, marked the effective end of the Powhatan Confederacy as a power.
