Young stellar object (YSO) denotes a star in its early stage of evolution, from a dense collapsing core through the protostar phase to a pre-main-sequence star that has not yet begun sustained hydrogen fusion in its core. YSOs are found in molecular clouds and star-forming regions such as Orion and Taurus–Auriga.

Classification

Astronomers classify YSOs by the slope of their spectral energy distribution in the infrared, reflecting how much circumstellar material remains. Class 0 objects are deeply embedded protostars visible mainly in the far-infrared and submillimetre, accreting from a dense envelope. Class I sources retain envelopes plus disks. Class II objects — classical T Tauri stars — have optically visible stars surrounded by accretion disks, the sites of planet formation. Class III objects (weak-line T Tauri stars) have largely dissipated their disks.

Properties and evolution

YSOs drive powerful bipolar outflows and jets (Herbig–Haro objects) that clear their surroundings. Evolution from collapse to the main sequence takes from under a million years for massive stars to tens of millions of years for the smallest. Infrared and millimetre observatories — Spitzer, Herschel, ALMA and JWST — have revealed YSO disks in detail, including rings and gaps attributed to forming planets.
