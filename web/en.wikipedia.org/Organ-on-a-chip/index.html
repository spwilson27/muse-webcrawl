Organ-on-a-chip

A lung-on-a-chip: living human cells cultured in a microfluidic device.

An organ-on-a-chip is a multi-channel microfluidic cell-culture device — typically a transparent polymer chip about the size of a USB stick — that simulates the activities, mechanics, and physiological responses of entire organs and organ systems. It is a major application of microphysiological systems and tissue engineering.

How it works

Chips contain hollow microchannels lined with living human cells, through which culture medium flows to mimic blood flow. By reproducing the physical microenvironment of an organ — including mechanical forces such as the stretching of breathing motions in the lung, or the shear stress of flowing blood — the devices reproduce organ-level functions that conventional flat cell cultures cannot. The landmark lung-on-a-chip, reported in 2010 by Donald Ingber's group at the Wyss Institute, recreated the alveolar–capillary interface and its response to bacteria and inflammatory stimuli.

Applications

Other organs modelled on chips include the liver, kidney, intestine, heart, blood–brain barrier, and skin. Researchers use them for drug screening and toxicity testing, aiming to predict human responses better than animal models and reduce reliance on animal testing, and for modelling diseases. Connecting several organ chips into "body-on-a-chip" systems seeks to reproduce whole-body pharmacology.

Outlook

Regulatory and pharmaceutical interest has grown substantially, including qualification efforts by agencies for drug-development tools, though standardisation and validation against human clinical outcomes remain ongoing challenges.
