The Gospel of Judas is a non-canonical Gnostic gospel. The content consists of conversations between Jesus and Judas Iscariot. Given that it includes late 2nd-century theology, it is thought to have been composed in the 2nd century by Gnostic Christians, rather than by the historic Judas himself. Its only known copy is the Codex Tchacos, a Coptic manuscript dating to the 3rd or 4th century.

The manuscript was rediscovered in the 1970s in Middle Egypt, passed through the antiquities trade for decades, and was finally translated and published by the National Geographic Society in 2006, generating worldwide publicity.

Contents

The gospel presents Judas not as the betrayer of Jesus but as his most favoured disciple — the only one who truly understood Jesus' teachings. In the text, Jesus teaches Judas about cosmology, the demiurge, and the divine realm, and asks Judas to hand him over so that his spirit may be freed from the material body. The other disciples are portrayed as misunderstanding Jesus and worshipping the creator god of this world rather than the true God.

Discovery and publication

The codex was reportedly found near El Minya, Egypt, around 1978. It changed hands among dealers in Egypt, Europe and the United States, deteriorating badly in storage. It was eventually purchased by the Maecenas Foundation and restored, translated and published in 2006. Some scholars have criticised the speed of the publication and aspects of the translation, particularly the characterisation of Judas; a later critical edition offered a more negative reading of the figure, translating key passages differently.

Historical references

The Gospel of Judas is plausibly the same work condemned by Irenaeus of Lyons in his treatise Against Heresies (c. 180 AD), who described it as the fictitious history used by a Gnostic sect he called the Cainites.
