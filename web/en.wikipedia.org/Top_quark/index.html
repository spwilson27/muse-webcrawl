The top quark, also known as the truth quark (symbol: t), is the most massive of all observed elementary particles. It derives its mass from its coupling to the Higgs boson. This coupling is very close to unity; in the Standard Model of particle physics it is the largest (strongest) coupling to the Higgs boson of any elementary particle. The top quark was discovered in 1995 by the CDF and DØ experiments at Fermilab.

Properties

Like all quarks, the top quark is an elementary fermion with spin 1/2, and experiences all four fundamental interactions: gravitation, electromagnetism, the weak interaction, and the strong interaction. It has an electric charge of +2/3 e. Its measured mass is approximately 172.7 GeV/c² — roughly the mass of a gold atom's nucleus and some 39 times the mass of its third-generation partner, the bottom quark. Because of its enormous mass, the top quark decays almost instantly, with a lifetime of about 5×10−25 seconds, before it can hadronise; it is therefore the only quark that can be studied as a "bare" quark. It decays almost exclusively into a W boson and a bottom quark.

History

The top quark was postulated in 1973 by Makoto Kobayashi and Toshihide Maskawa to explain observed CP violation in kaon decay within a three-generation model of quarks; their proposal later earned a share of the 2008 Nobel Prize in Physics. Following the discovery of the bottom quark in 1977, searches for its partner intensified. Experiments at SLAC, DESY, CERN and Fermilab progressively excluded lower masses, and hints of a very heavy top quark came from precision electroweak measurements. On March 2, 1995, the CDF and DØ collaborations at Fermilab's Tevatron announced the discovery of top–antitop pair production, with a mass around 175 GeV/c².

Production and study

At hadron colliders, top quarks are produced predominantly in top–antitop pairs through the strong interaction, and singly through the weak interaction. The Large Hadron Collider produces millions of top pairs, enabling precise measurements of the top mass, its couplings and rare decays; because the top couples so strongly to the Higgs boson, these measurements test the Standard Model's account of electroweak symmetry breaking and constrain possible new physics.
