The surviving hull of the Mary Rose in Portsmouth.

The Mary Rose was a carrack in the English Tudor navy of King Henry VIII. Launched in 1511, she served for 34 years in several wars against France, Scotland, and Brittany, and was one of the first ships able to fire a broadside. She sank on 19 July 1545 in the Solent during an engagement with a French invasion fleet, the Battle of the Solent, with the loss of nearly all of her crew of around 400.

Construction and service

Built in Portsmouth in 1510–1511, the Mary Rose was named in honour of the king's sister Mary Tudor and the Tudor rose emblem. She fought in the Anglo-French wars of 1512–1514, was rebuilt in 1527–1528, and was substantially reconstructed in 1536, when her tonnage and gun armament were increased. By 1545 she carried around 90 guns, including heavy bronze cannon and iron guns firing through lidded gunports — a relatively recent innovation.

The sinking

On 19 July 1545, as Henry VIII watched from Southsea Castle, the Mary Rose heeled over during a turn and water flooded in through her open lower gunports; she sank within minutes. The precise cause remains debated, with theories including mishandling, instability after her rebuild, and overloading. Only a few dozen of her crew survived.

Rediscovery and raising

The wreck was located by divers in 1971, in a project led by Alexander McKee and the Mary Rose Trust. After years of excavation recording thousands of Tudor artefacts — guns, longbows, medical equipment, musical instruments, and personal possessions — the surviving hull was raised on 11 October 1982 before a worldwide television audience and brought to Portsmouth.

Preservation and museum

The hull was conserved for decades by spraying with polyethylene glycol, followed by controlled air-drying. A purpose-built Mary Rose Museum opened in Portsmouth Historic Dockyard in 2013, displaying the hull and recovered artefacts, together with forensic reconstructions of crew members and even the ship's dog.
