Teletext

Teletext, or broadcast teletext, is a standard for displaying text and rudimentary graphics on suitably equipped television screens. Teletext sends data in the vertical blanking interval of a television signal and retrieves it with a decoder built into the television. It offers a range of text-based information, typically including news, weather, sports results, and television schedules.

History

Teletext was developed in the United Kingdom in the early 1970s. The BBC launched Ceefax in 1974, and the commercial broadcasters' ORACLE followed; a common standard, the World System Teletext, spread teletext services across Europe and beyond. Viewers selected numbered pages—for example, news on page 100 and sport on page 300—and waited a few seconds for the page to be captured from the broadcast carousel.

Technology

Teletext data is broadcast cyclically in otherwise unused lines of the analog TV signal. A standard page consists of 24 rows of 40 characters with simple color and block graphics. Later enhancements (Level 2.5 / "Hi-Text") increased the color palette and resolution in some countries.

Decline and legacy

The rise of the internet and digital television led most countries to discontinue teletext services in the 2000s and 2010s; the United Kingdom ended Ceefax with the digital switchover in 2012. Teletext nevertheless persisted for decades in countries such as Germany, the Netherlands, and Finland, and its page-based, low-bandwidth design is regarded as a precursor of later information services. Subtitling for television programs continues to use teletext technology in many regions.
