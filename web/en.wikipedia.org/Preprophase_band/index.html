Preprophase band

The preprophase band marks the future division plane in a plant cell.

The preprophase band is a transient ring of microtubules and actin filaments that forms in plant cells during the G2 phase of the cell cycle, just beneath the plasma membrane, and predicts the plane along which the cell will later divide. It was described by Jeremy Pickett-Heaps and Donald Northcote in 1966.

Function

Plant cells are enclosed by rigid walls and cannot divide by furrowing; instead they build a new wall internally using the phragmoplast and cell plate. The preprophase band marks the cortical division site: although the band itself disappears before prometaphase, molecular markers it deposits — including proteins such as TANGLED, RanGAP1 and kinesins of the POK family — persist at the site through mitosis and guide the expanding phragmoplast to fuse with the parent wall at exactly the marked position. The band is thus a classic example of cellular "memory," by which an early spatial cue is retained after the structure that created it has gone. Mutants lacking a normal band, such as maize tangled, show disorganised division planes and abnormal tissue patterning.
