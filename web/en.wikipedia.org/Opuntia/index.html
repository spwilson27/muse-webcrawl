Opuntia

Opuntia, commonly called the prickly pear, is a genus of flowering plants in the cactus family Cactaceae, native to the Americas. The genus is known for its flattened, paddle-shaped stem segments (cladodes), showy flowers, and edible fruits called tunas. Many species bear tiny barbed bristles called glochids in addition to spines.

Scientific classification
Kingdom:Plantae
Order:Caryophyllales
Family:Cactaceae
Subfamily:Opuntioideae
Genus:Opuntia Mill.

Description

Opuntias grow as shrubs or small trees with jointed stems made of flat pads that root readily where they touch the ground. Flowers are typically yellow, orange, or magenta, and are followed by fleshy, often brightly colored fruits. Like other cacti, they use crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM) photosynthesis, opening their stomata at night to conserve water.

Uses

Opuntia ficus-indica has been cultivated since pre-Columbian times. In Mexico the young pads (nopales) are a staple vegetable and the fruits are eaten fresh or made into juice; the plant also hosts cochineal insects, the source of a valuable red dye and the foundation of Mexico's historical cochineal trade. Opuntias are widely planted as ornamentals and living fences.

As an invasive plant

Introduced abroad, prickly pears became serious weeds: in Australia, Opuntia stricta overran some 24 million hectares by the 1920s before being spectacularly controlled by the cactus moth Cactoblastis cactorum, a landmark case of biological pest control. Various species are also invasive in South Africa, the Mediterranean, and elsewhere.
