History of the welfare state in the United Kingdom


William Beveridge, architect of the 1942 report

The history of the welfare state in the United Kingdom covers the development of state provision against poverty, sickness, unemployment and old age, from the Elizabethan Poor Law to the comprehensive system created after the Second World War. The modern British welfare state is conventionally dated to the reforms of Clement Attlee's Labour government (1945–51), built on the Beveridge Report of 1942 and the foundations laid by the Liberal reforms of 1906–14.

Poor Law origins

The Poor Law of 1601 made parishes responsible for relief of the destitute, administered locally through overseers and rates. The Speenhamland system (1795) supplemented wages in parts of southern England. Alarmed by costs and perceived dependency, the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834 imposed the principle of "less eligibility" and the workhouse test, centralising administration under a Poor Law Commission. Outdoor relief nevertheless persisted in much of the industrial north. Alongside the state, Victorian Britain relied on friendly societies, trade unions, charities and municipal enterprise; the Royal Commission on the Poor Laws (1905–09) split between a majority report defending reform of the system and Beatrice Webb's minority report demanding its break-up.

Liberal reforms, 1906–1914

The Liberal governments of Campbell-Bannerman and Asquith introduced old-age pensions (1908, non-contributory, from age 70), labour exchanges (1909) and the National Insurance Act 1911, championed by Lloyd George and Churchill, which provided contributory health insurance for workers and unemployment insurance for some trades. These reforms marked the transition from the Poor Law toward social insurance, influenced by German precedents and the social investigations of Booth and Rowntree.

Interwar period

The First World War expanded state intervention: rent control, the Ministry of Health (1919) and the Housing Act 1919 ("homes fit for heroes") added council housing. Mass unemployment in the 1920s–30s strained insurance funds; the household means test (1931) became a hated symbol of the era. The Unemployment Act 1934 created the Unemployment Assistance Board, and the remaining Poor Law functions were finally abolished by the National Assistance Act 1948.

Beveridge and the Attlee settlement

William Beveridge's report Social Insurance and Allied Services (December 1942) proposed a unified contributory insurance scheme attacking the "five giants" of Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor and Idleness. It became a wartime bestseller and shaped the 1944 white papers and the Butler Education Act 1944. Labour's 1945 landslide government implemented the programme: the National Insurance Act 1946, the National Health Service Act 1946 (the NHS began on 5 July 1948 under Aneurin Bevan, providing care free at the point of use), family allowances, and large-scale council house building. The system rested on full employment, which post-war Keynesian demand management broadly delivered.

Consensus, strain and reform

The "post-war consensus" saw Conservative governments accept the welfare state while expanding home ownership and, later, supplementary benefits (1966) replaced national assistance. From the 1970s, stagflation and fiscal crisis challenged the settlement. Margaret Thatcher's governments (1979–90) promoted marketisation, sold council houses ("Right to Buy", 1980), tightened benefit eligibility and introduced internal markets in the NHS, though core universal services survived. New Labour (1997–2010) raised health and education spending, introduced tax credits and a national minimum wage. After 2010, coalition and Conservative governments pursued austerity: benefit freezes and caps, the introduction of Universal Credit merging working-age benefits, and rises in the state pension age — reforms fiercely debated for their effects on poverty. The COVID-19 pandemic (2020) triggered temporary expansions such as the furlough scheme. The welfare state remains one of the largest components of British public spending, dominated by pensions and health.
