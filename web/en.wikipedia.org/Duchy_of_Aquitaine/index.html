Duchy of Aquitaine

StatusDuchy within the Frankish kingdoms and later France
CapitalBordeaux; later Poitiers
Periodc. 7th century – 1453 (absorbed into the French crown domain)
Notable rulersWilliam IX; Eleanor of Aquitaine; Richard I of England

The Duchy of Aquitaine was a medieval fiefdom in western and south-western France, roughly corresponding to the earlier Gallo-Roman province of Aquitania. At various points it was among the largest and most powerful principalities of medieval Europe, and its dukes held territory stretching from the Loire to the Pyrenees and from the Atlantic coast to Auvergne.

History

Aquitaine originated as a Roman province and passed under Visigothic and then Frankish rule. In the early eighth century its duke, Odo the Great, checked the Umayyad advance at the Battle of Toulouse (721), though Frankish forces under Charles Martel defeated the Aquitainians and their allies at Poitiers in 732. Under Charlemagne, Aquitaine became a sub-kingdom of the Carolingian Empire, ruled by his son Louis the Pious from 781.

As Carolingian authority weakened in the ninth and tenth centuries, the counts of Poitiers consolidated control and assumed the ducal title. Under William V (990–1030) and his successors the duchy reached a first height of power and courtly culture. Duke William IX (1071–1126) was famous as the "Troubadour Duke," one of the earliest known vernacular lyric poets.

Eleanor and the Angevin period

The duchy's most famous ruler was Eleanor of Aquitaine (1122–1204), who inherited it in 1137. Her marriage first to Louis VII of France and then, after annulment, to Henry Plantagenet — who became Henry II of England in 1154 — brought Aquitaine into the Angevin Empire and set the stage for centuries of Anglo-French conflict. Eleanor's sons Richard I and John held the duchy as English kings, although they owed nominal fealty to the French crown for it.

Hundred Years' War and absorption

Aquitaine, in its reduced later form often called Guyenne, was a central theatre of the Hundred Years' War. The Black Prince governed it as a principality from Bordeaux in the 1350s and 1360s. French forces under Charles V largely recovered it, and after the final French victory at the Battle of Castillon in 1453 the duchy was permanently absorbed into the royal domain, ending its existence as a semi-independent principality.

Legacy

The duchy gave its name to the modern administrative region of Nouvelle-Aquitaine. Its court was a cradle of troubadour poetry and of the courtly love tradition in European literature.
