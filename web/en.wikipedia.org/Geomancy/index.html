Geomancy

Geomancy (from Greek geōmanteía, "earth divination") is a method of divination that interprets markings on the ground or the patterns formed by tossed handfuls of soil, rocks, or sand. In its best-known form, the diviner generates sixteen figures composed of single and double points, which are combined and interpreted through a chart with astrological correspondences.

Method

The geomancer makes sixteen rows of random marks — traditionally in sand or earth — and reduces each row to a single or double point by parity. Grouped in fours, these form the first four figures, the Mothers. From them, further figures are derived by rule: the Daughters by reading the Mothers horizontally, the Nieces by adding pairs of figures (modulo two), and finally two Witnesses and a Judge. The fifteen resulting figures are placed in a shield or astrological house chart and interpreted according to the question.

The sixteen figures

Each figure has a Latin name and associations with planets, elements, and fortunes — for example, Fortuna Major and Fortuna Minor (greater and lesser fortune), Acquisitio (gain), Amissio (loss), Laetitia (joy), Tristitia (sorrow), Puer (the boy), Puella (the girl), Caput Draconis and Cauda Draconis (the dragon's head and tail). Because the derivation is binary addition, geomancy is sometimes noted as an early example of a binary combinatorial system.

History and spread

Geomancy in this form probably originated in the Arab world — where it is known as ʿilm al-raml, "the science of the sand" — and entered Latin Europe through translations in 12th-century Spain. It became one of the most popular divinatory arts of the medieval and Renaissance periods, treated by authors such as Hugh of Santalla, Gerard of Cremona, and later Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa. Related traditions include Malagasy sikidy and West African Ifá divination, which use similar binary figure generation.

Other usages

The word geomancy was also applied by early Jesuit translators to the Chinese practice of feng shui, the art of siting buildings and graves in harmony with landscape forces, though the two systems are historically unrelated.
