Anomie

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anomie (from the Greek a-, "without", and nomos, "law") is a state of society in which the normal bonds that hold individuals to the group are weakened or broken, so that social norms lose their power to regulate behaviour. The concept was given its modern form by the French sociologist Émile Durkheim and later developed by Robert K. Merton.

Durkheim's theory

Durkheim introduced the term in The Division of Labour in Society (1893), where he described the "anomic division of labour" — a pathological condition in which the organs of society are not sufficiently in contact for shared rules to arise. He developed the idea most famously in Suicide (1897), where he argued that anomie follows from rapid social or economic change: when the scale of legitimate aspirations collapses or expands suddenly, the "collective order" can no longer restrain individual desires.

Durkheim wrote that human appetites are naturally unlimited ("the more one has, the more one wants"), and only society can impose the moderation that makes contentment possible. When regulation fails, the result is anomic suicide, which rises during both economic crashes and sudden prosperity, because in both cases the individual's accustomed limits are swept away.

Modern usage

In the twentieth century Robert K. Merton recast anomie as a strain between culturally prescribed goals and the legitimate means available to reach them, giving rise to "strain theory" in criminology. The word is also used loosely in everyday speech for a feeling of aimlessness, alienation, or moral drift in modern life.
