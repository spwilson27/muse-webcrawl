Maximilian Karl Emil Weber (21 April 1864 – 14 June 1920) was a German sociologist, historian, jurist, and political economist who is regarded, together with Karl Marx and Émile Durkheim, as one of the founding figures of modern sociology.

Life and career

Weber was born in Erfurt, the eldest son of a prosperous lawyer and politician. He studied law and history at Heidelberg, Berlin, and Göttingen, completing his doctorate in 1889. After professorships at Freiburg and Heidelberg, a nervous breakdown forced him to withdraw from regular teaching in 1903, though he continued to write and remained a central figure in German intellectual life. He helped found the German Sociological Association in 1909, advised the German delegation at Versailles, and contributed to the drafting of the Weimar constitution. He died of pneumonia in Munich in 1920.

Major ideas

Weber's best-known work, The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1904–05), traced an affinity between ascetic Protestantism — especially Calvinist doctrines of predestination and the calling — and the methodical, accumulative ethos of modern capitalism. His broader theory of rationalization described the spread of calculation, bureaucracy, and disenchantment through modern institutions, a process he feared would trap humanity in an "iron cage" of rules. He defined the state as the institution claiming a "monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force," distinguished three types of legitimate authority (traditional, charismatic, and legal-rational), and developed the methodology of Verstehen — interpretive understanding — and of "ideal types."

Legacy

His posthumous Economy and Society (1922) remains one of the most influential works in the social sciences, shaping sociology, political science, organisation theory, and the study of religion.
