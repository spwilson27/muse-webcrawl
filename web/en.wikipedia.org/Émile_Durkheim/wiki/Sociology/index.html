Sociology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sociology is the scientific study of human society, social relationships, social interaction, and the institutions and processes that preserve and change them. It examines how human actions and consciousness both shape and are shaped by surrounding cultural and social structures.

History

The term was coined by Auguste Comte in the 1830s as the capstone of his positivist system of sciences. Sociology became an academic discipline in the late nineteenth century through the work of Émile Durkheim, who established the first European department of sociology at the University of Bordeaux in 1895 and defined the field's object as social facts — ways of acting external to the individual and capable of exercising constraint over them. Karl Marx analysed society through class conflict and the mode of production, while Max Weber emphasised meaning, rationalization, and the role of ideas. Others — Georg Simmel, Harriet Martineau, W. E. B. Du Bois — broadened the field's scope and methods.

Scope and methods

Sociologists study topics ranging from crime, religion, family, and education to the state, race, gender, social class, culture, and social change. Research methods include surveys, interviews, participant observation, historical comparison, and statistical analysis of large datasets. The discipline spans micro-level studies of face-to-face interaction and macro-level analyses of whole societies and global systems.

Major perspectives

  • Functionalism — society as a system of interdependent parts maintaining equilibrium.
  • Conflict theory — society as an arena of competing interests and inequalities.
  • Symbolic interactionism — social reality constructed through everyday interaction and shared meanings.
  • Feminist and critical approaches — how power, gender, and other hierarchies structure social life.
