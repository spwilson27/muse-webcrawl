David Émile Durkheim (15 April 1858 – 15 November 1917) was a French sociologist. He is widely regarded, with Karl Marx and Max Weber, as one of the founding figures of sociology, and he formally established the discipline as an academic subject in France, founding the first European department of sociology at the University of Bordeaux in 1895 and the journal L'Année sociologique in 1898.

Major works

In The Division of Labour in Society (1893), Durkheim argued that social cohesion in traditional societies rests on "mechanical solidarity" — shared beliefs and sentiments — while modern societies are held together by "organic solidarity", the interdependence created by specialisation. The Rules of Sociological Method (1895) proposed that "social facts" be treated as things: external to the individual and exercising constraint.

Suicide (1897), his landmark empirical study, showed that suicide rates vary systematically with the degree of social integration and regulation, distinguishing egoistic, altruistic, anomic, and fatalistic types. In The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life (1912), based on studies of Australian Aboriginal religion, he argued that religion is society worshipping itself, and developed the concepts of the sacred and the profane and of collective effervescence.

Education and influence

Durkheim moved to Paris in 1902, taking a chair at the Sorbonne where he lectured on education and sociology. His work shaped structural functionalism, anthropology (through his nephew Marcel Mauss), and theories of anomie and collective consciousness.
