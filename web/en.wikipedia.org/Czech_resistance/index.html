Czech resistance

The crypt of the Church of Saints Cyril and Methodius, where the Anthropoid parachutists died in June 1942.

The Czech resistance (Czech: český odboj) was the resistance movement against the Nazi German occupation of the Czech lands — the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia — from 1939 to 1945. It operated in two wings: a domestic underground inside the Protectorate and an external resistance centred on the Czechoslovak government-in-exile of Edvard Beneš in London, recognised by the Allies as a co-belligerent government.

Domestic resistance

Within weeks of the March 1939 occupation, officers, politicians, academics and Sokol members formed networks such as Obrana národa (Defence of the Nation, built by army officers), the Political Centre, and the Petition Committee "We Remain Faithful"; these united from 1940 in the Central Leadership of Home Resistance (ÚVOD). The underground gathered intelligence — most famously through the agent A-54, Paul Thümmel — maintained radio contact with London, published illegal journals such as V boj, and organised small-scale sabotage. Gestapo infiltration repeatedly shattered the networks; mass arrests after 1941 and again after the Heydrich assassination left the domestic resistance largely destroyed by 1943, though it revived in the war's final months.

Operation Anthropoid

The resistance's most famous act was Operation Anthropoid: on 27 May 1942 the Czechoslovak soldiers Jozef Gabčík and Jan Kubiš, trained in Britain and parachuted in, attacked Reinhard Heydrich, the acting Reich Protector and a principal architect of the Holocaust, who died of his wounds on 4 June. The Nazi reprisals were savage: the villages of Lidice and Ležáky were annihilated, thousands were executed in the "Heydrichiáda", and the parachutists died in a last stand at the Church of Saints Cyril and Methodius in Prague. The assassination nonetheless achieved its strategic purpose — Britain and France repudiated the Munich Agreement, strengthening the exile government's claim to restore Czechoslovakia.

External resistance and liberation

Abroad, Czechoslovak airmen fought in the RAF (including Nos. 310–313 Squadrons), and ground forces served in North Africa, on the Eastern Front and at the siege of Dunkirk. Parachute operations from Britain — Silver A, Silver B, Out Distance and dozens of later missions — supported the home networks. In May 1945, as the war ended, Prague rose in uprising (5–9 May), the Czech National Council negotiated the German withdrawal, and Soviet and American forces liberated the country.
