Cell migration

Cell migration is a central process in the development and maintenance of multicellular organisms. Tissue formation during embryonic development, wound healing and immune responses all require the orchestrated movement of cells in particular directions to specific locations. Cells often migrate in response to specific external signals, including chemical signals and mechanical signals. Errors during this process have serious consequences, including intellectual disability, vascular disease, tumor formation and metastasis. An understanding of the mechanism by which cells migrate may lead to the development of novel therapeutic strategies for controlling, for example, invasive tumour cells.

Mechanism

Migration of a cell over a substrate can be described as a cycle of four steps. First, the cell polarises and extends protrusions — broad sheet-like lamellipodia or spike-like filopodia — at its leading edge, driven by polymerisation of actin filaments orchestrated by small GTPases of the Rho family (Rac, Cdc42 and RhoA). Second, protrusions are stabilised by adhesions to the extracellular matrix or neighbouring cells, mediated largely by integrin receptors clustered into focal adhesions, which link the matrix to the actin cytoskeleton. Third, actomyosin contraction, powered by myosin II, translocates the cell body forward. Fourth, adhesions at the rear disassemble and the trailing edge retracts, sometimes leaving behind integrin-containing "footprints".

Modes of migration

Cells may move individually or collectively. Amoeboid migration, used by leukocytes and some tumour cells, involves rounded, weakly adherent cells that squeeze through gaps in the matrix. Mesenchymal migration is slower, strongly adhesion-dependent and often involves proteolytic remodelling of the matrix by matrix metalloproteinases. In collective migration, sheets or strands of cells move together while maintaining cell–cell junctions, as in wound closure and the migration of the lateral line primordium in fish. During development, cells of the neural crest undertake long-range collective and individual migrations.

Guidance cues

Directed migration toward a chemical attractant is called chemotaxis. Chemoattractants include growth factors, cytokines such as CXCL12 (SDF-1), and bacterial formyl peptides sensed by neutrophils. Cells can also follow gradients of substrate stiffness (durotaxis), of immobilised adhesive ligands (haptotaxis), of electrical potential (electrotaxis or galvanotaxis), and of fluid flow (rheotaxis). Guidance receptors integrate these cues and bias protrusion toward the signal source.

Cell migration in health and disease

During embryogenesis, migration builds the three germ layers at gastrulation and populates distant organs with neurons, pigment cells and primordial germ cells. In adults, immune cells migrate continuously: neutrophils are rapidly recruited to sites of infection, and lymphocytes patrol lymphoid tissues. Fibroblasts and keratinocytes migrate into wounds to close them. Defective migration contributes to chronic non-healing wounds, autoimmune and inflammatory disease, and congenital disorders such as lissencephaly. In cancer, acquisition of migratory and invasive behaviour underlies metastasis, the main cause of cancer mortality.

Studies of cell migration

In vitro, migration is studied with scratch (wound-healing) assays, Transwell/Boyden chamber assays and microfluidic gradient chambers, as well as by time-lapse microscopy of cells on two-dimensional substrates or embedded in three-dimensional collagen gels. Genetically tractable organisms such as the slime mould Dictyostelium discoideum, the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans (in which migration of the distal tip cells and sex myoblasts is well characterised) and the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster (border cell migration in the ovary) have been influential models.
