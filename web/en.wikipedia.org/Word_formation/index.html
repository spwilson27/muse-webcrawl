Word formation

In linguistics, word formation is the creation of a new word, often by combining existing words or altering them. It is the branch of morphology concerned with the processes by which a language expands its vocabulary, including derivation, compounding, blending, clipping, acronym formation, back-formation, conversion and borrowing.

Principal processes of word formation

Processes

Derivation adds affixes to a root, as in un- + happy or teach + -er. Compounding joins two free morphemes: blackbird, toothpaste. Blending merges parts of words, as in brunch (breakfast + lunch) and smog (smoke + fog). Clipping shortens a word, as in flu from influenza and lab from laboratory.

Acronyms form words from initial letters (laser, NATO), while initialisms are pronounced letter by letter (FBI). Back-formation removes a supposed affix to create a new root, as when the verb edit was derived from the older noun editor. Conversion (zero derivation) shifts a word to a new word class without any change in form, such as the noun email becoming a verb.

Productivity

Not all processes are equally productive: in contemporary English, suffixes such as -ness and -ity freely form new nouns, whereas others, such as -th (as in warmth), rarely do. Productivity is a central topic in morphological theory, with scholars debating the roles of analogy, frequency and blocking.

Coinage and borrowing

Coinage creates wholly new roots — rare in everyday language, though brand names such as aspirin and escalator have passed into general use. Borrowing transfers words from other languages, a major source of English vocabulary historically: algebra from Arabic, tsunami from Japanese, karaoke likewise Japanese.
