Diacritic

A diacritic (also diacritical mark, diacritical point, diacritical sign, or accent) is a glyph added to a letter or to a basic glyph. The term derives from the Greek διακριτικός (diakritikós, "distinguishing"), from διακρίνω (diakrī́nō, "to distinguish"). A diacritic changes the sound value of the letter it is added to, or else distinguishes between two words that would otherwise be homographs.

Types

Common diacritics include the acute accent (´), grave accent (`), circumflex (ˆ), tilde (˜), macron (¯), diaeresis/umlaut (¨), cedilla (¸) and ogonek (˛). They may be placed above, below, through, or between letters, and in some writing systems they indicate tone, vowel length, nasalisation or palatalisation.

Use in various languages

Many European languages use diacritics extensively: French uses the acute, grave, circumflex, cedilla and diaeresis; Spanish uses the tilde in ñ and the acute accent; German uses the umlaut; Scandinavian languages treat å, ä, ö and ø as distinct letters. Vietnamese combines multiple diacritics on a single vowel to mark both quality and tone. In English, diacritics survive mainly in loanwords such as café, naïve and résumé.

Transcription and computing

The International Phonetic Alphabet uses diacritics to denote fine phonetic detail. In Unicode, diacritics can be encoded as combining characters following a base letter, in addition to precomposed characters.
