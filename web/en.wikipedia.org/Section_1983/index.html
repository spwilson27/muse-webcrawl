Section 1983


The Civil Rights Act of 1871, source of Section 1983

Section 1983 is a provision of United States law, codified at 42 U.S.C. § 1983, that provides an individual the right to sue state government employees and others acting "under color of" state law for civil rights violations. Section 1983 does not provide civil rights; rather, it provides a remedy for when a person is deprived, under color of state law, of a right guaranteed by the United States Constitution or by federal statute.

History

Section 1983 was enacted as part of the Civil Rights Act of 1871, also known as the Ku Klux Klan Act, during Reconstruction. It was intended to give the federal government a tool to counter state officials' complicity in, or failure to stop, violence against African Americans and Union supporters. The provision lay largely dormant for ninety years until the Supreme Court's decision in Monroe v. Pape (1961) revived it as a remedy for unconstitutional conduct by state officials.

Elements of a claim

To succeed in a Section 1983 action, a plaintiff must prove (1) that a person acting under color of state law (2) deprived the plaintiff of a right secured by the Constitution or laws of the United States. Municipalities are "persons" subject to suit under Monell v. Department of Social Services (1978), but only where the violation results from an official policy or custom; states themselves are not "persons" under the statute.

Qualified immunity

Government officials sued in their individual capacities are generally shielded by qualified immunity unless they violated "clearly established" law, a doctrine developed by the Supreme Court in cases such as Harlow v. Fitzgerald (1982) and refined in later decisions. Qualified immunity has become a major point of public debate in cases involving alleged police misconduct.
