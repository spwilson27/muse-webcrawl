Statistical hypothesis testing

Statistical hypothesis testing is a method of statistical inference used to decide whether the data at hand sufficiently support a particular hypothesis. It typically proceeds by assuming a null hypothesis (H0) — usually a statement of no effect or no difference — computing a test statistic from a sample, and determining the probability of observing results at least as extreme as those obtained if the null hypothesis were true. That probability is the p-value.

The testing process

  1. State the null hypothesis H0 and an alternative hypothesis H1.
  2. Choose a significance level α, commonly 0.05.
  3. Select a test statistic appropriate to the data and design.
  4. Compute the p-value or compare the statistic with a critical value from its sampling distribution under H0.
  5. Reject H0 if the p-value is less than α; otherwise fail to reject it.

Errors

Two kinds of error are possible. A Type I error occurs when a true null hypothesis is rejected; its probability is controlled at α. A Type II error occurs when a false null hypothesis is not rejected; its probability is denoted β, and 1 − β is the statistical power of the test, the probability of correctly detecting a real effect of a given size.

Common tests

  • t-test — compares means, for one sample, paired samples, or two independent samples.
  • Chi-squared test — tests independence in contingency tables or goodness of fit.
  • ANOVA — compares means across three or more groups.
  • Z-test — for large samples or known population variance.
  • Non-parametric alternatives such as the Mann–Whitney U test and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which make fewer distributional assumptions.

History and criticism

The modern framework blends the significance-testing approach of Ronald Fisher with the decision-theoretic formulation of Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson. Critics note widespread misinterpretation of p-values — a p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true — and concerns about publication bias and "p-hacking" have driven interest in confidence intervals, effect sizes, and Bayesian methods. In 2016 the American Statistical Association issued a formal statement on the proper use of p-values.
