Søren Kierkegaard

Søren Aabye Kierkegaard (5 May 1813 – 11 November 1855) was a Danish philosopher, theologian, poet, social critic, and religious author who is widely considered to be the first existentialist philosopher. He wrote critical texts on organized religion, Christendom, morality, ethics, psychology, and the philosophy of religion, displaying a fondness for metaphor, irony, and parables.

Much of his philosophical work deals with the issues of how one lives as a "single individual", giving priority to concrete human reality over abstract thinking and highlighting the importance of personal choice and commitment. He was against literary critics who defined idealist intellectuals and philosophers of his time, and thought that Swedenborg, Hegel, Fichte, Schelling, Schlegel, and Hans Christian Andersen were all "understood" far too quickly by "scholars".

Life

Kierkegaard was born in Copenhagen to a wealthy family. His early life was marked by the melancholy piety of his father, Michael Pedersen Kierkegaard, and by the deaths of several family members. He broke off his engagement to Regine Olsen in 1841, an event that haunted his writings. In 1843 he published Either/Or and Fear and Trembling, works written under pseudonyms that explored the aesthetic, ethical, and religious stages of life. Later works include Philosophical Fragments (1844), The Concept of Anxiety (1844), Concluding Unscientific Postscript (1846), and The Sickness Unto Death (1849).

Thought and legacy

Key Kierkegaardian concepts include the leap of faith, angst, the absurd, authenticity, and "truth as subjectivity". Largely neglected in his lifetime outside Denmark, his work was rediscovered in the early 20th century and profoundly influenced existentialism, modern theology, and postmodern thought.
