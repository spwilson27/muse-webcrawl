Industrial heritage
ScopeRemains of industrial culture of historical, technological, social, or scientific value
Key bodyThe International Committee for the Conservation of the Industrial Heritage (TICCIH)
Key documentNizhny Tagil Charter (2003)

Industrial heritage consists of the remains of industrial culture which are of historical, technological, social, architectural, or scientific value. These remains include buildings and machinery, workshops, mills and factories, mines and sites for processing and refining, warehouses and stores, places where energy is generated, transmitted and used, transport infrastructure, and places used for social activities related to industry such as housing, religious worship, and education.

Emergence of the field

The study and preservation of industrial remains grew out of industrial archaeology, which developed in Britain in the 1950s as deindustrialization threatened the landscapes of the Industrial Revolution. Ironbridge Gorge in Shropshire, often called the birthplace of the Industrial Revolution, became emblematic of the movement and was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1986. The International Committee for the Conservation of the Industrial Heritage (TICCIH), founded in 1973, is the principal international body; its Nizhny Tagil Charter (2003) sets out principles for identifying, recording, and protecting industrial heritage.

World Heritage

UNESCO has inscribed numerous industrial sites, including the Völklingen Ironworks in Germany, Blaenavon Industrial Landscape and the Derwent Valley Mills in the United Kingdom, the Zollverein Coal Mine in Essen, the Iwami Ginzan Silver Mine in Japan, and the Fray Bentos industrial landscape in Uruguay. Industrial landscapes, canals, railways, and workers' housing are increasingly treated as integrated cultural landscapes rather than isolated monuments.

Adaptive reuse

Former factories have been widely converted to museums, housing, offices, and cultural venues—examples include Tate Modern in a former London power station and the factories of Łódź, Poland. Reuse is often promoted as a sustainable alternative to demolition, though it can be complicated by contamination and the sheer scale of industrial structures.
