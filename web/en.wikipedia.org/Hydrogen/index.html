Hydrogen

Hydrogen
Symbol: H; Atomic number: 1
Atomic mass: 1.008
Phase at STP: Colourless, odourless gas (H2)
Melting point: −259.16 °C; Boiling point: −252.88 °C
Discovered: Henry Cavendish (1766); named by Lavoisier

Hydrogen is the chemical element with the symbol H and atomic number 1. It is the lightest and most abundant chemical substance in the universe, constituting roughly 75% of all normal matter by mass. Under standard conditions hydrogen is a colourless, odourless, highly combustible diatomic gas with the formula H2.

Properties

A hydrogen atom consists of a single proton and one electron; the rare isotopes deuterium and tritium have one and two neutrons respectively. Hydrogen burns in oxygen to form water — the origin of its name, from Greek hydro- (water) and -genes (forming). Its spectral lines, described by the Rydberg formula and explained by the Bohr model, were central to the development of quantum mechanics.

Occurrence and production

Stars, including the Sun, fuse hydrogen into helium. On Earth hydrogen occurs mainly in water and organic compounds. Industrially it is produced mainly by steam reforming of natural gas (“grey” hydrogen), with electrolysis of water (“green” hydrogen when powered by renewables) a growing alternative. Global production exceeds 90 million tonnes per year.

Uses

Hydrogen is used in ammonia production for fertiliser, oil refining, methanol synthesis and hydrogenation of fats. It is a rocket fuel and is used in fuel cells that generate electricity with water as the only exhaust product. Hydrogen is proposed as a clean energy carrier for steelmaking, heavy transport and energy storage, though its low density, storage difficulty and flammability pose challenges.

History

Henry Cavendish recognised hydrogen as a distinct substance in 1766, calling it “inflammable air”. Antoine Lavoisier named it and showed water is a compound of hydrogen and oxygen. Liquid hydrogen was first produced by James Dewar in 1898.
