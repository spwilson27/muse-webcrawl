Magna Carta Libertatum ("Great Charter of Freedoms"), usually called Magna Carta, is a royal charter of rights agreed to by King John of England at Runnymede, near Windsor, on 15 June 1215. Drafted by Archbishop Stephen Langton and the English barons, it sought to make peace between the unpopular king and a group of rebel barons, promising protection of church rights, limits on feudal payments, standardized weights and measures, and — most famously — that no free man should be imprisoned or punished except by lawful judgment of his peers or by the law of the land.

Immediate fate

Neither side kept to the charter: Pope Innocent III annulled it within weeks, leading to the First Barons' War. After John's death in 1216, the charter was reissued in modified form by the regency of his young son Henry III, and again in 1225 in exchange for a tax; the 1225 version entered the statute roll under Edward I in 1297 and became part of English law.

Clauses and legacy

Of the original 63 clauses, only a few remain in statute today, but clauses 39 and 40 — on lawful judgment and the promise not to sell, deny or delay justice — evolved into foundational principles of due process and habeas corpus. Largely forgotten in Tudor times, Magna Carta was revived by 17th-century jurists such as Sir Edward Coke as a symbol of liberty against arbitrary power. It influenced the United States Constitution and Bill of Rights, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, and legal systems across the Commonwealth.

Surviving copies

Four exemplifications of the 1215 charter survive: two in the British Library and one each in Lincoln Cathedral and Salisbury Cathedral. The meadow at Runnymede is now marked by memorials, including one erected by the American Bar Association.
