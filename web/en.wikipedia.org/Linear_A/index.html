Linear A is a writing system used by the Minoan civilisation of Crete from about 1800 to 1450 BC. It was the primary script for palace and religious writings of the Minoan culture. Linear A remains undeciphered: the language it records — conventionally called "Minoan" — is unknown, though the values of many signs can be approximated because its successor script, Linear B, adapted most of its signs to write Mycenaean Greek and was deciphered by Michael Ventris in 1952.

Corpus

About 1,400 inscriptions survive, totalling some 7,400 signs — far fewer than for Linear B. Most are short administrative records on clay tablets, many from Hagia Triada, Khania, Knossos and Zakros; others appear on stone offering tables, gold pins, pottery and seals. The tablets record commodities with logograms and totals, using a decimal number system with fractions.

Signs and numerals

The script has roughly 70 syllabic signs in regular use, about 30 further syllabograms, and numerous ideograms representing commodities such as grain, wine, oil, figs and livestock. Its numerals are decimal: vertical strokes for units, horizontal strokes for tens, circles for hundreds and rayed circles for thousands.

Decipherment attempts

Because the underlying language is unknown — proposed identifications include Semitic, Anatolian (Luwian), Greek and pre-Greek Aegean languages — no decipherment has won general acceptance. Many sign values borrowed from Linear B produce pronounceable but unintelligible words. A few sequences are understood from context, such as a recurring formula on libation tables, and personal and place names.
