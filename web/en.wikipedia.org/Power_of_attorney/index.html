Power of attorney

A power of attorney (POA) is a legal authorisation by which one person (the principal or donor) grants another (the agent or attorney-in-fact) the authority to act on the principal's behalf in legal, financial, medical, or other matters. The agent need not be a lawyer; the word "attorney" here retains its older sense of a person appointed to act for another.

Types

  • General power of attorney — broad authority over the principal's affairs, typically ending if the principal becomes incapacitated.
  • Special or limited power of attorney — authority confined to a specified act or period, such as completing a property sale.
  • Durable power of attorney — remains effective (or takes effect) despite the principal's later incapacity; widely used in estate and elder planning.
  • Springing power of attorney — becomes effective only on a defined future event, usually certified incapacity.
  • Healthcare power of attorney — authorises the agent to make medical decisions when the principal cannot.
  • Lasting power of attorney (England and Wales) — a registered instrument, replacing the older enduring power of attorney, covering property and affairs or health and welfare.

Creation and termination

A power of attorney is generally created by a signed written instrument, often witnessed or notarised, and in some jurisdictions registered with a public office before use. The agent owes fiduciary duties of loyalty and care and must act within the granted scope. The authority ends on the principal's death, on revocation by a competent principal, on the occurrence of a stated terminating event, or — for non-durable powers — on the principal's incapacity.

Use and abuse

Powers of attorney are indispensable in commerce, conveyancing, military deployment, and incapacity planning, but their breadth makes them a known vehicle for financial exploitation of the elderly. Safeguards include capacity requirements at execution, registration, accounting duties, and criminal penalties for misuse.
