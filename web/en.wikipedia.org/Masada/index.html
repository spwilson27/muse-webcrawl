Masada (Hebrew: מְצָדָה, "fortress") is an ancient fortification in the Judaean Desert of southern Israel, situated on top of an isolated rock plateau overlooking the Dead Sea. Built as a palace-fortress by Herod the Great between 37 and 31 BC, it became famous as the last stronghold of Jewish rebels during the First Jewish–Roman War, where, according to the historian Josephus, its defenders died by mass suicide in 73 or 74 AD rather than surrender to the Roman army.

Geography and Herodian palace

The plateau rises about 400 metres above the Dead Sea, the lowest place on Earth, and is steep on all sides. Herod fortified the summit with a casemate wall, storerooms, cisterns and two palaces, including the spectacular three-tiered Northern Palace clinging to the cliff face. The site stored enough provisions, and captured enough rainwater in its enormous cisterns, to sustain inhabitants for long sieges.

The siege of Masada

After the fall of Jerusalem in 70 AD, a group of Sicarii led by Eleazar ben Ya'ir held Masada against the Roman Tenth Legion under Flavius Silva. The Romans built a circumvallation wall and a massive assault ramp against the western face, which survives to this day. Josephus relates that when the walls were breached, the Romans found 960 men, women and children dead, with only two women and five children surviving to tell the story. Modern scholarship debates the accuracy of Josephus's account, but the siege became a powerful symbol of resistance.

Excavation and today

Masada was excavated extensively between 1963 and 1965 by Yigael Yadin, uncovering palaces, synagogues, ritual baths, scrolls and the remains of the Roman siege works — the best-preserved Roman siege system anywhere. It is now one of Israel's most popular tourist attractions and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, accessible by cable car or the winding Snake Path.
