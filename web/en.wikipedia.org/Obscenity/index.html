Obscenity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Obscenity refers to material — words, images or acts — that offends prevailing standards of decency, typically in sexual matters, and which in many legal systems may be restricted or prohibited. The concept is notoriously hard to define: United States Supreme Court Justice Potter Stewart, in a 1964 concurrence, wrote of hard-core pornography, "I know it when I see it."

Legal treatment

In the United States, obscenity is not protected by the First Amendment. The governing standard is the Miller test (from Miller v. California, 1973), which asks whether the average person, applying contemporary community standards, would find that the work appeals to the prurient interest; whether it depicts sexual conduct in a patently offensive way; and whether it lacks serious literary, artistic, political or scientific value. In the United Kingdom, the Obscene Publications Act 1959 criminalises material tending to "deprave and corrupt" its audience. Other jurisdictions regulate obscene material through customs, broadcasting and internet law, with widely varying thresholds.

History

Modern obscenity law developed in the 19th century, with prosecutions of works by authors such as Flaubert and Baudelaire, and landmark 20th-century trials over Ulysses and Lady Chatterley's Lover that progressively liberalised standards in much of the West.
