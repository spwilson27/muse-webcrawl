Radiation hormesis

Hypothesised dose–response models: the linear no-threshold model (top) versus a hormetic model in which low doses reduce risk below background (bottom).

Radiation hormesis is the hypothesis that low doses of ionizing radiation (within the region of and just above background levels) are beneficial, stimulating the activation of repair mechanisms that protect against disease, whilst being harmful at high doses. The phenomenon is a special case of hormesis, the general observation that many substances have biphasic dose responses.

The hypothesis stands in contrast to the linear no-threshold (LNT) model used by most regulators, which holds that risk increases linearly with dose down to zero. Radiation hormesis remains controversial and is not accepted by major scientific bodies such as the US National Academies' BEIR committees or the International Commission on Radiological Protection, which continue to endorse LNT for radiation protection purposes.

Evidence and arguments

Proponents cite laboratory studies in which low-dose exposure stimulated DNA repair, antioxidant defences and immune responses in cells and animals, as well as epidemiological observations — such as studies of populations in high natural-background areas like Ramsar, Iran, and Kerala, India — that do not clearly show elevated cancer rates. They also point to the 2005 report of the French Academy of Sciences, which questioned the validity of LNT below about 100 mSv.

Critics respond that the epidemiological studies lack the statistical power or controls to detect small effects, that cellular adaptive responses do not necessarily translate into reduced cancer risk in whole organisms, and that the atomic-bomb survivor data are broadly consistent with linearity at low doses. For protection policy, regulators argue LNT is a prudent basis even if the true low-dose risk is somewhat lower. The debate continues in the literature, with major reviews concluding that the question of harm or benefit at doses below roughly 100 mSv cannot be resolved definitively with present data.
