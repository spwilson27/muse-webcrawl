The Kanak (or Kanaks, formerly spelled Canaque) are the indigenous Melanesian inhabitants of New Caledonia, an overseas collectivity of France in the southwest Pacific. They form the territory's largest ethnic group and descend from the Lapita cultures that settled the archipelago some 3,000 years ago.

History

France annexed New Caledonia in 1853, and colonization brought land confiscation, confinement to reserves, and devastating epidemics; the Kanak population fell sharply by the early twentieth century. Resistance included the revolt of chief Ataï in 1878. In the 1970s and 1980s a Kanak independence movement, led by figures such as Jean-Marie Tjibaou and organized in the FLNKS, pressed for self-determination, culminating in the Matignon Agreements (1988) and the Nouméa Accord (1998), which set a path of gradual transfer of powers and independence referendums.

Culture

Kanak society is organized around clans and customary chiefdoms, with social life governed by la coutume (custom). The Kanak speak around 28 distinct languages of the Oceanic family. Distinctive arts include carved wooden sculptures, ceremonial spears (flèches faîtières), and yam-centered agricultural ritual. The Jean-Marie Tjibaou Cultural Centre in Nouméa, designed by Renzo Piano, is a prominent symbol of Kanak cultural revival.
