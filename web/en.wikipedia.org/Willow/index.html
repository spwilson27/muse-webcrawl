Willow

Willows, also called sallows and osiers, form the genus Salix, comprising around 350 species of typically deciduous trees and shrubs found primarily on moist soils in cold and temperate regions. Most species are known as willow, but some narrow-leaved shrub species are called osier, and broader-leaved species are referred to as sallow.

Description

Willows have watery, bark-rich sap protected by thick scales of salicylic acid, soft and usually pliant wood, tough roots, and slender branches. Leaves are typically elongated, often with serrated margins, and the inconspicuous flowers are borne in catkins in early spring. Many species, such as the weeping willow (Salix × sepulcralis), are widely planted as ornamentals.

Uses

Willow bark has been used since antiquity as a pain reliever; its active compound, salicin, led to the development of aspirin. Flexible willow stems (withies) are woven into baskets and wickerwork, and willows are used in bioengineering for erosion control and in cricket bat manufacture (Salix alba 'Caerulea').
