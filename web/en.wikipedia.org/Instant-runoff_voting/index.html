Instant-runoff voting

Instant-runoff voting (IRV), also known as ranked-choice voting in the United States and as the alternative vote in the United Kingdom, is a single-winner electoral system in which voters rank candidates in order of preference. If no candidate receives a majority of first-preference votes, the candidate with the fewest votes is eliminated and those ballots are transferred to the next-ranked continuing candidate. The process repeats until one candidate holds a majority of the ballots remaining in play.

Use

IRV is used for national elections in Australia (House of Representatives) and Ireland (presidential elections), in Papua New Guinea, and in a growing number of U.S. cities and states, including New York City primaries and statewide elections in Maine and Alaska (the latter in a variant combined with a nonpartisan primary). Political parties and private organisations also use it for internal elections.

Advantages claimed

Supporters argue that IRV elects winners with broad support, reduces the "spoiler effect" of similar candidates splitting the vote, discourages negative campaigning (since candidates seek second preferences), and removes the need for separate runoff elections, saving cost.

Criticism

Critics note that IRV is non-monotonic — ranking a candidate higher can, in rare configurations, cause them to lose — and that it can fail to elect a Condorcet winner. Ballot exhaustion (ballots ranking no continuing candidate) means the final "majority" may be a majority of continuing ballots only. Counting is also more complex than plurality voting, though it is routinely administered in large jurisdictions.
