Chancellor of the Exchequer

The Chancellor of the Exchequer is the second-most powerful person in the British government, after the prime minister, and is responsible for all economic and financial matters in Britain. The Chancellor is the head of His Majesty's Treasury, and is usually considered one of the four great offices of state. The position is often referred to informally as simply "the chancellor," and its holder is a member of the Cabinet.

The Chancellor's official residence is 11 Downing Street in London, adjacent to the prime minister's residence at Number 10. The Chancellor presents the annual Budget statement to the House of Commons, setting out the government's plans for taxation and public spending.

History

The office originated in the medieval Exchequer, the department responsible for receiving and accounting for royal revenues; its name derives from the chequered cloth used to count money. The Chancellor of the Exchequer emerged as the under-treasurer's deputy in the early 16th century and grew in importance as Parliament gained control over supply. By the 19th century the Chancellor had become the government's chief financial officer and the minister responsible for the Budget. Notable holders include William Pitt the Younger, William Ewart Gladstone—who served four times and delivered landmark budgets—and Winston Churchill, whose controversial 1925 budget returned Britain to the gold standard.

Responsibilities

The Chancellor is in charge of HM Treasury and controls public spending, sets the overall direction of fiscal policy, and oversees taxation, public sector pay, and the government's relationship with the Bank of England, which has set interest rates independently since 1997. The Chancellor appoints members of the Bank's Monetary Policy Committee and agrees the Bank's remit. The role also carries responsibility for the government's borrowing and debt management strategy and for financial services regulation.

The Budget

The annual Budget, traditionally accompanied by the red dispatch box first used by Gladstone, is a central event in the parliamentary calendar. The Chancellor also delivers an Autumn Statement or autumn budget updating forecasts from the Office for Budget Responsibility. By convention, the Chancellor may drink alcohol while delivering the budget speech—the only occasion on which alcohol is permitted in the Commons chamber.

Chancellors are occasionally referred to by the name of the post they held at the Exchequer in former centuries. The longest-serving Chancellor of the 20th century was David Lloyd George; the office has historically been a stepping stone to the premiership, though no Chancellor has moved directly to Number 10 in recent decades except Gordon Brown in 2007, who had served a decade in post.
