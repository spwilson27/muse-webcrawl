Dyirbal is an Aboriginal Australian language spoken in north-east Queensland by the Dyirbal people. It is a member of the Pama–Nyungan language family. Dyirbal became famous in linguistics through R. M. W. Dixon's 1972 grammar, and through its remarkable system of noun classes and its special "mother-in-law language".

Noun classes

Dyirbal classifies nouns into four classes, often illustrated by the bayyi/balan/balam/bala distinction: (I) most animate objects and men; (II) women, water, fire, and dangerous things; (III) edible fruit and vegetables; and (IV) a residue miscellaneous class. George Lakoff's book Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things (1987) took its title from this system.

Mother-in-law language

Dyirbal speakers historically used a special avoidance register, Jalnguy, in the presence of taboo relatives such as a mother-in-law. Jalnguy shared Dyirbal grammar and phonology but used an entirely separate vocabulary, with roughly one word for every four in the everyday style (Guwal).

Ergativity

Dyirbal is a classic example of a morphologically ergative language, and it also displays split ergativity: nouns follow an ergative–absolutive pattern while pronouns follow a nominative–accusative pattern, a finding that influenced theories of case alignment.

Decline

Following colonization, Dyirbal transmission to children declined sharply during the twentieth century, and the language is now critically endangered, though documentation and revitalization efforts have drawn on Dixon's extensive recordings.
