Copula (statistics)

In probability theory and statistics, a copula (plural copulae or copulas) is a multivariate cumulative distribution function for which the marginal probability distribution of each variable is uniform on the interval [0, 1]. Copulas are used to describe and model the dependence (correlation) between random variables. Their name, introduced by Abe Sklar in 1959, comes from the Latin for "link" or "tie", similar in meaning to the grammatical copulas in linguistics.

Sklar's theorem

Sklar's theorem states that any multivariate joint distribution can be written in terms of univariate marginal distribution functions and a copula which describes the dependence structure between the variables. Copulas are popular in high-dimensional statistical applications because they allow one to easily model and estimate the distribution of random vectors by estimating marginals and copulas separately. There are many parametric copula families available, including Gaussian, Student's t, Clayton, Gumbel and Frank copulas, which differ in their ability to represent tail dependence and asymmetry.

Applications

Copulas are widely used in quantitative finance to model and minimise tail risk and for portfolio-optimisation applications. The use of a Gaussian copula by David X. Li to price collateralized debt obligations became controversial after the 2008 financial crisis, because it underestimated the joint risk of default; the model's simplicity nonetheless had made it a market standard for quickly assessing correlated default risk. Copulas are also applied in hydrology, actuarial science, reliability engineering, and biomedical studies of event dependence.
