The Suez Canal (Arabic: قناة السويس, Qanāt as-Suways) is an artificial sea-level waterway in Egypt, connecting the Mediterranean Sea to the Red Sea through the Isthmus of Suez. It divides Africa and Asia, and provides the shortest maritime route between Europe and the lands around the Indian and western Pacific oceans. Opened in 1869, it is one of the most heavily used shipping lanes in the world; roughly 12 percent of global trade passes through it.

History

Ancient canals linking the Nile to the Red Sea were dug under pharaohs including Senusret III and Necho II, and later completed by Darius I of Persia and maintained under the Ptolemies and Romans. The modern canal was promoted by the French diplomat Ferdinand de Lesseps, who obtained a concession from Sa'id Pasha of Egypt in 1854. The Suez Canal Company began construction in 1859, relying heavily on Egyptian corvée labour, and the canal opened to navigation on 17 November 1869.

In 1875 the British government of Benjamin Disraeli bought Egypt's shares, giving Britain a major stake; after the Anglo-Egyptian War of 1882 Britain controlled the canal zone. The Convention of Constantinople (1888) declared the canal neutral and open to all ships in war and peace. On 26 July 1956 President Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalised the canal, precipitating the Suez Crisis, in which Britain, France and Israel invaded Egypt; they withdrew under international pressure. The canal was closed by the Six-Day War of 1967 and did not reopen until 1975 under Anwar Sadat.

Layout and capacity

The canal extends from Port Said on the Mediterranean to Suez on the Red Sea, passing through Lake Timsah and the Great Bitter Lake. It has no locks, since the two seas are at nearly the same level. Originally a single-lane waterway with passing bays, it was substantially expanded by the "New Suez Canal" project opened in 2015, which added a parallel channel over part of the route and deepened the existing channel, allowing two-way traffic in sections and larger vessels.

Economic importance and incidents

Tolls paid by transiting ships are a major source of revenue for Egypt, earning on the order of several billion US dollars per year. In March 2021 the container ship Ever Given ran aground and blocked the canal for six days, disrupting global supply chains before being refloated. Since late 2023, attacks on shipping in the Red Sea associated with the Houthi movement have led many carriers to divert around the Cape of Good Hope, sharply reducing canal traffic and revenue.
