Immunosuppressive drug

Immunosuppressive drugs, also known as immunosuppressive agents, immunosuppressants and antirejection medications, are drugs that inhibit or prevent activity of the immune system. They are used in immunosuppressive therapy to prevent the rejection of transplanted organs and tissues, to treat autoimmune diseases, and to treat some non-autoimmune inflammatory diseases.

Classification

Immunosuppressive drugs can be classified into five groups:

  • Glucocorticoids — such as prednisone, which suppress inflammation and immune responses through multiple mechanisms.
  • Cytostatics — such as azathioprine and methotrexate, which inhibit cell division, including that of rapidly dividing lymphocytes.
  • Antibodies — polyclonal and monoclonal antibodies directed against T cells or their receptors, such as basiliximab and anti-thymocyte globulin.
  • Drugs acting on immunophilins — calcineurin inhibitors such as ciclosporin and tacrolimus, which block T-cell activation.
  • Other drugs — including mTOR inhibitors such as sirolimus and everolimus, and mycophenolate.

Uses

Immunosuppressive therapy is the mainstay of successful organ transplantation; after transplant surgery, recipients typically take a combination ("triple therapy") of a calcineurin inhibitor, an antimetabolite, and a glucocorticoid, often for life. Immunosuppressants are also used to treat autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, psoriasis, and lupus.

Adverse effects

Because most immunosuppressive drugs act non-selectively, the immune system loses its ability to resist infections and to control the spread of malignant cells. Common side effects include hypertension, dyslipidemia, hyperglycemia, nephrotoxicity, and increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections. Long-term use increases the risk of certain cancers, particularly skin cancers and lymphoma.
