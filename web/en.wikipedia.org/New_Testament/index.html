The New Testament (NT) is the second division of the Christian biblical canon. It discusses the teachings and person of Jesus, as well as events relating to first-century Christianity. The New Testament's background, the first division of the Christian Bible, is called the Old Testament, which is based primarily upon the Hebrew Bible; together they are regarded as sacred scripture by Christians.

The New Testament consists of 27 books, all originally written in Koine Greek: the four canonical Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John), which narrate the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus; the Acts of the Apostles, describing the early church; twenty-one epistles (letters), most attributed to Paul the Apostle, addressing early Christian communities and individuals; and the Book of Revelation, an apocalyptic prophecy.

Development of the canon

The earliest New Testament writings are Paul's letters, dating from about AD 50. The Gospels are generally dated between AD 66 and 110. Over the following centuries the church debated which writings were authoritative; the Muratorian fragment (late 2nd century) lists most of the present books, and Athanasius's Easter letter of 367 is the first surviving list of exactly the 27 books of the modern canon, which was ratified by regional councils at Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) and, much later, affirmed by the Council of Trent for Roman Catholics. The oldest near-complete manuscripts are the great 4th-century codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus.

Influence

The New Testament is the foundational text of Christianity and among the most influential writings in world history, shaping theology, ethics, law, art, and literature. Portions such as the Sermon on the Mount and the Parables of Jesus are among the most widely read texts ever written, and the book as a whole is the most translated and distributed text in history.
