Offshoring

Offshoring
DefinitionRelocation of a business process or production to a foreign country
Main motivesLower labour costs, access to skills, tax and regulatory advantages, proximity to markets
Major destinationsChina, India, Vietnam, Mexico, the Philippines, Eastern Europe
Related termsOutsourcing, nearshoring, reshoring, friendshoring

Offshoring is the relocation of a business process — such as manufacturing, customer support, software development or back-office services — from one country to another, typically to reduce costs or gain access to skills and markets. Unlike outsourcing, which describes contracting work to a third party, offshoring describes a geographic move and can occur within the same company, through a wholly owned subsidiary ("captive" operation) or through a foreign contractor (offshore outsourcing).

History

Large-scale offshoring accelerated from the 1960s and 1970s as multinational manufacturers moved production to low-wage economies in East and Southeast Asia, encouraged by export-processing zones and falling transport costs. The spread of fibre-optic telecommunications and the internet in the 1990s extended offshoring to services: India became a major centre for software and IT services, and the Philippines for English-language call centres. China's accession to the WTO in 2001 made it the "world's factory" for manufactures.

Advantages and criticisms

Companies cite labour-cost savings, large pools of skilled workers, round-the-clock "follow-the-sun" workflows and incentives from host governments. Offshoring can raise living standards and employment in destination countries. Critics argue it displaces workers in home countries, suppresses wages, creates supply-chain fragility, and can involve poor labour and environmental standards abroad. Studies of the "China shock" linked rapid import growth after 2000 to persistent manufacturing job losses in affected regions of the United States.

Nearshoring and reshoring

Variations include nearshoring — moving work to a nearby country sharing language, culture or time zones (for example, U.S. firms using Mexico or Canada, or Western European firms using Eastern Europe) — and reshoring (or onshoring), bringing production back home. Since the late 2010s, trade tensions, the COVID-19 pandemic and geopolitical risk have pushed governments and firms toward "friendshoring" of critical supply chains such as semiconductors and pharmaceuticals to politically allied countries.
