Trojan horse (computing)

Named after the wooden horse of Greek myth

In computing, a Trojan horse (or simply a Trojan) is malware that misleads users of its true intent by disguising itself as a legitimate program. The term is derived from the Ancient Greek story of the deceptive Trojan Horse that led to the fall of the city of Troy.

Behaviour

Unlike computer viruses and worms, Trojans generally do not attempt to inject themselves into other files or propagate across networks on their own. Instead, they are spread by social engineering — a user is duped into downloading and running an apparently useful or entertaining program, an email attachment, or a fake software update. Once executed, the Trojan performs malicious actions, which may include:

  • installing a backdoor giving an attacker remote control of the machine (a remote access Trojan, or RAT);
  • recruiting the computer into a botnet for spam or denial-of-service attacks;
  • stealing passwords, banking credentials, and other personal data, often through keystroke logging;
  • downloading further malware, including ransomware;
  • deleting, modifying, or encrypting the victim's files.

History

The concept was described in a 1974 U.S. Air Force report on vulnerabilities in the Multics operating system, and early examples appeared on personal computers in the 1980s. Famous Trojans include the banking Trojans Zeus and Emotet, which spread through malicious email attachments, and early remote-access tools such as Back Orifice (1998) and Sub7, which blurred the line between administration software and malware.

Defence

Defences against Trojans include installing software only from trusted sources, keeping systems patched, using reputable anti-malware software, and treating unexpected attachments and downloads with suspicion. Because a Trojan depends on deceiving the user, security awareness is a primary line of defence.
