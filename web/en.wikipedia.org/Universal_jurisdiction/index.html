Universal jurisdiction

Universal jurisdiction is a principle in international law allowing a national court to try a person suspected of certain grave crimes regardless of where the crime was committed, the nationality of the perpetrator or victim, or any other connection to the prosecuting state. It rests on the idea that some crimes — such as genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, piracy and torture — are so serious that they injure the international community as a whole, and any state may bring the perpetrator to justice.

Historical development

The classical example is piracy on the high seas, long treated as a crime against all nations (hostis humani generis). After the Second World War, the Geneva Conventions of 1949 required states to search for and prosecute or extradite persons accused of grave breaches. The principle was famously asserted in the 1961 Jerusalem trial of Adolf Eichmann. Later, the 1984 Convention Against Torture obliged states parties to prosecute or extradite alleged torturers found in their territory.

Notable cases

In 1998, former Chilean ruler Augusto Pinochet was arrested in London on a Spanish warrant alleging torture, though he was ultimately released on health grounds. Belgium's broad universal jurisdiction law of 1993 generated diplomatic controversy and was narrowed in 2003. German courts have convicted Syrian officials of crimes against humanity under universal jurisdiction, including the 2022 Koblenz trial of Anwar Raslan. Senegal tried former Chadian president Hissène Habré in 2016 under a mandate from the African Union.

Criticism and limits

Critics argue that universal jurisdiction can be politicised, applied selectively, and strain diplomatic relations; defences such as head-of-state immunity have limited some prosecutions. The International Court of Justice in the 2002 Arrest Warrant case held that incumbent foreign ministers enjoy immunity from foreign criminal jurisdiction. Supporters respond that the principle closes impunity gaps where territorial states are unwilling or unable to prosecute.
