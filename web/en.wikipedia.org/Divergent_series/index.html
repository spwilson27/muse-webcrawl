Divergent series

In mathematics, a divergent series is an infinite series that is not convergent, meaning that the infinite sequence of the partial sums of the series does not have a finite limit. If a series is convergent, the individual terms of the series must approach zero. Thus any series in which the individual terms do not approach zero diverges. The classic example is the harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + ⋯, whose terms approach zero yet whose sum grows without bound.

History

Divergent series were used freely by mathematicians such as Leonhard Euler in the 18th century, who obtained correct results by assigning them values through formal manipulation. In the 19th century, stricter standards of rigour led Augustin-Louis Cauchy and Niels Henrik Abel to reject such methods; Abel called divergent series "an invention of the devil". Work by Ernesto Cesàro, Émile Borel and others from the 1890s onwards put the summation of divergent series on a rigorous footing.

Summability methods

A summability method is a way of assigning a sum to certain divergent series that extends the usual notion of convergence and agrees with it for convergent series. Cesàro summation takes the average of the partial sums and assigns the series 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + ⋯ the value 1/2. Abel summation considers the limit of a related power series. Ramanujan summation and zeta function regularisation assign the series 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + ⋯ the value −1/12, a result that appears in physics, for example in string theory and the Casimir effect.

Properties

Useful summability methods are typically required to be regular (agreeing with ordinary sums), linear, and stable. Theorems such as Hardy's tauberian theorems relate summability to convergence under additional conditions on the terms of the series.
