Abstract expressionism

Abstract expressionism is a post–World War II art movement in American painting, centred in New York City in the 1940s and 1950s, that made New York the new focus of the western art world. Its artists, often called the New York School, produced large-scale abstract works characterised either by vigorous gestural brushwork — "action painting" — or by expansive areas of flat colour — "colour field painting". The term was applied to American art by critic Robert Coates in 1946, having earlier been used of Kandinsky's work.

Artists and ideas

Leading figures include Jackson Pollock, famous for his poured and dripped paintings made with the canvas on the floor; Willem de Kooning, whose slashing brushwork produced the Woman series; Mark Rothko, whose floating rectangles of colour aimed at enveloping, quasi-religious experience; Barnett Newman, known for his "zip" paintings; and Clyfford Still, Franz Kline, Arshile Gorky, Helen Frankenthaler and Lee Krasner. Influenced by Surrealist automatism, Jungian archetypes and wartime existentialism, the painters prized spontaneity, scale and the physical act of painting, which Harold Rosenberg described as an "arena" of existential action. Critic Clement Greenberg championed the movement as the culmination of modernist painting's drive toward flatness.

Legacy

Abstract expressionism dominated advanced art through the 1950s and was promoted internationally during the Cold War — a fact that has fuelled debate about the role of institutions such as the CIA in its export as a symbol of American freedom. It was succeeded and reacted against by Pop art and Minimalism, but its emphasis on process and monumentality continues to shape contemporary painting.
