Penrose triangle


The Penrose triangle

The Penrose triangle, also known as the Penrose tribar, impossible triangle, or impossible trinity, is an optical illusion and classic "impossible object": a triangular arrangement of three straight beams of square cross-section that appears to form a solid, three-dimensional object, yet cannot exist as a consistent solid in ordinary Euclidean space. Each corner looks locally plausible, but the whole is geometrically contradictory.

History

The figure was independently created by the Swedish artist Oscar Reutersvärd in 1934 and devised again in the 1950s by the British scientist Lionel Penrose and his son, the mathematician Roger Penrose, who published it in a 1959 article in the British Journal of Psychology alongside the Penrose stairs. The Dutch artist M. C. Escher popularised the family of impossible objects, using a variant in his lithograph Waterfall (1961) and the endless staircase in Ascending and Descending (1960).

Real-world constructions

Physical sculptures that appear to be Penrose triangles from one special viewpoint exist in several places, including Gotschuchen in Austria, Gotschuchen-style installations in Australia (Giles, Western Australia) and elsewhere: they are actually twisted or broken structures whose illusion resolves only from the intended camera position. The tribar has become a widely used symbol of impossibility in art, design, and paradox-themed logos.
