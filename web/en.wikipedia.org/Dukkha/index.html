Duḥkha (Sanskrit: दुःख; Pali: dukkha) is a fundamental concept in Indian religions, principally Buddhism, often translated as "suffering", "pain", "unsatisfactoriness" or "unease". It refers to the inherent unsatisfactoriness and painfulness of mundane life. In Buddhism, dukkha is the subject of the First Noble Truth (dukkha sacca) and one of the three marks of existence, along with impermanence (anicca) and non-self (anattā).

Meaning

The Buddha's first sermon, the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, states: "Birth is dukkha, ageing is dukkha, sickness is dukkha, death is dukkha; union with what is displeasing is dukkha; separation from what is pleasing is dukkha; not to get what one wants is dukkha; in brief, the five aggregates subject to clinging are dukkha." Commentators distinguish three levels: ordinary suffering (pain, illness, loss), the suffering of change (pleasant experiences are impermanent), and the all-pervasive suffering of conditioned existence itself.

Etymology

The word is often analysed as du- ("bad, difficult") plus kha ("hole", as in the hole for an axle in a wheel), suggesting a wheel out of true — an image matching the Buddha's metaphor of setting the wheel of Dhamma in motion. Scholars note the literal etymology is uncertain, and that no single English word captures the term's range.

Role in Buddhist thought

Dukkha arises from craving (taṇhā) conditioned by ignorance, as set out in the Four Noble Truths and the chain of dependent origination. Full understanding of dukkha — the task assigned to the First Truth — is the first step toward its cessation in nirvana. The concept also appears, with different emphases, in Hindu and Jain texts.
