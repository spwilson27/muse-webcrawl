Genovese crime family

The Genovese crime family, also sometimes referred to as the Westside, is an Italian-American Mafia crime family and one of the "Five Families" that dominate organized crime activities in New York City and New Jersey as part of the American Mafia. The Genovese family has generally maintained a varying degree of influence over many of the smaller mob families outside New York, including ties with the Philadelphia, Patriarca, and Buffalo crime families.

The modern family was founded by Lucky Luciano and takes its name from boss Vito Genovese. It is widely considered the most powerful and sophisticated of the Five Families, and law enforcement has described its leadership as unusually secretive; for decades the family used a "front boss" to draw attention away from its actual leadership.

History

The organization traces its origins to the Morello crime family and the early twentieth-century New York underworld. After the Castellammarese War (1930–31), Salvatore Maranzano reorganised the New York Mafia into Five Families; the family that became the Genovese family was led first by Lucky Luciano. Luciano's successors included Frank Costello, who was forced out in 1957 after an assassination attempt ordered by Vito Genovese, and Genovese himself, whose 1957 Apalachin meeting ended in a police raid and his subsequent imprisonment.

Under Vincent "the Chin" Gigante, boss from 1981 to 2005, the family reached a new peak of discipline and secrecy; Gigante famously feigned mental illness for decades to deflect prosecution. Despite a series of Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO) prosecutions that convicted much of its administration, the family remained active in labor and construction racketeering, gambling, loan-sharking, and extortion into the twenty-first century.

Organization

Like other American Mafia families, the Genovese family is organized into crews led by caporegimes (captains), each supervising soldiers and associates, under a boss, underboss, and consigliere. The family's ruling panel and the use of acting bosses have complicated law-enforcement efforts to identify its leadership.
