Air mass

In meteorology, an air mass is a large body of air with relatively uniform temperature and humidity at a given altitude, extending over hundreds or thousands of kilometres. Air masses form over source regions — large, uniform surfaces such as oceans, deserts, ice caps or plains — where light winds allow the air to take on the characteristics of the surface below.

Classification

The Bergeron classification describes air masses by source latitude and surface type:

  • c (continental, dry) or m (maritime, moist)
  • A (arctic), P (polar), T (tropical), E (equatorial)

Thus a cP air mass is cold and dry (e.g. winter air from Siberia or northern Canada), an mT air mass warm and moist (e.g. from the Gulf of Mexico), and an mP air mass cool and moist. A third letter sometimes indicates whether the air is colder (k) or warmer (w) than the surface it moves over, which affects stability.

Movement and weather

As an air mass moves away from its source region it is gradually modified by the surfaces it crosses. The boundaries between air masses of different densities are called fronts; the meeting of contrasting air masses at cold, warm, stationary and occluded fronts produces much of the weather of the mid-latitudes, including the development of extratropical cyclones. Air-mass thunderstorms, by contrast, form within a single warm, moist air mass.
