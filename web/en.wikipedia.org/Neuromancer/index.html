Neuromancer is a 1984 science fiction novel by American-Canadian writer William Gibson. It is widely regarded as the founding work of the cyberpunk genre and the first novel in Gibson's Sprawl trilogy. The novel won the Nebula Award, the Philip K. Dick Award, and the Hugo Award—science fiction's informal "triple crown"—and helped popularise concepts such as cyberspace and the matrix of interconnected computer networks before the public advent of the Internet.

Plot

Henry Dorsett Case is a washed-up computer hacker— a "console cowboy"—living in the slums of Chiba City, Japan. After stealing from his employers, Case's nervous system was damaged with a wartime mycotoxin, leaving him unable to interface with the global computer network, the matrix. A mysterious employer, Armitage, offers to cure him in exchange for his services on an impossibly difficult run against a powerful artificial intelligence owned by the Tessier-Ashpool corporation. Aided by the razorgirl Molly Millions and the preserved personality construct of the legendary hacker McCoy "Dixie" Pauley, Case penetrates the defences of the orbital Villa Straylight, where the AI Neuromancer seeks to merge with its sibling, Wintermute.

Themes and style

The novel is known for its dense, impressionistic prose, heavy use of brand names and neologisms, and its vision of a future dominated by multinational corporations, body modification, and computer networks. Its famous opening line compares the sky above the port to "the color of television, tuned to a dead channel". Gibson coined the term "cyberspace" in his 1982 short story "Burning Chrome", and Neuromancer elaborated the concept into a "consensual hallucination" of networked data.

Influence

Neuromancer profoundly influenced science fiction literature, film, and video games, shaping the aesthetic of works such as The Matrix film series and the Deus Ex and Cyberpunk games. It also anticipated the cultural impact of the World Wide Web, virtual reality, and artificial intelligence, and its depiction of cyberspace informed real-world discussions of networked computing.
