App store

[Image placeholder]

An app store (or app marketplace) is a type of digital distribution platform for computer software called applications, often in a mobile context. Apps provide a specific set of functions which, by definition, do not include the running of the computer itself. App stores typically take the form of an online store where users can browse, review and download applications, which may be free or paid, with the store operator usually taking a commission on sales and curating submissions for quality and security.

History

Early precursors included Handango and the Nokia Download! service, and the 2005 launch of Linux package repositories anticipated centralised software distribution. The concept was popularised by Apple's App Store, launched on 10 July 2008 for the iPhone with 500 applications; it passed 10 billion downloads by 2011. Google's Android Market (now Google Play) followed in October 2008, and most platforms — Microsoft, Amazon, Samsung, Huawei and games platforms such as Steam and the Epic Games Store — now operate stores.

Business model and controversies

The standard commission historically was 30% of app and in-app purchase revenue, reduced to 15% for small developers and subscriptions after the first year by Apple and Google in the 2020s. App stores have been criticised as monopolistic gatekeepers: disputes include Apple's removal of Fortnite in 2020 and the ensuing Epic Games v. Apple litigation, antitrust scrutiny in the United States, and the European Union's Digital Markets Act (2022), which requires large platforms to permit alternative app stores and payment systems on mobile devices.
