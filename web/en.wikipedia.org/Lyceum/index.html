Lyceum

The Lyceum (Greek: Λύκειον, Lykeion) was a temple in classical Athens dedicated to Apollo Lyceus ("Apollo the wolf-god"), and best known as the place where the philosopher Aristotle taught from about 335 BC until he fled Athens in 323 BC. The school Aristotle founded there, whose members were called Peripatetics after the covered walkway (peripatos) in which they walked and discussed, was one of the great philosophical institutions of antiquity.

History

The site was a gymnasium and sanctuary outside the city walls to the east, long used for military exercises and philosophical discussion before Aristotle's time; Socrates is said to have spent time there, and it held the shrine of the Muses. Aristotle gathered an extensive library and a collection of manuscripts, maps, and natural history specimens there — sometimes described as the first research institute. After Aristotle, the school was led by Theophrastus and later Strato of Lampsacus; it continued in some form until the Roman general Sulla's sack of Athens in 86 BC.

The remains of the Lyceum, long sought, were discovered in 1996 during excavation for the Museum of Modern Art in Athens, and the archaeological site is now open to the public.

Legacy of the name

The word passed into many languages as a term for schools and lecture institutions: the lycée in France, the Lyzeum in Germany, and the nineteenth-century American lyceum movement of public lectures and adult education, which hosted speakers such as Emerson and Thoreau. In modern Greek usage, lykeio denotes the upper secondary school.
