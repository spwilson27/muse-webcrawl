Mensural notation

Mensural notation is the musical notation system used for European vocal polyphonic music from the later part of the 13th century until about 1600. The term "mensural" refers to the ability of this system to describe precisely measured rhythms with a clear system of durational values, in contrast to the rhythmically freer chant notation that preceded it.

Note values

The basic note shapes, from longest to shortest, were the maxima, longa, breve, semibreve and minim, with smaller values (semiminim, fusa and semifusa) added later. Unlike modern notation, the duration of a note depended on context: whether time (tempus) and prolation were "perfect" (threefold division) or "imperfect" (twofold) determined whether a breve contained three or two semibreves, and so on. Signs such as circles and semicircles indicated the mensuration; a half-circle (C) for imperfect tempus is the ancestor of the modern "common time" symbol.

History

The system was first described systematically around 1280 by Franco of Cologne in his Ars cantus mensurabilis, which gave each note shape an inherent durational value. In the 14th century, theorists of the Ars nova such as Philippe de Vitry added smaller note values and new possibilities of division. Around 1450, "white mensural notation" replaced black noteheads with hollow ones for the longer values, owing to the use of paper instead of parchment. By the early 17th century the system had evolved into modern bar-line notation with fixed note values.
