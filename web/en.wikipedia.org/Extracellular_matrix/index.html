Extracellular matrix

In biology, the extracellular matrix (ECM) is a three-dimensional network consisting of extracellular macromolecules and minerals, such as collagen, enzymes, glycoproteins and hydroxyapatite that provide structural and biochemical support to surrounding cells. Because multicellularity evolved independently in different multicellular lineages, the composition of ECM varies between multicellular structures; however, cell adhesion, cell-to-cell communication and differentiation are common functions of the ECM.

Composition

In animal tissues, the ECM includes the interstitial matrix and the basement membrane. Interstitial matrices surround cells and contain collagen and elastin fibres embedded in a gel-like substance of proteoglycans and glycosaminoglycans such as hyaluronic acid. Key proteins include fibronectin, laminin and integrins, which link the matrix to the cell cytoskeleton.

Functions

The ECM provides:

  • Mechanical support and tensile strength to tissues
  • Scaffolding for cell adhesion and migration
  • Regulation of cell proliferation and differentiation through signalling molecules
  • Storage and presentation of growth factors

Diseases involving the ECM include fibrosis, where excessive matrix deposition stiffens tissues, and certain cancers, whose progression depends on matrix remodelling.
