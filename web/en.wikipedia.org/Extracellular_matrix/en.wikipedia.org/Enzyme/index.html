Enzyme

Enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts by accelerating chemical reactions. The molecules upon which enzymes may act are called substrates, and the enzyme converts the substrates into different molecules known as products. Almost all metabolic processes in the cell need enzyme catalysis in order to occur at rates fast enough to sustain life. Enzymes are known to catalyze more than 5,000 biochemical reaction types.

History

Fermentation was known to depend on living yeast until Eduard Buchner showed in 1897 that cell-free yeast extracts could ferment sugar, coining the term "enzyme" (from Greek enzymos, "in yeast"; the word had been used by Wilhelm Kühne in 1877). James Sumner crystallised urease in 1926 and showed enzymes are proteins. Emil Fischer proposed the lock-and-key model in 1894; Daniel Koshland's induced-fit model (1958) refined it.

Mechanism and kinetics

Enzymes bind substrates at an active site and stabilise the transition state, lowering activation energy without changing the overall thermodynamics. Activity depends on temperature, pH, and substrate concentration. Michaelis–Menten kinetics describes how reaction rate rises with substrate concentration and then saturates. Enzymes may require non-protein cofactors — metal ions or organic coenzymes such as NAD+ and ATP. Inhibitors may be competitive, non-competitive, or allosteric, and allosteric regulation and covalent modification are central to metabolic control.

Classification and uses

The Enzyme Commission divides enzymes into seven classes: oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, ligases, and translocases. Industrially, enzymes are used in detergents (proteases, lipases), food production (rennet, amylases, lactase), brewing, biofuels, textiles, paper, and medicine — including diagnostic assays and enzyme replacement and thrombolytic therapies.
