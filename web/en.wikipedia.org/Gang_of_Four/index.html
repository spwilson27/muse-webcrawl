The Gang of Four (simplified Chinese: 四人帮; traditional Chinese: 四人幫) was a Maoist political faction composed of four Chinese Communist Party (CCP) officials. They came to prominence during the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) and were later charged with a series of treasonous crimes. The gang's leading figure was Jiang Qing, the fourth wife of Mao Zedong and chair of the Politburo. The other members were Zhang Chunqiao, Yao Wenyuan and Wang Hongwen.

Rise to power

The four rose rapidly during the Cultural Revolution, controlling the CCP's propaganda organs and cultural policy and wielding enormous power, particularly over the arts, education and the media. Jiang Qing directed the creation of the "revolutionary model operas" that dominated Chinese stages. Wang Hongwen, a former factory worker and Red Guard leader from Shanghai, rose to become vice chairman of the party.

Downfall and trial

Within a month of Mao's death on 9 September 1976, the four were arrested in a bloodless coup on 6 October 1976, orchestrated by Hua Guofeng, Ye Jianying and Wang Dongxing. Their arrest was publicly celebrated across China. In the televised trial of 1980–81 before a Special Court, the Gang of Four and six surviving associates of Lin Biao were prosecuted for the persecution of hundreds of thousands during the Cultural Revolution. Jiang Qing and Zhang Chunqiao received death sentences with reprieve (later commuted to life imprisonment), Wang Hongwen received life imprisonment, and Yao Wenyuan received 20 years.

Legacy

Their fall marked the end of the Cultural Revolution era. Official Chinese historiography places disproportionate blame on the Gang of Four for the excesses of the period, a framing that some historians argue shields Mao and the party itself from fuller responsibility.
