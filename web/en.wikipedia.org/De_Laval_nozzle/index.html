De Laval nozzle

A de Laval nozzle (or convergent-divergent nozzle, CD nozzle or con-di nozzle) is a tube pinched in the middle into an hourglass shape, used to accelerate a compressible fluid to supersonic speeds. It was invented by the Swedish engineer Gustaf de Laval in 1888 for use in his impulse steam turbines, and is now fundamental to rocket engines, supersonic wind tunnels and steam and gas turbines.

Operation

Gas enters the wide converging section and accelerates as the passage narrows, reaching the speed of sound at the narrowest point, the throat. Because a converging passage alone cannot push a compressible flow past Mach 1, the diverging section beyond the throat is essential: there, paradoxically, the expanding cross-section allows the now-supersonic gas to accelerate further as it expands and its pressure and temperature fall. The balance between the gas pressure at the exit and the surrounding atmosphere determines performance: a nozzle is "ideally expanded" when the two match.

Analysis and applications

The behaviour of the nozzle follows from the area-velocity relation of compressible flow, derived from the conservation of mass and the equation of state; flow may be choked at the throat, and improperly matched exit pressures produce shock waves inside or outside the nozzle. Rocket nozzles — typically bell-shaped profiles derived from the de Laval form — use the principle to convert the thermal energy of combustion gases into thrust as efficiently as possible.
