Hand washing

PurposeHygiene; removal of pathogens and dirt
AgentsSoap and water; alcohol-based hand sanitiser
Key advocateIgnaz Semmelweis (1840s)

Hand washing (or handwashing), also known as hand hygiene, is the act of cleaning one's hands with soap and water or other agents to remove soil, dirt, and microorganisms. It is one of the most effective and inexpensive measures for preventing the spread of infectious diseases, including diarrhoeal and respiratory illnesses.

History

The medical importance of hand washing was famously demonstrated by Ignaz Semmelweis in Vienna in 1847, where requiring physicians to disinfect their hands with chlorinated lime sharply reduced deaths from puerperal fever; his findings were initially resisted. Florence Nightingale and later the germ theory of disease helped establish hand hygiene as standard practice in medicine and everyday life.

Practice

Health authorities such as the World Health Organization recommend washing with soap for at least 20 seconds, scrubbing all surfaces of the hands, at key moments: before eating and preparing food, after using the toilet, after coughing or sneezing, and in healthcare before and after patient contact (the WHO "Five Moments"). Where soap and water are unavailable, alcohol-based hand rubs with at least 60% alcohol are an alternative, though less effective against some pathogens and visible dirt.
