42 U.S.C. § 1983

Section 1983
EnactedApril 20, 1871
Part ofCivil Rights Act of 1871 (Ku Klux Klan Act)
Codified42 U.S.C. § 1983
PurposeCivil action for deprivation of constitutional rights under color of state law

42 U.S.C. § 1983, commonly referred to simply as Section 1983, is a federal law that provides a civil cause of action for the deprivation, under color of state law, of rights secured by the United States Constitution and federal laws. Originally enacted as Section 1 of the Civil Rights Act of 1871, also known as the Ku Klux Klan Act, the statute is one of the most important tools for vindicating constitutional rights against state and local officials.

Text

The statute provides that every person who, under color of any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage of any state, subjects or causes to be subjected any citizen of the United States or other person within its jurisdiction to the deprivation of any rights, privileges, or immunities secured by the Constitution and laws, shall be liable to the party injured in an action at law, suit in equity, or other proper proceeding for redress.

History

Congress enacted Section 1983 during Reconstruction in response to violence by the Ku Klux Klan and the failure of Southern states to protect the civil rights of newly freed Black citizens. The provision lay largely dormant for nearly a century until the Supreme Court's 1961 decision in Monroe v. Pape, which held that the statute could be invoked against officials acting under color of law even when their conduct violated state law.

Elements of a claim

To prevail, a plaintiff must show (1) that the conduct complained of was committed by a person acting under color of state law, and (2) that the conduct deprived the plaintiff of a right, privilege, or immunity secured by the Constitution or laws of the United States. Municipalities may be liable under Monell v. Department of Social Services (1978) when the deprivation results from an official policy or custom, though municipalities are not liable on a respondeat superior theory.

Qualified immunity

Individual officials sued under Section 1983 may claim qualified immunity, which shields them from damages unless they violated clearly established law of which a reasonable official would have known. The doctrine, developed in cases such as Harlow v. Fitzgerald (1982), remains a subject of significant public debate.
