Baconian theory

The Baconian theory of Shakespeare authorship holds that Sir Francis Bacon, the philosopher, statesman and essayist, wrote the plays and poems traditionally attributed to William Shakespeare of Stratford-upon-Avon. It was the first major alternative authorship theory to gain wide currency, emerging in the mid-19th century, and it forms part of the broader Shakespeare authorship question.

History

Hints that Bacon might have concealed work under another name surfaced in the 18th century, but the theory was developed in earnest by writers such as William Henry Smith, who published a pamphlet in 1856 arguing that Bacon wrote the plays, and Delia Bacon, an American writer whose 1857 book The Philosophy of the Plays of Shakspere Unfolded proposed that a circle of writers led by Bacon produced the works as vehicles for a hidden philosophical and political system. Delia Bacon was not related to Francis Bacon.

Arguments

Supporters point to parallels between passages in the plays and Bacon's acknowledged writings, including similarities in vocabulary, imagery and legal knowledge. Baconians also argue that the plays display learning in law, classical literature and courtly life that they consider beyond the documented education of the Stratford actor. Some adherents claim to find ciphers in the First Folio purportedly designed by Bacon to reveal his authorship.

Reception

Mainstream Shakespeare scholars overwhelmingly reject the theory, noting that no contemporary evidence connects Bacon to the plays, that records attach Shakespeare's name to the works during his lifetime, and that the alleged ciphers can be made to yield arbitrary messages. Other candidate authors proposed by later theories include Edward de Vere, Earl of Oxford, Christopher Marlowe and Mary Sidney.
