James Abram Garfield (November 19, 1831 – September 19, 1881) was the 20th president of the United States, serving from March 1881 until his death in September that year after being shot by an assassin. A preacher, lawyer, and Civil War general, he served nine terms in the House of Representatives and is the only sitting congressman ever elected president.

Early life and career

Born in poverty in a log cabin in Ohio and raised by his widowed mother, Garfield worked as a canal boatman before putting himself through school. He graduated from Williams College, became a teacher and college president, and studied law. When the Civil War began he joined the Union Army, rising to major general and winning distinction at Chickamauga, while also being elected to Congress from Ohio in 1862.

Presidency and assassination

Chosen as a compromise candidate at the 1880 Republican convention, Garfield narrowly defeated Winfield Scott Hancock. In office he asserted presidential authority over patronage against Senator Roscoe Conkling's Stalwart faction, pursued civil service reform, and strengthened the navy. On July 2, 1881, Charles J. Guiteau, a disgruntled office-seeker, shot him at a Washington railway station. Garfield lingered for 79 days before dying of infections worsened by his doctors' unsterile probing; Vice President Chester A. Arthur succeeded him.

Legacy

Garfield's death spurred passage of the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act of 1883. Historians credit his brief presidency with reasserting presidential power and advancing reform.
