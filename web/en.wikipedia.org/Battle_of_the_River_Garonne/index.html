The Battle of the River Garonne was fought in 732 between the Aquitanian army of Duke Odo the Great and the Umayyad forces of Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi, governor of al-Andalus, near the Garonne River by Bordeaux. The Umayyads won a complete victory, destroyed Aquitaine's military power, and opened the road north—setting the stage for their confrontation with Charles Martel at the Battle of Tours later the same year.

Background

Odo had humbled the Umayyads at Toulouse in 721, but a decade later he faced a new, larger invasion. After a revolt by the Berber lord Munuza in the Pyrenees—in which Odo was entangled by a marriage alliance—was crushed, Abd al-Rahman crossed the mountains in force in 732, aiming to punish Aquitaine and plunder northward.

The battle

Odo gathered his army north of Bordeaux to bar the crossing of the Garonne. The details, like much of the campaign, rest on terse chronicle notices—the Mozarabic Chronicle of 754 and Frankish annals—but the outcome was unambiguous: Odo's army was overwhelmed, cut to pieces, and scattered, with heavy loss of life as the Aquitanians were driven into or across the river. Bordeaux was then stormed and sacked, its churches burned, and the invaders advanced on Poitiers and Tours.

Aftermath

Defenceless, Odo fled north to seek help from his long-time rival, Charles Martel, accepting Frankish overlordship as the price. The combined Frankish-Aquitanian victory at Tours in October 732 rescued Aquitaine, but Odo's duchy never recovered its former independence of action, and his descendants were progressively subordinated to the Carolingians.
