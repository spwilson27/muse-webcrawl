1G refers to the first generation of wireless cellular telephone technology. These are the analogue telecommunications standards introduced in the 1980s, replacing the earlier "0G" mobile radio telephone systems and themselves replaced by digital 2G technology in the 1990s.

Standards and deployment

The first commercial 1G network was launched by Nippon Telegraph and Telephone (NTT) in Tokyo, Japan, in 1979, initially as a car-phone system. The Nordic Mobile Telephone (NMT) system, launched in 1981 in Scandinavia, was the first to offer international roaming and handover between cells. In the United States, the Advanced Mobile Phone System (AMPS) launched commercially in Chicago in 1983 — Motorola executive Martin Cooper had demonstrated the first handheld cellular call on an AMPS prototype a decade earlier, in 1973. Other 1G systems included TACS in the United Kingdom, C-Netz in West Germany, Radiocom 2000 in France and RTMI in Italy.

Characteristics

1G systems transmitted voice as analogue frequency-modulated signals using frequency-division multiple access (FDMA), with channels typically spaced 25–30 kHz apart. They offered no encryption, so calls could be overheard with radio scanners, and cloning of phone identities was a major source of fraud. Data services were effectively absent; the networks were designed for voice only. Handsets were large, heavy "brick" phones such as the Motorola DynaTAC, or permanently installed car phones, with short battery life.

Legacy

Despite their limitations, 1G networks proved the commercial viability of cellular telephony, introduced automatic handover and roaming, and built the subscriber base that drove the digital 2G revolution (GSM, IS-95) in the 1990s. Most 1G networks were shut down during the 2000s as spectrum was refarmed for digital services.
