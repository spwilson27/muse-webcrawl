In statistical modeling, regression analysis is a set of statistical processes for estimating the relationships between a dependent variable (often called the "outcome" or "response" variable) and one or more independent variables (often called "predictors", "covariates", "explanatory variables" or "features"). The most common form is linear regression, which finds the line (or hyperplane) that most closely fits the data according to a specific mathematical criterion — usually ordinary least squares, which minimizes the sum of squared residuals.

History

The method of least squares was published by Adrien-Marie Legendre in 1805 and Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1809, and applied to predicting the orbit of Ceres. The term "regression" was coined by Francis Galton in his 1886 study of heredity: he observed that the heights of descendants of tall ancestors tended to "regress" toward the population average. Karl Pearson and others later developed the mathematical framework, and Ronald Fisher established regression as a central tool of statistical inference in the 1920s.

Models and methods

A regression model relates the dependent variable Y to a function of independent variables X and unknown parameters β, together with a random error term. Variants include multiple linear regression, polynomial regression, logistic regression for binary outcomes, Poisson regression for counts, ridge and lasso regression with regularization, robust regression, and nonparametric approaches. Under the Gauss–Markov assumptions, ordinary least squares is the best linear unbiased estimator; inference typically relies on approximately normal, homoscedastic errors, and violations such as multicollinearity, autocorrelation, or omitted-variable bias can distort conclusions. Regression is used for prediction and forecasting and, more cautiously, for inferring causal relationships.

Applications

Regression analysis is ubiquitous: in economics (demand estimation, econometrics), epidemiology (risk factors), finance (the capital asset pricing model), psychology, engineering, and machine learning, where it forms the basis of supervised learning. Model fit is commonly summarized by the coefficient of determination, R², alongside residual diagnostics and cross-validation.
