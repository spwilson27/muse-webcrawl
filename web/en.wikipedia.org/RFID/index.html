RFID

RFID
Radio-frequency identification
Uses electromagnetic fields to identify and track tags attached to objects

Radio-frequency identification (RFID) uses electromagnetic fields to automatically identify and track tags attached to objects. An RFID system consists of a tiny radio transponder called a tag, a radio receiver, and a transmitter. When triggered by an electromagnetic interrogation pulse from a nearby RFID reader device, the tag transmits digital data, usually an identifying inventory number, back to the reader. This number can be used to track inventory goods.

Types

Passive tags are powered by energy from the RFID reader's interrogating radio waves and require no battery, making them inexpensive and small. Active tags have an onboard battery and periodically transmit their ID signal, allowing greater range but at higher cost. A battery-assisted passive tag has a small battery on board and is activated when an RFID reader is present.

Frequencies

RFID systems operate in several frequency bands: low frequency (LF, 125–134 kHz), high frequency (HF, 13.56 MHz, used by near-field communication devices), and ultra-high frequency (UHF, 860–960 MHz). Low-frequency tags are used in animal identification and access control; UHF tags dominate retail and logistics supply chains.

History

The technology's roots lie in Second World War radar and identification friend or foe (IFF) transponders. Leon Theremin's passive listening device of 1945 is often cited as a precursor. Commercial RFID tags emerged in the 1970s, and mass adoption followed standards development and Walmart's supply-chain mandates in the early 2000s.

Applications

RFID is used in access control badges, contactless payment, livestock tracking, toll collection, library systems, passports, race timing, and retail inventory management. See also RSS for a different technology with a similarly terse acronym.
