Jury instructions

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jury instructions are the directions given by a trial judge to a jury explaining the law that applies to the case before it, the standards the jury must use in evaluating the evidence, and the procedures it should follow in reaching a verdict. They form the formal link between the abstract law and the jury's role as finder of fact.

Content

Instructions typically cover the burden of proof — beyond a reasonable doubt in criminal cases, preponderance of the evidence in most civil cases — the elements of each claim or offence, relevant definitions, the presumption of innocence, how to assess witness credibility, and the forms of verdict available. Judges also give cautionary instructions about matters the jury must not consider, such as a defendant's silence or evidence admitted for a limited purpose.

Procedure

In many jurisdictions the parties propose instructions, the judge settles the final wording at a charge conference, and the instructions are read aloud before or after closing arguments and often provided in writing during deliberations. Standardised "pattern" or "model" instructions, drafted by committees of judges and lawyers, are widely used to reduce error. Errors in instructions are a common ground of appeal, since a misstated legal standard can require a new trial.

Jury nullification

Instructions generally do not tell jurors that they may acquit against the evidence — a power known as jury nullification. Courts usually hold that while juries possess the raw power to nullify, they have no right to be informed of it, and lawyers may be forbidden from arguing for it expressly.
