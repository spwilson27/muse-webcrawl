Frederick William, Elector of Brandenburg

Frederick William (German: Friedrich Wilhelm)
Born: 16 February 1620, Berlin
Died: 29 April 1688, Potsdam
Reign: 1 December 1640 – 29 April 1688
House: Hohenzollern

Frederick William (German: Friedrich Wilhelm; 16 February 1620 – 29 April 1688) was the Elector of Brandenburg and Duke of Prussia — and thus ruler of Brandenburg-Prussia — from 1640 until his death in 1688. A member of the House of Hohenzollern, he is popularly known as "the Great Elector" (der Große Kurfürst) because of his military and political achievements.

Reign

Frederick William inherited a scattered group of territories devastated by the Thirty Years' War. Over a long reign he welded Brandenburg, Ducal Prussia, and other holdings into an increasingly centralised state, built a standing army of some 30,000 men, and reformed taxation and administration, often at the expense of the local estates. In the Treaty of Königsberg (1656) and subsequent treaties during the Northern War of 1655–1660, he manoeuvred between Sweden and Poland, ultimately securing full sovereignty over Ducal Prussia by the Treaty of Wehlau (1657), confirmed at Oliva in 1660.

Wars and the Edict of Potsdam

In 1675 his army won a celebrated victory over the Swedes at the Battle of Fehrbellin, greatly enhancing his reputation. Although the Treaty of Saint-Germain (1679) forced him to return most of his conquests in Swedish Pomerania, he had established Brandenburg-Prussia as a significant military power. In 1685 he issued the Edict of Potsdam, inviting French Huguenots fleeing the revocation of the Edict of Nantes to settle in his lands; some 20,000 came, greatly benefiting Berlin's economy and culture.

Legacy

Frederick William laid the foundations on which his son Frederick built the Kingdom of Prussia. His state-building — a permanent army, a professional bureaucracy, and religious toleration for productive immigrants — set the course for Prussia's rise.
