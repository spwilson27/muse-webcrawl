Aperture

Different f-numbers produced by an adjustable aperture diaphragm

In optics, an aperture is a hole or opening through which light travels. In a camera or telescope, the aperture—usually controlled by an adjustable iris diaphragm—determines how much light reaches the image sensor or film, and strongly affects the brightness, depth of field, and sharpness of the image.

F-number

The size of the aperture is expressed by the f-number (or f-stop), defined as the focal length of the lens divided by the diameter of the entrance pupil. Common values are f/1.4, f/2, f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6, f/8, f/11, and f/16; each step ("stop") halves the light admitted. A smaller f-number means a wider aperture, admitting more light and producing a shallower depth of field; a larger f-number narrows the opening, increasing the range of distances in acceptable focus.

Effects

Aperture interacts with shutter speed and ISO sensitivity to set exposure. Wide apertures blur backgrounds for portraiture, while narrow apertures keep landscapes sharp throughout but, pushed too far, soften the image through diffraction. In telescopes and binoculars, aperture diameter determines light-gathering power and resolution.

Other senses

The term also denotes the effective diameter of an antenna in radio engineering (aperture synthesis in radio astronomy), and in anatomy any natural opening in the body, such as the thoracic aperture.
