Biome

A biome is a large-scale community of plants and animals that occupies a distinct region defined by its climate and geography. Biomes are characterised by the dominant forms of plant life — such as forest, grassland, or desert — which reflect long-term patterns of temperature and precipitation. Examples of terrestrial biomes include tropical rainforest, savanna, desert, chaparral, temperate grassland, temperate deciduous forest, taiga (boreal forest), and tundra; aquatic biomes are divided into freshwater and marine categories.

Classification

There is no single agreed list of biomes. Classification schemes by ecologists such as Robert Whittaker related biome types directly to mean annual temperature and precipitation. The World Wide Fund for Nature (WWF) scheme divides the land surface into 14 terrestrial biomes and some 867 smaller ecoregions, an approach widely used in conservation planning. Holdridge life zones offer an alternative, more finely divided system based on biotemperature, precipitation, and evapotranspiration.

Determinants

Climate is the primary control on biome distribution, and climate is in turn shaped by latitude, altitude, ocean currents, and the global circulation of the atmosphere. Similar climates on different continents produce strikingly similar vegetation structures — an example of convergent evolution — even though the species involved are unrelated: Mediterranean-type shrublands, for instance, occur in California, Chile, South Africa, Australia, and the Mediterranean Basin itself.

Human impact

Human activity has transformed large fractions of many biomes through agriculture, logging, urbanisation, and introduced species, and anthropogenic climate change is shifting biome boundaries poleward and upslope. Some scientists propose recognising "anthromes" — anthropogenic biomes — as a complementary way to map the human-shaped biosphere.
