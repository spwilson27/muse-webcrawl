German Americans (German: Deutschamerikaner) are Americans who have full or partial German ancestry. With a population of roughly 43 million, German Americans are the largest of the self-reported ancestry groups in the United States. Germans have been arriving in North America since the 1600s, and German immigrants founded many communities, particularly in Pennsylvania, the Midwest, and Texas.

History

The first permanent German settlement was Germantown, Pennsylvania, founded in 1683. Large-scale immigration peaked in the nineteenth century: between 1820 and the First World War, around six million Germans came to the United States, fleeing economic hardship, political unrest after the failed revolutions of 1848, and religious persecution. The Cincinnati, St. Louis, and Milwaukee "German triangle" became a centre of German-American cultural life, with German-language newspapers, schools, churches, breweries, and singing and gymnastics societies.

World Wars and assimilation

Anti-German sentiment during the First World War led to the suppression of German-language instruction and publications, the renaming of foods and places, and widespread pressure to assimilate. During the Second World War, German Americans, unlike Japanese Americans, were not subjected to mass internment, though some individuals were detained. In the post-war generations, German ethnic identity survived chiefly in food, festivals such as Oktoberfest, and family tradition.

Contributions

German Americans have made significant contributions to American life, from the generals von Steuben and Schurz to the founders of major brewing, piano-making, and industrial companies, and to foodways such as the hamburger, hot dog, lager beer, and the Christmas tree tradition.
