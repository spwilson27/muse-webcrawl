Polystyrene

Polystyrene (PS) is a synthetic polymer made from monomers of the aromatic hydrocarbon styrene. Polystyrene can be solid or foamed. General-purpose polystyrene is clear, hard, and brittle. It is an inexpensive resin per unit weight, a rather poor barrier to oxygen and water vapour, and has a relatively low melting point.

History

Polystyrene was discovered in 1839 by Eduard Simon, an apothecary in Berlin, who distilled storax resin into an oily substance he called styrol and noticed it thickened into a jelly. The polymer was studied by Hermann Staudinger in the 1920s, and commercial production began in Germany in the 1930s. Expanded polystyrene foam, developed in the 1940s and marketed by Dow Chemical under the trademark Styrofoam, became ubiquitous in insulation and packaging.

Forms and uses

Solid polystyrene is used for plastic cutlery, CD and DVD cases, model kits, Petri dishes, and yoghurt pots. High-impact polystyrene (HIPS), toughened with polybutadiene rubber, is used for refrigerator liners, toys and appliance housings. Expanded polystyrene (EPS), produced by foaming beads with steam, is a lightweight closed-cell foam used for packaging, disposable food containers, and building insulation; extruded polystyrene (XPS) is used mainly as rigid insulation board.

Environmental issues

Polystyrene is slow to biodegrade and is a common component of marine debris and litter; its lightness makes it easily dispersed by wind. Because foamed polystyrene is bulky but very light, it is rarely economical to recycle, and many municipalities have banned or restricted its use in food service. Efforts to develop depolymerization and alternative feedstocks continue.
