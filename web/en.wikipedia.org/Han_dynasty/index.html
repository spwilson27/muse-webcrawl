The Han dynasty (Chinese: 漢朝; pinyin: Hàncháo) was an imperial dynasty of China (202 BC – AD 220), preceded by the Qin dynasty and succeeded by the Three Kingdoms period. Founded by Liu Bang (Emperor Gaozu), it is considered a golden age in Chinese history, and China's majority ethnic group still calls itself the Han Chinese after it.

Western and Eastern Han

The dynasty is divided into the Western (Former) Han, with its capital at Chang'an, and the Eastern (Later) Han, with its capital at Luoyang, separated by the brief Xin dynasty of the usurper Wang Mang (AD 9–23). Under Emperor Wu (r. 141–87 BC), Confucianism became the state ideology, the empire expanded into Central Asia, Korea and Vietnam, and the Silk Road trade flourished. Zhang Qian's journeys west opened regular contact with Central Asia.

Society and achievements

The Han state ran a centralised bureaucracy recruited increasingly by recommendation and examination, maintained monopolies on salt and iron, and produced major advances: paper (refined by Cai Lun in AD 105), the seismometer of Zhang Heng, advances in astronomy, medicine and cartography, and the great histories of Sima Qian and Ban Gu.

Fall

Court factionalism, eunuch power, land concentration and peasant revolts — above all the Yellow Turban Rebellion of AD 184 — hollowed out the Eastern Han. In 220 the last emperor, Xian, abdicated to Cao Pi, ending the dynasty and beginning the Three Kingdoms period.
