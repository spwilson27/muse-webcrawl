Polymorphism (biology)

In biology, polymorphism is the occurrence of two or more clearly different morphs or forms, also referred to as alternative phenotypes, in the population of a species. To be classified as such, morphs must occupy the same habitat at the same time and belong to a panmictic population (one with random mating).

Terminology

Polymorphism is distinct from continuous variation such as height: the forms are discrete and sharply distinguished. Put simply, polymorphism is when there are two or more obvious variations of a trait within a population. The term was defined by E. B. Ford, who framed genetic polymorphism as the occurrence together in the same habitat of two or more discontinuous forms of a species in such proportions that the rarest of them cannot be maintained merely by recurrent mutation.

Examples

Classic examples include the light and dark (melanic) morphs of the peppered moth, whose frequencies shifted with industrial pollution; the multiple shell colour and banding morphs of grove snails; the mimicry morphs of butterflies such as Papilio memnon, in which females of a single species imitate several different unpalatable models; and the caste systems of social insects, where queens, workers and soldiers are dramatically different morphs of the same genome. Sexual dimorphism — marked differences between males and females — is sometimes regarded as a special case.

Mechanisms

Genetic polymorphism may be maintained by balancing selection, heterozygote advantage (as in sickle-cell trait and malaria resistance), negative frequency-dependent selection, or heterogeneous environments. Many polymorphisms are controlled by supergenes — tightly linked groups of loci inherited as a single unit, such as those controlling shell patterns in snails and social organisation in fire ants.
