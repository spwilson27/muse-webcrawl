Post-translational modification

FieldMolecular biology, biochemistry
Common typesPhosphorylation, glycosylation, ubiquitination, methylation, acetylation, proteolysis
WhenDuring or after protein biosynthesis (translation)
EffectAlters protein function, location, stability, interactions

Post-translational modification (PTM) is the enzymatic modification of proteins following their biosynthesis by ribosomes. Proteins are synthesized from messenger RNA through translation, and most undergo chemical changes — the addition of functional groups, the addition of other proteins or sugars, or proteolytic cleavage — that profoundly affect their activity, localization, lifespan, and interactions. PTMs greatly increase the diversity of the proteome beyond what the genome alone encodes.

Common modifications

Phosphorylation, the addition of a phosphate group by kinases (and its removal by phosphatases), is a central reversible switch in cell signaling; it occurs mainly on serine, threonine, and tyrosine residues. Glycosylation attaches sugar chains (glycans) to proteins in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, important for folding, cell recognition, and the function of antibodies. Ubiquitination tags proteins with the small protein ubiquitin, most famously targeting them for degradation by the proteasome, though it also serves signaling roles. Other widespread PTMs include methylation, acetylation (key in histone regulation of gene expression), lipidation such as prenylation and myristoylation, disulfide bond formation, and limited proteolysis — for example the cleavage of proinsulin to insulin.

Detection and study

PTMs are studied with mass spectrometry-based proteomics, modification-specific antibodies, and enrichment techniques. Because many PTMs are dynamic and low in abundance, mapping them comprehensively remains a major technical challenge.

Biological and medical significance

Dysregulated PTMs underlie many diseases: abnormal phosphorylation drives numerous cancers (and kinase inhibitors are major drugs), aberrant glycosylation occurs in congenital disorders and cancer, and defective protein modification features in neurodegenerative diseases. The reversible, enzyme-controlled nature of PTMs makes them prime drug targets.
