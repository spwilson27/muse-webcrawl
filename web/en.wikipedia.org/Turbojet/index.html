Turbojet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The turbojet is an airbreathing jet engine which is typically used in aircraft. It consists of a gas turbine with a propelling nozzle. The gas turbine has an air inlet which includes inlet guide vanes, a compressor, a combustion chamber and a turbine. The compressed air from the compressor is heated by burning fuel in the combustion chamber and then allowed to expand through the turbine. The turbine exhaust is then expanded in the propelling nozzle where it is accelerated to high speed to provide thrust.

History

The turbojet was developed independently in the 1930s by Frank Whittle in Britain and Hans von Ohain in Germany. Von Ohain's engine powered the Heinkel He 178, the world's first jet aircraft, in August 1939; Whittle's Power Jets W.1 powered the Gloster E.28/39 in 1941. Jet fighters such as the Messerschmitt Me 262 saw service late in the Second World War, and turbojets powered the first generation of jet airliners, including the de Havilland Comet (1949) and the Boeing 707.

Characteristics

Turbojets are efficient at high speeds (above roughly Mach 0.8) but are noisy and fuel-hungry at subsonic speeds compared with turbofans, which replaced them for most applications. Pure turbojets survive mainly in missiles and some military aircraft; the Olympus engines of Concorde were the most famous civil application.

Operation cycle

The engine works on the Brayton cycle: intake, compression, combustion at roughly constant pressure, expansion through the turbine (which drives the compressor), and exhaust through the nozzle to produce thrust.
