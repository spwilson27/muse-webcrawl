History of publishing

The history of publishing is the story of how written works have been produced and distributed, from clay tablets and hand-copied manuscripts to printing presses, mass media, and digital platforms.

Before print

Writing systems in Mesopotamia, Egypt, China, and Mesoamerica enabled the earliest "publication" — the multiplication and circulation of texts. Scribes copied works in scriptoria; Roman publishers such as Atticus employed teams of copyists. In China, woodblock printing produced complete books by the Tang dynasty (the Diamond Sutra of 868 is the oldest dated printed book), and Bi Sheng invented movable type in the 11th century; Korean printers developed metal movable type by the 13th century.

The printing press revolution

Johannes Gutenberg's movable-type press (c. 1440) in Mainz slashed the cost of books in Europe. Printing spread rapidly; by 1500 presses in hundreds of cities had produced millions of volumes. Print transformed religion (Reformation pamphlets), science, and vernacular literature. The 17th century brought newspapers; the 18th, copyright law (Statute of Anne, 1710) and lending libraries.

Industrial and mass publishing

Steam presses, cheap paper, and rail distribution enabled mass-circulation newspapers and novels in the 19th century, alongside professional publishing houses and literary agents. The 20th century added paperback revolutions, conglomeration of media firms, and desktop publishing in the 1980s. Since the 1990s the internet, e-books, print-on-demand, and self-publishing platforms have reshaped the industry, challenging traditional gatekeeping and business models.
