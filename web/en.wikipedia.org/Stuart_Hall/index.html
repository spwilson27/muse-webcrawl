Stuart Hall

Stuart McPhail Hall (3 February 1932 – 10 February 2014) was a Jamaican-born British Marxist sociologist, cultural theorist, and political activist. Hall, along with Richard Hoggart and Raymond Williams, was one of the founding figures of the school of thought that is now known as British Cultural Studies or the Birmingham School of Cultural Studies. In the 1950s Hall was a founder of the New Left Review, and at Hoggart's invitation he joined the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies (CCCS) at the University of Birmingham in 1964, becoming its director in 1968. In 1979 he became a professor of sociology at the Open University.

Work

Hall's work ranges across the topics of hegemony, cultural studies, and the study of race and postcolonial identity. His essay "Encoding/Decoding" (1973/1980) proposed that audiences actively decode media messages, taking dominant-hegemonic, negotiated or oppositional positions. He co-authored Policing the Crisis (1978), an influential study of moral panics and the authoritarian turn in 1970s Britain, and coined the term "Thatcherism" for the ideological project of Margaret Thatcher's governments. Hall wrote extensively on race, ethnicity and diaspora, arguing that identity is a production, never complete, always in process.

Life

Born in Kingston, Jamaica, Hall won a Rhodes Scholarship to Merton College, Oxford, in 1951. He became a central figure of the British New Left after 1956 and later a prominent public intellectual, broadcaster and chair of arts organisations including the Institute of International Visual Arts (Iniva) and Autograph ABP. He died in London in 2014. A posthumous memoir, Familiar Stranger: A Life Between Two Islands, appeared in 2017, and the Stuart Hall Foundation continues work in his name.
