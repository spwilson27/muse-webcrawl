Carnatic music, known as Karnāṭaka saṃgīta in the South Indian languages, is a system of music commonly associated with South India, including the modern Indian states of Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and Telangana. It is one of the two main subgenres of Indian classical music, the other being Hindustani music of the north.

A typical Carnatic concert ensemble.

Origins and history

Carnatic music traces its theoretical roots to ancient treatises such as the Natya Shastra and to devotional movements including the bhakti saints and Purandara Dasa (1484–1564), revered as the "father of Carnatic music" for systematizing its teaching exercises. The form crystallized in the courts and temples of South India, notably under the Vijayanagara Empire and later the Maratha court of Thanjavur.

Structure

Like all Indian classical music, Carnatic music is built on raga (melodic framework) and tala (rhythmic cycle). Melodic theory is organized into 72 melakarta parent scales. The principal composed form is the kriti, a structured devotional song, most famously those of the "Trinity" — Tyagaraja, Muthuswami Dikshitar, and Syama Sastri — who composed in Telugu and Sanskrit in the late 18th and early 19th centuries. Concerts balance composed pieces with improvisation, including alapana (unmetered raga exposition), neraval, and kalpanaswaram.

Instruments and performance

Vocals are central; the principal accompanists are the violin (adapted from European contact in the 19th century), the mridangam drum, and often the ghatam clay pot, kanjira, and morsing. The veena and flute are prominent solo instruments. Chennai's December–January "Music Season", centred on sabhas (music societies) and the Madras Music Academy founded in 1928, is among the world's largest classical music festivals.
