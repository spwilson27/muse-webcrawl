Wear is the damaging, gradual removal or deformation of material at solid surfaces. Causes of wear can be mechanical (e.g., erosion) or chemical (e.g., corrosion). The study of wear and related processes is tribology. Wear in machine elements, together with other processes such as fatigue and creep, is responsible for most mechanical failures and the degradation of components such as bearings, gears, seals, and cutting tools.

Types of wear

Adhesive wear occurs when asperities on sliding surfaces weld together and tear, transferring material between surfaces. Abrasive wear occurs when a hard, rough surface or hard particles plough through a softer surface, as in grinding. Surface fatigue wear arises from repeated cyclic loading, which nucleates cracks and eventually spalls material, as in rolling-element bearings. Erosive wear is caused by particles or fluid jets striking a surface. Corrosive (tribochemical) wear involves chemical reactions with the environment, and fretting wear occurs at contacts under small-amplitude oscillating motion.

Measurement and modelling

The classic model is the Archard equation (1953), which states that wear volume is proportional to the applied load and sliding distance and inversely proportional to the hardness of the softer material, scaled by a dimensionless wear coefficient K that varies by many orders of magnitude depending on materials and lubrication. Wear is measured by pin-on-disc tests, mass or volume loss, and profilometry, and is often described in stages: running-in, steady-state, and accelerated wear-out.

Prevention

Wear is mitigated by lubrication, hard and wear-resistant materials (such as hardened steels, ceramics, and cemented carbides), surface treatments and coatings (nitriding, hard chrome, diamond-like carbon), proper design to reduce contact stress, and filtration to remove abrasive particles. The economic cost of wear — through downtime, replacement, and energy losses — is estimated at several percent of GDP in industrial economies.
