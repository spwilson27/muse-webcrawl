Bernoulli trial

A Bernoulli trial (or binomial trial) is a random experiment with exactly two possible outcomes, conventionally called "success" and "failure", in which the probability of success is the same every time the experiment is conducted. It is named after Jacob Bernoulli, a Swiss mathematician of the 17th century, who treated such experiments in his work Ars Conjectandi (1713).

Definition

Mathematically, a Bernoulli trial is a random variable that takes the value 1 with probability p (success) and the value 0 with probability q = 1 − p (failure). A single toss of a fair coin, a single roll checked against a target number, or a single yes/no survey response can all be modeled as Bernoulli trials.

Related distributions

The sum of n independent Bernoulli trials with the same success probability follows the binomial distribution. The number of trials needed to obtain the first success follows the geometric distribution, while the probability distribution of a single trial is the Bernoulli distribution, the simplest discrete probability distribution.

Examples

Common examples include flipping a coin once, testing whether a manufactured item is defective, determining whether a patient responds to a treatment in a single observation, and polling one voter on whether they support a candidate. Bernoulli trials form the building blocks of many models in probability, statistics, and the theory of random processes, including the random walk and the binomial model of asset pricing in finance.
