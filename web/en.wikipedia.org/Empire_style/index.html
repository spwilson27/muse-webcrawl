Empire style

The Empire style (French: style Empire) is an early-nineteenth-century design movement in architecture, furniture, other decorative arts, and dress, directed by Napoleon Bonaparte's court and disseminated throughout Europe and North America. A second phase of Neoclassicism, it drew heavily on ancient Roman and Egyptian motifs to glorify the Napoleonic Empire (1804–1814/15), and continued to influence design into the 1820s and beyond.

Characteristics

Empire design is monumental and symmetrical, favouring bold masses, rich materials such as mahogany and ebony, and lavish gilt-bronze (ormolu) mounts. Motifs include the Napoleonic bee, eagles, laurel wreaths, classical figures, sphinxes, griffins, and military trophies. Architects and designers Charles Percier and Pierre Fontaine, authors of the influential Recueil de décorations intérieures (1801–1812), effectively codified the style for Napoleon's palaces at Malmaison, Saint-Cloud and the Tuileries.

Dissemination

The style spread through Napoleonic Europe and to Russia, where it shaped buildings such as those of Rossi in St. Petersburg. In Britain it overlapped with the Regency style, and in the United States it developed into the Federal style and American Empire furniture. In fashion, "Empire" gave its name to the high-waisted Empire silhouette dress for women.

Later revivals

A Second Empire style flourished under Napoleon III in the mid-nineteenth century, and Empire forms were revived again in Art Deco-era interiors. The term is still used by auction houses and dealers for early nineteenth-century French decorative arts.
