Propaganda

[Placeholder image: World War I recruiting poster, a classic propaganda medium]

Propaganda is communication that is primarily used to influence or persuade an audience to further an agenda, which may not be objective and may be selectively presenting facts to encourage a particular synthesis or perception, or using loaded language to produce an emotional rather than a rational response to the information presented. Propaganda is often associated with material which is prepared by governments as part of war efforts, political campaigns, health campaigns, and large-scale corporate public relations.

The term originated with the Catholic Church's Congregatio de Propaganda Fide ("Congregation for Propagating the Faith"), founded in 1622. It took on strongly negative connotations in the 20th century as totalitarian states used it systematically, though scholars note that propaganda techniques are also used in democracies, advertising, and public information campaigns.

History

Propaganda in some form has existed as long as recorded history: the Behistun Inscription (c. 515 BC) legitimised Darius I of Persia, and Roman emperors used coins and triumphal monuments to project power. The printing press enabled mass persuasion, seen in Reformation pamphlets and revolutionary broadsheets. The First World War saw the first modern state propaganda ministries, such as Britain's Wellington House and the United States' Committee on Public Information. In the Second World War, Joseph Goebbels's Reich Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda controlled German media, while all belligerents used radio, film, and posters. During the Cold War, propaganda was a central weapon of both superpowers.

Techniques

Common techniques identified by analysts, including the Institute for Propaganda Analysis (1937), include name-calling, glittering generalities, transfer, testimonial, plain folks, card stacking, and bandwagon appeals. Modern propaganda also employs repetition, fear appeals, demonisation of the enemy, disinformation, and the exploitation of social media algorithms and bot networks. A distinction is often drawn between white propaganda (openly sourced), grey propaganda (source unclear), and black propaganda (falsely attributed).

Propaganda and related concepts

Propaganda overlaps with advertising, public relations, education, and political campaigning, though it is distinguished by its one-sidedness and its aim of shaping attitudes rather than informing. Critics such as Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky, in their "propaganda model" of media, argued that structural filters bias news coverage in democratic societies toward elite interests. Jacques Ellul's Propaganda: The Formation of Men's Attitudes (1962) treated propaganda as a pervasive sociological phenomenon of technological society.
