In Christianity, the Incarnation is the belief that Jesus Christ, the second person of the Trinity — God the Son — "became flesh" by being conceived in the womb of the Virgin Mary and born as a human being, while remaining fully God. It is a central doctrine of mainstream Christianity, summarised in the Gospel of John: "The Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us" (John 1:14).

Biblical basis

The birth narratives of Matthew and Luke describe Jesus' conception by the Holy Spirit and his birth in Bethlehem. John's Gospel presents Jesus as the pre-existent Logos (Word) who was with God and was God, and who became flesh. Paul's letter to the Philippians quotes an early hymn describing Christ as being "in very nature God" yet emptying himself, "being made in human likeness" — the passage from which the doctrine of kenosis (self-emptying) is drawn.

Doctrinal development

How one person could be both fully God and fully man was the defining question of early Christology. Successive councils rejected positions judged to divide or blur the two natures: adoptionism (Jesus a mere man adopted by God), docetism (his humanity only apparent), Arianism (the Son a created being), Nestorianism (two loosely joined persons) and monophysitism (a single, merged nature). The Council of Chalcedon (451) settled the classical formula: Christ is one person (hypostasis) in two natures, divine and human, united "without confusion, change, division or separation" — the hypostatic union. Because Mary gave birth to that one divine person, she is honoured as Theotokos, "God-bearer".

Significance

In Christian teaching the Incarnation is the foundation of salvation: only one who is truly God can save, and only one who is truly human can represent humanity and undergo death on its behalf — a logic classically developed in Anselm's Cur Deus Homo ("Why God Became Man"). It also grounds the Christian affirmation of the material world: because God has taken flesh, matter can mediate grace, which underlies sacramental theology and, historically, the defence of religious images in the iconoclastic controversy. The Incarnation is celebrated at Christmas, and its announcement to Mary at the Annunciation (25 March, nine months earlier).
