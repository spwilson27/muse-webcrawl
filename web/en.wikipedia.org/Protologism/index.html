Protologism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Protologism
TypeNewly coined word or expression
Coined byMikhail Epstein, 2003
StageProposed, but not yet in general use

A protologism is a newly proposed word or expression that has been coined but has not yet become accepted into mainstream language. The term itself is an example: it was introduced by the literary theorist Mikhail Epstein in 2003 (from Greek protos, "first", and logos, "word"), as part of the study of how new words enter a language.

Life cycle of new words

Epstein's scheme distinguishes stages in the career of a coinage. A protologism exists only in the act of proposal and a handful of early uses; if it begins to circulate more widely among a community it becomes a neologism; and only if it gains durable, general currency does it become an ordinary word of the language. The vast majority of protologisms never advance beyond the first stage.

Related concepts

A nonce word is coined for a single occasion, while a hapax legomenon is a word attested only once in a corpus. Dictionaries track candidate words for years before admission, requiring evidence of sustained, independent use. Deliberate coinages that succeeded include "quark" and "robot"; invented vocabularies proposed by reformers, such as the Solresol language or systematic word-building projects, are rich sources of protologisms.
