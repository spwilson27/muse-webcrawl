Substitution cipher

In cryptography, a substitution cipher is a method of encryption in which each unit of plaintext is systematically replaced with a unit of ciphertext according to a fixed rule. The units may be single letters (the most common case), pairs of letters, or larger groups. The receiver deciphers the text by performing the inverse substitution.

Types

A simple substitution cipher replaces each letter with a fixed other letter throughout the message; the Caesar cipher, in which each letter is shifted a fixed number of positions (ROT13 is the shift-by-13 special case), and the Hebrew Atbash cipher are examples. Despite an astronomically large key space — 26! possible alphabets — simple substitution is easily broken by frequency analysis, first described by the Arab polymath Al-Kindi in the 9th century, because letter frequencies in the ciphertext mirror those of the language.

Polyalphabetic ciphers, such as the Vigenère cipher, use multiple substitution alphabets selected by a keyword, flattening frequency statistics and resisting casual analysis for three centuries before general solution methods emerged in the 19th century. Homophonic substitution, nomenclators (mixing code words with cipher), and digraphic systems such as the Playfair cipher were all developed to strengthen substitution against cryptanalysis.

Legacy

Substitution survives in modern cryptography as a component: the S-boxes of block ciphers such as AES perform (much more sophisticated) substitution within a substitution–permutation network. Simple substitution ciphers also persist as newspaper cryptogram puzzles.
