Incest taboo

An incest taboo is a cultural rule or norm of a society which prohibits sexual relations between closely related persons ("consanguinity"). Nearly all human societies forbid sexual relations and marriage between parents and children and between siblings, though the range of prohibited relatives — cousins, for example — varies widely, and some societies historically permitted royal sibling marriage (as in Ptolemaic Egypt, Inca Peru, and Hawai'i) to preserve dynastic purity.

Explanations

Anthropologists and psychologists have offered several explanations. The Westermarck effect proposes an evolved sexual aversion between individuals raised together in childhood. Édouard Westermarck's hypothesis has been supported by studies of Israeli kibbutz peer groups and Taiwanese "minor marriages." Sigmund Freud, by contrast, argued in Totem and Taboo that the taboo suppresses an innate desire. Claude Lévi-Strauss famously treated the taboo as the foundation of culture itself, compelling exogamy and the exchange of spouses that creates alliances between groups.

Biological considerations

Inbreeding increases the probability of inheriting two copies of deleterious recessive alleles (inbreeding depression), which provides an evolutionary rationale for incest avoidance observed in many animal species as well.

Legal treatment

Most legal systems criminalize incest within defined degrees of kinship, though definitions differ between jurisdictions.
