Liverworts are non-vascular land plants of the division Marchantiophyta, among the earliest-diverging lineages of land plants. Together with mosses and hornworts they form the group informally called the bryophytes. The English name derives from the old doctrine of signatures: the lobed, liver-shaped thallus of species such as Marchantia was thought to resemble a liver and to cure liver ailments.

Description

Like other bryophytes, liverworts have a life cycle dominated by the haploid gametophyte generation. They occur in two main growth forms: thallose liverworts, whose bodies are flat, ribbon-like thalli without distinct stems and leaves (e.g., Marchantia), and leafy liverworts, which bear two or three ranks of small overlapping leaves on slender stems (the large order Jungermanniales accounts for most species). Liverwort cells characteristically contain unique membrane-bound oil bodies. Lacking true roots, stems, and vascular tissue, they anchor themselves with unicellular rhizoids and absorb water over their surfaces.

Reproduction

Sexual reproduction involves motile sperm swimming to egg cells held in flask-shaped archegonia; the resulting sporophyte remains attached to and dependent on the gametophyte, releasing spores from a capsule, often aided by hygroscopic elaters that twist as they dry. Many species also reproduce asexually by fragmentation, and thallose forms such as Marchantia polymorpha produce gemmae—small discs of tissue borne in splash cups on the thallus surface.

Ecology and evolution

Liverworts grow on soil, rock, bark, and decaying wood, from tropical rainforests to Arctic tundra and even a few aquatic or desert habitats. They are important pioneers on bare ground and can dominate the ground layer of cool, moist forests. Fossil evidence and molecular studies place liverworts among the first land plants, with origins in the Ordovician period or earlier, more than 450 million years ago; the model species Marchantia polymorpha is now widely used in evolutionary developmental biology.
