David George Kendall FRS (15 January 1918 – 23 October 2007) was an English statistician and mathematician, known for his work on probability, statistical shape analysis, and queueing theory. He spent much of his career at the University of Oxford and the University of Cambridge.

Early life and career

Kendall was born in Ripon, Yorkshire, and educated at Ripon Grammar School and Queen's College, Oxford, where he read mathematics. During the Second World War he worked on rocketry research. After the war he joined Magdalen College, Oxford, where he developed the study of stochastic processes, branching processes, and queues.

In 1962 Kendall moved to Cambridge as the first Professor of Mathematical Statistics, founding a group that became the Statistical Laboratory. He remained in Cambridge for the rest of his career, becoming director of the laboratory and a fellow of Churchill College.

Contributions

Kendall introduced the standard notation for queueing systems (Kendall's notation, of the form A/S/c), in which the letters describe the arrival process, the service-time distribution, and the number of servers. He made foundational contributions to the theory of Markov processes, population processes, and random processes on groups.

In later life he pioneered the statistical theory of shape, developing Kendall's shape space: the study of configurations of points in Euclidean space modulo similarity transformations. This "Kendall shape theory" has applications in archaeology, biology, and image analysis. Kendall was also an ordained Anglican priest, one of the few senior academics to combine a chair with holy orders.

Honours

Kendall was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1964. The Royal Statistical Society awarded him its Guy Medal in Silver (1955) and in Gold (1981), and he received the Sylvester Medal of the Royal Society in 1976 and the De Morgan Medal of the London Mathematical Society in 1989.
