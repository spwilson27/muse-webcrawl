Gnosticism

Gnosticism (from Koine Greek γνωστικός, gnōstikós, "having knowledge") is a collection of religious ideas and systems that coalesced in the late 1st and early 2nd centuries AD among early Christian and Jewish sects. These diverse groups shared a conviction that the material world was created by an imperfect or malevolent god — the demiurge — distinct from the supreme, hidden God, and that salvation comes through gnosis, direct esoteric knowledge of the divine spark imprisoned within the human self. The early Church condemned Gnostic teachers such as Valentinus, Basilides and Marcion (whose inclusion is debated) as heretics.

Beliefs

Classic Gnostic systems describe a remote, unknowable God who emanates a series of divine beings (aeons) forming the Pleroma, or fullness. Through a crisis — often the fall of the aeon Sophia (Wisdom) — the demiurge comes into being and, in ignorance or arrogance, fabricates the material cosmos and proclaims himself the only god. Humanity carries a fragment of the divine light; a savior figure (often Christ, Seth, or another revealer) descends to awaken the elect to their true origin. Ethics ranged from strict asceticism to, in hostile reports, libertinism, both expressing contempt for the material order.

Major schools and texts

Scholars distinguish Sethian and Valentinian traditions, among others. Sethian texts include the Apocryphon of John, the most widely attested Gnostic work, while the Valentinian school — founded by Valentinus, who taught in Rome in the mid-2nd century — produced the Gospel of Truth and the Tripartite Tractate and came closest to forming a church within the Church. Related literature includes the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Philip, and the Hermetic and Manichaean corpora, whose boundaries with "Gnosticism" remain contested.

Sources

Until the 20th century, knowledge of Gnosticism depended largely on hostile heresiologists such as Irenaeus (Against Heresies, c. 180), Hippolytus, Tertullian and Epiphanius. The discovery in 1945 of the Nag Hammadi library — thirteen Coptic codices buried near the Egyptian town of Nag Hammadi — gave scholars direct access to the texts themselves and transformed the field.

Decline and legacy

Suppressed by the orthodox Church and the Christianized Roman Empire, the classic Gnostic sects had largely vanished by the 5th century, though related movements (Paulicians, Bogomils, Cathars) echoed some themes in the Middle Ages, and Mandaeism survives today. Modern scholars such as Michael Williams and Karen King have questioned whether "Gnosticism" is a coherent category at all, preferring terms like "biblical demiurgical" traditions. The movement nonetheless remains a byword for esoteric, world-denying spirituality and has strongly influenced modern esotericism, depth psychology (Jung), and popular culture.
