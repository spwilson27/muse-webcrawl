Biochar

Biochar is a charcoal-like substance produced by heating biomass in a low-oxygen environment in a process called pyrolysis. It is used as a soil amendment and is studied as a means of carbon sequestration, because the carbon it contains can remain locked in soils for hundreds to thousands of years, potentially mitigating climate change.

Production

Biochar is made by pyrolysis — heating organic material such as wood chips, crop residues, or manure at temperatures typically between 300 °C and 700 °C with little or no oxygen. The process also yields bio-oil and syngas, which can be used as fuels. Slow pyrolysis maximizes the proportion of solid char produced.

Uses in soil

Interest in biochar was spurred by the discovery of terra preta, the unusually fertile dark soils created by pre-Columbian peoples in the Amazon Basin, in which charcoal was mixed with organic waste. Added to soil, biochar can improve water retention, nutrient availability, and microbial habitat, and it can raise crop yields, particularly in acidic and degraded tropical soils. Because its porous structure and aromatic carbon are highly resistant to decay, biochar persists in soil far longer than uncharred organic matter.

Climate role

By converting biomass carbon into a stable form and burying it in soil, biochar production is considered a potential carbon-negative technology: the carbon dioxide absorbed by the growing plants is partly kept out of the atmosphere for centuries. Estimates of its global potential vary, and researchers note that benefits depend on sustainable feedstock sourcing and soil type.
