The Buchenwald trial was a war crimes trial held by the United States Army as part of the Dachau trials, conducted from 11 April to 14 August 1947 at Dachau, Germany. Thirty-one former staff and officials of the Buchenwald concentration camp and its subcamps were tried for violations of the laws and customs of war in connection with the abuse and murder of Allied prisoners and other inmates between 1937 and 1945.

Background

Buchenwald, near Weimar, was one of the largest concentration camps in Nazi Germany; some 280,000 people were imprisoned there and over 56,000 died. American forces liberated the camp on 11 April 1945 — the trial deliberately opened on the second anniversary. The case formed part of the American program of trying camp personnel under a "common design" theory, in which participation in the operation of a criminal system constituted a war crime.

Defendants and verdicts

Prominent defendants included Josias, Prince of Waldeck and Pyrmont, the senior SS officer for the region, who received life imprisonment; Hermann Pister, the camp commandant, sentenced to death but died before execution; Hans Schmidt, adjutant, executed in 1951 — one of the last war criminals hanged at Landsberg; and Ilse Koch, widow of the first commandant Karl-Otto Koch, whose life sentence (reduced on review, provoking a U.S. Senate inquiry) was followed by a separate German trial that again sentenced her to life. All 31 defendants were convicted; 22 were initially sentenced to death, though most sentences were later reduced in the clemency reviews of the early 1950s. A follow-on proceeding, the Buchenwald "subsequent" cases, tried additional personnel, and West and East German courts conducted later trials of individual perpetrators.

Legacy

Together with the Dachau, Mauthausen and other camp trials, the Buchenwald trial documented the machinery of the concentration camp system for the historical record and established precedents for prosecuting systematic atrocity.
