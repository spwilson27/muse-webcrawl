Go motion

Go motion is a technique of stop-motion animation in which the model is moved slightly by computer-controlled motors during each frame's exposure, producing realistic motion blur that conventional stop motion lacks. It was developed at Industrial Light & Magic (ILM) around 1979–1980 by Phil Tippett and Jon Berg for the film The Empire Strikes Back (1980).

Development and use

Traditional stop-motion puppets appear unnaturally sharp and staccato on screen because each frame is photographed with the model perfectly still, unlike live-action footage where moving objects blur. For the tauntaun and AT-AT sequences of The Empire Strikes Back, Tippett's team attached stepper motors to the puppet joints, connected by rods to a computer that nudged the model during exposure, smearing each frame slightly. The technique was refined for the dragon Vermithrax in Dragonslayer (1981) — often considered its finest showcase — and used in films including RoboCop (1987), Willow (1988) and the Tippett Studio sequences of Jurassic Park (1993), before computer-generated imagery largely displaced model animation.

Comparison with stop motion

Whereas in stop motion the animator repositions the puppet between frames, in go motion the movements are programmed and partially executed during the exposure itself. The result is smoother, more fluid motion with natural blur, at the cost of complex and expensive rigging. The related technique of "blurring" puppets by hand-moving them during exposure had earlier precedents, but go motion was the first systematic, computer-controlled implementation.
