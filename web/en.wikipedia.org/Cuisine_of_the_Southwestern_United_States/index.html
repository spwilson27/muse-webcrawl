Cuisine of the Southwestern United States

Enchiladas "Christmas style" — with both red and green chile — and ristras of dried peppers, emblematic of New Mexican cuisine.

The cuisine of the Southwestern United States is food styled after the rustic cooking in the Southwestern United States. It comprises a fusion of recipes for things that might have been eaten by Spanish colonial settlers, Mexican and Native American peoples throughout the post-Columbian era; though there is broad overlap in style with Mexican cuisine, the key difference is in the use of particular varieties of chile peppers unavailable south of the border.

Regional styles

New Mexican cuisine is the most distinctive branch, built on the New Mexico chile (in red and green forms; ordering both is "Christmas"), blue corn, piñon nuts, sopapillas with honey, posole, carne adovada, and stacked enchiladas topped with a fried egg. Tex-Mex, from Texas, features chili con carne (the official state dish), fajitas, breakfast tacos, nachos and yellow-cheese-laden combination plates, blending Mexican staples with Anglo and German/Czech immigrant influences. Arizona cooking adds Sonoran-style flour tortillas, chimichangas (claimed for Tucson), and the Sonoran hot dog wrapped in bacon.

Ingredients and influences

The foundation crops are the Indigenous "three sisters" — corn, beans and squash — grown for millennia in the region's drylands, supplemented by chiles and tomatoes from Mesoamerica and wheat, beef, pork, cheese and frying techniques brought by the Spanish from 1598 onward. Native nations including the Pueblo, Navajo (Diné) — originators of frybread — and Tohono O'odham shaped the tradition profoundly. The cuisine is today celebrated in song and story alongside country music as an emblem of borderlands identity, and it draws on dryland farming traditions akin to agroforestry in its use of drought-adapted heritage crops.
