Verisimilitude

Verisimilitude (from Latin verisimilitudo, "likeness to truth") is the appearance of being true or real. The term is used in two main contexts: in literary and narrative theory, where it denotes the internal plausibility or believability of a fictional work, and in the philosophy of science, where it denotes the closeness of a (strictly false) theory to the truth — also called truthlikeness.

In literature

Since Aristotle's Poetics, critics have held that a convincing impossibility is preferable to an unconvincing possibility: fiction succeeds when events follow probability or necessity within the world the work establishes. Verisimilitude in narrative is achieved through consistent characterisation, realistic detail, coherent motivation, and conformity to the conventions of a genre. It is related to, but distinct from, realism as a literary movement; even fantasy may be praised for verisimilitude when its invented elements are consistently developed. Samuel Taylor Coleridge's "willing suspension of disbelief" describes the reader's side of the bargain.

In philosophy of science

Karl Popper proposed verisimilitude as the goal of science: although theories such as Newtonian mechanics are, strictly, false, one theory can be closer to the truth than another by having greater true content and less false content. Popper's formal definition (a comparison of truth-content and falsity-content classes) was later shown by David Miller and Pavel Tichý (1974) to fail for false theories, prompting a continuing research programme in formal truthlikeness, with measures based on similarity between possible worlds or constituents.
