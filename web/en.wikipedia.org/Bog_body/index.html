Bog body
DefinitionA human cadaver naturally mummified in a peat bog
Main regionsNorthern Europe (Denmark, Germany, the Netherlands, Britain, Ireland)
Notable examplesTollund Man, Grauballe Man, Lindow Man, Yde Girl, Windeby I
Datesc. 8000 BCE – early medieval period; most Iron Age

A bog body is a human cadaver that has been naturally mummified in a peat bog. Such bodies, sometimes known as bog people, are both geographically and chronologically widespread, having been dated to between 8000 BCE and the Second World War. The unifying factor of the bog bodies is that they have been found in peat and are partially preserved; however, the actual levels of preservation vary widely from perfectly preserved to mere skeletons.

Unlike most ancient human remains, bog bodies retain their skin and internal organs due to the unusual conditions of the surrounding area. Combined, highly acidic water, low temperature and a lack of oxygen preserve but severely tan their skin. While the skin is well-preserved, the bones are generally not, due to the dissolution of the calcium phosphate of bone by the peat's acidity.

Preservation chemistry

The preservation of bog bodies is dependent on a set of natural chemical phenomena. In raised bogs, Sphagnum moss releases a substance called sphagnan that tans the skin and inhibits bacterial growth. The cold, oxygen-free, acidic water prevents the decay that would normally destroy soft tissue. Hair, nails and skin survive, often turned a deep red-brown colour, while bone may be demineralised and rubbery.

Famous examples

The Tollund Man, found in Denmark in 1950, is among the best preserved, with his face seemingly asleep and a noose still around his neck. The Grauballe Man had his throat cut. Lindow Man, discovered in Cheshire, England, in 1984, suffered multiple violent injuries — the so-called "triple death" of blows to the head, strangulation and a cut throat — suggesting ritual killing. The Yde Girl from the Netherlands and the Windeby bodies from Germany add to the evidence that many bog people died violently.

Interpretation

Many bog bodies show signs of violent death, and scholars have long debated whether they were executed criminals, human sacrifices, or victims of robbery. Tacitus, in his Germania, described Germanic peoples drowning cowards and the "infamous" in bogs, a passage often linked to the Iron Age finds. More recent work emphasises the diversity of the phenomenon: some individuals show no trauma at all and may simply have been buried in peat.
