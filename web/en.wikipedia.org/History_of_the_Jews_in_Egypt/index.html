History of the Jews in Egypt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of the Jews in Egypt reaches back to antiquity: Jewish communities existed in Egypt from at least the Persian period, most famously the military colony at Elephantine (5th century BC) and the great Hellenistic community of Alexandria, which produced the Septuagint translation of the Bible and the philosophy of Philo. Egyptian Jewry flourished under Islam—Cairo's Ben Ezra Synagogue preserved the famous Cairo Geniza—and a large, cosmopolitan community existed into the mid-20th century, numbering some 75,000–80,000 in the 1940s. Following the creation of Israel, the 1948 war, the 1952 revolution, and especially the Suez Crisis of 1956, nearly all Egyptian Jews emigrated or were expelled; only a handful remain today.

Ancient period

The Hebrew Bible's narratives of the Exodus are set in Egypt, though direct historical evidence begins later: the Elephantine papyri document a Jewish garrison colony with its own temple of Yahu on Elephantine island near Aswan under Persian rule. Under the Ptolemies, Alexandria became the greatest centre of the Jewish diaspora; Jews formed a large share of the city's population, translated the Torah into Greek (the Septuagint, 3rd century BC), and created a rich Greek-Jewish literature culminating in Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC – AD 50). Tensions with the Greek population erupted in pogroms under Caligula (AD 38), and the great revolt of AD 115–117 (the Kitos War) effectively destroyed Egyptian Jewry for a time.

Under Islam

Jewish life revived under Arab rule from the 7th century. Fustat (Old Cairo) became a major centre of learning and commerce; the documents of the Cairo Geniza—a storeroom of the Ben Ezra Synagogue preserving some 300,000 manuscript fragments—illuminate Mediterranean Jewish life across the medieval centuries, as documented by the historian S. D. Goitein. Maimonides (1138–1204), the pre-eminent medieval Jewish philosopher and physician to the court of Saladin's vizier, lived and worked in Fustat and led Egyptian Jewry. The community included both Rabbanites and Karaites, and Egyptian Jews traded throughout the Mediterranean and to India.

Modern era

In the 19th and early 20th centuries, under Muhammad Ali's modernisation and the British occupation, the Jewish population grew with immigration from across the Mediterranean and the Middle East. Egyptian Jews—Sephardi, Ashkenazi, and Karaite—were prominent in banking, retail, the press, and the cotton trade; families such as the Cicurels, Rolos, and Suares were pillars of the economy, and Jews were prominent in Egyptian cultural life, including early Egyptian cinema. The Capitulations and foreign protection gave many Jews foreign nationality, creating a complex legal status.

Departure

The 1948 Arab–Israeli war brought riots, arrests, and sequestration of property; after the 1952 revolution and Nasser's rise, conditions worsened. In November 1956, during the Suez war with Britain, France, and Israel, the government expelled thousands of Jews holding foreign nationality and interned and expropriated many others; some 25,000 left within months. Further departures followed the 1967 Six-Day War, when hundreds of Jewish men were imprisoned for up to three years. By the 1970s the community had dwindled to a few hundred; today only a small number of elderly Jews remain in Cairo and Alexandria, while synagogues and cemeteries are preserved as heritage—including the restored Ben Ezra Synagogue and Alexandria's Eliyahu Hanavi Synagogue, reopened in 2020.
