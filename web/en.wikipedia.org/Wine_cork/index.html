Wine cork

A wine cork is a stopper made from the bark of the cork oak (Quercus suber), used to seal wine bottles. Cork's elasticity, impermeability to liquids and slow permeability to oxygen have made it the traditional closure for wine since the 17th century.

Production

Most cork comes from Portugal and Spain. Bark is stripped by hand every nine to twelve years without harming the tree; the bark is boiled, seasoned and punched into stoppers. Natural corks are graded by quality, while technical corks are made from agglomerated granules, sometimes with natural discs at each end.

Cork taint and alternatives

A small percentage of cork-sealed wines are affected by "cork taint", caused mainly by the compound 2,4,6-trichloroanisole (TCA), which gives a musty, mouldy aroma. This, together with cost and consistency concerns, drove adoption of screw caps and synthetic closures, especially for wines meant to be drunk young, though natural cork remains dominant for premium and age-worthy wines.
