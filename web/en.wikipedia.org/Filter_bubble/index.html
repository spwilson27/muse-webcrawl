Filter bubble

Filter bubble
Term coined byEli Pariser (2011)
MechanismAlgorithmic personalisation of search results and feeds
Alleged effectIntellectual isolation, reinforced prior beliefs
Related conceptsEcho chamber, confirmation bias, recommender system

A filter bubble is a state of intellectual isolation that can arise when websites and platforms use algorithms to selectively guess what information a user would like to see, based on their search history, clicks, location and other data — and accordingly show them content that confirms their existing views while hiding contrary material. The term was coined by internet activist Eli Pariser in his 2011 book The Filter Bubble: What the Internet Is Hiding from You, which described personalised Google search results and Facebook's News Feed as creating "a unique universe of information for each of us".

Concerns

Pariser and others argue that filter bubbles can deepen political polarisation, entrench confirmation bias, reduce serendipitous exposure to new ideas, and make personalisation invisible and unaccountable — unlike a newspaper's slant, which readers choose knowingly. The concept is closely related to, but distinct from, the echo chamber: a filter bubble is imposed by algorithmic curation, whereas an echo chamber arises mainly from people's own choices of sources and communities.

Evidence and debate

Empirical research gives a mixed picture. Some studies find that search engines and social media actually increase the diversity of news users encounter compared with their offline habits, and that most users are exposed to cross-cutting views; others find measurable ideological segregation in particular platforms and periods, and strong effects among highly partisan minorities. Critics note that "filter bubble" arguments can understate user agency, while supporters reply that engagement-optimising algorithms amplify emotive and congenial content in ways that matter democratically regardless of average exposure.

Responses

Proposed responses include algorithmic transparency and user controls, chronological feeds, "burst your bubble" tools that surface opposing perspectives, public-service media, and digital literacy education. Some platforms have introduced diversity nudges and explanations of why content is recommended.
