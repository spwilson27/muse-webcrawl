Good Samaritan law

Good Samaritan law
TypeLegal protection for rescuers
PurposeEncourage bystanders to assist others in peril
Name originParable of the Good Samaritan

Good Samaritan laws offer legal protection to people who give reasonable assistance to those who are, or whom they believe to be, injured, ill, in peril, or otherwise incapacitated. The protection is intended to reduce bystanders' hesitation to assist, out of fear of being sued or prosecuted for unintentional injury or wrongful death. The laws take their name from the biblical Parable of the Good Samaritan.

Principles

Common features of Good Samaritan statutes include: the rescuer must act voluntarily and without expectation of payment; the aid must have been given in good faith and as a reasonable person would act; the victim's consent is presumed if they are unconscious; and protection is generally lost if the rescuer acts with gross negligence or willful misconduct. Typically the laws apply only at the scene of an emergency, outside a hospital or facility where the rescuer is employed to give such care.

United States

All fifty U.S. states and the District of Columbia have some form of Good Samaritan statute, though their scope varies. Some states protect only medically trained rescuers, while others protect any person. Many states have added specific protections for administering naloxone to reverse opioid overdoses and for using automated external defibrillators (AEDs). A few states, such as Minnesota and Vermont, go further and impose a legal duty to rescue, a concept far more common in civil-law countries.

Other jurisdictions

Canada's provinces have enacted broad Good Samaritan acts; China's Good Samaritan law took effect in 2017 following public concern over cases such as the Peng Yu incident; India adopted protections in 2016 after studies showed bystanders feared police harassment and legal entanglement.
