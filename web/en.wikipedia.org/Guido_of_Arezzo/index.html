Guido of Arezzo

Guido's innovations made it possible to learn new chants in days rather than years.

Guido of Arezzo (Italian: Guido Aretinus; c. 991 – after 1033) was an Italian Benedictine monk and music theorist of the medieval era. He is regarded as the inventor — or at least the first codifier — of modern musical staff notation, which replaced the earlier neumatic notation and revolutionised the teaching and transmission of Gregorian chant.

Innovations

In his treatise Micrologus (c. 1026) and related writings, Guido described the four-line staff, the use of lines and spaces to fix pitch, clefs, and a system of solmisation derived from the hymn Ut queant laxis: the syllables ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la taken from its successive phrases gave rise to the hexachord system and ultimately to the modern solfège syllables (with ut later replaced by do and si/ti added). The "Guidonian hand" mapped pitches onto the joints of the hand as a mnemonic device.

Life

Guido trained at the abbey of Pomposa, where his methods aroused jealousy, and later settled in Arezzo under Bishop Tedald, for whom he wrote the Micrologus. Pope John XIX summoned him to Rome to demonstrate his method.
