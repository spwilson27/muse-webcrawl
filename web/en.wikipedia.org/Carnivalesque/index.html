Carnivalesque

Carnivalesque is a literary mode that subverts and liberates the assumptions of the dominant style or atmosphere through humour and chaos. The concept originates in the work of the Russian theorist Mikhail Bakhtin, above all in Rabelais and His World (1965) and Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics.

Bakhtin's theory

Bakhtin drew on the tradition of carnival in medieval and early modern Europe — festivals in which social hierarchies were temporarily inverted, fools were crowned kings, and sacred things were parodied. He argued that this "second life of the people" produced a distinct worldview: free and familiar contact between people, eccentricity, profanation, and crowning/decrowning, all expressed in grotesque imagery of the body and the "material bodily lower stratum."

In literature

Bakhtin traced the carnivalesque through Rabelais's Gargantua and Pantagruel and the polyphonic novels of Dostoevsky. Later critics have applied the idea to writers such as Shakespeare, Cervantes, Dickens, and Angela Carter, and to popular culture more broadly.

Criticism and influence

Scholars debate whether carnival genuinely liberates or merely provides a licensed safety valve that reinforces authority. The concept has nonetheless become central to literary studies, cultural studies, and theories of festivity, parody, and the grotesque.
