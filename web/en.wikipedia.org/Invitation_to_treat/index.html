Invitation to treat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An invitation to treat (or invitation to bargain in the United States) is a concept in contract law: an action inviting other parties to make an offer to form a contract, rather than an offer itself. Because it is not an offer, an invitation to treat cannot be "accepted" to create a binding contract. The distinction matters practically: a shopkeeper who displays priced goods is generally not obliged to sell them at the marked price to anyone who demands them.

Examples

  • Shop displays: In Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots Cash Chemists [1953], goods on self-service shelves were held to be invitations to treat; the customer's offer is made at the till and accepted by the cashier. In Fisher v Bell [1961], a flick knife displayed in a shop window was likewise an invitation to treat, not an offer for sale.
  • Advertisements: advertisements are generally invitations to treat (Partridge v Crittenden [1968]), unless they amount to a unilateral offer, as in the reward poster in Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co [1893].
  • Auctions: calls for bids are invitations to treat; each bid is an offer that the auctioneer may accept or reject, a sale without reserve being a recognised exception.
  • Tenders and price lists: invitations to tender and circulated catalogues are usually invitations to treat, though the tendering process itself may give rise to a collateral "process contract".

Rationale

The doctrine protects sellers from being bound to unlimited numbers of customers when stock is limited, preserves freedom of contract (including refusing particular buyers where lawful), and reflects the commercial understanding that displays and advertisements are preliminaries to negotiation rather than promises to sell.

Online commerce

Website product pages are usually treated as invitations to treat, with the customer's order constituting the offer and the retailer's dispatch or confirmation constituting acceptance — a structure that has been tested when pricing errors led to floods of orders for absurdly cheap goods.
