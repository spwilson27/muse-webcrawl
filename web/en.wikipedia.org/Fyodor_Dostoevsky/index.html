Fyodor Dostoevsky

Fyodor Mikhailovich Dostoevsky (11 November 1821 – 9 February 1881) was a Russian novelist, short-story writer, essayist, and journalist, regarded as one of the greatest novelists in world literature. His works explore the human condition in the troubled political, social, and spiritual atmosphere of 19th-century Russia, and engage with a range of philosophical and religious themes.

Life

Born in Moscow, Dostoevsky was introduced to literature early through fairy tales and the works of Russian and European writers. He studied military engineering in Saint Petersburg and briefly served as an officer before resigning to write. His first novel, Poor Folk (1846), brought him recognition. In 1849 he was arrested for membership in the Petrashevsky Circle, a literary discussion group critical of the regime, and subjected to a mock execution before his sentence was commuted to four years of Siberian exile followed by military service. The experience, together with his epilepsy, profoundly shaped his later writing and his turn toward Orthodox Christianity and conservative views.

Major works

After his return he published Notes from Underground (1864), often considered an early existentialist work, followed by the great novels Crime and Punishment (1866), The Idiot (1869), Demons (1872), and The Brothers Karamazov (1880). His fiction is characterised by polyphonic narration — multiple independent voices in dialogue — and intense psychological exploration of faith, morality, free will, and murder. He also wrote the novellas The Gambler and The Double, and the memoir of prison life The House of the Dead.

Legacy

Dostoevsky influenced countless writers and thinkers, from Nietzsche and Freud (who called The Brothers Karamazov "the most magnificent novel ever written") to modernist and existentialist literature. His works have been translated into more than 170 languages. He died in Saint Petersburg in 1881 and was buried in the Tikhvin Cemetery of the Alexander Nevsky Monastery.
