A dike or dyke, in levee usage, is a long wall or embankment built to prevent flooding from the sea or from rivers. Dikes are among the oldest forms of flood defence and are characteristic of low-lying coastal regions such as the Netherlands, where roughly a third of the country lies below sea level and would be inundated without them.

Construction

Traditional dikes consist of a core of sand or clay covered with a protective layer of clay, grass, basalt blocks, or asphalt. The seaward slope is usually gentle, allowing waves to dissipate their energy, while the landward side may be steeper. Modern Dutch sea dikes are designed to withstand storm surges with return periods of up to 10,000 years in the most densely populated provinces.

History

Artificial mounds called terpen were raised in the northern Netherlands and Germany from around 500 BC, and organised dike building began in the early Middle Ages. The catastrophic St. Lucia's flood of 1287, which created the Zuiderzee, spurred large-scale investment in sea defences. After the North Sea flood of 1953, the Netherlands undertook the Delta Works, one of the largest hydraulic engineering projects in the world.

Failure

Dikes fail by overtopping, when water flows over the crest, or by piping, when seepage erodes the interior. A breach can cause rapid, deep flooding of the protected polder. For this reason dikes are continuously inspected and maintained by regional water boards (waterschappen), among the oldest democratic institutions in the Netherlands.
