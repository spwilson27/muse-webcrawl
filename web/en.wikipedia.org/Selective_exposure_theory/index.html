Selective exposure theory

Selective exposure is a theory within the practice of psychology and the academic discipline of communications that, historically, referred to individuals' tendency to favour information that reinforces their pre-existing views while avoiding contradictory information. In this view, people actively select media and messages that are consistent with their attitudes and beliefs, a tendency linked to Leon Festinger's theory of cognitive dissonance (1957).

Origins and evidence

Early communication research of the 1940s and 1950s, including studies of election campaigns, found that voters mostly encountered messages supporting their existing choice and that media reinforced more than converted. Experiments showed people prefer congenial information and spend more time with it, especially when they are committed to a position, when the topic is important, and when the opposing information is perceived as low in quality or the decision is irreversible. Meta-analyses have found a consistent, moderate preference for attitude-consistent information.

Related concepts

Selective exposure is accompanied by selective perception (interpreting ambiguous information in line with one's views) and selective retention (remembering congenial messages better). In contemporary media studies the theory is connected to partisan media diets, "echo chambers" and "filter bubbles" online, though empirical studies find most people's actual information diets are more varied than the metaphor suggests.

Criticism and limits

Researchers note that exposure choices are constrained by availability, habit, and social context; incidental exposure to opposing views is common, and motives such as accuracy, entertainment, and surveillance compete with the drive to avoid dissonance. Some studies find curiosity about opposing views increases when people feel secure in their identity or when the opposing content is novel and useful.
