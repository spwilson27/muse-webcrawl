Flaxseed oil

Flaxseed oil, also known as linseed oil or flax oil (in its edible form), is a colourless to yellowish oil obtained from the dried, ripened seeds of the flax plant (Linum usitatissimum). The oil is obtained by cold pressing, sometimes followed by solvent extraction. It is one of the oldest commercial oils and has been used for centuries both as a food and, because it dries to a hard film on exposure to air, as a finish and binder in painting and woodworking.

Composition

Flaxseed oil is unusually rich in alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), an omega-3 fatty acid, which typically makes up roughly half or more of its fatty acids, alongside linoleic acid (omega-6), oleic acid and smaller amounts of saturated fats. Its high polyunsaturated content makes it reactive: the oil polymerises on exposure to oxygen, the basis of its use as a drying oil, but also makes edible oil prone to rancidity, so it is sold cold-pressed, kept refrigerated and not used for frying.

Uses

As linseed oil, it has been a traditional ingredient in oil paints, putty, wood finishes, varnishes, linoleum and oilcloth, and boiled linseed oil (processed with driers) is applied to wood and cricket bats. Industrially it also enters animal feed and some supplements. As a food and dietary supplement, flaxseed oil is consumed for its omega-3 content; health authorities note that ALA can be converted only inefficiently by the human body into the long-chain omega-3s EPA and DHA found in fish oil, and evidence for specific clinical benefits is limited, though the oil is a useful source of essential fatty acids, especially in plant-based diets.

Safety

Linseed-oil-soaked rags can spontaneously combust as the oil oxidises and heats, and should be stored in sealed metal containers or dried flat. Edible flaxseed oil is generally regarded as safe in culinary amounts, though high doses may have laxative effects and can interact with anticoagulant medication.
