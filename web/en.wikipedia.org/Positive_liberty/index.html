Positive liberty

Positive liberty is the concept of liberty as the possession of the power and resources to act and to fulfil one's own potential — freedom to do or be something, as distinct from negative liberty, freedom from interference by others. The distinction was given its canonical formulation by the philosopher Isaiah Berlin in his 1958 lecture "Two Concepts of Liberty."

Berlin's account

For Berlin, positive liberty answers the question "What, or who, is the source of control or interference that can determine someone to do, or be, this rather than that?" It concerns self-mastery: being one's own master, acting on one's rational will rather than being driven by appetite, ignorance, or circumstance. A person may be formally free from interference yet, through poverty, illness, or lack of education, lack any real ability to act — and so, in the positive sense, be unfree. Positive liberty thus underlies arguments for education, public health, and welfare provision as conditions of genuine freedom, an idea developed philosophically in T. H. Green's "freedom to make the best of ourselves" and, in a different register, in Amartya Sen's capability approach.

Criticisms

Berlin warned that positive liberty carried a characteristic danger: if the "true" or rational self is identified with a collective body — a class, nation, or party — coercion of actual people can be justified as liberating their real selves, a logic he associated with totalitarianism. Defenders reply that Berlin caricatured the concept, and that self-realisation need imply no split between higher and lower selves imposed from outside. The debate remains central to liberal political theory, structuring arguments over social justice, paternalism, and the welfare state.
