Roe

Roe is the general term for the fully ripe, unfertilised egg masses of fish and certain marine animals, such as shrimp, scallops, sea urchins, and squid. Roe can refer both to eggs still held together in the ovarian membrane — in which case they are eaten as a single mass — and to loose eggs consumed as a food, of which caviar from sturgeon is the most famous example.

Types

Roes vary widely in size, colour, and culinary use around the world. Caviar traditionally comes from sturgeon of the Caspian and Black Seas, with beluga, osetra, and sevruga grades. Salmon roe (ikura in Japanese cuisine) consists of large orange-red eggs used in sushi. Flying fish roe (tobiko) is small and crunchy, often dyed. Herring roe (kazunoko) is a New Year delicacy in Japan. Cod roe is eaten fresh, smoked, or as taramasalata when mixed into a dip common in Greek and Turkish cooking. Sea urchin gonads, called uni, are sometimes loosely referred to as roe.

Preparation and use

Roe may be eaten raw, cured in salt, smoked, fried, or cooked into dishes. In Scandinavia it is spread as a paste from tubes; in Britain, soft and hard cod roes are fried or served on toast. Bottarga — salted, pressed, and dried roe of grey mullet or tuna — is grated over pasta in Sardinian and Sicilian cooking.

Nutrition and sustainability

Roe is rich in protein, vitamin B12, and omega-3 fatty acids, but can also be high in sodium and cholesterol. Overfishing of sturgeon for caviar led to CITES protections and a shift toward farmed production.
