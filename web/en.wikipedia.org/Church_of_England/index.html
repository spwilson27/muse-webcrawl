The Church of England (C of E) is the established Christian church in England and the mother church of the international Anglican Communion. It traces its history to the Christian church recorded as existing in the Roman province of Britain by the 3rd century and to the 6th-century Gregorian mission to Kent led by Augustine of Canterbury. The church became independent of the authority of the pope in 1534, when Henry VIII broke with Rome over the annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon.

History

During the English Reformation the church adopted reformed theology under Edward VI, returned to Roman obedience under Mary I, and reached a lasting settlement under Elizabeth I in 1559. Its doctrine is expressed in the Thirty-nine Articles, its worship in the Book of Common Prayer (1549, revised 1662), and its polity retains bishops, priests, and deacons in historic succession. The church survived the Civil War, the Commonwealth's abolition of episcopacy, the Restoration, and the evangelical and Oxford movements of the 18th and 19th centuries, which reshaped Anglican spirituality.

Structure and worship

The monarch is the Supreme Governor of the Church of England, and the Archbishop of Canterbury is its senior bishop and spiritual leader. The church is organized into the provinces of Canterbury and York, 42 dioceses, and some 12,500 parishes. Its governing body is the General Synod, comprising houses of bishops, clergy, and laity; certain synod measures require approval by Parliament. Women have been ordained priests since 1994 and bishops since 2015.

Worship ranges from the choral tradition of cathedrals and the Book of Common Prayer to contemporary and charismatic styles. The church maintains around 16,000 church buildings, many of them listed historic structures, and runs thousands of schools.

Doctrine and identity

The Church of England describes itself as both Catholic and Reformed. Its identity has been summarized by the Chicago-Lambeth Quadrilateral and by an appeal to Scripture, tradition, and reason. It is the mother church of the Anglican Communion, a worldwide family of autonomous churches in communion with Canterbury numbering tens of millions of members.
