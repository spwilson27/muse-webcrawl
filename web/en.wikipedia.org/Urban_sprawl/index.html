Urban sprawl

Urban sprawl (also known as suburban sprawl or urban encroachment) is defined as "the spreading of urban developments (such as houses, dense multi-family apartments, office buildings and shopping centers) on undeveloped land near a more or less densely populated city". Urban sprawl has been described as the unrestricted growth in many urban areas of housing, commercial development, and roads over large expanses of land, with little concern for urban planning.

Characteristics

Sprawl is typically characterized by low-density, single-use zoning that separates homes from shops, offices, and schools; dependence on the private automobile; dispersed, leapfrog development; and road networks of cul-de-sacs and arterials designed for cars rather than pedestrians. The term emerged in debates about post-war American suburbanization, when federally backed mortgages, highway construction, and mass-produced suburbs such as Levittown drove explosive peripheral growth.

Criticisms

Critics associate sprawl with increased traffic congestion and air pollution, loss of farmland and wildlife habitat, higher public infrastructure costs per resident, obesity and reduced physical activity, and social segregation. Defenders argue that sprawl reflects consumer preferences for larger homes and yards, that suburban land and housing are more affordable, and that density has its own costs in congestion and prices.

Counter-movements

Responses include New Urbanism, smart growth policies, urban growth boundaries (as in Portland, Oregon), transit-oriented development, and infill redevelopment. Similar patterns of sprawl are now observed around rapidly growing cities in Asia, Africa, and Latin America, though often at higher densities and with greater informality than in the United States.
