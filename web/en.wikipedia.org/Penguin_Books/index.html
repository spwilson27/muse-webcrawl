Penguin Books

Penguin Books is a British publishing house founded in 1935 by Allen Lane and his brothers Richard and John. It revolutionised publishing by making high-quality fiction and non-fiction available cheaply in paperback, sold not only through bookshops but also in railway stations, newsagents and chain stores such as Woolworths.

History

Lane conceived the idea after finding only poor-quality reading material on a railway station platform. The first ten Penguin titles, published in 1935, included works by Ernest Hemingway, André Maurois and Agatha Christie, and cost sixpence each — the price of a packet of cigarettes. Their distinctive three-band colour-coded covers, designed by Edward Young, used orange for fiction, green for crime and blue for biography.

Penguin's success helped launch the paperback revolution. In 1937 it launched the Pelican imprint for non-fiction, and in 1940 Puffin for children's books. Penguin Classics, founded under E. V. Rieu in 1946 with his translation of Homer's Odyssey, became a major scholarly and popular series.

The Lady Chatterley trial

In 1960 Penguin published the first unexpurgated English edition of D. H. Lawrence's Lady Chatterley's Lover and was prosecuted under the Obscene Publications Act. Its acquittal at the Old Bailey in November 1960 was a landmark in British cultural history and a turning point in censorship law; the edition sold millions of copies.

Later history

Penguin became part of the Pearson group in 1970. In 2013 Penguin merged with Random House to form Penguin Random House, the world's largest trade book publisher, owned by Bertelsmann. The Penguin brand and its famous flightless-bird logo — refined over the years, notably by Jan Tschichold and later designers — remain among the most recognisable in publishing.
