The Maasai (also spelled Masai) are a Nilotic ethnic group inhabiting northern, central and southern Kenya and northern Tanzania, near the African Great Lakes region. They are among the best-known local populations internationally, owing to their residence near game parks, their distinctive dress — red checked shuka cloth, elaborate beadwork and ochred hair — and their semi-nomadic pastoralist culture centred on cattle.

The Maasai speak the Maa language, an Eastern Nilotic language related to Samburu and Turkana. Their population has been variously estimated at between one and three million; census figures in Kenya (about 1.2 million in 2019) and Tanzania are considered undercounts because of the group's mobility and contested enumeration.

History

Maasai oral tradition holds that they migrated south from the lower Nile valley, arriving in the Rift Valley region by the 17th–18th centuries and reaching their present territory in the 19th. Cattle-raiding expansion brought them into conflict with neighbouring agricultural peoples. The late 19th century brought catastrophe: the Emutai period (c. 1883–1902) combined rinderpest epidemics that destroyed their herds, smallpox, cholera and drought. In 1904 and 1911, agreements with the British colonial government moved the Kenyan Maasai into reserves, alienating much of their best land; in Tanzania, evictions from the Serengeti (1959) and continuing pressure around Ngorongoro and Loliondo have made land rights a defining political issue into the 21st century.

Culture and society

Maasai society is organised into patrilineal clans and an age-set system. Boys are circumcised into the moran (warrior) age grade, traditionally living apart, growing their hair and guarding the community's cattle; the transition to elder status is marked by the eunoto ceremony. Cattle are the measure of wealth and the centre of religious life: tradition holds that the god Enkai (Engai) gave all cattle to the Maasai. The traditional diet is based on milk, meat and (historically) blood drawn from live cattle.

Houses (enkaji) are built by women from mud, dung, sticks and grass, arranged in settlements (enkang or manyatta) ringed by thorn fences. Music consists of vocal harmonies without instruments, and the famous adumu "jumping dance" is performed by moran competing to leap highest from a standing position. Beadwork in red, blue, black and white encodes social status and identity. Female genital mutilation has traditionally been part of initiation for girls, a practice now opposed by Kenyan and Tanzanian law and by Maasai-led reform groups promoting alternative rites; early marriage and limited girls' schooling remain contested issues alongside rapid uptake of education and wage labour.

Land and conservation conflicts

Much Maasai land overlaps East Africa's celebrated protected areas — the Maasai Mara National Reserve (administered by the Maasai-majority Narok County), Amboseli, and Tanzania's Ngorongoro Conservation Area and Serengeti ecosystem. Tourism revenue-sharing has brought income and schools in Kenya, while in Tanzania state-backed conservation and hunting-concession policies have led to violent eviction disputes, notably in Loliondo in 2022. Maasai campaigners argue that fortress conservation dispossesses the very pastoralists whose grazing shaped the savanna ecosystem.
