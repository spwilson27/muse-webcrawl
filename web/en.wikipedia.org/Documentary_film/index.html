A documentary film or documentary is a non-fictional motion-picture intended to "document reality, primarily for instruction, education or maintaining a historical record". The Scottish filmmaker John Grierson, who first used the term in 1926 to describe Robert Flaherty's Moana, defined documentary as the "creative treatment of actuality".

Early film (pre-1900) was dominated by actualities — short non-fiction events. From 1900 onward, documentary grew into an established form, and by the mid-20th century it encompassed everything from sponsored industrial films to cinema vérité and, later, the popular feature documentary.

Definition

Documentary practice spans a spectrum from straightforward reportage to essayistic and poetic forms. What unites the genre is an indexical claim on the real: the footage, however shaped in editing, purports to record actual people, places and events rather than staged fiction.

History

Landmarks include the Lumière brothers' actualities (1895), Flaherty's Nanook of the North (1922), Vertov's Man with a Movie Camera (1929), the British documentary movement of the 1930s (Grierson, Night Mail), wartime propaganda, the direct cinema and cinéma vérité movements of the 1960s (enabled by lightweight sync-sound cameras), and the late-20th-century rise of the theatrical feature documentary (Hoop Dreams, Fahrenheit 9/11, March of the Penguins) and, in the 21st century, streaming-platform true-crime and nature series.

Modes

Bill Nichols's influential taxonomy identifies six modes of documentary representation:

  • Poetic — emphasizes visual associations and rhythm over argument
  • Expository — voice-of-God narration advancing an argument
  • Observational — fly-on-the-wall, minimal intervention
  • Participatory — filmmaker visibly interacts with subjects
  • Reflexive — draws attention to the filmmaking process itself
  • Performative — emphasizes subjective, embodied experience

Techniques

Common techniques include interviews, archival footage, reenactments, narration, on-screen text, and observational shooting. Ethical debates recur around staging, selective editing, consent, and the filmmaker's responsibility to subjects.
