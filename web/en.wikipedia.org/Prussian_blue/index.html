Prussian blue

Prussian blue pigment.

Formula (idealised)Fe7(CN)18
ColourDeep blue

Prussian blue is a dark blue pigment, chemically a hydrated iron(III) ferrocyanide complex, produced by the oxidation of ferrous ferrocyanide salts. It was discovered accidentally in Berlin around 1706 by the colour maker Johann Jacob Diesbach, and is regarded as the first modern synthetic pigment. Its intense colour, low cost, and stability made it one of the most important blue pigments of the 18th and 19th centuries.

History and art

Diesbach was attempting to make a red lake pigment when a contaminated batch of potash — adulterated with animal oil from the alchemist Johann Konrad Dippel — produced a deep blue instead. By 1710 the pigment was being sold across Europe as "Berlin blue" or Prussian blue. It transformed painting: Katsushika Hokusai used imported Prussian blue for the famous blue tones of The Great Wave off Kanagawa, and the pigment coloured countless European oil paintings, watercolours, and the blueprints of engineering drawing. Pablo Picasso used it prominently during his Blue Period.

Chemistry

Prussian blue's colour arises from intervalence charge transfer between iron(II) and iron(III) centres linked by cyanide bridges. The same material forms when iron salts react with ferrocyanides; the related test for ferric ions gives the pigment directly. Insoluble Prussian blue has the idealised formula Fe7(CN)18·xH2O. It is the archetype of the Prussian blue analogues, a large family of coordination-framework materials now studied for batteries and catalysis.

Medical and other uses

Pharmaceutical-grade Prussian blue (ferric hexacyanoferrate) is an approved antidote for poisoning with thallium and radioactive caesium (such as caesium-137): swallowed capsules bind the ions in the gut and accelerate their excretion, and it was used on a large scale after the 1987 Goiânia accident in Brazil. The pigment also underlies the cyanotype photographic process and blueprinting, gives a blue coloration for marking in histology (Perls' Prussian blue stain for iron), and appears in laundry "blueing" and some inks and paints.
