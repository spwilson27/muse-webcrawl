Malagasy is an Austronesian language spoken by the Malagasy people across Madagascar, where it is the national language and shares official status with French. It is the westernmost Austronesian language: its closest relatives are the East Barito languages of southern Borneo, especially Ma'anyan, reflecting the migration of seafarers from Island Southeast Asia to Madagascar roughly a millennium and a half ago. Malagasy also carries layers of loanwords from Bantu languages (notably Swahili), Arabic, French, and English.

Dialects and standard

Malagasy comprises a dozen or more regional dialects, often grouped into western and eastern varieties. The standard language is based on the Merina dialect of the central highlands around Antananarivo, which became dominant through the Merina kingdom in the 19th century. Missionaries from the London Missionary Society helped fix a Latin-based orthography in the 1820s–30s.

Grammar

Malagasy is famous among linguists for its basic verb–object–subject (VOS) word order, one of the rarest orders among the world's languages. Like other western Austronesian languages it has a symmetrical voice system, in which the verb marks whether the clause-final subject is the actor, patient, or circumstantial participant. It has a small phoneme inventory with prenasalised stops, and stress typically falls on the penultimate syllable. Reduplication is widely used for plurality, distribution, and attenuation.

Vocabulary

Core vocabulary is Austronesian — for example, maso "eye", tany "earth/land", and rano "water" are shared with Malay and other relatives. Bantu loans include many animal names, such as omby "cattle"; Arabic contributed weekday names and terms of divination; French supplies much modern administrative and technical vocabulary.
