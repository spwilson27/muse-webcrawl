Fibonacci (c. 1170 – c. 1240–50), also known as Leonardo of Pisa, was an Italian mathematician from the Republic of Pisa, considered one of the most talented Western mathematicians of the Middle Ages. His book Liber Abaci (1202) introduced the Hindu–Arabic numeral system to European commerce and scholarship.

Life

Leonardo was the son of Guglielmo Bonacci, a Pisan customs official posted to Béjaïa in North Africa, where the young Leonardo learned the Arabic system of numerals and recognised its superiority over Roman numerals. Travelling around the Mediterranean to study with Arab mathematicians, he returned to Pisa around 1200 and published Liber Abaci two years later. He later presented work at the court of Emperor Frederick II, whose court mathematician posed him problems he solved in Flos and the Liber Quadratorum.

The Fibonacci sequence

In Liber Abaci, Fibonacci posed a problem about the growth of a rabbit population whose solution generates the sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, … in which each term is the sum of the two preceding ones. Named after him in the 19th century, the Fibonacci sequence is connected to the golden ratio — the ratio of consecutive terms approaches φ — and appears throughout mathematics and computer science (see Fibonacci heap and Fibonacci search technique).

Legacy

Beyond the sequence, Fibonacci's lasting contribution was the adoption in Europe of positional decimal arithmetic, which transformed bookkeeping, banking, and measurement. The city of Pisa honoured him with a stipend in 1240, and a statue of him stands at the Camposanto.
