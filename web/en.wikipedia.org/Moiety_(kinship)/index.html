Moiety (kinship)

In the anthropological study of kinship, a moiety is one of two complementary groups into which an entire society is divided by descent. Each moiety is typically exogamous — members must marry into the other moiety — and the two halves often stand in a reciprocal relationship, providing services such as burial, ritual, or ceremony for one another.

Distribution

Moiety systems are documented among Indigenous Australian peoples, where the two halves (often associated with natural emblems such as eaglehawk and crow) structure marriage and ritual life, and among peoples of the Northwest Coast of North America and parts of South America, Africa, and Asia. Moieties may be patrilineal or matrilineal, and they may be combined with finer subdivisions such as sections and subsections.

Function

Anthropologists such as Claude Lévi-Strauss analysed moieties as examples of dual organisation, in which the pairing of opposed halves expresses reciprocity and alliance. Because each moiety depends on the other for spouses and ritual obligations, the system binds the society together through structured exchange.
