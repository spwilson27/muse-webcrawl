Quattro (four-wheel-drive system)

Quattro (meaning "four" in Italian) is the trademark used by the automotive brand Audi to indicate that all-wheel drive (AWD) technologies or systems are used on specific models of its cars. The term was first used in 1980 for Audi's original permanent four-wheel-drive coupé, the Audi Quattro, and has since become one of the company's signature technologies and marketing pillars.

History

The quattro system was inspired by the Volkswagen Iltis military vehicle, whose four-wheel drive performed impressively in winter testing in Scandinavia. Led by engineer Jörg Bensinger, Audi developed a system using a hollow shaft in the gearbox to drive both axles with a centre differential. Unveiled at the 1980 Geneva Motor Show, the Audi Quattro went on to dominate rallying: Audi won the World Rally Championship manufacturers' titles in 1982 and 1984, and drivers' titles in 1983 (Hannu Mikkola) and 1984 (Stig Blomqvist), forcing rivals to adopt four-wheel drive for competition cars.

Technology

Successive generations of quattro have used different hardware. Early systems used manually lockable differentials. From 1987 many models adopted a Torsen (torque-sensing) centre differential that automatically varies the torque split, normally 50:50, between front and rear axles. Transverse-engine models (such as the A3 and TT) use a Haldex-type multi-plate clutch system, badged quattro but marketed by other Volkswagen Group brands as 4Motion. Newer e-tron electric models use separate motors per axle, marketing the system as "electric quattro".

Impact

Quattro effectively created the market for high-performance all-wheel-drive road cars and remains central to Audi's brand identity; the company claims to have produced more than ten million quattro-equipped vehicles.
