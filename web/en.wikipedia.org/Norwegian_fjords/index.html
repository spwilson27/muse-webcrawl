The coast of Norway is famed for its fjords — long, deep, narrow sea inlets carved by glaciers during the ice ages and later flooded by the sea. There are more than a thousand named fjords along the Norwegian coastline, concentrated on the west coast.

Formation

Glaciers gouged U-shaped valleys below sea level; when the ice retreated, seawater filled them. Many fjords have a shallow sill at their mouth and plunge to great depths inland — Sognefjorden, the "King of the Fjords", exceeds 1,300 metres deep.

Notable fjords

Geirangerfjord and Nærøyfjord were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2005 as outstanding examples of fjord landscapes. Hardangerfjord is known for fruit orchards, Lysefjord for the Pulpit Rock (Preikestolen) and Kjerag cliffs, and Trollfjorden in Lofoten for its narrow entrance.

Significance

The fjords historically served as sheltered transport routes and fishing grounds, and today anchor a major tourism industry based on cruising, kayaking and hiking, while also hosting aquaculture and hydropower development.
