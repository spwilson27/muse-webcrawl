Hofmann voltameter


The Hofmann voltameter: hydrogen collects at twice the volume of oxygen

A Hofmann voltameter is an apparatus for electrolysing water, invented by August Wilhelm von Hofmann in 1866. It consists of three joined vertical glass cylinders, usually filled with water acidulated with a little sulfuric acid to make it conductive. Platinum electrodes at the bottom of the two outer tubes are connected to a direct-current supply. As current flows, hydrogen gas collects at the cathode and oxygen at the anode, in the volume ratio 2:1 — a classic demonstration that water is composed of two parts hydrogen to one part oxygen by volume.

Description and use

The central tube serves as a reservoir and filling funnel, with stopcocks at the top of each outer limb allowing the gases to be drawn off and tested: hydrogen gives a characteristic "pop" with a lighted splint, while oxygen relights a glowing splint. Because the volume of gas produced is proportional to the quantity of electricity passed, the voltameter was historically used to measure electric charge, in line with Faraday's laws of electrolysis — hence the name "voltameter" (distinct from the voltmeter, which measures potential difference).

Significance

The apparatus became a standard lecture and school-laboratory demonstration of electrolysis and of the composition of water, complementing the earlier experiments of Nicholson and Carlisle (1800), who first electrolysed water using a voltaic pile. Hofmann's design, part of his celebrated lecture demonstrations at the Royal College of Chemistry in London, survives in teaching laboratories today, though modern versions may use safer electrolytes and Hofmann's apparatus is also sold as the "Hofmann electrolysis apparatus".
