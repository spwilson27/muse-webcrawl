Defeat device

A defeat device is any motor vehicle hardware, software, or design feature that reduces the effectiveness of the vehicle's emission control system under conditions that the vehicle may reasonably be expected to encounter in normal operation and use. The term is used in the emissions regulations of the United States, the European Union, and other jurisdictions, where such devices are generally prohibited because they allow vehicles to emit far more pollution in real-world driving than during official certification tests.

Background

Modern vehicles rely on engine control units (ECUs) and after-treatment systems such as selective catalytic reduction, exhaust gas recirculation, and diesel particulate filters to keep pollutant emissions within legal limits. Because these systems can reduce fuel economy, performance, or convenience, manufacturers have sometimes been tempted to design controls that behave differently during laboratory testing than on the road. A defeat device exploits the predictability of the test cycle: when the vehicle detects that it is being tested, it operates its emission controls at full effectiveness, but under other conditions it reduces or disables them.

The Volkswagen emissions scandal

The most famous case involving defeat devices emerged in September 2015, when the United States Environmental Protection Agency issued a notice of violation to Volkswagen Group. Researchers at West Virginia University, working with the International Council on Clean Transportation, had found that Volkswagen diesel cars equipped with certain 2.0-litre engines emitted nitrogen oxides at up to 40 times the legal limit during on-road testing. The cars contained software that recognised test conditions — based on factors such as steering position, speed, and barometric pressure — and changed engine behaviour accordingly. Volkswagen eventually admitted that about 11 million vehicles worldwide contained the software. The scandal led to criminal prosecutions, tens of billions of dollars in fines, settlements and buybacks, and a lasting loss of confidence in diesel passenger cars.

Other cases and regulatory response

Investigations in Europe and North America subsequently examined diesel models from several other manufacturers, and regulators tightened test procedures. The European Union introduced Real Driving Emissions (RDE) testing using portable emissions measurement systems, and the Worldwide Harmonised Light Vehicles Test Procedure (WLTP) replaced the older, more predictable laboratory cycle. Enforcement agencies also increased on-road surveillance testing of vehicles already in customers' hands.
