Yang–Mills theory

Yang–Mills theory is a gauge theory based on a special unitary group SU(N), or more generally any compact Lie group, describing a field whose quanta carry a non-Abelian charge. Developed by physicists Chen Ning Yang and Robert Mills in 1954, it provides the framework for the unified theory of the electroweak interaction and for quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory of the strong interaction.

A visualization of the vacuum structure of SU(3) Yang–Mills theory from lattice simulation.

Overview

Unlike the Abelian gauge theory of electromagnetism, Yang–Mills fields interact with themselves because their gauge bosons carry the charge of the interaction: gluons carry color charge in QCD, giving rise to phenomena such as asymptotic freedom and color confinement. The theory was initially set aside because its quanta appeared to be massless, but the discovery of the Higgs mechanism and of asymptotic freedom (1973) made it the basis of the Standard Model.

Mathematics

In mathematical terms, a Yang–Mills field is a connection on a principal bundle, and its field strength is the curvature. The Yang–Mills equations are a non-linear generalization of Maxwell's equations.

Yang–Mills existence and mass gap

Proving that quantum Yang–Mills theory exists and has a mass gap is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize Problems, reflecting its foundational importance.
