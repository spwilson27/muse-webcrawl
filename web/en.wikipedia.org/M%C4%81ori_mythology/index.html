Māori mythology

Māori mythology and Māori traditions are the body of myths, legends, and beliefs of the Māori people of New Zealand (Aotearoa). They were transmitted orally through whakapapa (genealogies), whaikōrero (oratory), waiata (songs), and carving, and many were first recorded in writing in the nineteenth century. Māori mythology shares roots with the wider family of Polynesian narrative traditions.

Creation

In the best-known creation tradition, in the beginning there was Te Kore, the void, and then Te Pō, the long night. Ranginui, the sky father, and Papatūānuku, the earth mother, lay clasped together in darkness, their children trapped between them. The atua (gods) forced their parents apart so that light — Te Ao Mārama — entered the world. Tāne Mahuta, god of forests and birds, is commonly credited with pushing the sky upward with the help of his brothers.

Principal figures

The children of Rangi and Papa include Tāwhirimātea (weather), Tangaroa (the sea), Rongo (cultivated food), Haumia-tiketike (wild food), and Tūmatauenga (war and humanity). The demigod Māui, the youngest and most cunning of his brothers, is the great trickster-hero: he fished up the North Island (Te Ika-a-Māui) from the sea, snared the sun to slow its course, stole fire from the ancestress Mahuika, and died attempting to win immortality for humankind by entering the body of Hine-nui-te-pō, goddess of death.

Other traditions

Other important narratives include the voyage of Kupe, said to have discovered Aotearoa, and the migrations of the great ocean-going waka (canoes) from Hawaiki, from which iwi trace their descent. Concepts such as tapu (sacred restriction), mana (prestige and authority), and mauri (life force) run throughout the traditions and continue to shape Māori culture.
