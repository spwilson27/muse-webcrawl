Bonaventura Francesco Cavalieri (Latin: Cavalerius; 1598 – 30 November 1647) was an Italian mathematician and a Jesuat. He is known for his work on the problems of optics and motion, work on indivisibles, the precursors of infinitesimal calculus, and the introduction of logarithms to Italy.

Life

Born in Milan, Cavalieri joined the Jesuat order as a teenager and studied mathematics at Pisa, where he became a disciple of Galileo Galilei. In 1629 he was appointed professor of mathematics at the University of Bologna, a chair he held until his death, despite chronic illness with gout.

Method of indivisibles

His principal work, Geometria indivisibilibus continuorum nova quadam ratione promota (1635), presented Cavalieri's principle: if two solids have equal cross-sectional areas at every height, they have equal volume. The method of indivisibles, though lacking rigorous foundations, anticipated integral calculus and influenced later mathematicians such as Torricelli and Wallis.

Other work

Cavalieri published tables of logarithms, wrote on conic sections and optics — including formulas for parabolic mirrors — and corresponded widely with the leading scientists of his day. The lunar crater Cavalerius is named after him.
