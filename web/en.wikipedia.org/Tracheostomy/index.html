Tracheostomy

A tracheostomy is a medical procedure — surgical or percutaneous — that creates an opening (stoma) in the front of the neck into the trachea (windpipe), through which a tube is placed to secure an airway. The resulting opening is also called a tracheostomy, and the tube a tracheostomy tube. The procedure is distinct from the emergency front-of-neck access known as cricothyrotomy.

Indications

Tracheostomy is performed to bypass an obstructed upper airway, to allow prolonged mechanical ventilation with greater comfort and easier weaning than an endotracheal tube, to protect the airway in patients with impaired consciousness or swallowing, and to facilitate clearance of secretions. It is one of the most common procedures in intensive care units for patients expected to need ventilation for more than one to two weeks.

Technique

In a surgical tracheostomy, an incision is made in the neck and a window or flap is created in the second to fourth tracheal rings. Percutaneous dilational tracheostomy, now widely used at the bedside in intensive care, uses a needle, guidewire, and dilators (often with bronchoscopic or ultrasound guidance) to place the tube through a small puncture. The tube is secured with ties or sutures and usually has an inflatable cuff to seal the airway during ventilation.

History and care

Although openings into the windpipe were described in ancient Egyptian and Indian texts and by Greek and Roman physicians, tracheotomy became established in the nineteenth century, notably in the treatment of diphtheria. Modern care emphasizes humidified air, suctioning, stoma hygiene, speaking valves, and timely decannulation (tube removal) once the underlying need resolves. Complications include bleeding, infection, tube displacement, and tracheal stenosis.
