Perylene diimide

The perylene-3,4,9,10-tetracarboxylic diimide chromophore.

Perylene diimide (PDI), formally perylene-3,4,9,10-tetracarboxylic diimide, is an organic compound consisting of a perylene core flanked by two imide groups. It belongs to the class of rylene dyes and is the parent compound of a family of industrial pigments and n-type organic semiconductors. PDIs are noted for their intense color, exceptional photostability and thermal stability, strong electron affinity, and high fluorescence quantum yields in solution.

Properties

PropertyDescription
AppearanceRed to violet solid (pigment grades)
AbsorptionStrong visible absorption, typically around 450–550 nm
Electronic characterElectron-poor; readily reduced to radical anion and dianion
StabilityVery high resistance to light, heat, and solvents

The flat aromatic cores of PDIs stack strongly through π–π interactions, driving self-assembly into columns, tapes, and nanobelts. Substituents at the imide nitrogen atoms (which sit at electronic nodes) tune solubility and packing without greatly changing the chromophore's absorption, while substitution in the "bay" region twists the core and alters the optics.

Applications

Unsubstituted and alkylated PDIs have long been used as high-performance red and violet pigments (for example Pigment Red 179 and related vat dyes) in automotive paints, plastics, and artists' colors. In organic electronics, PDIs and their fused or polymeric derivatives serve as electron-transporting and electron-accepting materials in organic solar cells, where they are major non-fullerene acceptors, as well as in field-effect transistors, light-emitting diodes, photodetectors, and as fluorescent probes and supramolecular building blocks in materials chemistry.
