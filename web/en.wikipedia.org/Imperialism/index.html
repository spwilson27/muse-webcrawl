Imperialism

Imperialism
[Image: 19th-century political cartoon of European powers dividing a map of Africa]
DefinitionExtension of a state's authority by territorial acquisition or indirect control over other polities
Peak era"New Imperialism", c. 1870–1914
Key theoristsJ. A. Hobson, V. I. Lenin, Joseph Schumpeter

Imperialism is the practice, theory, or attitude of maintaining or extending power and dominion, especially through the acquisition of territory or the establishment of political and economic control over other peoples. Although empires are as old as civilisation, the term is most often applied to the "New Imperialism" of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, when European powers, the United States, and Japan carved up most of Africa, Asia, and the Pacific.

Causes and theories

Explanations have stressed economics, strategy, and ideology. In Imperialism: A Study (1902) J. A. Hobson argued that surplus capital seeking investment drove expansion; Lenin's Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism (1917) recast this as a structural stage of monopoly capitalism and influenced anti-colonial movements worldwide. Other historians emphasise great-power rivalry, naval strategy, missionary and "civilising mission" ideologies, racial hierarchies, and the initiatives of men on the periphery. The Berlin Conference of 1884–85 and the ensuing "Scramble for Africa" left only Ethiopia and Liberia formally independent on that continent.

Decline and legacy

The two world wars weakened the imperial powers, and the decades after 1945 saw rapid decolonisation across Asia and Africa. Scholars debate the lasting economic and political legacies of empire, and the term "imperialism" is still used critically of great-power influence exercised without formal annexation, alongside concepts such as neo-colonialism and cultural imperialism.
