Psychedelic drug

Psychedelics are a subclass of hallucinogenic drugs whose primary effect is to trigger non-ordinary mental states (known as psychedelic experiences or "trips") and a perceived "expansion of consciousness". The classic psychedelics — LSD, psilocybin, mescaline, and DMT — act primarily as agonists at the serotonin 5-HT2A receptor.

History

Psychedelic plants and fungi have been used in indigenous religious rituals in the Americas and elsewhere for millennia — mescaline in peyote, psilocybin in mushrooms, and DMT in ayahuasca. Albert Hofmann synthesized LSD in 1938 and discovered its effects in 1943. The 1950s and 1960s saw extensive psychiatric research and cultural diffusion, followed by prohibition in most countries under the 1971 UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances.

Effects

Effects include altered perception (visual patterns, synesthesia), changes in mood and thought, a sense of unity, and at high doses, ego dissolution. Experiences depend heavily on set (mindset) and setting. Adverse reactions can include anxiety and panic; psychedelics are generally considered non-addictive and physiologically low in toxicity, though they can precipitate psychosis in predisposed individuals.

Therapeutic research

Since the 2000s a "psychedelic renaissance" has produced clinical trials of psilocybin for depression, end-of-life anxiety, and addiction, with some trials reporting large and durable benefits. Psilocybin received breakthrough therapy designations from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for treatment-resistant depression. Some jurisdictions, including Oregon and Colorado, have decriminalized or legalized supervised psilocybin use.

Terminology

The word psychedelic was coined in 1956 by psychiatrist Humphry Osmond, from Greek psyche (soul/mind) and delos (manifest) — "mind-manifesting." Aldous Huxley's 1954 book The Doors of Perception popularized the subjective exploration of mescaline.
