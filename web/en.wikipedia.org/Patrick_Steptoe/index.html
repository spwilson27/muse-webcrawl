Patrick Steptoe

Born9 June 1913, Oxford, England
Died21 March 1988 (aged 74), Canterbury, England
OccupationObstetrician and gynaecologist
Known forPioneering in vitro fertilisation (IVF) with Robert Edwards

Patrick Christopher Steptoe (9 June 1913 – 21 March 1988) was an English obstetrician and gynaecologist who, together with physiologist Robert Edwards and nurse Jean Purdy, developed in vitro fertilisation (IVF), leading to the birth of the first "test-tube baby", Louise Brown, in 1978.

Early life and career

Steptoe studied medicine at King's College London and St George's Hospital Medical School. During the Second World War he served in the Royal Navy and spent time as a prisoner of war in Italy. Afterwards he worked in obstetrics, becoming a consultant at Oldham General Hospital in 1951. He became a leading British practitioner of laparoscopy — which he learned partly from Raoul Palmer in Paris and Hans Frangenheim in Germany — and published a pioneering textbook on the technique in 1967.

IVF breakthrough

Steptoe's laparoscopic skill in retrieving mature eggs from ovaries was the clinical key that Edwards' laboratory work on fertilisation required. From 1968 the two collaborated — joined by embryologist Jean Purdy — first at Oldham and from 1980 at Bourn Hall Clinic in Cambridgeshire, which they founded as the world's first IVF clinic. On 25 July 1978 Lesley Brown gave birth to Louise Brown at Oldham after a natural-cycle IVF procedure, a landmark that transformed fertility medicine.

Recognition

Steptoe was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society and received a CBE, but he died in 1988, long before Edwards received the Nobel Prize in 2010 — an honour many believed Steptoe and Purdy would have shared had they lived. A blue plaque and the naming of facilities at Oldham commemorate his work.
