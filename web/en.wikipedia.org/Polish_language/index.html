Polish (język polski, polszczyzna) is a West Slavic language of the Lechitic group, written in the Latin script. It is the official language of Poland and one of the official languages of the European Union. With approximately 50 million speakers, it is the second most widely spoken Slavic language after Russian.

Polish is spoken primarily in Poland, home to one of the world's most linguistically homogeneous populations, and by large Polish minorities and diaspora communities in Germany, the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, Brazil, and elsewhere.

Features

Polish retains the Old Slavic nasal vowels ą and ę, lost in most other Slavic languages, and has a complex consonant system including retroflex and alveolo-palatal fricatives and affricates. Stress almost always falls on the penultimate syllable. The grammar is highly inflected: nouns and adjectives decline in seven cases and three genders (with a masculine animate/personal distinction), and verbs mark aspect (perfective/imperfective) as a fundamental category. The Polish alphabet has 32 letters, using diacritics such as ą, ć, ę, ł, ń, ó, ś, ź, ż and digraphs such as cz, sz, dż.

History

Polish began to emerge as a distinct language around the 10th century, with the oldest written sentences dating from the 13th century (the Book of Henryków contains the famous sentence "Day, ut ia pobrusa, a ti poziwai", considered the oldest recorded sentence in Polish). The language flourished during the Polish Golden Age of the 16th century. Despite the partitions of Poland (1795–1918), when Germanization and Russification policies tried to suppress it, Polish survived as the carrier of national identity, and its literature includes four Nobel laureates in literature: Henryk Sienkiewicz, Władysław Reymont, Czesław Miłosz, Wisława Szymborska, and Olga Tokarczuk.
