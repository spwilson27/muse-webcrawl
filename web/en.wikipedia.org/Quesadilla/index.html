A quesadilla (Spanish: [kesa'ðiʎa]) is a Mexican dish consisting of a tortilla filled primarily with cheese, and sometimes meats, spices, and other fillings, which is then heated on a griddle or comal until the cheese melts. The name is a diminutive derived from queso, Spanish for cheese. It is usually made with a corn tortilla in much of Mexico, while flour tortillas are more common in northern Mexico and in the United States.

History

The quesadilla has its origins in colonial Mexico. The dish developed when Spanish settlers introduced cheese and wheat to Mesoamerica, combining European dairy with the indigenous corn tortilla. By the 16th and 17th centuries, cheese-filled tortillas had become a standard food. Regional forms proliferated: in central and southern Mexico, quesadillas are typically corn tortillas folded over fillings such as Oaxaca cheese, huitlacoche (corn smut), squash blossoms, mushrooms, or tinga; in the north, flour tortillas and grilled meats predominate.

Preparation

A tortilla is placed on a hot comal, filled on one half with stringy melting cheese (traditionally queso Oaxaca, or substitutes such as Chihuahua cheese or Monterey Jack), folded in half, and cooked until the tortilla is lightly crisped and the cheese has melted. In the United States, quesadillas are often made with a full tortilla as a cover, grill-pressed or fried in butter or oil, and cut into wedges like a pizza. They are commonly served with salsa, guacamole, pico de gallo, and sour cream.

The Mexico City debate

A famous point of culinary contention in Mexico is whether a quesadilla must contain cheese. In Mexico City, vendors routinely ask "con queso?" ("with cheese?"), implying that the default quesadilla may contain only other fillings — a usage most other Mexicans find illogical given the dish's name. The debate is a recurring topic of humour and regional pride.

Variations

Related dishes include the sincronizada (two tortillas sandwiching ham and cheese), the gringa (flour tortilla with al pastor pork and cheese), and the quesabirria, a birria-filled variant that became a viral international trend in the late 2010s. Outside Mexico, quesadillas have been adapted with countless fillings in Tex-Mex and Californian cuisine.
