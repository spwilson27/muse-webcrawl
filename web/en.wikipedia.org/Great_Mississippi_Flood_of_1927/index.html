Great Mississippi Flood of 1927

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great Mississippi Flood of 1927 was the most destructive river flood in the history of the United States. After months of heavy rain across the Mississippi basin in 1926–27, the river's levees broke in more than a hundred places, inundating roughly 70,000 km² (27,000 square miles) across seven states — Arkansas, Illinois, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, Missouri, and Tennessee — in places to depths of 9 metres.

Course of the disaster

The crisis peaked in April and May 1927. On 29 April, to save New Orleans, officials deliberately dynamited the levee at Caernarvon, Louisiana, flooding St. Bernard and Plaquemines parishes — a decision whose displaced residents were promised compensation that largely never came. At Greenville, Mississippi, thousands of Black labourers were held on the levee by armed guards and forced to work on flood defences and relief under brutal conditions, an episode that became a national scandal. The official death toll was 246, though estimates run higher; more than 600,000 people were displaced, and some 185,000 African Americans were housed in Red Cross refugee camps.

Consequences

Commerce Secretary Herbert Hoover, who directed the relief effort, emerged as a national hero, propelling him to the presidency in 1928. The disaster transformed federal policy: the Flood Control Act of 1928 committed the federal government to a comprehensive Mississippi River and Tributaries Project of levees, floodways, and spillways, ending the old "levees only" doctrine. Socially, the flood's racial injustices accelerated the Great Migration and helped shift Black voters toward the Democratic Party. The flood entered American culture through blues songs such as Charley Patton's "High Water Everywhere," Bessie Smith's "Back-Water Blues," and, decades later, Randy Newman's "Louisiana 1927."
