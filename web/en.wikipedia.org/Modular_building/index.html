Modular building

Volumetric modules being craned into position on site

A modular building is a prefabricated building consisting of repeated sections called modules, manufactured in a factory away from the building site and then transported and assembled on site to form a complete structure. Modularity allows construction work to proceed in parallel — foundations on site while modules are built indoors — which can shorten programme time substantially, and factory conditions can improve quality control and reduce waste.

Types

Volumetric (3D) modular construction delivers complete three-dimensional units — rooms or groups of rooms with finishes, services, and fittings already installed — which are stacked and connected on site. Panelised (2D) systems ship flat wall, floor, and roof panels for assembly on site. Modules may be permanent, forming schools, hospitals, hotels, and housing, or relocatable, providing temporary classrooms and site offices that can be moved and reused.

History and adoption

Prefabricated buildings date to the 19th century — kit houses shipped by rail, and Britain's post-war prefabs. Modern volumetric construction has been widely used in Japan and Scandinavia, and prominent projects include the 32-storey B2 tower in Brooklyn (461 Dean Street) and high-rise modular housing in Singapore and London. Hotel chains favour the method because identical guest-room modules can be finished entirely in the factory.

Advantages and limitations

Advantages cited include speed (often 30–50% faster than conventional construction), reduced weather delay, safer factory working, and less site disruption. Limitations include transport size constraints, the need for design decisions to be frozen early, financing and valuation unfamiliarity in some markets, and structural duplication — module walls and floors double up where units join.
