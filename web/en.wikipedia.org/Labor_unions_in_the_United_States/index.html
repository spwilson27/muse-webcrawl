Labor unions in the United States

Union members have organised in the United States since the nineteenth century

Labor unions in the United States are organizations formed by workers to represent their collective interests in negotiations with employers over wages, working conditions and benefits. In 2024, about 9.9 percent of American wage and salary workers were union members, down from a peak of roughly 35 percent in the mid-1950s, though membership density in the public sector (about 32 percent) remains far higher than in the private sector (about 6 percent).

History

Craft unions emerged in the early republic, and national organisation began with federations such as the National Labor Union (1866) and the Knights of Labor (1869). The American Federation of Labor (AFL), founded in 1886 under Samuel Gompers, focused on skilled craft workers and "pure and simple" unionism. The Great Depression and the National Labor Relations Act of 1935 (the Wagner Act), which guaranteed workers the right to organise and bargain collectively, triggered a surge of industrial unionism led by the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO). The rival federations merged in 1955 as the AFL–CIO.

The Taft–Hartley Act of 1947 restricted union tactics and allowed states to pass "right-to-work" laws. Union density declined from the 1960s onward under pressure from deindustrialisation, employer opposition, globalisation and shifts to service employment, even as public-sector unions grew after many states authorised government-worker bargaining.

Structure and law

Most private-sector unions operate under the National Labor Relations Act, administered by the National Labor Relations Board, which certifies elections and polices unfair labor practices. Major federations include the AFL–CIO and the Strategic Organizing Center (formerly Change to Win). Large national unions include the National Education Association, the Service Employees International Union (SEIU), the Teamsters, the United Auto Workers (UAW) and the United Food and Commercial Workers (UFCW).

Effects

Research generally finds a substantial union wage premium, commonly estimated at 10–20 percent, along with stronger benefits and greater job security for members, partially offset by spillover and employment effects that remain debated among economists.
