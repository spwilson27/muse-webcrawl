Icelandic sagas

Language: Old Norse / Old Icelandic
Composed: Mainly 13th–14th centuries
Setting: Settlement era, c. 870–1030
Notable examples: Njáls saga, Egils saga, Laxdæla saga

The Icelandic sagas, most narrowly the Sagas of Icelanders (Íslendingasögur), are prose narratives written in Old Norse in Iceland mainly in the 13th and 14th centuries, recounting the lives, feuds, voyages, and loves of settlers and their descendants during the Saga Age, roughly 870 to 1030.

Characteristics

The family sagas are famed for their laconic, objective style, understatement, and large casts tracked through detailed genealogies. Stories centre on honour and feud: killings demand vengeance or compensation at the Althing, and characters are presented through action and dialogue rather than authorial comment. About forty family sagas survive, alongside kings' sagas, contemporary sagas, legendary sagas (fornaldarsögur), and chivalric romances.

Famous sagas and origins

Njáls saga, the longest and most celebrated, follows the lawyer Njáll Þorgeirsson and the warrior Gunnar Hámundarson toward a tragic burning. Egils saga tells of the poet-warrior Egill Skalla-Grímsson, and Laxdæla saga of Guðrún Ósvífrsdóttir. Whether the sagas preserve oral history or are literary creations of their Christian-era authors remains a central scholarly debate; the Book of Settlements (Landnámabók) provides their shared backdrop.

Legacy

Preserved in vellum manuscripts and later paper copies, the sagas anchor Icelandic national identity and have influenced writers from Sir Walter Scott to Jorge Luis Borges and modern historical fiction.
