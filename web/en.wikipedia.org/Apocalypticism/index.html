Apocalypticism

Apocalypticism
DefinitionReligious belief that the end of the world is imminent, to be followed by divine judgment and renewal
Key textsBook of Daniel, Book of Revelation, Book of Enoch
Related conceptsEschatology, millenarianism, messianism

Apocalypticism is the religious belief that the end of the world is imminent, even within one's own lifetime. This belief is usually accompanied by the idea that civilisation will soon come to a tumultuous end due to some catastrophic global event, after which God will intervene, judge humanity, reward the faithful and establish a renewed order. Apocalyptic beliefs arose in various religious traditions and have recurred throughout history, often in times of crisis.

Origins

Apocalyptic thought developed most fully in ancient Judaism, drawing on Persian Zoroastrian ideas of cosmic dualism, resurrection and final judgment. Jewish apocalypses such as the Book of Daniel (written during the persecution under Antiochus IV in the 2nd century BCE), the Book of Enoch and works found among the Dead Sea Scrolls depicted heavenly revelations mediated by angels, a period of escalating tribulation, and the imminent triumph of God over evil.

Apocalyptic literature

The apocalypse is also a literary genre in which a human recipient receives revelations — often through an angelic intermediary — disclosing a transcendent reality: a heavenly world and a future eschatological judgment. The most famous example is the Book of Revelation (the Apocalypse of John) in the New Testament, with its imagery of the Beast, the Four Horsemen, Armageddon and the New Jerusalem.

In Christianity and Islam

Early Christianity was strongly apocalyptic: Jesus and his first followers expected the imminent coming of the Kingdom of God, and movements expecting the end have arisen repeatedly, from the Montanists to the Millerites of the 1840s, whose failed prediction of Christ's return in 1844 is known as the Great Disappointment. In Islam, belief in the Day of Judgment (Yawm al-Qiyamah), preceded by signs including the coming of the Mahdi and the return of Jesus (Isa), is a central article of faith; apocalyptic hadith literature developed elaborate end-times narratives.

Secular and modern forms

Apocalypticism also exists in secular registers: fears of nuclear war, pandemics, climate catastrophe, or runaway artificial intelligence often take apocalyptic form, structured by narratives of impending collapse and possible renewal. Scholars note that apocalyptic frameworks offer meaning in crisis — history is not random but moving toward a revealed conclusion — even when specific predictions fail, an outcome classically explained by the psychological theory of cognitive dissonance.
