Orbit insertion

Orbit insertion is the process by which a spacecraft is placed into orbit around a celestial body. For interplanetary missions it is typically a major propulsive manoeuvre — a sustained engine burn near the target body that slows the spacecraft enough for gravity to capture it into a closed orbit; without it the craft would fly past.

TypeOrbital manoeuvre
PurposeCapture into orbit around a body
MethodsPropulsive burn; aerobraking follow-up
ExampleMars orbit insertion (MOI)

Procedure

As the spacecraft approaches periapsis of its hyperbolic arrival trajectory, its main engine fires retrograde for a precisely calculated duration, reducing velocity below local escape velocity. The resulting orbit is usually highly elliptical; later burns (orbit trimming), sometimes assisted by aerobraking, circularise it or shape it for the mission's science phase.

Significance and risks

Orbit insertion is often the most critical event of a mission: the burn must start on time, in the right attitude, and last the right duration. The 1993 loss of NASA's Mars Observer is thought to have occurred shortly before its planned insertion burn, and ESA's Schiaparelli lander and other missions illustrate the hazards of arrival phases. Because of light-time delay, the burn is executed autonomously.

Alternatives

Some spacecraft use aerocapture, passing through a planet's upper atmosphere to shed velocity, though this has not yet been used operationally. Low-energy ballistic capture trajectories are used by certain lunar and Lagrange-point missions.
