Et in Arcadia ego

Literally"And in Arcadia, I [am]"
Interpretations"Even in Arcadia, there am I" (Death speaking); "I too lived in Arcadia" (the deceased speaking)
Famous artworksGuercino (c. 1618–1622); Nicolas Poussin (two versions, 1627–28 and 1637–38)
ThemesMemento mori, pastoral, vanitas

Et in Arcadia ego is a Latin phrase that serves as the title of two paintings by Nicolas Poussin, the leading painter of the classical French Baroque style. The phrase is a memento mori: its most common reading is "Even in Arcadia, there am I" — as if Death itself speaks in the idyllic land of Arcadia. Because the phrase lacks a verb, an alternative elegiac reading takes it as the words of the dead shepherd in the tomb: "I too [lived] in Arcadia."

Guercino and Poussin

The motif first appeared in a painting by Guercino (c. 1618–1622) showing two shepherds contemplating a skull on a tomb inscribed with the phrase. Poussin painted his first version in 1627–28, depicting shepherds gathered around a tomb, and a second, more contemplative version about a decade later (1637–38, now in the Louvre), in which shepherds and a classical female figure trace the inscription on a simple tomb — the skull replaced by quiet meditation on mortality.

Interpretation

Art historian Erwin Panofsky wrote the classic essay on the phrase's shifting meaning, arguing that the earlier, Venetian-Guercinian tradition emphasized Death intruding on bliss, while Poussin's mature version transformed it into a reflective elegy: the deceased speaks, and the living contemplate the passage from earthly joy to the grave. The phrase has since been widely reused as a literary and artistic allusion, from Evelyn Waugh's use of it as a book title in Brideshead Revisited to Tom Stoppard's play Arcadia.
