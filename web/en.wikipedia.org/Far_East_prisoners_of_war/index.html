Far East prisoners of war

ConflictSecond World War, Pacific theatre
Captured byEmpire of Japan
Period1941–1945
Notable projectsBurma Railway, Sandakan, hell ships

Far East prisoners of war is a term used in the United Kingdom to describe former British and Commonwealth prisoners of war held in the Far East during the Second World War. The term is also used as the initialism FEPOW, or as an abbreviation such as Far East POWs.

Captivity

Tens of thousands of Allied service personnel were captured following the fall of Hong Kong, Malaya, and Singapore in late 1941 and early 1942, and the fall of the Dutch East Indies. Prisoners endured harsh conditions in camps across Southeast Asia and Japan, suffering from malnutrition, disease, and brutal treatment. Many were used as forced labour on projects including the Burma Railway (the "Death Railway") between Thailand and Burma, on which an estimated 12,000–13,000 Allied prisoners died, and in mines and factories in Japan, to which they were transported in overcrowded "hell ships".

After the war

Survivors were liberated in August and September 1945 following Japan's surrender. Many suffered long-term physical and psychological effects. FEPOW associations and memorials, including the National Memorial Arboretum in Staffordshire, commemorate their experience, and the suffering of Far East prisoners has been depicted in memoirs and works such as The Bridge on the River Kwai.
