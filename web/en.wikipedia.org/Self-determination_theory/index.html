Self-determination theory

Self-determination theory (SDT) is a macro-theory of human motivation and personality developed by psychologists Edward L. Deci and Richard M. Ryan, beginning with their 1985 book Self-Determination and Intrinsic Motivation in Human Behavior. It holds that people have innate growth tendencies whose expression depends on the satisfaction of three basic psychological needs.

Basic needs

The theory identifies autonomy (experiencing behaviour as volitional and self-endorsed), competence (feeling effective in one's interactions) and relatedness (feeling connected to and cared for by others). Environments that support these needs foster intrinsic motivation, deeper learning and well-being; controlling environments thwart them, producing disengagement or ill-being.

Types of motivation

SDT distinguishes intrinsic motivation — acting for the inherent satisfaction of the activity — from extrinsic motivation, which it arranges on a continuum of internalisation: external regulation (rewards and punishments), introjected regulation (guilt and ego), identified regulation (personal value), and integrated regulation, the most autonomous extrinsic form. Amotivation lies at the non-self-determined extreme. A well-known finding, the undermining effect, is that expected tangible rewards can reduce intrinsic interest in an already enjoyable task, a result Deci demonstrated in experiments from 1971 onward.

Applications

The theory has been applied extensively in education (autonomy-supportive teaching), work (job design and leadership), sport, health behaviour change, psychotherapy and game design. Its constituent mini-theories include cognitive evaluation theory, organismic integration theory, causality orientations theory, basic psychological needs theory, goal contents theory and relationships motivation theory.
