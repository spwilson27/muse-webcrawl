The Tomahawk is a long-range, all-weather, subsonic cruise missile used primarily by the United States Navy and Royal Navy for land-attack strikes from ships and submarines. Named after the Native American axe, the missile flies at very low altitude and uses sophisticated guidance to strike fixed targets over a thousand kilometres away with high precision.

Design and guidance

The Tomahawk is about 5.6 metres long (6.25 m with booster), powered after a rocket-boosted launch by a small turbofan engine. Early variants navigated by TERCOM — radar matching of terrain contours — corrected at the target by DSMAC scene-matching. The current Block IV/V (Tactical Tomahawk) adds GPS guidance, a two-way satellite data link allowing retargeting in flight, and the ability to loiter over a target area. The standard warhead is a 450 kg (1,000 lb) unitary high-explosive or submunition payload.

History

Developed in the 1970s at General Dynamics, the missile entered service in 1983 in nuclear (TLAM-N) and conventional land-attack and anti-ship variants; the nuclear and anti-ship versions were later withdrawn, the former under arms-control policy by the early 2010s. Tomahawks were first used in combat in the 1991 Gulf War, where they opened the air campaign against Iraq, and have since been used in Kosovo, Afghanistan, Iraq in 2003, Libya in 2011 and strikes on Syria in 2017 and 2018, among many other operations.

Operators and variants

Besides the US Navy, the Royal Navy operates Tomahawk from its Astute-class submarines — the only export customer for many years — and Australia has ordered the missile for its Hobart-class destroyers and future submarines. Successive blocks have added improved seekers, anti-jam GPS, and in the Block V Maritime Strike variant a seeker enabling engagement of moving ships.
