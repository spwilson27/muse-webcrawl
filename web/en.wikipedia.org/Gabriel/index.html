In the Abrahamic religions, Gabriel (Hebrew: גַּבְרִיאֵל, "God is my strength"; Arabic: جبريل, Jibrīl) is an archangel and messenger of God. He first appears in the Book of Daniel, where he explains Daniel's visions, and later in the New Testament, where he announces the births of John the Baptist and of Jesus. In Islam, Gabriel is the angel who revealed the Qur'an to Muhammad.

In Judaism

Gabriel is named in the Book of Daniel (chapters 8–12), where he interprets the prophet's visions of the ram and the goat and of the seventy weeks. In Rabbinic literature Gabriel is one of the chief archangels alongside Michael, Raphael and Uriel, and traditions credit him with roles such as announcing the birth of Samson, burying Moses, and destroying the armies of Sennacherib. In Kabbalah, Gabriel is associated with the sephirah of Yesod and with severity and judgement.

In Christianity

In the Gospel of Luke, Gabriel appears to Zechariah in the Temple to announce the birth of John the Baptist, and then to the Virgin Mary in Nazareth with the Annunciation: "Hail, full of grace, the Lord is with thee." Because of this message Gabriel is often depicted holding a white lily, symbol of Mary's purity. Roman Catholic tradition counts Gabriel among the three named archangels, and his feast is celebrated on 29 September together with Michael and Raphael. He is the patron saint of messengers, postal workers, and telecommunications workers.

In Islam

As Jibrīl, Gabriel is the angel of revelation in Islam, regarded as the chief of the angels. Muslims believe he dictated the Qur'an to Muhammad beginning with the first revelation in the cave of Hira in 610 CE (commemorated in Laylat al-Qadr), accompanied him on the Isra and Mi'raj (night journey), and is identified with the "holy spirit" (Rūḥ al-qudus) mentioned in the Qur'an. He is also said to have given Mary the glad tidings of Jesus.

In art and culture

Gabriel is one of the most frequently depicted figures in Western art, particularly in Annunciation scenes by painters from Fra Angelico to Leonardo da Vinci. His trumpet is a common symbol of the Last Judgement, and his name has been widely adopted as a given name in Christian, Jewish, and Muslim communities.
