Ezra

Ezra reading the Law, as imagined in later art.

Ezra (Hebrew: עֶזְרָא, ʿEzrāʾ) was a Jewish scribe (sofer) and priest of the 5th century BCE who led a group of Judean exiles from Babylon back to Jerusalem under Persian rule. He is the central figure of the biblical Book of Ezra and a key figure in the formation of post-exilic Judaism.

Biblical account

According to the Book of Ezra, Ezra was a descendant of Aaron and a skilled scribe in the Law of Moses. Commissioned by the Persian king Artaxerxes with a letter granting provisions and authority, he travelled to Jerusalem (traditionally dated to 458 BCE, though some scholars place him in the following century) with a company of returnees, temple vessels, and offerings. In Jerusalem he confronted the problem of intermarriage with neighbouring peoples, leading a public confession and the dissolution of such marriages.

Ezra and the Torah

In the Book of Nehemiah, Ezra publicly reads the "book of the law of Moses" to the assembled people, with Levites interpreting it—an event often regarded as a foundational moment in the public authority of the Torah. Later Jewish tradition magnified his role: the Talmud counts him worthy of receiving the Torah had Moses not preceded him, and tradition credits him with establishing institutions and fixing the square Hebrew script. Some scholars have called him a "second Moses" for his role in reshaping Jewish identity around the written law after the exile.

Texts and scholarship

Ezra appears in Ezra–Nehemiah, the deuterocanonical 1 Esdras, and the apocalyptic 2 Esdras (4 Ezra). The historicity and dating of his mission remain debated, as does the relation between Ezra's and Nehemiah's careers.
