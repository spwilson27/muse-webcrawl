James Mark Baldwin

James Mark Baldwin (12 January 1861 – 8 November 1934) was an American philosopher and psychologist, a founder of experimental psychology in North America and an influential theorist of child development and evolution. He is best remembered for the "Baldwin effect" in evolutionary biology.

Career

Born in Columbia, South Carolina, Baldwin studied at Princeton and in Germany under Wilhelm Wundt. He founded psychology laboratories at the University of Toronto (1889) and Princeton (1893), co-founded the journal Psychological Review, and later taught at Johns Hopkins University. A scandal involving a police raid on a brothel, in which he was arrested in 1908, ended his American career; he spent the rest of his life in France.

Ideas

Baldwin's multi-volume Mental Development in the Child and the Race (1895) set out stages of cognitive development that influenced Jean Piaget, who acknowledged his debt. His theory of the self, imitation and the "socius" informed social psychology.

The Baldwin effect

In 1896 Baldwin proposed that learned behaviours could, over generations, guide evolutionary change: organisms that learn advantageous responses survive, and innate variants producing the same response are then selected. Revived by later theorists, the Baldwin effect became a standard topic in discussions of the interaction between learning, development and evolution.
