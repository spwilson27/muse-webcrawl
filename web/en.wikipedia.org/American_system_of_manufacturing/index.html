American system of manufacturing

The American system of manufacturing was a set of manufacturing methods that advanced in the United States during the 19th century, centered on the fabrication and assembly of products from interchangeable parts made with specialized machinery. It grew largely out of the federal armories at Springfield, Massachusetts, and Harpers Ferry, Virginia, where uniform firearms components were produced to close tolerances and verified with gauges.

Development

Inventors and armorers such as Eli Whitney (who promoted the concept), Simeon North, and John H. Hall advanced techniques of milling, jigging, and gauging that allowed unskilled workers to produce standardized parts at scale. The system spread from firearms to clocks, locks, sewing machines, bicycles, and ultimately automobiles, laying groundwork for mass production.

Influence

British observers coined the term "American system" after the Crystal Palace Exhibition of 1851, where American machinery drew international attention. The approach contrasted with European craft production and became a foundation of industrial manufacturing worldwide.
