Senescence

Senescence is the gradual deterioration of functional characteristics in living organisms. The word senescence can refer to cellular senescence or to senescence of the whole organism. Organismal senescence involves an increase in death rates and/or a decrease in fecundity with increasing age, at least in the later part of an organism's life cycle. Senescence is the inevitable fate of almost all multicellular organisms with germ-soma separation, but it can be delayed.

Cellular senescence

Cellular senescence is the phenomenon by which normal diploid cells lose the ability to divide after a finite number of divisions — about 50 for human fibroblasts in culture, a limit known as the Hayflick limit. It is associated with telomere shortening, DNA damage responses, and the expression of cell-cycle inhibitors such as p16 and p21. Senescent cells remain metabolically active and secrete a cocktail of inflammatory factors (the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, SASP), which can influence neighbouring tissue and is implicated in aging and cancer.

Evolutionary theories

Evolutionary biologists explain senescence through declining selection pressure with age: deleterious mutations acting late in life are weakly selected against (mutation accumulation), genes beneficial early but harmful late persist (antagonistic pleiotropy), and resources are allocated to reproduction at the expense of somatic maintenance (disposable soma theory).

Senescence in plants

In plants, senescence is a highly regulated developmental program that remobilizes nutrients from aging leaves and other organs to growing tissues and seeds before the organs die; it is controlled by hormones including ethylene and cytokinins.
