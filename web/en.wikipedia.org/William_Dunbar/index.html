William Dunbar (c. 1460 – c. 1520) was a Scottish makar (court poet) and priest who served at the court of King James IV of Scotland. He is regarded as one of the greatest poets of the Scottish Renaissance, noted for the extraordinary range of his work, which moves between devotional verse, courtly allegory, satire, comedy and flyting.

Life

Dunbar was probably born in East Lothian and educated at the University of St Andrews, graduating as a master of arts in 1479. He may have travelled in France and the Low Countries as a Franciscan novice before entering royal service. By about 1500 he was a salaried member of James IV's household, receiving a pension that was substantially increased in 1507. He took part in diplomatic missions, including the negotiations surrounding the king's marriage to Margaret Tudor in 1503, and celebrated the wedding in his allegorical poem The Thrissil and the Rois. His career was badly affected by the king's death at Flodden in 1513, and little is known of his later years.

Works

Around eighty poems are attributed to Dunbar. They include the dream vision The Golden Targe, the hellish satire The Dance of the Sevin Deidly Synnis, the petitionary and comic poems addressed to the king and queen, and the famous "Lament for the Makaris", whose refrain Timor mortis conturbat me commemorates dead fellow poets including Robert Henryson. His Flyting of Dunbar and Kennedie, exchanged with Walter Kennedy, is a virtuoso display of comic invective. Dunbar's mastery of Scots diction, metre and register influenced later Scottish poetry and has been praised by critics including C. S. Lewis.
