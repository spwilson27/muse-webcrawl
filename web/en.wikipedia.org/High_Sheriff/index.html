High Sheriff

A high sheriff is a ceremonial officer for each shrieval county of England and Wales and for Northern Ireland. High sheriffs are appointed annually in March and take office for one year. Although the office has existed since Anglo-Saxon times — the "shire reeve" was the King's representative in the county — its modern duties are largely ceremonial and symbolic, exercised on behalf of the monarch and the judiciary, neither role involving law-enforcement functions of the kind associated with sheriffs in the United States.

Duties

Modern responsibilities include attendance on royal visits, support for the judiciary (including the well-being of High Court judges on circuit), support for law enforcement and crime-prevention agencies, and promoting voluntary and charitable work in the county. Historically, sheriffs maintained the King's peace, collected taxes and fines, executed writs, raised the posse comitatus, and presided over the county court; enforcement functions now rest largely with High Court enforcement officers and the police. In London, the two sheriffs of the City are elected by the liverymen and support the Lord Mayor.

Nomination and appointment

Three names per county are nominated each November at a ceremony in the Royal Courts of Justice; the monarch "pricks" the chosen name with a silver bodkin, a tradition traditionally said to date from Elizabeth I. High sheriffs serve without pay and fund the office themselves. Similar offices exist in some Canadian provinces and Indian states (sheriffs of Mumbai, Kolkata and Chennai), descended from the same tradition.
