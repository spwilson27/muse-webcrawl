The Edo period (Japanese: 江戸時代, Edo jidai), also known as the Tokugawa period, is the period of Japanese history between 1603 and 1868, during which Japan was ruled by the Tokugawa shogunate and some 300 regional daimyo. It is named after Edo (present-day Tokyo), the seat of the shogunate. The period is characterised by over two and a half centuries of relative peace, political stability, economic growth and a policy of limited foreign contact.

Establishment

Tokugawa Ieyasu, victor at the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600, was appointed shogun in 1603. The shogunate (bakufu) governed from Edo while the emperor remained a largely ceremonial figure in Kyoto. Control over the daimyo was maintained through the sankin-kōtai system of alternate attendance, which required lords to reside in Edo in alternate years and leave family members there as hostages, and through laws regulating castle construction and marriages.

Society

Society was formally ordered into four classes — samurai, peasants, artisans and merchants — with outcaste groups below them. In practice, wealthy merchants came to wield growing economic power while many samurai became indebted administrators. Villages were organised through collective responsibility systems, and Confucian values of loyalty and hierarchy were promoted as state ideology.

Foreign relations

After initial engagement with European traders and missionaries, the shogunate expelled the Portuguese, suppressed Christianity, and in the 1630s issued edicts — later called sakoku — restricting foreign contact. Trade continued through controlled channels: the Dutch and Chinese at Nagasaki, Korea via Tsushima, and the Ryukyu Islands via Satsuma. The arrival of Commodore Matthew Perry's American fleet in 1853 forced the opening of Japan and destabilised the shogunate.

Culture and economy

Edo grew into one of the largest cities in the world, with a population of around one million by the early eighteenth century. Urban culture flourished in the pleasure districts and theatres: kabuki, bunraku puppet theatre, ukiyo-e woodblock prints and popular literature all reached wide audiences. Commercial agriculture, roads, money markets and rice exchanges developed despite periodic famines and peasant unrest.

End of the period

Fiscal strain, samurai discontent, foreign pressure and the movement to restore imperial rule culminated in the Boshin War of 1868–69. The last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, resigned in 1867, and the Meiji Restoration of 1868 formally returned power to the emperor, ending the Edo period.
