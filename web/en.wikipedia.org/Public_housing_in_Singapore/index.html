Public housing in Singapore

Public housing in Singapore is managed by the Housing and Development Board (HDB), a statutory board under the Ministry of National Development. About 80 percent of Singapore's resident population lives in HDB flats, and roughly 90 percent of those households own their homes on 99-year leases — making Singapore's system, unusual among public housing programmes, one centred on subsidised home ownership rather than rental.

History

The HDB was established on 1 February 1960, replacing the colonial Singapore Improvement Trust, and was charged with solving a severe housing shortage. Under its first chairman Lim Kim San, the board built more than 50,000 low-cost flats in its first five-year programme, largely emergency one- and two-room units. The Home Ownership for the People Scheme, launched in 1964, allowed citizens to buy flats using savings in the Central Provident Fund. Construction expanded into self-contained "new towns" such as Toa Payoh, Ang Mo Kio, Bedok, and Jurong, each with its own schools, markets, and transport links. From the 1990s, upgrading programmes renewed older estates, and later schemes such as Build-To-Order (BTO) sales tied construction to demand.

Features and policies

Flats range from two-room units to executive apartments and are allocated with eligibility conditions on citizenship, family nucleus, and income. The Ethnic Integration Policy (1989) sets quotas by block and neighbourhood to prevent ethnic enclaves. Owners may resell flats on the open market after a minimum occupation period, and resale prices in mature estates have become a major public-policy issue. Singapore's approach is frequently studied internationally as a model of large-scale, high-rise public housing combined with asset-based welfare, though critics point to lease decay as 99-year leases shorten.
