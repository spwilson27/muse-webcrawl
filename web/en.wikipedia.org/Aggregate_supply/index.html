Aggregate supply

In economics, aggregate supply (AS) is the total supply of goods and services that firms in a national economy plan to sell during a specific time period at a given overall price level. It is commonly depicted as an aggregate supply curve relating the price level to the quantity of real output firms are willing to produce, and is analyzed together with aggregate demand in the AS–AD model.

Short run and long run

The short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve slopes upward: with some input prices — especially nominal wages — sticky, a higher price level raises real profits and induces firms to produce more. The long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) curve is vertical at potential output (full-employment GDP), determined by the economy's labor force, capital stock, and technology rather than the price level. Keynesian presentations sometimes draw a horizontal section at depressed output levels before the curve turns upward.

Shifts

Supply shocks shift the AS curve: rises in oil prices, wages, or other input costs shift SRAS leftward, producing stagflation, while productivity improvements, increased labor supply, or cheaper inputs shift it rightward. Long-run shifts reflect economic growth — investment, technological progress, education, and institutional change.

Policy implications

Whether demand management can raise real output depends on the economy's position relative to potential: far below it, expansion mainly raises output; near it, expansion mainly raises prices.
