Axonometric projection

Axonometric projection is a type of orthographic projection used for creating a pictorial drawing of an object, where the lines of sight are perpendicular to the plane of projection, and the object is rotated around one or more of its axes to reveal multiple sides. The word axonometric means "to measure along axes".

Classification

Axonometric projections are classified according to the foreshortening of the three coordinate axes:

  • Isometric projection — all three axes are equally foreshortened; the angles between them are 120°. The most commonly used axonometric projection in engineering and in video games.
  • Dimetric projection — two of the three axes are equally foreshortened.
  • Trimetric projection — all three axes are unequally foreshortened.

History

The principles of axonometric drawing were developed in the 19th century, notably by William Farish, who described isometric drawing in 1822, and were formalised within the broader theory of descriptive geometry. Axonometric methods became standard in engineering drawing because measurements can be taken directly along the axes, unlike in perspective drawing.

Limitations

Because the projection is parallel rather than perspective, distant objects appear the same size as near ones, which can create optical ambiguities famously exploited in the impossible objects of M. C. Escher and in the Penrose triangle. Military projection and "Chinese scroll" painting traditions use related parallel-projection schemes.
