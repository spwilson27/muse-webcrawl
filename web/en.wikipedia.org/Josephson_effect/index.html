Josephson effect

A Josephson junction: two superconductors separated by a thin barrier.

The Josephson effect is a macroscopic quantum phenomenon in which a supercurrent — a current of Cooper pairs — flows between two superconductors separated by a thin insulating, normal-metal or weak-link barrier, a device known as a Josephson junction. The effect was predicted theoretically by Welsh physicist Brian Josephson in 1962, while he was a 22-year-old PhD student at Cambridge, and confirmed experimentally the following year; Josephson received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1973.

The DC and AC effects

In the DC Josephson effect, a constant supercurrent flows across the junction with no applied voltage, its magnitude depending on the difference in quantum-mechanical phase between the two superconductors. In the AC effect, applying a constant voltage V causes the current to oscillate at frequency f = 2eV/h, an exact relation that makes the Josephson effect the basis of the modern standard of the volt.

Applications

Josephson junctions are the active elements in SQUIDs (superconducting quantum interference devices), the most sensitive magnetometers known, used in medicine, geology and fundamental research. They also underpin superconducting digital circuits, voltage standards maintained by national laboratories, and the superconducting qubits used in many quantum computers.
