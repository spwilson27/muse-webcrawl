The Spanish Golden Age (Siglo de Oro) is a period of flourishing in Spanish arts and literature, roughly coinciding with the rise and decline of the Spanish Habsburg monarchy. It is conventionally dated from 1492 — the year of Nebrija's Castilian grammar, the fall of Granada and Columbus's first voyage — to the death of the playwright Calderón de la Barca in 1681.

Literature and theatre

The era produced Miguel de Cervantes, whose Don Quixote (1605, 1615) is often called the first modern novel, and the picaresque tradition begun by Lazarillo de Tormes. Theatre reached mass audiences in open-air corrales; Lope de Vega, author of over a thousand plays, codified the comedia nueva, and Calderón perfected the philosophical drama and the auto sacramental. Poets such as Garcilaso de la Vega, Luis de Góngora and Francisco de Quevedo revolutionised Spanish verse.

Painting

Painting flourished under royal and ecclesiastical patronage: El Greco in Toledo; Jusepe de Ribera in Naples; Zurbarán's austere religious works; Murillo in Seville; and above all Diego Velázquez, court painter to Philip IV and creator of Las Meninas.

Context

The cultural flowering rested on the wealth of Spain's American empire, even as war, inflation and plague strained the monarchy — a tension reflected in the era's preoccupations with honour, illusion and decline.
