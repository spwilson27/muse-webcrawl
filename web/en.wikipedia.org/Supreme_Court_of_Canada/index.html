Supreme Court of Canada

The Supreme Court of Canada (French: Cour suprême du Canada) is the highest court in the judicial system of Canada. It comprises nine justices, headed by the Chief Justice of Canada, and is the final court of appeal for all Canadian courts, with jurisdiction over both the common law and the civil law of Quebec. Its decisions are the ultimate expression of Canadian law.

History

The Court was created by the Supreme and Exchequer Courts Act of 1875, authorised by section 101 of the British North America Act, 1867. For its first decades, decisions could be appealed further to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London; appeals were abolished in criminal cases in 1933 and in all cases in 1949. The entrenchment of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms in 1982 greatly expanded the Court's role in reviewing legislation.

Composition and appointment

Justices are appointed by the Governor General on the advice of the Prime Minister. By statute, three of the nine must come from Quebec to ensure expertise in its civil-law tradition; by convention the remaining seats are divided among Ontario (three), the western provinces (two) and Atlantic Canada (one). Justices must retire at age 75. Richard Wagner has served as Chief Justice since 2017.

Jurisdiction and references

Most appeals require leave of the Court, which it grants in cases of public importance. Uniquely among apex courts, the Court also answers reference questions put by the federal government, producing advisory opinions such as the 1998 Quebec Secession Reference and the 2004 Same-Sex Marriage Reference. Landmark decisions include R v Morgentaler (1988), the Patriation Reference (1981) and Carter v Canada (2015) on medical assistance in dying.
