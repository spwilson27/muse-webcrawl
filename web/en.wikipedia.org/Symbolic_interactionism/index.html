Symbolic interactionism

Symbolic interactionism
FieldSociology, social psychology
Key figuresGeorge Herbert Mead, Herbert Blumer, Charles Horton Cooley, Erving Goffman
OriginEarly 20th century, University of Chicago

Symbolic interactionism is a sociological theory and methodological perspective that examines how individuals create and maintain society through repeated face-to-face interactions. It holds that people act toward things based on the meanings those things have for them, that these meanings arise out of social interaction, and that meanings are handled and modified through an interpretive process.

Origins

The perspective grew out of American pragmatism and the work of George Herbert Mead, who taught at the University of Chicago in the early twentieth century. Mead's lectures, published posthumously as Mind, Self, and Society (1934), argued that the self emerges from social experience: individuals come to see themselves from the standpoint of others, a process Mead called taking the role of the other. His student Herbert Blumer coined the term "symbolic interactionism" in 1937 and codified its three premises in Symbolic Interactionism: Perspective and Method (1969).

Core concepts

Central concepts include the looking-glass self of Charles Horton Cooley, Mead's distinction between the spontaneous "I" and the socialized "me," and the generalized other — the organized attitudes of the whole community that individuals internalize. Language and gesture function as significant symbols that allow minds and selves to develop.

Later developments

Erving Goffman extended the tradition with his dramaturgical analysis of everyday life. Variants include the Iowa school of Manford Kuhn, which favored quantitative self-concept tests, and ethnomethodology, which studies the methods people use to produce social order. The perspective has influenced studies of deviance, labeling theory, identity, and qualitative research methods such as participant observation and grounded theory.

Criticism

Critics argue that symbolic interactionism neglects large-scale social structures, power, and historical context, and that its concepts are difficult to test empirically. Defenders respond that it captures the lived texture of social life that structural theories miss.
