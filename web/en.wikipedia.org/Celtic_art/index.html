Celtic art

Celtic art is associated with the peoples known as Celts; those who spoke the Celtic languages in Europe from pre-history through to the modern period, as well as the art of ancient peoples whose language is uncertain, but have cultural and stylistic similarities with speakers of Celtic languages. Celtic art is a difficult term to define, covering a huge expanse of time, geography and cultures. A case has been made for a continuity of art in Europe from the Bronze Age, and indeed the preceding Neolithic age; however archaeologists generally use "Celtic" to refer to the culture of the European Iron Age from around 1000 BC onwards, until the conquest by the Roman Empire of most of the region.

Hallstatt art

The Hallstatt culture (c. 800–450 BC), named after a cemetery near Hallstatt in Austria, produced geometric ornament applied to bronze and iron weapons, jewellery, vessels and wagon fittings from rich "princely" burials such as Hochdorf and Vix. Greek and Etruscan imports influenced the repertoire, adding palmettes and lotus motifs that Celtic craftsmen transformed into abstract patterns.

La Tène art

The La Tène culture (c. 450 BC to the Roman conquests) marks the classic phase of Celtic art. Its style is dominated by curvilinear ornament: swirling spirals, triskeles, trumpet shapes and lyre-scrolls, often resolving into stylised human and animal faces hidden within the pattern. Masterpieces include the bronze Battersea Shield and Waterloo Helmet from Britain, the gold torcs of the Snettisham Hoard, and the painted pottery and bronze flagons of Gaul and the Rhineland. La Tène decoration favoured stylisation over naturalism, and eschewed straight lines and symmetry in favour of flowing, ambiguous forms.

Insular art

After the Roman period, Celtic art survived and flourished in Ireland and parts of Britain that were never Romanised or that reverted after Rome's withdrawal. The resulting Insular art of the 7th to 9th centuries AD fused La Tène curvilinear traditions with Germanic interlace and Mediterranean figure style. Its supreme achievements are illuminated manuscripts such as the Book of Durrow, the Lindisfarne Gospels and the Book of Kells, and metalwork such as the Tara Brooch, the Ardagh Chalice and the Moylough Belt Shrine. Dense carpet pages of interlace, spiral work and zoomorphic ornament are characteristic, as is fine stone carving on high crosses.

Celtic Revival

From the 18th century, and especially in the 19th and 20th centuries, a Celtic Revival in Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Brittany and the diaspora revived Celtic motifs in jewellery (such as penannular brooches), architecture, typography and graphic design. The Arts and Crafts movement and designers such as Archibald Knox for Liberty & Co. popularised interlace and knotwork patterns that remain widely used in jewellery and tattoo art today.
