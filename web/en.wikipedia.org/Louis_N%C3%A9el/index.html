Louis Néel

Louis Eugène Félix Néel (22 November 1904 – 17 November 2000) was a French physicist who received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1970 (with Hannes Alfvén) for his fundamental work and discoveries concerning antiferromagnetism and ferrimagnetism, which have led to important applications in solid state physics.

Research

Working at Strasbourg and then in Grenoble, where he built a major school of magnetism, Néel proposed in the 1930s that in some materials neighbouring atomic magnetic moments align antiparallel, cancelling out macroscopic magnetisation — a state he called antiferromagnetism, occurring below a characteristic Néel temperature. He later extended the idea to ferrimagnetism, in which antiparallel moments of unequal size leave a net magnetisation, explaining the properties of ferrites that became essential in electronics.

Applications and later work

Néel's theories underpinned post-war advances in magnetic materials, permanent magnets, computer memory, and microwave devices. During the Second World War he worked on the magnetic protection of naval vessels against magnetic mines. He also contributed to rock magnetism and paleomagnetism and helped found research institutions in Grenoble, including its electrostatics and metal physics laboratories, shaping the city into a major European science centre.
