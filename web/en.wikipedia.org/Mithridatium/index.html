Mithridatium

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mithridatium, also called mithridate, was a legendary semi-mythical panacea — a purported universal antidote to poison — said to have been perfected by Mithridates VI of Pontus (r. 120–63 BC), the Hellenistic king famous for his fear of assassination by poisoning. Mithridates is said to have dosed himself with sub-lethal amounts of poisons to build immunity, a practice now termed mithridatism.

History

After Mithridates' defeat by Pompey in 66 BC, his medical notebooks were captured and translated into Latin, and the antidote bearing his name entered Roman medicine. Later versions, refined by physicians including Andromachus the Elder, physician to Nero, grew into elaborate concoctions — theriac — containing dozens of ingredients such as viper flesh, opium, myrrh, cinnamon, and honey. Mithridatium remained in European pharmacopoeias into the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, manufactured publicly with ceremony in cities such as Venice, before being discredited as medicine advanced; William Heberden's 1745 critique is a landmark in its fall from favour.

Composition and legacy

Recipes attributed to Mithridates listed as many as 65 ingredients. Modern historians consider the original mixture unrecoverable and its supposed universal efficacy legendary, though the practice of graduated self-dosing it inspired retains the king's name, and "mithridate" survives in literature as a synonym for a universal antidote.
