Dizziness

Dizziness is an imprecise term used to describe a range of sensations, including feeling faint, woozy, weak or unsteady. Dizziness that creates the false sense that you or your surroundings are spinning or moving is called vertigo. It is one of the most common reasons adults visit a doctor, and it most often results from a disturbance in the vestibular system of the inner ear or in the circulation and nervous system.

Classification

Clinicians usually sort a patient's complaint into one of four categories:

  • Vertigo — an illusion of spinning or movement, usually vestibular in origin, as in benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), Ménière's disease, labyrinthitis and vestibular neuritis.
  • Presyncope — a feeling of impending faint, typically cardiovascular, as in orthostatic hypotension, dehydration or arrhythmia.
  • Disequilibrium — unsteadiness while walking without a spinning sensation, often from neurological or musculoskeletal causes.
  • Lightheadedness — a vague feeling of disconnectedness, sometimes associated with anxiety or hyperventilation.

Causes

Causes range from benign to life-threatening. Common causes include BPPV (brief vertigo triggered by head movements), low blood pressure on standing, medication side effects, dehydration, anaemia and low blood sugar. Central causes include stroke or transient ischaemic attack in the posterior circulation, migraine (vestibular migraine), and multiple sclerosis. Sudden dizziness with hearing loss, focal weakness, double vision or inability to walk is an emergency sign.

Diagnosis and treatment

Evaluation focuses on the timing and triggers of episodes, an examination of eye movements and balance, and manoeuvres such as the Dix–Hallpike test for positional vertigo. The HINTS examination can help distinguish peripheral from central vertigo in acute cases. Treatment is aimed at the cause: repositioning manoeuvres (such as the Epley manoeuvre) for BPPV, medication review, hydration and blood-pressure management for presyncope, vestibular rehabilitation exercises for chronic imbalance, and urgent stroke care where indicated. Short courses of anti-vertiginous medication may be used for severe symptoms.

Epidemiology

About 20–30 percent of people experience significant dizziness at some point, and prevalence rises with age. Dizziness increases the risk of falls and fractures in older adults and is a frequent cause of emergency-department visits.
