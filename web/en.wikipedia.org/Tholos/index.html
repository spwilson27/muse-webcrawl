Tholos

A tholos (plural: tholoi; from Ancient Greek θόλος, "dome, vault") is a term with several related meanings in the archaeology and architecture of the ancient world. In its most famous sense it denotes the large beehive-shaped tombs of Mycenaean Greece; it is also used for circular buildings of classical Greece and Rome, and for round Neolithic structures in various regions.

Mycenaean tholos tombs

Tholos tombs were built in the Late Bronze Age (c. 1500–1100 BC) as elite burial monuments. A circular chamber was constructed with a corbelled dome, approached by an open passageway (dromos) leading to a doorway (stomion), and the whole structure was covered with an earthen mound. The finest surviving example is the so-called Treasury of Atreus at Mycenae (c. 1250 BC), whose dome is about 14.5 m in diameter and 13.5 m high and was the largest unsupported dome in the world for well over a thousand years. Nine such tombs are known at Mycenae alone, and others at Orchomenos (the "Treasury of Minyas"), Pylos and elsewhere in Greece.

Classical tholoi

In classical architecture, a tholos was a circular building, typically a temple or monument with a ring of columns supporting a conical or domed roof. Famous examples include the Tholos of Delphi in the sanctuary of Athena Pronaia (c. 380 BC), whose purpose remains uncertain; the Thymele at Epidaurus; the Philippeion at Olympia; and the choragic Monument of Lysicrates in Athens. Roman round temples such as the Temple of Hercules Victor in Rome continue the type.

Other uses

The term is also applied to circular Neolithic structures such as the tholos tombs of Los Millares in Spain and round houses in the Halaf culture of the Near East, and in Athens the Tholos in the Agora was a round building where the prytaneis (executive committee of the Council) dined at public expense.
