Madhubani painting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Madhubani painting, also known as Mithila painting, is a traditional style of Indian folk art practised in the Mithila region of Bihar in India and in the adjoining Terai of Nepal. It is characterised by intricate line work, geometric patterns, dense compositions that leave little empty space, and natural pigments applied with sticks, fingers, twigs and matchsticks. Traditionally created by women on the walls and floors of their homes, it is now also painted on paper, cloth and canvas for a wide market.

Origins and themes

According to local tradition, the art originated in the time of the Ramayana, when King Janaka of Mithila commissioned paintings to celebrate the wedding of his daughter Sita to Rama. Historically documented wall painting in the region served ritual purposes: designs decorated the kohbar, the nuptial chamber, and courtyards during festivals and life-cycle ceremonies. Subjects are drawn from Hindu mythology — Rama, Krishna, Shiva, Durga, Lakshmi and Saraswati are frequent — as well as from nature: fish (symbols of fertility), peacocks, the tree of life, the sun and moon, and scenes of village life.

Styles

Several community styles are distinguished, including the Bharni ("filled") style of Brahmin women, with coloured-in figures; the Katchni ("line") style of Kayastha women, built from fine hatched lines; and the Godna style based on tattoo motifs, developed by Dalit artists such as Chano Devi. Twentieth-century artists including Sita Devi, Ganga Devi and Bharati Dayal brought the art to national and international attention, and Sita Devi received the Padma Shri in 1981.

Recognition

After a severe drought in the 1960s, officials encouraged transferring the paintings to paper as an income source, transforming Madhubani into one of India's best-known folk-art exports. Madhubani paintings received a Geographical Indication (GI) tag in 2006–07. Today the art also appears on saris, stationery, railway stations and public buildings in Bihar.
