Deir el-Medina

Deir el-Medina (Arabic: دير المدينة) is an ancient Egyptian workmen's village on the west bank of the Nile opposite Luxor. During the New Kingdom (roughly 1550–1070 BC) it housed the craftsmen and labourers who built and decorated the royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings and the Valley of the Queens.

The village

The settlement, known to its inhabitants as Set Maat, "the Place of Truth", contained around seventy houses inside a mudbrick enclosure wall, together with temples, chapels and the villagers' own richly decorated tombs. Because the community was literate to an unusual degree, it left behind thousands of ostraca — inscribed pottery and limestone flakes — recording work registers, letters, legal disputes, poems and laundry lists.

Life and labour

The workmen were state employees organised in two gangs, paid in grain, fish, vegetables and other rations. In about 1152 BC, under Ramesses III, delayed rations provoked what is often described as the first recorded labour strike in history, documented in the Turin Strike Papyrus. The village was abandoned in the reign of Ramesses XI amid instability and tomb robbery trials, many recorded on papyri.

Excavation

Major excavations by Ernesto Schiaparelli and Bernard Bruyère uncovered the village layout, the Great Pit with its mass of ostraca, and intact tombs such as that of the foreman Kha and the artisan Sennutem.
