Augur

An augur was a priest and official in the classical Roman world. Their main role was to interpret the will of the gods by studying the flight of birds (auspices): whether they were flying in groups or alone, what noises they made as they flew, their direction of flight, and what kind of birds they were. This was known as "taking the auspices".

The augur's curved staff, the lituus, marked out the templum of the sky.

Role and practice

The augural ceremony and function of the augur were prominent in the Roman Republic and early Empire. No significant public business — elections, assemblies, military campaigns, the founding of colonies — was properly undertaken without favourable auspices. The augur marked out a ritual space in the sky (a templum) with his curved staff, the lituus, and watched for signs within it. Unfavourable signs could suspend public proceedings for the day.

College of augurs

Augurs formed one of the four great colleges of priests at Rome. Originally patrician, the college was opened to plebeians by the Lex Ogulnia of 300 BC, which increased its number. Members were elected for life and were typically leading senators; Cicero, himself an augur, wrote extensively on the discipline in De Divinatione, expressing scepticism about its truth while defending its political utility.

Augury in language

The words "augur", "auspicious" and "inaugurate" all derive from this practice; to inaugurate a magistrate originally meant to install him after favourable auspices had been taken.
