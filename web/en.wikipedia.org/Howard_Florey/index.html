Howard Walter Florey, Baron Florey (24 September 1898 – 21 February 1968) was an Australian pharmacologist and pathologist who shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1945 with Ernst Chain and Alexander Fleming for his role in the development of penicillin.

[Image: Howard Florey in his Oxford laboratory]

Early life and education

Florey was born in Adelaide, South Australia, and studied medicine at the University of Adelaide. Awarded a Rhodes Scholarship, he continued his studies at Oxford and Cambridge, later working in the United States and Spain before returning to Britain. In 1935 he became professor of pathology at Oxford.

Penicillin

At the Sir William Dunn School of Pathology, Florey led a team — including Ernst Chain and Norman Heatley — that turned Fleming's 1928 observation of penicillin into a practical drug. The team purified penicillin, demonstrated its dramatic therapeutic effect in 1940–41, and drove the development of industrial-scale production in the United States. Penicillin transformed the treatment of bacterial infection and saved countless lives during and after the Second World War.

Later career

Florey was knighted in 1944 and made a life peer in 1965. He was president of the Royal Society from 1960 to 1965 and played a key role in founding the Australian National University, serving as its chancellor. His portrait appeared on the Australian fifty-dollar note for many years.
