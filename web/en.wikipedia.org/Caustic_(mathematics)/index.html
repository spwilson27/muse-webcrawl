Caustic (mathematics)

FieldDifferential geometry; geometrical optics
DefinitionEnvelope of rays reflected or refracted by a curved surface or object
Familiar examplesCardioid in a coffee cup; nephroid; cusp patterns on water

In differential geometry, a caustic is the envelope of rays reflected or refracted by a curved surface or object, or the projection of that envelope onto another surface. The term also applies to the curve to which light rays are tangent, constituting a boundary of an envelope of rays. Concentrated light along a caustic can be intense enough to burn, which is the origin of the name (from Latin causticus, "burning", via Greek).

Reflection and refraction

A catacaustic is the caustic formed by reflection, and a diacaustic the caustic formed by refraction. For a point source of light, the catacaustic is the evolute of the orthotomic of the reflecting curve. Familiar examples include the cardioid formed by rays reflecting inside a circle from a source on its circumference — the bright curve seen in a cup of coffee — and the nephroid formed when the source is at infinity. The cusp of light patterns on the bottom of a swimming pool are caustics produced by refraction through the rippled surface.

Mathematical description

Caustics are singularities of the Lagrangian or ray map and are a central subject of catastrophe theory and symplectic geometry: near a generic caustic, the ray family is locally described by standard normal forms such as the fold and the cusp. In three dimensions, caustic surfaces have features such as cusp ridges and swallowtails.

Applications

Caustics matter in lens and mirror design (where spherical aberration produces a caustic instead of a perfect focus), in computer graphics, where rendering caustics requires methods such as photon mapping, in seismology, and in the study of gravitational lensing, where caustics in the lens map produce high-magnification events and multiple images.
