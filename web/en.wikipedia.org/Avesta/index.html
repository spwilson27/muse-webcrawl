The Avesta is the primary collection of religious texts of Zoroastrianism. Its texts were composed in the Avestan language and transmitted orally for many centuries before being written down, most likely in the Sasanian period (3rd–7th centuries CE), when a special alphabet was devised to record them precisely. The surviving Avesta is only a fraction of the canon described in Pahlavi literature: of the twenty-one nasks (books) of the Sasanian Avesta, roughly a quarter of the text survives.

Contents

The principal divisions are the Yasna, the central liturgy, which embeds the Gathas — seventeen hymns attributed to the prophet Zoroaster himself and written in Old Avestan — and the Yashts, hymns addressed to individual divinities (yazatas) such as Mithra, Anahita and Tishtrya. The Vendidad (Vidēvdād) is a late compendium of purity laws and mythic material, including the legend of Yima's winter shelter. The Visperad supplements the Yasna, and the Khordeh Avesta ("Little Avesta") is a book of daily prayers for laypeople.

Language and transmission

Avestan is an Old Iranian language closely related to Vedic Sanskrit. The texts were memorised by priests with great fidelity of pronunciation even after the language ceased to be understood; medieval manuscripts, the oldest dating to the 13th–14th centuries, preserve them together with Middle Persian (Pahlavi) translations and commentaries known as Zand. The Avesta was introduced to European scholarship by Abraham Hyacinthe Anquetil-Duperron, who published a translation in 1771.
