The western theater of the American Civil War encompassed major military operations in the states of Alabama, Georgia, Florida, Mississippi, North Carolina, Kentucky, South Carolina, Tennessee, and portions of Louisiana east of the Mississippi River. In contrast to the eastern theater, where the Union struggled for years to achieve breakthroughs, Union forces in the west won a series of strategic victories that progressively split the Confederacy.

Course of the war

Union strategy in the west followed Winfield Scott's Anaconda Plan of seizing the Mississippi River and blockading Southern ports. In early 1862, Ulysses S. Grant captured Forts Henry and Donelson in Tennessee, opening the Tennessee and Cumberland rivers, and won the hard-fought Battle of Shiloh. The Union capture of New Orleans in April 1862 and the fall of Vicksburg on July 4, 1863 — after a prolonged siege — gave the Union control of the Mississippi and cut the Confederacy in two.

In 1863 Union forces under William Rosecrans and then George Henry Thomas drove Braxton Bragg's army out of Tennessee, culminating in the Battles of Chickamauga and Chattanooga. In 1864 William Tecumseh Sherman captured Atlanta and then conducted his March to the Sea across Georgia, followed by the Carolinas campaign in 1865, devastating Confederate infrastructure and morale.

Significance

The western theater produced the Union's most successful generals, including Grant — promoted to overall command in 1864 — Sherman, and Thomas. Historians often argue the war was effectively decided in the west, where Union logistical and riverine advantages were combined with more effective generalship than in the east.
