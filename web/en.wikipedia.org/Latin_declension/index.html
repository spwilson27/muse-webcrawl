Latin declension is the set of patterns according to which Latin nouns, adjectives, and pronouns are inflected — that is, change their endings — to express grammatical case, number, and gender. Latin has five declensions, identified by the ending of the genitive singular: -ae (first), -ī (second), -is (third), -ūs (fourth), and -eī (fifth). Every Latin noun belongs to one declension and has a fixed grammatical gender: masculine, feminine, or neuter.

Cases

Latin nouns inflect for six main cases: the nominative (subject), vocative (direct address, usually identical to the nominative), accusative (direct object), genitive (possession), dative (indirect object), and ablative (separation, means, and many prepositional uses). A seventh, the locative, survives only for names of cities, small islands, and a few common nouns such as domus ("house"). Neuter nouns always have identical nominative, vocative, and accusative forms.

Adjectives likewise decline, generally following the first/second declension pattern (bonus, bona, bonum) or the third declension (fortis, forte), and must agree with the nouns they modify in case, number, and gender. The declension system was largely lost in the transition from Vulgar Latin to the Romance languages, which replaced case endings with prepositions and fixed word order.
