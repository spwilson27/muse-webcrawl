Gangster film

A gangster film is a subgenre of crime film centred on organised crime — the lives of mobsters, their rise and fall, and their conflicts with rivals and the law. The genre crystallised in the United States during Prohibition and the early sound era.

History

The classic cycle began with films such as Little Caesar (1931), The Public Enemy (1931) and Scarface (1932), whose charismatic anti-heroes reflected Depression-era anxieties. Enforcement of the Production Code after 1934 softened the genre, which later merged with film noir. A revival came with Bonnie and Clyde (1967) and Francis Ford Coppola's The Godfather (1972) and The Godfather Part II (1974), often ranked among the greatest films ever made.

Later developments

Martin Scorsese's Goodfellas (1990) and Casino (1995), Brian De Palma's Carlito's Way (1993), and television's The Sopranos (1999–2007) reshaped the genre for modern audiences. Parallel traditions flourish internationally: Hong Kong triad films of John Woo and Johnnie To, Japanese yakuza films, British gangster films such as Get Carter (1971), and Indian underworld films set in Mumbai.

Themes

Recurring themes include the immigrant experience and the dark side of the American Dream, loyalty and family, codes of honour, and the inevitable fall of the gangster protagonist. Critics debate whether the genre glamourises crime even when it punishes its heroes.
