The Creek War (1813–1814), also known as the Red Stick War and the Creek Civil War, was a regional war fought between opposing Creek factions, European empires, and the United States, taking place largely in modern-day Alabama and along the Gulf Coast. The conflict began as a civil war within the Creek Nation between the traditionalist Red Sticks, influenced by the Shawnee prophet Tenskwatawa and his brother Tecumseh, and the accommodationist Lower Creek leadership.

Background

American expansion into Creek lands, the construction of the Federal Road through Creek territory, and the prophecies of Tecumseh's movement split the Creek Confederacy. In February 1813 a Red Stick party returning from Detroit killed two settler families on the Duck River, prompting demands for punishment that ignited open civil war.

Course of the war

On 27 August 1813, Red Sticks attacked Fort Mims in present-day Baldwin County, Alabama, killing hundreds of settlers, militia and their families — the Fort Mims massacre, which brought the United States fully into the war. General Andrew Jackson led Tennessee militia and allied Native forces in a campaign that destroyed Red Stick towns at Tallushatchee and Talladega. The decisive engagement came at the Battle of Horseshoe Bend on 27 March 1814, where Jackson's forces killed more than 800 Red Stick warriors, effectively ending Red Stick resistance.

Aftermath

The Treaty of Fort Jackson, imposed in August 1814, forced the Creek Nation — including Jackson's own Creek allies — to cede some 23 million acres, roughly half of modern Alabama and part of southern Georgia, to the United States. The land cessions set the stage for the later forced removal of the Creek and other southeastern nations under the Indian Removal Act.
