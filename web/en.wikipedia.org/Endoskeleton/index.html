Endoskeleton

An endoskeleton (from Greek éndon, "within", and skeletos, "skeleton") is a structural frame on the inside of an animal, usually composed of mineralized tissue in the group that is most commonly termed the "true" endoskeletons. It is the internal support structure of vertebrates, as well as of some invertebrates such as sponges and echinoderms.

Vertebrate endoskeletons

In vertebrates the endoskeleton is made of bone and cartilage. It provides support and protection for internal organs, serves as an attachment point for muscles, stores minerals such as calcium and phosphorus, and, in mammals, produces blood cells within the bone marrow.

Comparison with exoskeletons

Unlike an exoskeleton, which forms a rigid outer shell and must be molted as the animal grows, an endoskeleton grows with the body and lies beneath the skin and musculature. This allows vertebrates to reach much larger sizes than most exoskeleton-bearing animals.
