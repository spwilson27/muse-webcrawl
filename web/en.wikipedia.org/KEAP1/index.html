Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) is a protein that in humans is encoded by the KEAP1 gene. It is the principal negative regulator of Nrf2 (encoded by NFE2L2), the transcription factor that coordinates the cell's defence against oxidative and electrophilic stress.

Function

Keap1 acts as a substrate adaptor for a Cullin-3-based E3 ubiquitin ligase. Under resting conditions it binds Nrf2 in the cytoplasm and targets it for continuous ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, keeping Nrf2 levels low. Keap1 contains reactive cysteine residues that function as sensors: when electrophiles or reactive oxygen species modify these cysteines, the ligase can no longer degrade Nrf2. Newly synthesised Nrf2 then accumulates, enters the nucleus, and activates antioxidant response element (ARE)-driven genes encoding detoxifying and antioxidant enzymes such as NQO1, heme oxygenase-1, and glutamate-cysteine ligase.

Clinical significance

Somatic mutations in KEAP1 that disable its repressor function are common in lung cancer — particularly non-small-cell lung cancer — where they cause constitutive Nrf2 activation, metabolic reprogramming, and resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Germline KEAP1 mutations have been associated with multinodular goitre. Because the Keap1–Nrf2 pathway is druggable, electrophilic compounds that modify Keap1 cysteines — including dimethyl fumarate, used in multiple sclerosis — act as pharmacological Nrf2 activators.
