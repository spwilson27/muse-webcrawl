Time travel in fiction

Time travel is a common theme in fiction, mainly science fiction. In these stories, a time traveller moves between different points in time, usually using a hypothetical device known as a time machine. The idea of a time machine was popularized by H. G. Wells's 1895 novel The Time Machine.

Early examples

Stories of travel to the future through sleep or supernatural means are ancient, appearing in Hindu mythology (the story of King Raivata Kakudmi), in the Japanese tale of Urashima Tarō, and in Washington Irving's "Rip Van Winkle" (1819) and Charles Dickens's A Christmas Carol (1843), in which Scrooge witnesses past and future events. Travel into the past by a machine or deliberate method is a later development: Edward Everett Hale's "Hands Off" (1881) and Mark Twain's A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court (1889) precede Wells's canonical treatment.

Mechanics and paradoxes

Fictional time travel mechanisms include machines, natural portals, relativistic effects, magic, and dreams. The theme is a vehicle for paradoxes that writers treat in different ways:

  • The grandfather paradox — preventing one's own existence by altering the past.
  • The bootstrap paradox — objects or information with no origin, existing in a causal loop.
  • Alternate timelines and the many-worlds interpretation, in which changes spawn branching histories.
  • Fixed timelines, in which the traveller's actions are part of history and cannot change outcomes.

Common subgenres

Time travel fiction includes alternate history, time-loop stories in which a period repeats (as in the film Groundhog Day), communication with the past via messages or signals, romance across time, and military science fiction about "time wars." Major examples include Wells's The Time Machine, Robert A. Heinlein's "—All You Zombies—", the Back to the Future films, Doctor Who, and Audrey Niffenegger's The Time Traveler's Wife.
