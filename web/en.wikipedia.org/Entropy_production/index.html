Entropy production

Entropy production (or generation) is the amount of entropy generated in thermodynamic processes, i.e. the entropy created within a system by irreversible processes such as friction, mixing, chemical reactions, heat transfer across a finite temperature difference, and unrestrained expansion. It is a central concept of non-equilibrium thermodynamics.

Definition

The entropy balance for a system can be written as: the change of entropy of the system equals the entropy exchanged with the surroundings plus the entropy produced inside. The second law of thermodynamics requires entropy production to be non-negative: it is zero only for reversible processes, and strictly positive for all real (irreversible) processes.

History and development

The concept grew out of the work of Rudolf Clausius on entropy and was formalised in the 20th century in the thermodynamics of irreversible processes by Lars Onsager and Ilya Prigogine, who studied entropy production in terms of fluxes (heat flow, diffusion, reaction rates) and their conjugate thermodynamic forces. Prigogine's principle of minimum entropy production applies to steady states near equilibrium in the linear regime.

Examples

Heat conduction, viscous dissipation in fluid flow, electrical resistance, diffusion and chemical reactions all produce entropy. The Gouy–Stodola theorem relates destroyed exergy (lost work potential) directly to entropy production multiplied by the environmental temperature.
