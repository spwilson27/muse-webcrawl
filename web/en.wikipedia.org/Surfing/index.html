Surfing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Surfing is a surface water sport in which an individual, a surfer (or two in tandem surfing), uses a board to ride on the forward section, or face, of a moving wave of water, which usually carries the surfer towards the shore. Waves suitable for surfing are primarily found on ocean shores, but can also be found in standing waves in the open ocean, in lakes, in rivers in the form of a tidal bore, or in wave pools.

The term surfing refers to a person riding a wave using a board, regardless of the stance. There are several types of boards. The Moche of Peru would often surf on reed craft, while the native peoples of the Pacific surfed waves on alaia, paipo, and other such watercraft. Ancient cultures often surfed on their belly and knees, while the modern-day definition of surfing most often refers to a surfer riding a wave standing on a surfboard; this is also referred to as stand-up surfing.

History

Surfing originated among the Indigenous peoples of Polynesia, where it was a central cultural activity in Hawaii, Tahiti, and Samoa; in Hawaii (he'e nalu) it was practised by ali'i (chiefs) and commoners alike and was woven into religion and social rank. Captain James Cook's crews observed surfing in Hawaii in 1778. In the early 20th century the Hawaiian swimmer and surfer Duke Kahanamoku popularised the sport internationally, demonstrating it in California and Australia, and George Freeth brought surfing to the United States mainland. Surfing became a mass-participation sport and a major cultural phenomenon in the 1950s and 1960s, aided by lightweight foam-and-fibreglass boards, and professional competition developed from the 1970s onward. Surfing made its Olympic debut at the Tokyo 2020 Games.

Equipment

The surfboard is the central piece of equipment. Modern boards are shaped from polyurethane or polystyrene foam, covered with fibreglass cloth and resin, and fitted with fins. Major types include shortboards (high-performance boards roughly 1.8 m long), longboards (2.7 m or more, suited to a smoother, gliding style), funboards, fish, and stand-up paddle boards. Surfers wear wetsuits in cold water and may attach the board to an ankle with a urethane leash. Variants of the sport include bodyboarding, bodysurfing, kneeboarding, and tow-in surfing for very large waves.

Waves and breaks

Swell generated by distant winds travels across the ocean and breaks where the water shallows. Breaks are classified by the bottom contour: beach breaks over sand, reef breaks over coral or rock, and point breaks where waves wrap around a headland. The quality of a wave is judged by its shape, length of ride, and the "barrel" or tube formed when the lip throws over. Famous breaks include Pipeline on Oahu's North Shore, Teahupo'o in Tahiti, Jeffreys Bay in South Africa, and Uluwatu in Bali.

Competitive surfing

The World Surf League runs the Championship Tour, in which surfers are judged on commitment, difficulty, innovative manoeuvres, combinations, variety, speed, power, and flow. Manoeuvres include the cutback, floater, snap, aerials, and tube riding. Big-wave events, such as those at Nazaré in Portugal, reward rides on faces exceeding 18 metres (60 feet).

Culture

Surfing has generated a distinctive subculture centred on beach life, surf music, film, and fashion, with its own slang and codes of etiquette, including rules of priority on a wave. The search for uncrowded, perfect waves drives surf travel worldwide.
