Samson Agonistes

Author: John Milton

Published: 1671 (with Paradise Regained)

Form: Dramatic poem / closet drama

Subject: Samson (Book of Judges, chapters 13–16)

Samson Agonistes (from Greek ἀγωνιστής, “Samson the Champion”) is a tragic dramatic poem by John Milton, first published in 1671 in the same volume as Paradise Regained. Modelled on Greek tragedy, with a chorus and unities of time, place and action, it retells the final day of the biblical judge Samson, blinded and imprisoned by the Philistines in Gaza. Milton, blind himself by the time of composition, gives the poem an intense inwardness: nearly everything happens in dialogue, and the catastrophe itself takes place offstage.

Synopsis

On a festival day of the Philistine god Dagon, Samson is allowed rest from grinding in the prison. A chorus of Danites, his father Manoa, his treacherous wife Dalila, and the Philistine champion Harapha visit him in turn. Manoa’s hopes of ransoming him, Dalila’s plea for forgiveness, and Harapha’s taunts each test Samson’s despair and stir his recovery of purpose. When a Philistine officer summons Samson to perform feats of strength at the temple feast, he at first refuses, then, feeling “some rousing motions” within, agrees to go. A messenger reports that Samson pulled down the temple on the Philistine lords and himself. The chorus closes with the famous lines of consolation: “calm of mind, all passion spent.”

Themes and interpretation

The poem explores blindness, betrayal, divine justice, patience and violent deliverance. Scholars have long debated its relation to Milton’s own life — his blindness, his failed marriages, and the defeat of the Puritan cause he had served — and, in modern criticism, whether Samson should be read as a regenerated hero or as a terrorist figure whose final act is condemned by the text. Milton’s preface, defending tragedy as a moral form and attacking the rhymed heroic drama of the Restoration stage, is itself a classic of English critical prose.
