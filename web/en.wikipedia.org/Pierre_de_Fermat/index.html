Pierre de Fermat (1607–1665).

Pierre de Fermat (French: [pjɛʁ də fɛʁma]; 1607 – 12 January 1665) was a French mathematician who is given credit for early developments that led to infinitesimal calculus, including his technique of adequality. He made notable contributions to analytic geometry, probability, and optics, and is best known for his Fermat's principle for light propagation and his Fermat's Last Theorem in number theory.

Life

Fermat worked as a lawyer and magistrate in Toulouse, pursuing mathematics as an amateur of extraordinary ability. He published little in his lifetime, communicating results in letters to other mathematicians such as Marin Mersenne, Blaise Pascal, and Christiaan Huygens. His correspondence with Pascal in 1654 laid the foundations of probability theory.

Fermat's Last Theorem

Around 1637 Fermat wrote in the margin of his copy of Diophantus's Arithmetica that no three positive integers a, b, c satisfy an + bn = cn for n > 2, claiming a "truly marvellous proof" too large for the margin. The claim resisted proof for 358 years until Andrew Wiles, building on work by many others, completed a proof in 1994–95.

Other work

Fermat's little theorem, his method of infinite descent, his work on maxima and tangents, and Fermat's principle of least time in optics all remain fundamental. His disputes and collaborations placed him at the centre of 17th-century mathematics despite his amateur status.
