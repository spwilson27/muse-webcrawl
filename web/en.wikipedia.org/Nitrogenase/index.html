Nitrogenase

Nitrogenase
EC number1.18.6.1
ReactionN₂ + 8 H⁺ + 8 e⁻ + 16 ATP → 2 NH₃ + H₂ + 16 ADP + 16 Pi
CofactorsIron, molybdenum (or vanadium), sulphur
Found inDiazotrophic bacteria and archaea

Nitrogenases are enzymes produced by certain bacteria and archaea that are responsible for biological nitrogen fixation: the reduction of atmospheric dinitrogen (N₂) to ammonia (NH₃). They are the only known enzymes capable of breaking the exceptionally strong triple bond of N₂ under ambient conditions, and they underpin the natural nitrogen cycle on which most life depends.

Structure and mechanism

The best-studied nitrogenase, the molybdenum-dependent enzyme, consists of two proteins: the Fe protein (dinitrogenase reductase), which delivers electrons using energy from ATP hydrolysis, and the MoFe protein, which contains the iron–molybdenum cofactor (FeMoco) where N₂ binds and is reduced. The overall reaction consumes at least 16 ATP per N₂ and obligatorily produces one molecule of hydrogen. Alternative nitrogenases substitute vanadium or only iron for molybdenum. The enzyme is rapidly and irreversibly inactivated by oxygen, so diazotrophs protect it through strategies such as leghemoglobin in legume root nodules, specialised heterocyst cells in cyanobacteria, and high respiratory rates.

Biological and agricultural importance

Nitrogen-fixing organisms include free-living bacteria such as Azotobacter, cyanobacteria, and symbionts such as Rhizobium in legume root nodules. Before the industrial Haber–Bosch process, nitrogenase supplied nearly all fixed nitrogen entering the biosphere. Understanding its mechanism remains a major goal of chemistry, since a low-energy artificial analogue could transform fertiliser production; efforts to engineer nitrogen fixation into cereals are also under way.
