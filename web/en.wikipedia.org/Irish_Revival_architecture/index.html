Irish Revival architecture

Irish Revival architecture, also known as the Hiberno-Romanesque Revival or Celtic Revival style, was an architectural movement of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries that drew on Ireland's early medieval heritage — round towers, Romanesque churches such as Cormac's Chapel at Cashel, and Celtic interlace ornament — as part of the broader Celtic Revival in Irish culture.

Characteristics

Buildings in the style feature round-headed arches, steeply pitched roofs, carved interlace and knotwork decoration, Celtic crosses, and references to monastic round towers. The style was used especially for churches, both Catholic and Church of Ireland, and for public buildings, libraries and railway stations intended to express a distinctively Irish identity.

Context

The revival paralleled the Gaelic Revival in literature and language promoted by figures such as Douglas Hyde, and the Arts and Crafts movement in Britain. Antiquarian scholarship, notably George Petrie's work on Irish ecclesiastical architecture, provided architects with a documented vocabulary of early Irish forms.

Examples

Notable examples include Honan Chapel in Cork (1916), celebrated for its stained glass by Harry Clarke, and numerous parish churches built across Ireland in the decades around 1900. After independence, the new Irish Free State favoured more restrained styles, though Celtic ornament persisted in public art.
