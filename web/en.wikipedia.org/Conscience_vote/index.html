Conscience vote

A conscience vote, also called a free vote, is a type of vote in a legislative body where legislators are allowed to vote according to their own personal conscience rather than according to an official line set down by their political party. Conscience votes are typically granted on moral or social issues such as abortion, euthanasia, capital punishment, same-sex marriage, and stem cell research.

Practice by country

In the United Kingdom, free votes are a long-standing convention on questions of conscience, with MPs unbound by the party whip. In Australia, conscience votes have been held on issues including the republic, euthanasia, and same-sex marriage; the Liberal Party traditionally permits them more often than the Labor Party, whose members pledge to vote with the caucus. In New Zealand, conscience votes are common on alcohol, abortion, and prostitution law reform, and individual MPs may request that a bill be treated as a conscience issue.

Rationale

The practice rests on the idea that some questions cut across party platforms and touch deeply held personal or religious convictions, so party discipline would be inappropriate; critics argue it can allow legislators to avoid collective accountability.
