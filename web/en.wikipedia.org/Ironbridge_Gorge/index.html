The Ironbridge Gorge is a deep gorge formed by the River Severn in Shropshire, England. It takes its name from the Iron Bridge, the first major bridge in the world to be made of cast iron, which was erected across the river in 1779. The gorge and its industrial settlements are celebrated as one of the birthplaces of the Industrial Revolution.

Industrial history

The steep valley exposed seams of coal, ironstone, limestone and clay near the surface, and the Severn provided transport to distant markets. In 1709 at nearby Coalbrookdale, Abraham Darby I perfected smelting iron with coke rather than charcoal, a breakthrough that made cheap cast iron widely available. Ironworks, foundries, potteries and tileworks — including the Coalport china works and the Jackfield tile works — grew up along the gorge, served by early railways and canals.

The Iron Bridge

Designed by Thomas Farnolls Pritchard and cast at Coalbrookdale under Abraham Darby III, the bridge's ribs were assembled using joinery-like techniques adapted to iron. It opened on New Year's Day 1781 and became a celebrated symbol of the new industrial age, depicted by many artists.

Heritage

Designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1986, the gorge is now interpreted by the Ironbridge Gorge Museum Trust through ten museum sites, including Blists Hill Victorian Town and the Coalbrookdale Museum of Iron. The settlements of Ironbridge, Coalbrookdale, Coalport and Jackfield retain dense evidence of the 18th- and 19th-century industrial landscape.
