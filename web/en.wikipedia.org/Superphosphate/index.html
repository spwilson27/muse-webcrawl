Superphosphate

Superphosphate is a widely used phosphate fertiliser produced by treating phosphate rock with acid to convert its insoluble calcium phosphate into a water-soluble form available to plants. It was the first commercial chemical fertiliser, developed in the 1840s, and remains a major source of phosphorus for agriculture.

Types

Single superphosphate (SSP) is made by reacting ground phosphate rock with sulfuric acid; the product is a mixture of monocalcium phosphate and gypsum containing about 16–20 percent available P₂O₅, together with useful calcium and sulfur. Triple superphosphate (TSP) is made by reacting rock with phosphoric acid instead, yielding a more concentrated product of about 44–48 percent P₂O₅ with little sulfur.

History

John Bennet Lawes, experimenting at Rothamsted in England, patented the manufacture of superphosphate from bones and mineral phosphates in 1842 and founded the commercial industry; Justus von Liebig's work on mineral nutrition provided the scientific rationale. Superphosphate transformed 19th-century agriculture by restoring phosphorus to depleted soils.

Production and chemistry

In SSP manufacture, fluorapatite rock reacts with sulfuric acid to give monocalcium phosphate and calcium sulfate. The reaction also releases hydrogen fluoride gases, which must be scrubbed. Major producing regions include Morocco, China, the United States and Russia, which hold the largest phosphate rock reserves.

Environmental issues

Phosphate rock contains trace cadmium and naturally occurring radionuclides that can accumulate in soils, and excess phosphorus run-off contributes to eutrophication of waterways. These concerns drive interest in phosphorus recycling and precision application.
