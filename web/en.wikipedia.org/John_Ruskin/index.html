John Ruskin (8 February 1819 – 20 January 1900) was an English polymath — writer, lecturer, art historian, art critic, draughtsman and philanthropist — and the leading art critic of the Victorian age. His writing ranged across art, architecture, political economy, education, geology and botany, and his ideas profoundly influenced the Arts and Crafts movement, the Gothic Revival, and movements for social reform.

Early life and Modern Painters

Ruskin was born in London, the only child of a wealthy sherry merchant, John James Ruskin, and was educated largely at home before attending Christ Church, Oxford. His five-volume Modern Painters (1843–1860) began as a defence of the landscape painter J. M. W. Turner and grew into a vast study of art, nature and morality, grounded in close observation: "go to Nature", he urged, "rejecting nothing, selecting nothing and scorning nothing".

Architecture and the Pre-Raphaelites

In The Seven Lamps of Architecture (1849) and The Stones of Venice (1851–1853), Ruskin celebrated Gothic architecture as the expression of free, joyful craftsmanship, and contrasted it with what he saw as the degrading mechanisation of industrial labour. He championed the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, and his letter to The Times in 1851 helped establish artists such as John Everett Millais — whose marriage to Ruskin's former wife Effie Gray (their own marriage was annulled in 1854) became a famous scandal.

Social criticism and later life

From the 1860s Ruskin turned increasingly to political economy and social criticism. Unto This Last (1860), his critique of laissez-faire economics, famously influenced Mahatma Gandhi. As Slade Professor of Fine Art at Oxford (1869–1879, 1883–1885) he founded the Ruskin School of Drawing, endowed the Guild of St George, and undertook practical schemes such as road-building with undergraduate volunteers (including Oscar Wilde). His later years at Brantwood, Coniston, were marked by recurrent mental illness. He died there in 1900 and was buried in Coniston churchyard.

Influence

Ruskin's emphasis on craftsmanship, honesty to materials and the moral value of work shaped William Morris and the Arts and Crafts movement; his social writings influenced the early British Labour movement; and his conservation ethic anticipated aspects of modern environmentalism and the National Trust.
