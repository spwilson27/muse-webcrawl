Prostate-specific antigen

Prostate-specific antigen (PSA), also known as gamma-seminoprotein or kallikrein-3 (KLK3), is a glycoprotein enzyme encoded in humans by the KLK3 gene. PSA is a member of the kallikrein-related peptidase family and is secreted by the epithelial cells of the prostate gland. It is produced for the ejaculate, where it liquefies semen in the seminal coagulum and allows sperm to swim freely.

Biochemistry

PSA is a single-chain glycoprotein with a molecular weight of about 34 kDa. It is a serine protease whose physiological substrate is semenogelin I and II, proteins produced by the seminal vesicles. In healthy men only small amounts of PSA leak into the bloodstream; serum concentrations normally rise slowly with age as the prostate enlarges.

Clinical significance

Elevated blood PSA levels may indicate prostate cancer, benign prostatic hyperplasia, prostatitis, or recent prostate manipulation. Because PSA is organ-specific but not cancer-specific, the PSA test is widely used — though controversial — for early detection of prostate cancer. Values above roughly 4 ng/mL have traditionally prompted consideration of a prostate biopsy, while the rate of change ("PSA velocity") and the ratio of free to total PSA also inform clinical decisions.

History

PSA was first identified in semen in the late 1960s and purified from prostate tissue by Ming Chu and colleagues at Roswell Park in 1979. It was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 1986 for monitoring prostate cancer and in 1994, together with the digital rectal examination, for early detection.
