Histones are highly basic proteins found in eukaryotic cell nuclei that package and order the DNA into structural units called nucleosomes. They are the chief protein components of chromatin, acting as spools around which DNA winds, and they play a central role in regulating gene expression through post-translational modifications. Because they must interact with the negatively charged DNA backbone, histones are rich in the basic amino acids lysine and arginine and are among the most highly conserved proteins known — histone H4 from a pea and a cow differ by only two amino acids.

Structure

Each nucleosome core particle consists of an octamer of histones — two copies each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4 — around which about 147 base pairs of DNA are wrapped. Linker histone H1 binds the DNA where it enters and exits the nucleosome, folding chromatin into higher-order structures. The core histones share a structural motif, the histone fold, and each has a flexible N-terminal "tail" protruding from the nucleosome that is the main site of modification.

Modifications and the histone code

Histone tails are extensively modified: acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, sumoylation and others. These marks alter chromatin accessibility and recruit regulatory proteins ("writers", "erasers" and "readers" of the marks) in a combinatorial scheme often called the histone code. For example, acetylation of lysines by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) generally activates transcription by loosening chromatin, while histone deacetylases (HDACs) reverse it; trimethylation of H3 lysine 4 (H3K4me3) marks active promoters, whereas H3K27me3 is associated with Polycomb-mediated repression.

Variants and history

Variant histones (such as H2A.Z, H3.3 and the centromere-specific CENP-A) are deposited independently of replication and confer specialised functions. Histones were discovered in 1884 by Albrecht Kossel in nuclei of goose red blood cells. Mutations in histone genes are now recognised as drivers of certain cancers (for example the H3K27M oncohistone in diffuse midline gliomas), and HDAC inhibitors are in clinical use as anticancer drugs.
