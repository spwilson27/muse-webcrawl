Crop diversity

Crop diversity is the variety and variability of crops and their wild relatives, encompassing the genetic differences within and between species used in agriculture. It is a component of agricultural biodiversity and underpins food security: genetically uniform crops are vulnerable to pests and diseases, whereas diverse varieties provide resistance traits and resilience to changing climates.

Importance

Over human history, farmers selected thousands of landraces adapted to local conditions. The Irish potato famine of the 1840s, in which a largely uniform potato crop succumbed to late blight, is the classic demonstration of the danger of low diversity. Modern plant breeding draws on crop diversity to introduce genes for disease resistance, drought tolerance and nutritional quality.

Loss of diversity

The Food and Agriculture Organization has estimated that a large share of crop genetic diversity was lost during the twentieth century as farmers adopted a small number of high-yielding modern varieties. Of the several thousand plant species once cultivated for food, only a few dozen now provide the great majority of the world's calories, with rice, wheat and maize predominating.

Conservation

Diversity is conserved ex situ in seed banks and gene banks — most famously the Svalbard Global Seed Vault — and in situ through on-farm conservation of landraces and protection of crop wild relatives. International frameworks include the International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture and the work of the Crop Trust.
