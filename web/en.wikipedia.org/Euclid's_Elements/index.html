The Elements (Ancient Greek: Στοιχεῖα, Stoicheia) is a mathematical treatise consisting of 13 books attributed to the Greek mathematician Euclid of Alexandria, written around 300 BC. It presents definitions, postulates, and propositions — theorems and constructions — proved deductively from a small set of axioms. The Elements is the most influential mathematics book ever written and was used as the standard geometry textbook for more than two thousand years.

Contents

Books I–IV treat plane geometry, including the Pythagorean theorem (I.47) and the construction of regular polygons. Book V develops Eudoxus' theory of proportion; Book VI applies it to similar figures. Books VII–IX are number theory, containing the Euclidean algorithm for the greatest common divisor and a proof that there are infinitely many prime numbers (IX.20). Book X classifies incommensurable magnitudes. Books XI–XIII treat solid geometry, culminating in the construction and classification of the five regular (Platonic) solids.

Method and influence

Euclid's method — deriving a vast structure from five postulates and five "common notions" — became the model of rigorous proof. The fifth (parallel) postulate's awkwardness provoked two millennia of attempts to prove it from the others, which ended only with the discovery of consistent non-Euclidean geometries in the nineteenth century. The Elements was transmitted through Greek, Syriac, and Arabic commentaries and translations, printed at Venice in 1482 as one of the first mathematical books set in type, and reprinted in more editions than any book except the Bible. Abraham Lincoln famously taught himself logic from it, and Edna St. Vincent Millay wrote that "Euclid alone has looked on Beauty bare."
