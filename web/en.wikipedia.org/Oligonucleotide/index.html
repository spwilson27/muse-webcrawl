Oligonucleotide

Oligonucleotides are short sequences of nucleic acid — DNA or RNA — typically between about 13 and 25 nucleotides long, though the term covers strands of a few up to a hundred bases. They are usually single-stranded and are produced by solid-phase chemical synthesis, most commonly the phosphoramidite method, which allows any desired sequence to be manufactured routinely.

Applications in research

Synthetic oligonucleotides are fundamental tools of molecular biology. Short DNA oligos serve as primers for the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and for DNA sequencing, as probes for detecting specific sequences by hybridisation (for example in Southern and Northern blots and in fluorescence in situ hybridisation), and as the building blocks of synthetic genes. DNA microarrays consist of thousands of immobilised oligonucleotide probes.

Therapeutic oligonucleotides

Several oligonucleotide drugs have reached clinical use. Antisense oligonucleotides bind a target messenger RNA and alter its expression or splicing; examples include nusinersen, used in spinal muscular atrophy, and fomivirsen, the first approved antisense drug. Related classes include small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) such as patisiran, aptamers selected to bind proteins, and CpG oligonucleotides used as vaccine adjuvants. Chemical modifications such as phosphorothioate backbones and 2′-sugar substitutions improve stability against nucleases.

Properties

Because binding follows Watson–Crick base pairing, an oligonucleotide's specificity is determined by its sequence; its melting temperature rises with length and GC content. These predictable hybridisation properties make oligonucleotides central to DNA nanotechnology as well as to diagnostics and forensic DNA profiling.
