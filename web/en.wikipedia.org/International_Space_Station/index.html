The International Space Station (ISS) is a large space station assembled and maintained in low Earth orbit by a collaboration of five space agencies and their contractors: NASA (United States), Roscosmos (Russia), ESA (Europe), JAXA (Japan), and CSA (Canada). The station is the largest human-made object in space and the largest satellite in low Earth orbit, regularly visible to the naked eye from Earth's surface.

Construction

The first module, Russia's Zarya, was launched on 20 November 1998, followed by the US Unity node in December 1998 and Russia's Zvezda service module in 2000. The first long-duration crew, Expedition 1, arrived in November 2000; the station has been continuously occupied ever since. Assembly, conducted over more than 40 flights (mostly by Space Shuttle and Russian rockets), was largely completed by 2011, with Russia's Nauka and Prichal modules added in 2021.

Structure and systems

The station consists of pressurised habitation modules, structural trusses, photovoltaic solar arrays spanning about 109 metres, and external radiators. Its two main parts are the Russian Orbital Segment and the US Orbital Segment (which includes European, Japanese, and Canadian modules such as Columbus, Kibō, and the Canadarm2). Power comes from large solar arrays; attitude and orbit are maintained with gyroscopes and periodic reboosts, often by visiting Progress spacecraft.

Purpose and research

The ISS serves as a microgravity and space-environment research laboratory where experiments in astrobiology, astronomy, meteorology, physics, materials science, and human physiology are performed. It also tests spacecraft systems for long-duration missions to the Moon and Mars. Crews are ferried by Roscosmos Soyuz and, since 2020, SpaceX Crew Dragon spacecraft; cargo arrives via Progress, Cygnus, Cargo Dragon, and previously ATV and HTV vehicles.

Future

Operations are funded through at least 2030 by the United States and partner agencies (Russia has announced plans to withdraw after 2024 and build its own station, though cooperation continues). NASA plans a controlled deorbit of the ISS around January 2031 into the South Pacific Oceanic Uninhabited Area near Point Nemo, using a specially developed deorbit vehicle, with commercial stations intended to succeed it in low Earth orbit.
