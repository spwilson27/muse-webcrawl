Excitatory postsynaptic potential

An excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP) is a postsynaptic potential that makes the postsynaptic neuron more likely to fire an action potential. It is a temporary depolarization of the postsynaptic membrane caused by the flow of positively charged ions into the postsynaptic cell, resulting from the opening of ligand-gated ion channels.

Mechanism

When a presynaptic neuron releases an excitatory neurotransmitter such as glutamate into the synaptic cleft, it binds to receptors (for example AMPA receptors) on the postsynaptic membrane. The resulting channels let sodium (and other cations) flow into the cell, producing a small local depolarization — typically a fraction of a millivolt to a few millivolts — that decays with distance and time.

A single EPSP is usually too small to trigger an action potential. Instead, EPSPs sum: temporal summation occurs when several EPSPs arrive in rapid succession at one synapse, and spatial summation occurs when EPSPs from many synapses arrive simultaneously. If the summed depolarization at the axon hillock crosses threshold (about −55 mV), an action potential fires. EPSPs are opposed by inhibitory postsynaptic potentials (IPSPs), which hyperpolarize the membrane, and the balance between excitation and inhibition shapes neural computation throughout the brain.
