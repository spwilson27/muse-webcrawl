The Tsushima Strait (Japanese: 対馬海峡, Tsushima Kaikyō) is the eastern channel of the Korea Strait, lying between the Japanese archipelago and the Korean Peninsula. It is split by Tsushima Island into two main passages: the strait proper between Tsushima and Kyushu, and the Western Channel (also part of the Korea Strait) between Tsushima and Korea. The strait connects the East China Sea to the Sea of Japan and carries one of the world's busiest shipping routes as well as the warm Tsushima Current, a branch of the Kuroshio.

Geography

The strait is roughly 200 kilometres long. Tsushima Island, administered as part of Nagasaki Prefecture, lies midway between the Korean port city of Busan and the Japanese island of Kyushu. The waters around Tsushima are relatively shallow, generally less than 100–150 metres deep. Ferry services link Busan with Tsushima and with Fukuoka (Hakata) across the strait.

History

The strait has been a corridor of trade, migration and conflict between Korea and Japan for millennia. It was the route of the Mongol invasions of Japan in 1274 and 1281, in which Tsushima was overrun. Most famously, it gave its name to the Battle of Tsushima of 27–28 May 1905, during the Russo-Japanese War, in which the Japanese Combined Fleet under Admiral Tōgō Heihachirō annihilated the Russian Baltic Fleet — one of the most decisive naval battles in history and the only fleet action of the dreadnought era decided by battleships.

Today the strait remains strategically important, monitored by Japanese and South Korean forces and crossed by dense commercial traffic between East Asian ports.
