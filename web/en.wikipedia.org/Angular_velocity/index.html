Angular velocity

In physics, angular velocity (symbol ω, or sometimes Ω), also known as angular frequency vector, is a pseudovector representation of how the angular position of an object changes with time — i.e. how quickly it rotates around an axis of rotation and the direction of that axis. The magnitude of angular velocity, also called angular frequency or angular speed, is the rotation rate in radians per second; the SI unit is rad/s. For a particle, the (orbital) angular velocity describes the rate at which its position vector sweeps out angle relative to a chosen origin; for a rigid body, the (spin) angular velocity governs the rotation of the body as a whole.

Particle in three dimensions

For a particle at position r moving with velocity v, the orbital angular velocity is ω = (r × v)/|r|2, perpendicular to the plane of motion, with magnitude ω = v⊥/|r|. In uniform circular motion this reduces to ω = 2π/T, where T is the period, and the speed is v = ωr.

Rigid body

Every point of a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis shares the same angular velocity. Mathematically, if R(t) is the rotation matrix carrying body-fixed coordinates to space coordinates, then the angular velocity tensor is Ω = (dR/dt) RT, an antisymmetric matrix whose associated vector is ω. Because finite rotations do not commute, angular velocity — unlike angular displacement — is a genuine (pseudo)vector, and time derivatives taken in a rotating frame relate to the inertial frame through the transport theorem, the basis of the Euler equations for rigid-body dynamics and of the Coriolis effect.

Spin versus orbital

The total angular velocity of a body can be decomposed into a spin part, about its own centre of mass, and an orbital part, of the centre of mass about an external origin — as with the Earth, whose spin gives the day and whose orbital angular velocity about the Sun gives the year. Angular momentum is linked to spin angular velocity by the moment of inertia: L = Iω for rotation about a principal axis.

Applications

Angular velocity is measured directly by gyroscopes and tachometers and computed in navigation systems, robotics, centrifuges, flywheels, planetary science and biomechanics. In engineering it is commonly quoted in revolutions per minute (rpm) rather than rad/s.
