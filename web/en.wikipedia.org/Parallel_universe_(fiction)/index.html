Parallel universe (fiction)

A parallel universe, also known as an alternate universe, parallel world, or parallel dimension, is a self-contained plane of existence coexisting with one's own. In fiction, parallel universes form a common setting and plot device, particularly in science fiction and fantasy, allowing stories to explore alternative histories, mirror versions of characters, and "what if" scenarios. A collection of parallel universes is often called a multiverse.

Concept

Fictional parallel universes vary in how different they are from the "home" reality: some diverge at a single historical event (the premise of alternate history), others follow different physical laws, and still others mirror the home world with inverted moral alignments or roles. Travel between worlds may occur through portals, magical objects, advanced technology, or dreams. The idea in fiction predates but was popularised alongside scientific speculation, including the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics proposed by Hugh Everett in 1957.

In literature

Antecedents include the otherworlds of mythology and fairy realms reached through magical passage. Edwin A. Abbott's Flatland (1884) explored dimensions mathematically. H. G. Wells used a parallel world in Men Like Gods (1923). Murray Leinster's "Sidewise in Time" (1934) introduced the notion of timelines branching sideways. Later landmarks include Philip K. Dick's The Man in the High Castle (1962), Michael Moorcock's multiverse of the Eternal Champion, Philip Pullman's His Dark Materials trilogy, and Stephen King's Dark Tower series.

In comics, film, and television

DC Comics built much of its continuity around a multiverse of Earths, dramatised in the 1961 story "Flash of Two Worlds" and the 1985 crossover Crisis on Infinite Earths. Marvel Comics likewise maintains numbered alternate Earths. On television, the Star Trek "Mirror Universe" (introduced 1967) gave characters evil counterparts; Sliders, Fringe, and Counterpart revolve around parallel worlds. Films such as Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse (2018) and Everything Everywhere All at Once (2022) made the multiverse central to mainstream cinema.
