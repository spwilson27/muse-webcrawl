Slavophilia

Slavophilia (Russian: Славянофильство) was a movement in 19th-century Russia that argued Russia should develop along its own historical path, based on Orthodox Christianity, the peasant commune (obshchina) and the supposed unity of tsar and people, rather than imitating the rationalism, individualism and materialism of Western Europe.

The Slavophiles — among them Aleksey Khomyakov, Ivan Kireyevsky, Konstantin Aksakov and Yuri Samarin — idealised pre-Petrine Russia, before Peter the Great's Westernising reforms, and contrasted Orthodox Russia's sobornost (spiritual communal unity) with the fragmented West. They debated their rivals, the Westernizers (zapadniki), in Moscow salons and journals of the 1840s and 1850s.

Relations with pan-Slavism

Slavophilia influenced the later, more political movement of pan-Slavism, which sought the unity of all Slavic peoples, though the early Slavophiles were chiefly concerned with Russia's internal development and culture rather than foreign expansion. Slavophile themes — Russian distinctiveness, suspicion of the West, the moral value of the commune — have recurred in later Russian thought, from the pochvenniki around Dostoevsky to modern debates about Russian identity.
