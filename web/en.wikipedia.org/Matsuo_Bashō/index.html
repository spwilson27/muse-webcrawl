Matsuo Bashō

Japanese haiku master (1644–1694)

Matsuo Bashō (1644 – 28 November 1694), born Matsuo Kinsaku and later Matsuo Munefusa, was the most famous poet of the Edo period in Japan. He elevated the comic linked-verse form of haikai and its opening verse, the hokku, into a serious art — the origin of what is now known worldwide as haiku. His most famous poem begins "furu ike ya / kawazu tobikomu / mizu no oto" — "an old pond / a frog jumps in / the sound of water."

Life and travels

Born near Ueno in Iga Province to a low-ranking samurai family, Bashō served a local lord, studied poetry, and after his master's death withdrew from service. Settling in Edo (Tokyo), he taught poetry, took the name Bashō ("banana plant") from a tree planted by his hut, and gathered a school of disciples. From 1684 he undertook a series of long journeys on foot across Japan, combining pilgrimage, poetry and teaching.

Works

His travel journals blend prose and verse (haibun); the masterpiece is Oku no Hosomichi (The Narrow Road to the Deep North, published 1702), recounting his 1689 journey to the northern provinces. Bashō's late aesthetic ideals — sabi (the beauty of solitude and age), wabi, and karumi (lightness) — shaped Japanese poetry permanently. He died in Osaka in 1694 while travelling.
