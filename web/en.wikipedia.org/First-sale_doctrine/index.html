First-sale doctrine

The first-sale doctrine (also called the doctrine of exhaustion) is a limitation on copyright law, found in United States law at 17 U.S.C. § 109, that allows the legitimate owner of a lawfully made copy of a copyrighted work to sell, lend or otherwise dispose of that particular copy without the copyright holder's permission. The doctrine underlies used bookshops, libraries' lending, and the resale of physical media such as CDs and DVDs.

Origins and scope

The doctrine was articulated by the U.S. Supreme Court in Bobbs-Merrill Co. v. Straus (1908), which held that a publisher's copyright did not include the right to control future retail prices of copies already sold. It applies only to the particular copy sold, and does not permit reproduction. In Kirtsaeng v. John Wiley & Sons (2013), the Court held the doctrine applies to copies lawfully made abroad, enabling importation and resale of foreign editions.

Digital works

Courts have generally declined to extend first sale to digital files, because transferring a file creates a new copy; in Capitol Records v. ReDigi (2018) the Second Circuit held that a service reselling "used" music files infringed the reproduction right. Software licences further complicate the picture, as transactions structured as licences rather than sales may avoid the doctrine altogether.
