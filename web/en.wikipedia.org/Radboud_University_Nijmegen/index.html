Radboud University Nijmegen

The Heyendaal campus of Radboud University, Nijmegen.

Radboud University (Dutch: Radboud Universiteit, abbreviated as RU), formerly known as Catholic University Nijmegen, is a public research university located in Nijmegen, the Netherlands. The university was founded in 1923 as the Catholic University of Nijmegen — the first university established by Dutch Roman Catholics after their emancipation — and bears the name of Saint Radboud of Utrecht, a bishop and scholar of the early Middle Ages.

The university counts around 25,000 students and some 5,000 staff, organised into seven faculties covering arts, law, social sciences, science, philosophy and theology, medical sciences, and management. Its medical school is attached to the Radboud University Medical Center (Radboudumc), one of the largest academic hospitals in the Netherlands.

Research

Radboud is internationally known for several research institutes, among them the Donders Institute for Brain, Cognition and Behaviour, the Institute for Molecules and Materials, and the FELIX Laboratory, which houses free-electron lasers for infrared and terahertz research. The university also hosts the High Field Magnet Laboratory, whose magnets are among the strongest in Europe. Two Nobel laureates have been associated with the university: Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov, who carried out their graphene work partly at Nijmegen before moving to Manchester, and received the 2010 Nobel Prize in Physics.

History

The university opened on 17 October 1923 with 189 students. It suffered heavily in the Second World War, closing in 1943 after refusing to require a loyalty declaration from students. Post-war growth moved the university to the Heyendaal estate, where the present campus was built. In 2004 the university adopted the name Radboud University Nijmegen, shortened to Radboud University in 2014, to highlight its roots in the Catholic intellectual tradition while emphasising openness to all. In 2020 the Dutch bishops withdrew the university's "Catholic" designation after a governance dispute, a decision the university contested.
