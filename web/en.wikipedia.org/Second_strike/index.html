Second strike

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ballistic missile submarines are the most survivable leg of second-strike capability.

In nuclear strategy, a second strike or retaliatory strike is a country's assured ability to respond to a nuclear attack with powerful nuclear retaliation against the attacker nation. To have such an ability — essential for nuclear deterrence — a state must retain enough surviving nuclear forces after absorbing a first strike to inflict unacceptable damage in return.

Role in deterrence

Second-strike capability is the foundation of the doctrine of mutually assured destruction (MAD): if both sides know that any first strike will be answered with devastating retaliation, neither has an incentive to attack. A credible second strike therefore depends on survivability — dispersing weapons, hardening missile silos, concealing mobile launchers, and, most importantly, deploying nuclear-armed submarines (SSBNs) that can hide in the oceans for months.

The nuclear triad

Major nuclear powers organize their forces into a "triad" of land-based intercontinental ballistic missiles, submarine-launched ballistic missiles, and strategic bombers, so that no single enemy strike can eliminate all retaliatory capacity. Submarine forces are generally considered the most survivable and hence the backbone of a second strike.

Strategic implications

A state that lacks a survivable second strike may face pressure to launch early in a crisis ("use it or lose it"), which destabilizes deterrence. Conversely, doctrines such as launch on warning, no-first-use policies (declared by countries such as China and India), and debates over missile defense all revolve around preserving or undermining assured retaliation. Analysts regard the second-strike capabilities of emerging nuclear states, particularly China's and India's seaward deterrents, as key variables in modern strategic stability.
