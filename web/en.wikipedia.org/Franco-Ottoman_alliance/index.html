Franco-Ottoman alliance

The alliance between Francis I of France and Suleiman the Magnificent scandalised Christian Europe.

The Franco-Ottoman alliance, also called the Franco-Turkish alliance or "the impious alliance", was an alliance between King Francis I of France and Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent of the Ottoman Empire, formalised from 1536 and lasting, with interruptions, for more than two and a half centuries until Napoleon's invasion of Ottoman Egypt in 1798.

Origins

After the French defeat at the Battle of Pavia (1525), in which Francis I was captured by the forces of Emperor Charles V, the king's mother, Louise of Savoy, sought Ottoman help against the encircling Habsburgs. Suleiman responded favourably, and in 1536 the two powers concluded capitulations granting France commercial privileges in the Levant and establishing a strategic partnership. The alliance was shocking to contemporaries: a Christian king in league with the leading Muslim power against other Christians.

Cooperation

Cooperation included joint military operations in the Mediterranean, such as the Ottoman fleet's wintering at Toulon in 1543–1544 and the joint siege of Nice (1543), as well as coordinated pressure on the Habsburgs in Hungary and Italy. France obtained preferential trading rights, protection of Christian holy places in the Levant and consular rights that underpinned French commerce in the eastern Mediterranean for centuries. Later renewals under Henry IV, Louis XIV and others kept the alliance a cornerstone of French diplomacy; France often acted as the Ottoman Empire's principal European partner and protector of its Catholic subjects.
