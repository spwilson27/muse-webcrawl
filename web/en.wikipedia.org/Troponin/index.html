Troponin

Troponin
ComponentsTroponin C, troponin I, troponin T
LocationStriated muscle (cardiac and skeletal)
FunctionCalcium-dependent regulation of muscle contraction
Clinical useBlood biomarker of myocardial infarction

Troponin is a complex of three regulatory proteins (troponin C, troponin I and troponin T) that is integral to muscle contraction in skeletal muscle and cardiac muscle, but not smooth muscle. The complex sits on the actin filament of the muscle fibre, where it controls the interaction between actin and myosin in response to changes in intracellular calcium concentration.

Blood tests measuring cardiac troponin I or cardiac troponin T are widely used in clinical medicine as sensitive and specific biomarkers for the diagnosis of myocardial infarction (heart attack) and other conditions involving injury to heart muscle.

Structure

Troponin is attached to the protein tropomyosin and lies within the groove between actin filaments in muscle tissue. Its three subunits have distinct roles: troponin C binds calcium ions, troponin I inhibits the actin–myosin interaction, and troponin T binds the complex to tropomyosin. In a relaxed muscle, troponin masks the binding sites for the myosin cross-bridges on actin. When calcium binds to troponin C, a conformational change shifts tropomyosin and exposes the myosin-binding sites, allowing contraction to proceed.

Function

Muscle contraction is driven by the cycling of myosin heads along actin filaments. Troponin acts as the calcium-sensitive switch of this system. A rise in cytosolic calcium, triggered by an action potential, switches the thin filament "on"; removal of calcium by pumps in the sarcoplasmic reticulum switches it "off" and the muscle relaxes.

Diagnostic use

When heart muscle cells are damaged, cardiac troponin is released into the bloodstream. High-sensitivity troponin assays can detect very small amounts of the protein and have become the preferred biomarker in the evaluation of suspected acute coronary syndrome. Elevated troponin indicates myocardial injury, although it does not by itself identify the cause, which may include heart attack, myocarditis, pulmonary embolism or severe systemic illness.
