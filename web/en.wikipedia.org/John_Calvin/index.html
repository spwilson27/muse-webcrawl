John Calvin (1509–1564) was a French theologian and pastor who was a principal figure in the Protestant Reformation and the namesake of the Reformed tradition of Christianity known as Calvinism. From his base in Geneva, Switzerland, his writings and church organisation influenced Reformed churches across Europe and the wider world.

Life

Trained in law and humanist letters, Calvin broke with the Roman Catholic Church around 1530 and fled persecution in France. In 1536 he published the first edition of his Institutes of the Christian Religion and was persuaded by Guillaume Farel to help reform Geneva. Expelled in 1538, he ministered to French refugees in Strasbourg before returning to Geneva in 1541, where he established a disciplined church order — the Ecclesiastical Ordinances — combining pastors, teachers, elders, and deacons. Geneva became a refuge and training ground ("the Protestant Rome") for reformers including John Knox.

Theology

Calvin's system emphasises the sovereignty of God, the authority of Scripture, salvation by grace through faith, and the doctrine of predestination — that God has elected some to salvation. His theology, later summarised in the "five points" (TULIP), shaped Presbyterian, Congregational, and Dutch Reformed churches, and — per Max Weber's famous thesis — arguably fostered a disciplined work ethic linked to capitalism. Controversies of his Geneva years include the execution of Michael Servetus for heresy in 1553, which Calvin approved.
