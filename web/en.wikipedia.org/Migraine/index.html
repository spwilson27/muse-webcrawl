Migraine

Migraine is a neurological condition characterised by recurrent headaches that are typically moderate to severe, throbbing, and often one-sided, accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and sensitivity to light (photophobia) and sound (phonophobia). Attacks usually last 4 to 72 hours. About one third of people with migraine experience an aura — transient visual or sensory disturbances preceding the headache. Migraine affects roughly 15 per cent of the world's population and is about three times more common in women than men.

Phases of an attack

A migraine attack may unfold in up to four phases. The prodrome, hours to days before, can include mood changes, food cravings, and yawning. The aura phase involves fully reversible neurological symptoms developing over 5–20 minutes — commonly shimmering zigzag visual patterns (fortification spectra) or tingling — in about a third of patients. The headache phase follows, after which a postdrome of fatigue and cognitive difficulty may persist for a day or more.

Causes and mechanisms

Migraine has a strong genetic component; genome studies have identified many susceptibility loci, and rare monogenic forms such as familial hemiplegic migraine are linked to ion-channel genes. The mechanism involves activation of the trigeminovascular system and the neuropeptide CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide); cortical spreading depression is believed to underlie aura. Common triggers include hormonal changes, stress and its release, sleep disruption, certain foods, and sensory stimuli, though triggers vary between individuals.

Treatment

Acute treatment uses simple analgesics and NSAIDs for mild attacks, and triptans (serotonin 5-HT1B/1D agonists) for moderate to severe ones. Newer options include gepants (CGRP receptor antagonists) and ditans. Preventive therapy — for frequent or disabling attacks — includes beta blockers, topiramate, tricyclics, and monoclonal antibodies against CGRP or its receptor, introduced from 2018. Medication-overuse headache is a risk of frequent acute drug use.

Epidemiology

Migraine typically begins around puberty, peaks in prevalence in the thirties and forties, and is a leading cause of disability worldwide, particularly among women under 50. It is associated with increased risk of ischaemic stroke in people with aura.
