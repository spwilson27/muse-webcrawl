Skin is the layer of tissue covering the body of an animal. In humans it is the largest organ, with a surface area of about two square metres and a weight of around 4–5 kg in an adult. Together with hair, nails and glands it forms the integumentary system, protecting the body from pathogens, ultraviolet radiation, chemicals and mechanical injury, and preventing excessive water loss.

Structure

Epidermis

The outermost layer, the epidermis, is a stratified squamous epithelium composed mainly of keratinocytes that are produced at its base and shed from the surface over about a month. It contains melanocytes, which produce the pigment melanin responsible for skin colour and UV protection, Langerhans cells of the immune system, and Merkel cells involved in touch. The epidermis has no blood vessels of its own.

Dermis

Beneath lies the dermis, a tough layer of collagen and elastin containing blood vessels, nerves, hair follicles, sweat glands and sebaceous (oil) glands. Sensory receptors here detect touch, pressure, vibration, temperature, pain and itch.

Hypodermis

The deepest layer, the hypodermis or subcutaneous tissue, consists largely of fat and connective tissue; it insulates the body, absorbs shocks and anchors the skin to underlying muscle and bone.

Functions

Besides its barrier role, skin regulates temperature through sweating and control of blood flow, synthesises vitamin D when exposed to sunlight, and serves as a major sensory interface with the environment. Skin colour varies widely in humans, largely as an adaptation balancing UV protection against vitamin D production at different latitudes.

Clinical significance

Skin diseases are among the most common human ailments: acne, eczema, psoriasis, infections such as cellulitis, and skin cancers including melanoma. Because skin is visible, its appearance often signals systemic illness — jaundice, pallor or cyanosis. Wounds heal by haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling; severe burns destroy the barrier and require intensive care and grafting.
