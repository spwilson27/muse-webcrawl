Drusen

Drusen, from the German word for node or geode, are tiny yellow or white accumulations of extracellular material that build up between Bruch's membrane and the retinal pigment epithelium of the eye. They are the most common early sign of age-related macular degeneration (AMD), though their presence alone does not necessarily indicate disease.

Classification

Drusen are classified by size and appearance. Small drusen (diameter under about 63 micrometres) are common with ageing and considered low-risk. Medium and large "soft" drusen — with indistinct borders and diameters over 125 micrometres — are associated with a substantially increased risk of progression to advanced AMD. Drusenoid pigment epithelial detachments and reticular pseudodrusen (subretinal drusenoid deposits) are related findings with distinct prognostic significance.

Pathophysiology

Drusen are composed of lipids, proteins — including complement components and apolipoproteins — and cellular debris. Their formation is thought to reflect impaired transport across the ageing retinal pigment epithelium and chronic local inflammation involving the complement system, a connection supported by genetic risk variants in complement factor H (CFH) and ARMS2/HTRA1.

Diagnosis and management

Drusen are detected by ophthalmoscopy, fundus photography and, most sensitively, optical coherence tomography (OCT). No treatment removes drusen themselves; management of intermediate AMD focuses on monitoring, lifestyle measures such as not smoking, and antioxidant/mineral supplements in the AREDS2 formulation, which reduce the risk of progression to late disease.
