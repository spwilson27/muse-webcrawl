Sufism

Sufism (Arabic: تصوف, taṣawwuf), also known as Tasawwuf, is a mystic body of religious practice found within Islam, characterised by a focus on Islamic spirituality, ritualism, asceticism and esotericism. Practitioners of Sufism are generally referred to as "Sufis" (from صُوفِيّ, ṣūfiyy), and they engage in a variety of practices intended to bring them closer to God.

History

Sufism emerged in the early centuries of Islam as an ascetic and devotional movement, partly in reaction to the worldliness of the expanding Islamic empire. Early figures such as Hasan of Basra (642–728) and Rabia of Basra (c. 714–801), famed for her doctrine of disinterested love of God, laid its foundations. By the 12th century, Sufi orders (tariqas) organised around particular masters and chains of transmission had spread across the Muslim world.

Prominent Sufi thinkers include al-Ghazali, whose Revival of the Religious Sciences reconciled Sufism with orthodox theology; Ibn Arabi, the Andalusian metaphysician; and Jalal al-Din Rumi, the Persian poet whose Masnavi became one of the most widely read works of Islamic literature.

Practices

Central Sufi practices include dhikr, the rhythmic remembrance of God through repetition of His names or formulas; sama, listening to music and poetry, most famously embodied in the whirling ceremony of the Mevlevi order; meditation and contemplation (muraqaba); retreats; and visitation of saints' tombs (ziyara). Disciples (murids) follow a spiritual guide (murshid or shaykh) through stages (maqamat) on the path.

Orders

The major Sufi orders include the Qadiriyya, founded in the name of Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani; the Naqshbandiyya, distinctive for its silent dhikr; the Chishtiyya of South Asia; the Shadhiliyya of North Africa; and the Mevleviyya of Anatolia, associated with Rumi.

Influence

Sufism played a major role in the spread of Islam into sub-Saharan Africa, Central and Southeast Asia, and the Balkans, and its poetry and music remain influential worldwide. Within Islam, Sufi practices have sometimes been criticised by literalist and revivalist movements, while remaining central to the devotional life of many Muslim communities.
