Abraham (originally Abram) is the common patriarch of the Abrahamic religions: Judaism, Christianity and Islam. In the biblical Book of Genesis, God calls Abram to leave his father's house in Mesopotamia and journey to Canaan, promising to make of him a great nation and to give the land to his descendants.

Biblical narrative

Genesis recounts Abraham's covenant with God, marked by circumcision; his sojourn in Egypt; the separation from his nephew Lot; the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah; and the births of Ishmael by Hagar and Isaac by Sarah. The binding of Isaac (the Akedah), in which Abraham is commanded to sacrifice his son and is stopped at the last moment, is one of the most interpreted episodes in scripture. Abraham is said to have died at 175 and to have been buried in the Cave of Machpelah at Hebron, which he purchased as a family tomb.

Religious significance

In Judaism Abraham is the first patriarch and the model of faithfulness and hospitality; in Christianity he is the exemplar of justification by faith (Romans 4); in Islam, as Ibrāhīm, he is a prophet and the builder, with Ishmael, of the Kaaba at Mecca, and the archetypal monotheist (ḥanīf). Modern scholarship generally treats the patriarchal narratives as traditions whose historical setting is debated rather than as straightforward biography.
