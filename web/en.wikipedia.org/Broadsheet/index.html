Broadsheet

A broadsheet is the largest newspaper format and is characterised by long vertical pages, typically of 22.5 by 15 inches (57.2 cm × 38.1 cm) or larger. Other common newspaper formats include the smaller Berliner and tabloid/compact formats. In many countries the term "broadsheet" has also become a byword for serious, high-quality journalism, as distinct from the more sensational style associated with tabloids — though format and content are no longer strictly linked.

History

The broadsheet format developed in the eighteenth century, in part because British newspapers were taxed by the sheet, encouraging publishers to use the largest possible pages. Broadsheets allowed for several columns of dense text and lengthy articles, suiting an elite readership. Iconic broadsheets included The Times of London, The New York Times, The Washington Post, and The Wall Street Journal.

Decline and format changes

From the late twentieth century, many broadsheets switched to smaller formats to reduce paper and printing costs, ease reading on public transport, and attract younger readers. The Independent in Britain pioneered a compact edition in 2003 and soon abandoned broadsheet entirely; The Times followed in 2004. The Guardian adopted the mid-sized Berliner format in 2005 and moved to tabloid size in 2018. In the United States, most major papers narrowed their web width while retaining broadsheet proportions. The word "broadsheet" survives in journalistic usage to denote quality newspapers regardless of their physical size.

Broadsides

Historically, "broadsheet" also describes a broadside: a single sheet printed on one side only, used for proclamations, ballads, advertisements, and political statements from the sixteenth to the nineteenth century. Broadside ballads were a principal medium of popular literature and news.
