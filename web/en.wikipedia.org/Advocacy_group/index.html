Advocacy group

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An advocacy group, also known as an interest group, special interest group, lobby group or pressure group, is an organisation that seeks to influence public policy and resource allocation decisions within political, economic and social systems. Advocacy groups use methods such as lobbying, media campaigns, public demonstrations, research, litigation and electioneering, but generally do not themselves seek to hold governmental office, distinguishing them from political parties.

Types

Groups are commonly divided into sectional groups, which represent a specific segment of society—trade unions, professional associations and business federations—and promotional or cause groups, which campaign on an issue open to anyone who shares the concern, such as environmental organisations, human rights groups and consumer associations. Some classifications also distinguish insider groups, regularly consulted by government, from outsider groups that rely on public mobilisation.

Activities

Lobbying—directly communicating with legislators and officials—is the most characteristic activity; in many countries it is regulated through registration and disclosure rules. Groups also shape the public agenda through advertising and social media, fund or endorse candidates (in the United States through political action committees and 501(c)(4) organisations), bring strategic litigation, and supply expertise to policymakers.

Debate

Political scientists debate whether advocacy groups strengthen democracy by representing interests and informing debate, or distort it by amplifying the voices of the wealthy and well-organised—the "upper-class accent" of what E. E. Schattschneider called the pluralist chorus. Mancur Olson's The Logic of Collective Action (1965) explained why small, concentrated interests often organise more effectively than large, diffuse ones such as consumers or taxpayers.
