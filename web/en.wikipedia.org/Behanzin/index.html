Behanzin

Behanzin (c. 1845 – 10 December 1906) was a King of Dahomey, in present-day Benin, from 1889 until 1894. He succeeded his father, Glele, and became the eleventh king of Dahomey, and the last independent ruler of the kingdom before its conquest by France. Behanzin was renowned for his resistance to French colonial expansion during the Franco-Dahomean Wars, for his intelligence as a military strategist, and for commanding an army that included the famed Dahomey Amazons, the women soldiers known in Fon as the Mino.

Early life

Behanzin, born Prince Kondo, was the son of King Glele. As crown prince he refused to meet the French envoy Jean-Marie Bayol when Bayol requested to see him, a slight that foreshadowed their later conflict. He took the strong name Behanzin — often interpreted as "the universe carries the egg which the world desires" — upon his accession, and adopted the shark as his royal symbol, a reference to his claim that, like a shark in the sea, he would defend the coasts of his kingdom.

Conflict with France

Relations between Dahomey and France deteriorated over French claims to the port of Cotonou and the kingdom's continued involvement in the Atlantic slave trade, as well as raids on territories under French protection. In the First Franco-Dahomean War (1890), Behanzin's forces attacked the French protectorate of Porto-Novo, and the French won a costly victory at the Battle of Atchoupa. In the Second Franco-Dahomean War (1892–1894), a French expedition under Alfred-Amédée Dodds advanced on the capital, Abomey. Despite fierce resistance, French forces with superior firepower, including machine guns and artillery, defeated Dahomean armies at Dogba, Poguessa, and Cana. Before the French entered Abomey in November 1892, Behanzin ordered the royal palaces burned rather than surrendered intact.

Exile and death

Behanzin continued guerrilla resistance for a time but surrendered at the end of January 1894 after his brother Agoli-agbo was installed by the French as a puppet king. He was exiled first to Martinique and later to Blida, Algeria, where he died on 10 December 1906. His remains were returned to Abomey in 1928.

Legacy

Behanzin is remembered in Benin as a national hero and a symbol of resistance to colonialism. Statues of him stand in Abomey and Cotonou, and in the epic film Camp de Thiaroye and other works of African art and literature he is celebrated as the "Shark King".
