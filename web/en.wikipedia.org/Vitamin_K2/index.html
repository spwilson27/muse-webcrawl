Vitamin K₂

Vitamin K₂ (menaquinones)
ClassFat-soluble vitamin
FormsMK-4 through MK-13
SourcesFermented foods (e.g. nattō), cheese, egg yolk, meats

Vitamin K₂ refers to the menaquinones, a group of fat-soluble compounds that are one of the three forms of vitamin K, the others being vitamin K₁ (phylloquinone) and vitamin K₃ (menadione). Menaquinones are designated MK-n, where n is the number of isoprenoid residues in the side chain; MK-4 and MK-7 are the forms most commonly studied and sold as supplements.

Sources

Menaquinones are produced by bacteria. MK-7 is abundant in nattō, a Japanese fermented soybean dish, while MK-4 occurs in animal products such as egg yolk, butter, and meat, partly from conversion of vitamin K₁ in animal tissues. Gut bacteria also synthesize long-chain menaquinones, though the extent of their absorption and contribution to vitamin K status is debated.

Function

Like other K vitamins, menaquinones serve as cofactors for the enzyme gamma-glutamyl carboxylase, which activates proteins involved in blood coagulation. Vitamin K₂ is especially associated with extra-hepatic carboxylation, including osteocalcin in bone and matrix Gla protein in blood-vessel walls. Observational studies, such as the Rotterdam Study (2004), associated higher menaquinone intake with reduced arterial calcification and coronary heart disease risk, but clinical-trial evidence for supplement benefits remains limited.

History

Weston A. Price's "Activator X", described in the 1930s from butter oil of grass-fed cows, was later proposed to have been vitamin K₂. The distinct menaquinone homologues were characterized chemically in the mid-20th century.
