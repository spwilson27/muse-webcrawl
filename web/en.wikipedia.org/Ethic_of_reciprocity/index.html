Ethic of reciprocity

The ethic of reciprocity, often called the Golden Rule, is the principle of treating others as one would want to be treated by them. It is arguably the most essential basis for the modern concept of human rights, appearing in some form in nearly every major religion and ethical tradition. A key element of the ethic of reciprocity is that a person attempting to live by it must treat all people with consideration, not only members of their own group.

Forms

The rule exists in both positive and negative forms:

  • Positive form: "Treat others as you would like others to treat you" — a directive to extend kindness.
  • Negative form (sometimes called the "Silver Rule"): "Do not treat others in ways that you would not like to be treated" — a directive to refrain from harm.

History

Versions of the principle appear in ancient Egyptian wisdom literature, in the teachings of Confucius ("Do not impose on others what you do not wish for yourself"), in Hindu texts such as the Mahabharata, in Buddhism, and in Zoroastrianism. In Judaism, Hillel the Elder summarized the Torah with the negative formulation: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow." In Christianity, Jesus stated the positive form in the Sermon on the Mount: "In everything, do to others what you would have them do to you" (Matthew 7:12). Islam expresses the idea in a hadith: "None of you truly believes until he wishes for his brother what he wishes for himself."

Philosophical discussion

Immanuel Kant reformulated the idea in his categorical imperative, requiring that one act only according to maxims that could be universal laws. Critics note that the Golden Rule can fail when others' preferences differ from one's own, leading some ethicists to propose the "Platinum Rule": treat others as they wish to be treated. Nevertheless, the ethic of reciprocity remains a widely shared cross-cultural moral benchmark, affirmed in the 1993 Declaration Toward a Global Ethic by the Parliament of the World's Religions.
