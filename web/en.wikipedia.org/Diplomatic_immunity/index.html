Diplomatic immunity

Diplomatic immunity is a principle of international law by which certain foreign government officials are not subject to the jurisdiction of local courts and other authorities for both their official and, to a large extent, their personal activities. The principle was codified in the 1961 Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, which has been ratified by nearly every state.

Embassies and accredited diplomats are protected under the Vienna Convention.

Scope

Diplomatic agents enjoy inviolability of person and residence, immunity from criminal jurisdiction, and (with exceptions) from civil and administrative jurisdiction; their families and mission premises and archives are also protected. Immunity belongs to the sending state, which may waive it. The receiving state's principal remedy against abuse is to declare the diplomat persona non grata and require their departure.

History and abuses

Immunity for envoys is ancient, appearing in Greek, Roman, and Asian practice, and was elaborated by early-modern jurists such as Grotius. Modern controversies involve unpaid fines and traffic offences, smuggling, and occasionally serious crimes, which attract public criticism but rarely change the framework, because states value reciprocal protection of their own diplomats.
