Volcanic winter

A volcanic winter is a reduction in temperature caused by volcanic ash and droplets of sulfuric acid and water obscuring the Sun and raising the albedo of the Earth (increasing the reflection of solar radiation) after a large, particularly explosive volcanic eruption. Sulfate aerosols in the stratosphere can persist for years, cooling the surface while warming the stratosphere itself.

Mechanism

Explosive eruptions inject sulfur gases—chiefly sulfur dioxide—into the stratosphere, above the reach of rain that would quickly wash them out of the lower atmosphere. There the gases oxidize into sulfate aerosol particles, which spread around the globe within weeks. These particles scatter and absorb solar radiation, reducing the energy reaching Earth's surface. Cooling can persist for several years until the aerosols settle out. Unlike ash, which falls within weeks, the sulfate veil is responsible for most of the climatic effect.

Historical examples

Toba (c. 74,000 years ago)

The super-eruption of Toba in present-day Indonesia is hypothesized to have produced a volcanic winter lasting six to ten years, with global cooling that may have persisted for a millennium. Some scientists have linked it to a bottleneck in human genetic diversity, though the severity of its effect on human populations remains debated.

536 mystery cloud

Beginning in 536, a mysterious fog darkened the Sun over Europe and Asia for about 18 months, initiating the coldest decade in the Northern Hemisphere in over two thousand years. Ice-core evidence points to major volcanic eruptions, possibly in Iceland or North America, with further eruptions in 540 and 547 compounding the effect. Crop failures and famine followed across many regions.

Laki (1783)

The Icelandic Laki fissure eruption released huge quantities of sulfur dioxide, producing a dry fog over Europe and North America, severe winters, and crop failures; global temperatures dropped by roughly 1 °C.

Tambora and the Year Without a Summer (1816)

The 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora, the largest of the historical era, ejected enough material to cause the "Year Without a Summer" in 1816: snow fell in New England in July, crops failed across the Northern Hemisphere, and food riots and famine spread in Europe and Asia.

Krakatoa (1883) and Pinatubo (1991)

Krakatoa's eruption lowered global temperatures by about 1.2 °C for several years. Pinatubo's 1991 eruption cooled the planet by roughly 0.5 °C for two to three years and provided a natural experiment that improved climate models' treatment of aerosol forcing.

Effects on life

Volcanic winters shorten growing seasons and can trigger crop failures, famine, and disease outbreaks. Exceptionally large eruptions, such as flood basalt events, have been implicated in mass extinctions, although those events also emit warming gases and their climatic effects unfold over much longer timescales.
