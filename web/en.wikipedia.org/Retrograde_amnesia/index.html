Retrograde amnesia

Retrograde amnesia (RA) is a loss of memory-access to events that occurred or information that was learned in the past. It is caused by brain trauma, disease, or psychological factors, and contrasts with anterograde amnesia, in which a person cannot form new memories after the precipitating event.

Retrograde amnesia
SpecialtyNeurology, psychiatry
SymptomsLoss of memories of events before onset

Characteristics

In retrograde amnesia, memory loss usually follows a temporal gradient described by Ribot's law (1881): memories closest to the time of injury are the most vulnerable, while remote, older memories are relatively spared. The affected period may span minutes to decades, although dense loss of remote memories is rare in purely retrograde cases. Personal identity is usually preserved, unlike in some dissociative disorders; patients typically retain language and procedural skills.

Memory types and retrograde amnesia

Causes

Retrograde amnesia can result from:

  • Traumatic brain injury and concussion
  • Stroke or cerebral hypoxia
  • Encephalitis and other infections
  • Korsakoff syndrome associated with chronic alcohol misuse and thiamine deficiency
  • Electroconvulsive therapy (usually limited in extent)
  • Neurodegenerative disease, including Alzheimer's disease
  • Psychological trauma — psychogenic or dissociative amnesia, in which the loss is often selective for personal memories

Related research

The study of patients with retrograde amnesia has informed theories of memory consolidation. Standard consolidation theory holds that the hippocampus is needed for a limited time after learning, after which memories are stored in the neocortex; multiple trace theory instead proposes that episodic memories remain hippocampus-dependent. Famous cases, such as patient H.M. (Henry Molaison), who had both retrograde and dense anterograde amnesia after bilateral medial temporal lobe surgery, have been central to this debate.

Treatment and prognosis

There is no specific drug treatment for retrograde amnesia. Management addresses the underlying cause; memory for the period immediately before an injury often partially returns as recovery proceeds ("shrinking" retrograde amnesia), although a permanent gap for moments around the event is common. Rehabilitation focuses on compensatory strategies and relearning of lost information.
