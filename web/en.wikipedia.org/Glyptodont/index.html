Glyptodont

Glyptodonts are a group of extinct large, heavily armoured armadillos (family Glyptodontidae within the order Cingulata) that lived in the Americas from the late Eocene until the end of the Pleistocene, about 10,000 years ago. They are characterised by a rigid, domed carapace made of fused bony scutes (osteoderms), quite unlike the flexible banded shell of living armadillos.

Scientific classification
Kingdom: Animalia
Order: Cingulata
Family: Glyptodontidae
Range: Late Eocene – Holocene (South and North America)

Description

Glyptodonts ranged from the size of a large dog to animals over 3 metres long and weighing up to about 2 tonnes, comparable to a small car. The solid carapace covered the body like a tortoise shell, and the head was protected by a bony shield; many species also carried a heavily armoured tail, in some — such as Doedicurus — ending in a spiked club that may have been used in combat with rivals or predators. They were herbivores, with high-crowned, lobed teeth adapted to grazing tough grasses; the name means "carved tooth".

Evolution and distribution

Glyptodonts evolved in South America, which was an island continent for most of the Cenozoic. After the formation of the Isthmus of Panama about 3 million years ago, during the Great American Interchange, some genera such as Glyptotherium spread north into Central and North America. DNA extracted from a Doedicurus fossil in 2016 confirmed that glyptodonts are nested within the armadillos, representing a subfamily (Glyptodontinae) of giant armadillos rather than a wholly separate lineage.

Extinction and humans

Glyptodonts survived until the very end of the last Ice Age and overlapped with early human inhabitants of the Americas; there is evidence that humans hunted them and may occasionally have used their great shells as shelters. They disappeared in the Quaternary extinction event around 12,000–10,000 years ago, which removed most American megafauna — a die-off attributed to some combination of climate change and human hunting. Charles Darwin collected glyptodont fossils in South America during the voyage of the Beagle, and their resemblance to living armadillos contributed to his thinking on evolution.
