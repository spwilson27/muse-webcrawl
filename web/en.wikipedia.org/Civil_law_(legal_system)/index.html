Civil law (legal system)

OriginsRoman law; Corpus Juris Civilis (6th century AD)

Civil law is a legal system originating in Italy and France and adopted in much of the world. It is distinct from case law (common law), which has developed from English jurisprudence. The civil law system is intellectualized within the framework of Roman law, and with core principles codified into a referable system, which serves as the primary source of law. The civil law system is often contrasted with the common law system, which originated in medieval England.

Historically, a civil law is the group of legal ideas and systems ultimately derived from the Corpus Juris Civilis, but heavily overlain by Napoleonic, Germanic, canonical, feudal, and local practices, as well as doctrinal strains such as natural law, codification, and legal positivism.

Overview

Conceptually, civil law proceeds from abstractions, formulates general principles, and distinguishes substantive rules from procedural rules. It holds case law secondary and subordinate to statutory law. Civil law is often paired with the inquisitorial system, but the terms are not synonymous: civil law countries employ varying procedures, and the judge's role in investigating the facts differs by jurisdiction.

Civil law is the most widespread system of law in the world, in force in various forms in about 150 countries. It draws heavily on Roman law, arguably the most intricate known legal system before the modern era. Its principal features include codification into civil codes, a systematic treatment of obligations, property, and persons, and legal scholarship (doctrine) as an influential source of interpretation.

History

The law of the Roman Empire, compiled under Justinian I in the 6th century, was rediscovered in 11th-century Italy and taught at the University of Bologna, becoming the learned law (ius commune) of continental Europe. The Napoleonic Code of 1804 decisively shaped modern codification and was carried by conquest and imitation across Europe and to Latin America, the Near East, and beyond. Germany's Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch (1900) and the Swiss Civil Code (1912) were also highly influential.

Subcategories

  • Napoleonic: France, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, Belgium, and their former colonies.
  • Germanic: Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Greece, Turkey, Japan, South Korea.
  • Nordic: Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, Sweden — sometimes treated as a separate family.
  • Chinese: a mixture of civil law and socialist law influences.
