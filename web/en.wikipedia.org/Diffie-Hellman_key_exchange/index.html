Diffie–Hellman (DH) key exchange is a mathematical method of securely exchanging cryptographic keys over a public channel, first published by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman in their landmark 1976 paper "New Directions in Cryptography." It was the first practical example of public-key cryptography: two parties who have never met can jointly establish a shared secret over an insecure network, and an eavesdropper who observes the entire exchange cannot (with adequate parameters) compute that secret. The shared secret can then seed a symmetric cipher for the actual communication.

Protocol

The classic protocol works in a multiplicative group of integers modulo a prime:

1. Alice and Bob publicly agree on a large prime p and a generator g.
2. Alice chooses a secret a, computes A = ga mod p, and sends A to Bob.
3. Bob chooses a secret b, computes B = gb mod p, and sends B to Alice.
4. Alice computes s = Ba mod p; Bob computes s = Ab mod p.
Both obtain the same value, s = gab mod p, which serves as the shared secret.

Security rests on the difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem: given p, g and ga mod p, recovering a is computationally infeasible for suitably large parameters (2048-bit groups or more are recommended). Modern deployments usually employ the elliptic-curve variant (ECDH), which achieves equivalent security with much smaller keys.

Properties and attacks

Diffie–Hellman by itself provides no authentication: an active attacker can mount a man-in-the-middle attack, exchanging separate keys with each party. Real protocols therefore authenticate the exchange, typically with digital signatures, as in TLS. When fresh ephemeral keys are generated for each session (DHE/ECDHE), the protocol provides forward secrecy: compromise of a long-term key does not expose past session keys. The 2015 Logjam attack showed that widely shared 512- and 1024-bit groups could be attacked, including precomputation against common primes, pushing the field toward larger groups and elliptic curves. Diffie–Hellman is vulnerable in principle to quantum computers running Shor's algorithm.

History

The concept of key agreement via one-way functions grew from Ralph Merkle's work on "Merkle's puzzles" (1974). Unknown to the public, researchers at Britain's GCHQ — James Ellis, Clifford Cocks and Malcolm Williamson — had developed equivalent ideas in classified work between 1969 and 1975, declassified only in 1997. Diffie and Hellman received the ACM Turing Award in 2015 for the invention of public-key cryptography.
