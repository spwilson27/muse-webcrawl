Speaker recognition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

FieldBiometrics, signal processing, machine learning

ApplicationsBanking authentication, forensics, smart speakers, call analytics

Speaker recognition is the process of identifying or confirming the identity of a person from characteristics of their voice. It is a biometric technology distinct from speech recognition, which identifies what is said: speaker recognition asks who is speaking, exploiting the fact that the shape of each person's vocal tract and their manner of speaking make their voice distinctive.

Tasks

  • Speaker verification (authentication): a one-to-one check of a claimed identity, used to authorise access — the system accepts or rejects the claim. Performance depends on the required false-accept and false-reject rates.
  • Speaker identification: a one-to-many search to determine which of a set of known speakers produced an utterance, or whether it is someone unknown.
  • Speaker diarisation: partitioning a recording by "who spoke when", without necessarily knowing identities — used to structure meetings, broadcasts and calls.

Systems may be text-dependent, requiring a fixed phrase, or text-independent, working with free speech.

Technology

Classical systems extracted mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) and modelled them with Gaussian mixture models adapted from a universal background model. The i-vector framework (2009) compressed utterances into fixed low-dimensional vectors scored by probabilistic linear discriminant analysis. Since about 2017, deep neural networks have dominated: networks trained to classify speakers output embeddings such as x-vectors from an internal layer, giving robust representations from short samples. Performance is benchmarked in evaluations run by NIST since 1996.

Applications and challenges

Banks and call centres use voice verification for fraud-resistant authentication; law enforcement and forensic phonetics use it to compare recordings; smart speakers use it to personalise responses. Performance degrades with channel mismatch (telephone versus microphone), background noise, illness, ageing and short samples. The technology is vulnerable to spoofing — replay attacks and, increasingly, synthetic voices generated by text-to-speech and voice cloning — driving research into anti-spoofing countermeasures. Voice data also raises privacy questions, since recordings can reveal identity, health and emotion.
