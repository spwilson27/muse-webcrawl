Carnivorous plant

Known speciesOver 800
GeneraAbout 19 (e.g. Nepenthes, Drosera, Dionaea)
HabitatNutrient-poor wetlands and bogs
EvolutionIndependent origins at least 12 times

Carnivorous plants are plants that derive some or most of their nutrients from trapping and consuming animals, typically insects and other arthropods. They have adapted to grow in places where the soil is thin or poor in nutrients, especially nitrogen, such as acidic bogs. More than 800 species of true carnivorous plants are known.

The Venus flytrap (Dionaea muscipula), the best-known carnivorous plant.

Trap types

  • Pitfall traps (pitcher plants such as Nepenthes and Sarracenia) use a rolled leaf holding digestive fluid.
  • Flypaper traps (sundews, Drosera; butterworts, Pinguicula) use sticky mucilage on leaf surfaces.
  • Snap traps (Venus flytrap and waterwheel plant) close modified leaves around triggering prey.
  • Bladder traps (bladderworts, Utricularia) suck prey into a vacuum bladder.
  • Lobster-pot traps (corkscrew plants, Genlisea) force prey inward past one-way hairs.

Evolution and ecology

Plant carnivory has evolved independently at least twelve times in five different orders of flowering plants — a striking case of convergent evolution. The traps are modified leaves; the benefits of prey capture outweigh the costs of trap construction only in bright, wet, nutrient-poor habitats. Charles Darwin's 1875 treatise Insectivorous Plants established the carnivory of many species experimentally.

Cultivation and conservation

Venus flytraps, pitcher plants, and sundews are widely cultivated by hobbyists, requiring pure water and nutrient-free soil. Many wild populations are threatened by habitat destruction, drainage of wetlands, and poaching; several Nepenthes species are critically endangered.
