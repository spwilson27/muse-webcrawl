Crime mapping

Crime mapping is used by analysts in law enforcement agencies to map, visualise, and analyse crime incident patterns. It is a key component of crime analysis and of the CompStat policing strategy. Mapping crime, using geographic information systems (GIS), allows law enforcement analysts to identify crime hot spots, along with other trends and patterns.

Techniques

Analysts plot recorded incidents as points on a map and apply spatial statistics to detect structure. Common methods include:

  • Hot spot analysis — identifying concentrations of incidents, often with kernel density estimation to produce continuous "heat" surfaces;
  • Spatial statistical tests — statistics such as the nearest neighbour index and Getis–Ord Gi* to test whether clustering exceeds chance;
  • Repeat and near-repeat analysis — measuring how victimisation raises short-term risk nearby;
  • Risk terrain modelling — combining layers of environmental features (bars, transport stops, vacant lots) associated with crime opportunities.

These techniques draw on criminological theories such as crime pattern theory, routine activity theory, and rational choice theory, and on the observation that crime concentrates heavily: a small fraction of places typically accounts for a large share of offences.

Applications

Police departments use crime maps for patrol allocation, problem-oriented policing projects, and public transparency — many agencies publish public crime maps online. Mapping also supports tactics such as predictive policing, whose use of historical patterns to forecast likely incident locations has drawn criticism over potential feedback loops and bias against over-policed communities. Researchers caution that maps reflect reporting and enforcement practices as much as underlying offending.
