Takaful

Takaful (Arabic: التكافل, from kafala, "guaranteeing each other") is a type of Islamic insurance in which members contribute money into a pooled fund in order to guarantee one another against loss or damage. It is structured to comply with sharia (Islamic law), avoiding the elements of conventional insurance that many scholars consider prohibited: excessive uncertainty (gharar), gambling (maysir), and interest (riba).

Principles

Participants (musytarik) contribute donations (tabarru') to a common fund, from which claims are paid. The takaful operator manages the fund as an agent (wakil) for a fee, or as a mudarib sharing in investment profits. Any surplus remaining after claims and reserves may be returned to participants or retained in the fund, according to the contract. Investments of the fund must themselves be sharia-compliant, avoiding interest-bearing instruments and prohibited industries.

Historical development

Mutual guarantee arrangements have precedents in early Islamic practice, such as the 'aqila system of collective blood-money payment. The modern takaful industry emerged in the late 1970s: the first takaful company was established in Sudan in 1979, followed by companies in Saudi Arabia and Malaysia, where the Takaful Act of 1984 created a regulatory framework. Malaysia and the Gulf states remain the largest markets, and takaful has expanded into South and Southeast Asia, Africa, and, in niche form, Western markets.

Retakaful

Because conventional reinsurance involves the same sharia concerns, takaful operators cede risk to retakaful companies, or, under dispensation recognised by many scholars, to conventional reinsurers where no retakaful capacity exists.
