Rocket engine

A rocket engine is a reaction engine that produces thrust by expelling mass — usually high-temperature gas generated from stored propellants — at high velocity through a nozzle. Unlike air-breathing jet engines, a rocket carries both its fuel and its oxidizer, so it can operate in the vacuum of space. Rocket engines power missiles, launch vehicles, and spacecraft propulsion systems.

Principle of operation

A rocket engine works on Newton's third law: expelling exhaust backward produces an equal and opposite forward force. In a chemical rocket, fuel and oxidizer burn in a combustion chamber at high pressure and temperature; the hot gas expands through a converging–diverging (de Laval) nozzle, accelerating to supersonic speed. Performance is measured by specific impulse (exhaust velocity expressed in seconds), the thrust-to-weight ratio, and chamber pressure. The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation relates the velocity change a rocket can achieve to its exhaust velocity and mass ratio.

Types

Chemical rockets

  • Solid-propellant rockets burn a premixed grain of fuel and oxidizer (as in the Space Shuttle and Ariane boosters). They are simple, storable, and high-thrust, but cannot be throttled or shut down once ignited.
  • Liquid-propellant rockets store fuel and oxidizer separately — for example liquid hydrogen/liquid oxygen (RS-25, Vulcain), kerosene/oxygen (Merlin, RD-180), or methane/oxygen (Raptor, BE-4) — and feed them to the chamber by turbopumps or tank pressure. They offer higher specific impulse, throttling, and restart capability at the cost of complexity.
  • Hybrid rockets combine a solid fuel with a liquid or gaseous oxidizer.
  • Monopropellant rockets decompose a single propellant such as hydrazine over a catalyst, and are used for spacecraft attitude control; cold-gas thrusters are simpler still.

Electric and nuclear propulsion

Ion thrusters and Hall-effect thrusters accelerate ions electrically to very high exhaust velocities, yielding high specific impulse but very low thrust — ideal for deep-space missions such as Dawn. Nuclear thermal rockets, tested on the ground under the NERVA program but never flown, would heat hydrogen in a nuclear reactor to achieve roughly twice the specific impulse of chemical engines.

Cooling and engineering

Chamber walls must survive gas temperatures above 3,000 K. Methods include regenerative cooling (circulating fuel through channels in the wall, as in most large liquid engines), film cooling, ablative liners (common in solid motors and some liquid chambers), and radiation cooling for small thrusters. Injector design — ensuring stable, efficient mixing — is critical to avoiding destructive combustion instability.

History

Gunpowder rockets originated in medieval China and were used militarily for centuries. Modern rocketry was founded theoretically by Konstantin Tsiolkovsky and experimentally by Robert H. Goddard, who flew the first liquid-fueled rocket in 1926, and by Hermann Oberth and the German VfR society. Germany's V-2 (1942) was the first large liquid-propellant ballistic missile; postwar American and Soviet programs derived from it produced the engines of the space race, including the giant F-1 engines of Saturn V, still among the most powerful single-chamber engines ever flown.
