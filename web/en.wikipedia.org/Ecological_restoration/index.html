Ecological restoration is the process of assisting the recovery of an ecosystem that has been degraded, damaged, or destroyed. It is distinguished from other forms of land management by its explicit intent to return an ecosystem to a historic trajectory of development, re-establishing its structure, species composition, and ecological functions. The scientific study of restoration practice is known as restoration ecology.

Definition and principles

The Society for Ecological Restoration (SER) defines ecological restoration as the process of assisting the recovery of an ecosystem that has been degraded, damaged, or destroyed. Practitioners aim to restore native species assemblages, ecosystem processes such as nutrient cycling and hydrology, and the capacity of the system to persist and evolve without continued human intervention. Reference ecosystems — relatively intact sites of the same type — are often used to set targets and measure progress.

History

Early restoration work included the reforestation of degraded lands and prairie reconstruction experiments in the 1930s, notably at the University of Wisconsin Arboretum under Aldo Leopold. The field professionalised in the 1980s with the founding of the Society for Ecological Restoration in 1988 and grew rapidly as environmental legislation in many countries began to require mitigation for habitat damage.

Methods

Common techniques include removing invasive species, replanting native vegetation, reintroducing extirpated species, re-establishing natural disturbance regimes such as fire or flooding, remediating contaminated soils, and reconnecting fragmented habitats. Restoration may be active, involving direct intervention, or passive, relying on natural regeneration once stressors are removed.

Challenges

Restoration faces scientific and practical limits: altered soils and hydrology may prevent full recovery, climate change shifts suitable conditions away from historical baselines, and restored sites can take decades or centuries to approach reference conditions. Critics note that restoration is sometimes used to justify destruction elsewhere through offsetting schemes of doubtful equivalence.
