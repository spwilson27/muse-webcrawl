Hypercapnia

Hypercapnia (from the Greek hyper = "above" and kapnos = "smoke"), also known as hypercarbia and CO2 retention, is a condition of abnormally elevated carbon dioxide (CO2) levels in the blood. Carbon dioxide is a gaseous product of the body's metabolism and is normally expelled through the lungs. Hypercapnia causes a decrease in the pH of the blood (respiratory acidosis) because dissolved CO2 forms carbonic acid.

Normal arterial CO2 partial pressure (PaCO2) is roughly 35–45 mmHg; hypercapnia is generally defined as a PaCO2 above 45 mmHg.

Causes

Hypercapnia generally results from hypoventilation — breathing too slowly or shallowly relative to the body's CO2 production — as in severe COPD, obesity hypoventilation syndrome, neuromuscular weakness, chest wall deformity, or depressed respiratory drive from sedative drugs or brainstem injury. Rebreathing exhaled air, as in a faulty diving rebreather or an enclosed space, can also cause it.

Symptoms

Early signs include headache, confusion, drowsiness, flushed skin, sweating and rapid breathing. Rising CO2 levels cause cerebral vasodilation and increased intracranial pressure; severe hypercapnia can produce tremor (asterixis), depressed consciousness, seizures, coma and respiratory arrest ("CO2 narcosis").

Treatment

Treatment addresses the underlying cause and supports ventilation, up to non-invasive or invasive mechanical ventilation. Permissive hypercapnia is sometimes tolerated deliberately in protective ventilation strategies for acute respiratory distress syndrome.
