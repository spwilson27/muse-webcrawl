Enemy of the people

Enemy of the people is a phrase and political designation used to stigmatise opponents of a regime or ruling group as enemies of the nation or society as a whole. The term has a long history, from Roman law's hostis publicus ("public enemy") through the French Revolution to the Soviet Union, where it became a formal and deadly category of political repression.

Origins

The Roman concept of hostis publicus denoted a person declared an enemy of the state, who could be killed with impunity. During the French Revolution, the phrase ennemi du peuple was applied to opponents of the revolutionary government, notably by the Committee of Public Safety during the Reign of Terror.

Soviet usage

In the Soviet Union, vrag naroda ("enemy of the people") was applied from 1917 onward to alleged counter-revolutionaries, kulaks, former officials, Trotskyists and many others. Under Joseph Stalin the label was used extensively during the Great Purge (1936–1938); those so designated faced arrest, deportation to the Gulag, or execution, and their families often suffered persecution as "family members of enemies of the people". Article 58 of the RSFSR Penal Code codified offences associated with the label.

Other uses

Henrik Ibsen's 1882 play An Enemy of the People popularised the phrase in literature, telling of a doctor who is ostracised for reporting contaminated water in his town's baths. The phrase has since been used rhetorically in many countries, including by political leaders to attack the press and opposition movements.
