Celestial spheres

The celestial spheres, or celestial orbs, were the fundamental entities of the cosmological models developed by Plato, Eudoxus, Aristotle, Ptolemy, Copernicus, and others. In these celestial models, the apparent motions of the fixed stars and planets are accounted for by treating them as embedded in rotating spheres made of an aethereal, transparent fifth element (quintessence), like jewels set in orbs.

Since it was believed that the fixed stars did not change their positions relative to one another, it was argued that they must be on the surface of a single starry sphere.

History

Greek astronomy

Eudoxus of Cnidus constructed a model of 27 interlocking spheres to explain planetary motions, which Callippus expanded and Aristotle made physical in his On the Heavens and Metaphysics, arriving at a system of some 55 spheres. Aristotle held that the spheres were made of aether and were moved by unmoved movers, the outermost by the Prime Mover.

Medieval cosmology

In medieval thought, the spheres were often identified with the ranks of angels, and the outermost sphere, the primum mobile, set the heavens in motion. Dante's Paradiso maps the nine heavens onto the angelic orders.

Dissolution

Tycho Brahe's observations of the comet of 1577, which moved through the region of the spheres without resistance, undermined the doctrine of solid orbs; Kepler abandoned spheres for elliptical orbits, and by the seventeenth century the concept had largely disappeared from serious astronomy.
