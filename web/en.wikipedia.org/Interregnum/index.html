Interregnum

An interregnum (plural interregna or interregnums) is a period of discontinuity or gap in a government, organisation or social order. The term, from Latin inter- ("between") and regnum ("reign"), originally denoted the time between the death of one monarch and the accession of the next, but is now used for any interval in which normal authority is suspended or vacant.

In monarchies

In hereditary monarchies an interregnum is usually brief, since succession is immediate in law ("The king is dead, long live the king"). In elective monarchies, such as the Holy Roman Empire and the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, interregna could last months or years while electors chose a new ruler, and were governed by officials such as the Polish primate, who acted as interrex. The term is also used in the Catholic Church for the period between the death or resignation of a pope and the election of his successor (sede vacante).

Famous examples

The English Interregnum (1649–1660) followed the execution of Charles I: England was ruled first as the Commonwealth under Parliament and then as the Protectorate under Oliver Cromwell and his son Richard, until the Restoration of Charles II. Other notable interregna include the chaotic period in the Roman Empire during the third century, the Great Interregnum in the Holy Roman Empire (1250–1273), and Russia's Time of Troubles (1598–1613).

Broader usage

By extension, "interregnum" describes transitions in republics and organisations — for example, the interval between a president's election and inauguration, or any pause in leadership — and is used metaphorically in cultural and political commentary for periods when an old order has collapsed but a new one has not yet emerged.
