Ba'athism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ba'athism is an Arab nationalist ideology combining pan-Arab unity, freedom from foreign control, and Arab socialism. Founded in Syria in the 1940s by Michel Aflaq, Salah al-Din al-Bitar and Zaki al-Arsuzi, it was embodied in the Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party, whose slogan was "Unity, Freedom, Socialism." Ba'athist governments ruled Iraq (1968–2003) and Syria (1963–2024), though the two branches became bitter rivals after the party split in 1966.

Doctrine

The name Ba'ath means "renaissance" or "resurrection": Aflaq taught that the Arab nation, though divided into states by colonialism, was a single eternal nation whose rebirth required political unity, liberation from imperialism, and a socialist transformation that was national rather than Marxist in character. The party was officially secular and attracted support from religious minorities as well as Sunni Arabs in both Syria and Iraq.

In power

Ba'athists seized power in Iraq and Syria in 1963, and permanently in Iraq in 1968 under Ahmed Hassan al-Bakr and then Saddam Hussein. In Syria, Hafez al-Assad's 1970 "Corrective Movement" consolidated power, passing to his son Bashar al-Assad in 2000. Both regimes became highly authoritarian single-party states; the Syrian Ba'athist government fell in December 2024 when rebel forces took Damascus, ending over six decades of Ba'ath rule in Syria.

Legacy

Though Ba'athism promised unity, the Syrian and Iraqi branches denounced each other as traitors for decades, and the ideology is widely associated with the repressive rule of the regimes that claimed it, even as its pan-Arab and developmentalist aims influenced the wider region's politics.
