Beluga (sturgeon)

The beluga (Huso huso), also known as the beluga sturgeon or great sturgeon, is a species of anadromous fish in the sturgeon family (Acipenseridae) found primarily in the Caspian and Black Sea basins, and occasionally in the Adriatic Sea. It is one of the largest freshwater fish in the world and is famous as the source of beluga caviar, among the most expensive animal products in the world.

Description

The beluga is among the largest bony fishes: mature individuals commonly measure 1.8 to 3.5 metres and weigh several hundred kilograms, and historical specimens reportedly exceeded 7 metres and 1.5 tonnes, though such giants have not been recorded since the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Like other sturgeons it has a largely cartilaginous skeleton, a heterocercal tail, rows of bony scutes, and a protrusible, toothless mouth with barbels used to locate prey. Belugas are long-lived, potentially surviving more than 100 years, and females may not spawn until 15–25 years of age.

Biology

Belugas are anadromous, migrating from the sea up rivers such as the Volga, Danube, Ural, and Kura to spawn over gravel beds. Unlike most sturgeons, which feed mainly on invertebrates, adult belugas are active predators of other fish, and occasionally of waterbirds and seals. Females produce millions of eggs, and the roe harvested from them is the prized beluga caviar.

Conservation

Overfishing for caviar, poaching, and the damming of spawning rivers have devastated populations. The species is listed as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List and is protected under CITES Appendix II; the United States banned beluga caviar imports in 2005. Captive breeding and restocking programs operate in several countries, and aquaculture now supplies a growing share of legal caviar.
