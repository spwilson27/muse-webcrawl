Basic oxygen steelmaking (BOS), also known as the Linz–Donawitz process or basic oxygen process, is the dominant method of mass steel production in which carbon-rich molten pig iron from a blast furnace is converted into steel by blowing pure oxygen through it. The oxygen oxidizes the carbon and other impurities in the iron, and the exothermic reactions keep the metal molten without external fuel.

Process

Steel scrap is charged into a pear-shaped refractory-lined vessel (converter), followed by molten hot metal. A water-cooled lance is lowered and pure oxygen is blown onto the surface at supersonic speed for about 15–20 minutes. Lime and other fluxes are added; the lime forms a basic slag that absorbs impurities such as phosphorus and sulphur. Impurities oxidize in sequence — silicon and manganese first, then carbon, which burns off as carbon monoxide, producing the characteristic violent boil and flame.

When carbon reaches the target level the steel is "tapped" into a ladle, where ferro-alloys are added to achieve the desired grade. A complete cycle (tap-to-tap) takes roughly 40 minutes, and converters commonly hold 100–350 tonnes.

History

Earlier processes, such as the Bessemer converter, blew air, whose nitrogen made steel brittle. Cheap tonnage oxygen from industrial gas separation made pure-oxygen blowing practical. The process was developed in Austria by Robert Durrer and commercialized in 1952–1953 at steelworks in Linz and Donawitz — hence "LD." It rapidly displaced the open-hearth furnace.

Economics and emissions

BOS is integrated with blast furnace ironmaking, so its CO2 emissions are tied to coke-based iron production; it accounts for the majority of global steel output, with electric arc furnaces making up most of the remainder.
