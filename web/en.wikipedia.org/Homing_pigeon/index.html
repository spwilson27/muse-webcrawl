Homing pigeon

Homing pigeon
Species: Columba livia domestica
Derived from: Rock dove
Use: Message carrying, racing
Average speed: 40–60 km/h, up to ~1,800 m/min in races

The homing pigeon, also called the mail pigeon or messenger pigeon, is a variety of domestic pigeon (Columba livia domestica) selectively bred for its ability to find its way home over extremely long distances. Because of this skill, homing pigeons were historically used to carry messages — as "pigeon post" — and trained birds still compete today in pigeon racing. Flights of 1,000 km (620 miles) are routine for racing birds, and exceptional homers have returned over distances of more than 1,800 km.

History

Pigeons' homing ability was exploited in the ancient world: Egyptians and Persians used messenger pigeons by around 3,000 years ago, and Julius Caesar used them in his conquest of Gaul. In the medieval Islamic world, pigeon posts were highly organised, and the Mamluk sultan Baybars maintained a network across his empire. In 19th-century Europe, pigeon post carried financial news: the Rothschilds famously used pigeons, and Paul Reuters founded his news agency on a pigeon link between Aachen and Brussels in 1850. Paris was supplied by balloon and pigeon post during the siege of 1870–71, with microfilmed dispatches carried into the city.

Navigation

Homing is thought to rest on several senses. Pigeons possess magnetoreception — magnetite-containing structures in the beak and an inner-ear mechanism have been proposed — allowing them to read the Earth's magnetic field for a "map". They also navigate by the sun's position with an internal clock (a sun compass), by landmarks near home, by low-frequency infrasound, and by smell: experiments show that pigeons deprived of their sense of smell become disoriented. Young birds learn routes by following experienced birds, and flocks can navigate more accurately than singletons.

Pigeon post and wartime service

In the First World War both sides used hundreds of thousands of pigeons; mobile lofts followed the armies, and individual birds became heroes — the American pigeon Cher Ami delivered a message that saved the "Lost Battalion" in 1918 despite grave wounds, and the British bird Mary survived multiple injuries. In the Second World War Britain's National Pigeon Service supplied some 200,000 birds to the armed forces and resistance networks; 32 pigeons received the Dickin Medal for gallantry, including G.I. Joe and Paddy. Pigeons remained valuable because they could not be jammed and emitted no radio signals. Armies maintained pigeon services into the late 20th century; Switzerland disbanded its service in 1994 and India retained police pigeon posts into the 2000s.

Pigeon racing

Pigeon racing — releasing trained birds to race home to their lofts, with times and distances used to compute velocity — became a mass working-class sport in Belgium in the 19th century and spread to Britain, the Netherlands and worldwide. Modern races range from sprints of 100 km to marathons over 1,000 km, and elite racing pigeons command extraordinary prices: the Belgian bird New Kim was sold at auction in 2020 for about €1.6 million, a world record. The sport has faced criticism over losses of birds and welfare in long races.
