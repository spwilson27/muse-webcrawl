Indigenous peoples of the Northeastern Woodlands

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Indigenous peoples of the Northeastern Woodlands are the Indigenous peoples of the forested northeastern region of North America, roughly extending from the Atlantic coast west to the Great Lakes and the Mississippi River, and from the St. Lawrence River south to the Carolinas. Cultural anthropologists generally include the region in the Eastern Woodlands culture area, distinguishing its northern and coastal peoples from those of the Southeastern Woodlands.

Peoples

Major peoples of the region include the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy: Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, and later Tuscarora), the Anishinaabe (Ojibwe, Odawa, Potawatomi), the Lenape (Delaware), the Abenaki and other Wabanaki peoples, the Shawnee, the Miami, the Huron-Wendat, the Narragansett, the Pequot, the Mohegan, and the Powhatan of the Chesapeake. Most spoke languages of the Algonquian family; the Haudenosaunee and Wendat spoke Iroquoian languages.

Culture

Northeastern peoples combined horticulture — centred on the "Three Sisters" of maize, beans, and squash — with hunting, fishing, and gathering. Birch bark was a key material for canoes, containers, and wigwam coverings in the north, while peoples further south built bark-covered longhouses, most famously the Iroquois longhouse, housing for multiple related families under matrilineal clan organisation. Wampum — beads made from quahog and whelk shells — served ceremonial, diplomatic, and record-keeping functions.

Political organisation ranged from villages and chiefdoms to confederacies. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy, founded under the Great Law of Peace attributed to the Peacemaker and Hiawatha, maintained a council-based league whose structure is sometimes cited as an influence on later American federal thought.

European contact

From the 16th century, trade in beaver pelts drew Indigenous nations into European rivalry networks, and epidemic diseases introduced from Europe caused catastrophic population losses. The Beaver Wars, King Philip's War (1675–76), the French and Indian War, and the American Revolutionary War reshaped the region. During the 19th century many nations were displaced westward under US removal policies, including the Indian Removal Act of 1830 and the Trail of Tears era, while in Canada treaties and the reserve system confined communities; many nations nonetheless persist today with recognised governments in both countries.
