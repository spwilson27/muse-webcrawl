Stride (music)

Stride jazz piano, often simply called stride, is a jazz piano style that was developed in the large cities of the East Coast of the United States, particularly New York City, during the 1920s and 1930s. The left hand characteristically plays a four-beat pulse with a single bass note, octave, seventh or tenth on the first and third beats, and a chord on the second and fourth beats, "striding" across the keyboard over wide intervals. Occasionally this pattern is reversed by playing the chord on the first beat and bass notes afterward.

Stride grew out of ragtime, adding improvisation, blues elements, and greater rhythmic drive, and was the most advanced piano style of its day, demanding large hands and virtuoso technique. It flourished in Harlem, where pianists competed in "cutting contests" at rent parties.

Notable pianists

James P. Johnson, known as the "Father of Stride", composed "The Charleston" and "Carolina Shout". Willie "the Lion" Smith, Thomas "Fats" Waller, and Luckey Roberts were other masters of the Harlem school; Eubie Blake bridged ragtime and stride. Art Tatum, though usually classed with later styles, built on stride technique. The style influenced later pianists, and younger players such as Dick Hyman and Ralph Sutton kept it alive; stride elements also underlie much of swing and bebop piano.

Characteristics

Beyond the striding left hand, the style features a lively right hand playing melodies, riffs, and fills, frequent tenths in the bass, and occasional use of backbeats and breaks. Stride compositions often serve as set pieces demonstrating a pianist's virtuosity.
