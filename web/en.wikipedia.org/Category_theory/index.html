Category theory

Introduced bySamuel Eilenberg, Saunders Mac Lane (1945)
Key conceptsObjects, morphisms, functors, natural transformations
ApplicationsAlgebra, topology, logic, computer science

Category theory is a general theory of mathematical structures and their relations. It was introduced by Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane in 1945 in connection with algebraic topology. Category theory is used in almost all areas of mathematics, and in theoretical computer science and mathematical physics.

A category consists of a collection of objects together with, for each pair of objects, a collection of morphisms (or arrows) between them, equipped with a composition operation that is associative and has identity morphisms. Unlike set theory, category theory characterises structures by how they relate to one another rather than by their internal composition.

Functors and natural transformations

A functor is a mapping between categories that preserves objects, morphisms, composition, and identities. A natural transformation is a mapping between functors. Mac Lane remarked that categories were introduced in order to define functors, and functors in order to define natural transformations. These notions allow results proved in one area of mathematics to be transferred to another.

Universal constructions

Many familiar constructions — products, coproducts, limits, colimits, exponentials, duals — can be characterised uniformly by universal properties expressed with commutative diagrams. The Yoneda lemma, one of the foundational results of the subject, shows that an object is determined up to isomorphism by the network of its relationships to all other objects.

History

The subject grew out of algebraic topology in the 1940s, was reshaped by Alexander Grothendieck's work in algebraic geometry, and by William Lawvere's categorical approaches to logic and set theory. It now also underlies functional programming languages such as Haskell.
