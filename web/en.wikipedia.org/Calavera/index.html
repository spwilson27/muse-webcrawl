Calavera

A calavera (Spanish for "skull") is a representation of a human skull associated with Mexican culture, most prominently with the Day of the Dead (Día de los Muertos) festivities on 1–2 November. The term is most often applied to edible or decorative skulls made of sugar or clay, but it also refers to satirical verses and to the iconic skeletal figures popularised by printmaker José Guadalupe Posada.

Sugar skulls

Calaveras de azúcar (sugar skulls) are made by pressing a mixture of granulated sugar, meringue powder and water into skull-shaped moulds. Once dried, they are decorated with brightly coloured royal icing, foil and sequins, and often inscribed with the name of a deceased loved one — or, jokingly, of a living person. They are placed on ofrendas (home altars) and on graves during Day of the Dead celebrations, and are eaten as treats. Clay and chocolate versions are also common.

La Calavera Catrina

The most famous calavera is La Calavera Catrina, a zinc etching created around 1910–1913 by Mexican illustrator José Guadalupe Posada. Originally published with a satirical verse mocking the Mexican elite's imitation of European fashions, the elegantly dressed female skeleton became an enduring symbol of Day of the Dead and of Mexican folk art. Diego Rivera later gave the figure her full body and the name "La Catrina" in his mural work.

Calaveras literarias

A related tradition is the calavera literaria: short, humorous, satirical poems written for Day of the Dead that imagine living people — often politicians and celebrities — as dead, recounting their fictional encounters with Death. They are widely published in Mexican newspapers during the season.
