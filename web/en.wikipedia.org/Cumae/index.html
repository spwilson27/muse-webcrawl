Cumae

Cumae (Ancient Greek: Κύμη, Kyme; Italian: Cuma) was the first ancient Greek colony on the mainland of Italy, founded by settlers from Chalcis and Cumae in Euboea in the 8th century BC. It is located near the modern village of Cuma in the comune of Bacoli, in Campania, west of Naples. Cumae became a rich and powerful city and was a crucial conduit through which Greek culture — including the alphabet, adapted by the Etruscans and then the Romans — spread to the Italian mainland.

History

According to tradition, the colony was founded around 750–740 BC. At its height, Cumae controlled the finest stretch of Campanian coastline. In 524 BC and again in 474 BC its forces, the latter under Hieron I of Syracuse, defeated the Etruscans, most famously at the naval Battle of Cumae (474 BC), which curbed Etruscan expansion in southern Italy. The city was conquered by the Samnites around 421 BC and became a Roman civitas sine suffragio (city without the vote) in 338 BC. Cumae remained a prosperous Roman town and a stronghold during the Gothic Wars, falling to the Lombards in the sixth century AD before being abandoned.

The Cumaean Sibyl

Cumae is famed as the seat of the Cumaean Sibyl, the prophetess whose cave — a trapezoidal gallery cut into the rock of the acropolis — can still be visited. In Book 6 of Virgil's Aeneid, the Sibyl guides Aeneas through the underworld. The Sibyl was also said to have offered the Sibylline Books, collections of oracular prophecies, to Tarquin, the last king of Rome; these were consulted by the Roman state in times of crisis.

Archaeology

Excavations have revealed the acropolis with temples of Apollo and Jupiter, the Sibyl's cave (Antro della Sibilla), the Roman forum, baths, and extensive necropolises. The site is part of the Phlegraean Fields archaeological park.
