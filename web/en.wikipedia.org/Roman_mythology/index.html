Roman mythology is the body of myths of ancient Rome as represented in the literature and visual arts of the Romans. It draws heavily on Greek mythology, which the Romans adapted through their contact with Greek colonies in Italy and, later, the Hellenistic world, yet it also preserves distinctively Italic and Etruscan elements and a strong concern with Rome's foundation, history, and civic virtues.

Character and sources

Unlike the Greeks, the early Romans had few narrative myths of their own gods; their deities were initially functional powers (numina) associated with agriculture, the household, and the state. Narrative mythology arrived largely through Greek influence and was reshaped by Roman poets. The most important literary sources are Virgil's epic Aeneid, which linked Rome's origins to the Trojan hero Aeneas; Ovid's Metamorphoses and Fasti; and Livy's history, which preserves the legendary early kings and heroes of Rome.

Major gods

The Romans identified their gods with Greek counterparts: Jupiter with Zeus, Juno with Hera, Neptune with Poseidon, Mars with Ares (though Mars, as father of Romulus and god of soldiers and farmers, was far more central at Rome), Venus with Aphrodite, Diana with Artemis, Minerva with Athena, Apollo (retaining his Greek name), Vulcan with Hephaestus, Mercury with Hermes, Ceres with Demeter, and Bacchus with Dionysus. The Capitoline Triad of Jupiter, Juno, and Minerva stood at the head of the state cult.

Foundation myths

Rome's foundation legends tell of Aeneas fleeing Troy and settling in Latium, and of his descendant Romulus, suckled with his twin Remus by a she-wolf, who founded the city in 753 BC after killing his brother. Other legends — the rape of the Sabine women, the Horatii and Curiatii, Cincinnatus returning to his plough — served as moral exempla of Roman virtues such as pietas, discipline, and self-sacrifice.

Worship and practice

Roman religion was orthopraxic, emphasizing correct ritual performance — sacrifices, vows, auspices, and festivals — rather than doctrine. Household cults of the Lares and Penates, ancestor veneration, and the state priestly colleges (pontifices, augurs, flamens) structured religious life. From Augustus onward, the imperial cult added worship of the deified emperor. In late antiquity these traditions gave way to Christianity, though Roman myth survived as a dominant theme of Western literature and art.
