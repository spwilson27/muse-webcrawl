Ice storm

An ice storm, also known in some regions as a glaze storm or silver thaw, is a type of winter storm characterised by freezing rain. It is defined by the United States National Weather Service as a storm that results in an accumulation of at least 0.25 inches (6.4 mm) of ice on exposed surfaces; such accumulations are hazardous because the weight of the ice brings down trees, branches and power lines.

Formation

Ice storms form when a layer of warm air is trapped between two layers of cold air. Precipitation begins as snow aloft, melts completely in the warm layer, and then falls through a shallow layer of sub-freezing air near the surface. The droplets become supercooled and freeze on contact with the ground, vegetation, roads and structures, forming a coating of clear glaze ice.

Effects

Heavy ice accretion can add hundreds of kilograms to a tree or span of power line, toppling utility poles and causing power outages lasting days or weeks. Roads become extremely slippery, and thick ice can damage roofs and crops. Notable events include the North American ice storm of 1998, which devastated parts of Ontario, Quebec, New Brunswick and the northeastern United States, leaving millions without power, and the 2009 ice storm across the central United States.
