The 1972 United States presidential election was the 47th quadrennial presidential election, held on Tuesday, 7 November 1972. Incumbent Republican President Richard Nixon defeated Democratic Senator George McGovern of South Dakota in one of the largest landslides in American electoral history, winning 49 states and more than 60 percent of the popular vote.

Background

Nixon ran on his administration's record of winding down American involvement in the Vietnam War, opening relations with China, and pursuing détente with the Soviet Union. McGovern, who had chaired the commission that reformed the Democratic nomination process, won the Democratic nomination on an anti-war platform, defeating among others Hubert Humphrey and Edmund Muskie. Alabama Governor George Wallace's campaign ended after he was shot and paralysed in an assassination attempt in May 1972.

The Eagleton affair

McGovern's campaign was badly damaged by his choice of running mate. Shortly after the Democratic convention, it emerged that Senator Thomas Eagleton of Missouri had previously been hospitalised for depression and treated with electroconvulsive therapy, a fact not disclosed to McGovern before his selection. After initially declaring himself "1,000 percent" behind Eagleton, McGovern replaced him on the ticket with Sargent Shriver. The episode reinforced perceptions of disorganisation in the McGovern campaign.

Results

Nixon won every state except Massachusetts, along with the District of Columbia going to McGovern. A single faithless elector in Virginia voted for the Libertarian candidate John Hospers, making his running mate Tonie Nathan the first woman to receive an electoral vote in a United States presidential election. The 1972 result remains one of only two elections (with 1984) in which a candidate carried 49 states.

Watergate

The election was overshadowed in retrospect by the Watergate scandal. In June 1972, burglars connected to Nixon's re-election committee were arrested breaking into the Democratic National Committee headquarters at the Watergate complex in Washington, D.C. The cover-up that followed ultimately forced Nixon's resignation in August 1974 — less than two years after his landslide re-election.
