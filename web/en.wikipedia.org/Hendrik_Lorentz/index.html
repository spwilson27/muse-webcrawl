Hendrik Antoon Lorentz (18 July 1853 – 4 February 1928) was a Dutch physicist who shared the 1902 Nobel Prize in Physics with Pieter Zeeman for the discovery and theoretical explanation of the Zeeman effect. He developed the mathematical framework of the Lorentz transformations, which form the basis of Albert Einstein's special theory of relativity.

Early life and education

Lorentz was born in Arnhem, Gelderland, the son of Gerrit Frederik Lorentz, a nursery owner. He studied at the University of Leiden, obtaining his bachelor's degree in mathematics and physics in 1873 and his doctorate in 1875 with a thesis on the theory of reflection and refraction of light, in which he refined James Clerk Maxwell's electromagnetic theory.

Career

In 1878, aged only 24, Lorentz was appointed to the newly established chair in theoretical physics at the University of Leiden, a position he held for most of his career. He remained professor extraordinarius at Leiden even after becoming curator of the physics laboratory at Teylers Museum in Haarlem in 1912.

Scientific work

Lorentz's electron theory treated matter as composed of electrically charged particles whose interactions with the electromagnetic field explained a wide range of optical and electrical phenomena. In 1895 he introduced the Lorentz force law describing the force on a charged particle in electric and magnetic fields. Between 1892 and 1904 he developed the Lorentz transformations relating space and time coordinates between observers in relative motion through the luminiferous ether; Henri Poincaré named them after him. These transformations, together with the Lorentz contraction hypothesis (also proposed by George FitzGerald), accounted for the null result of the Michelson–Morley experiment.

Later life and legacy

Lorentz chaired the first Solvay Conference in 1911 and was a central figure in the international physics community. After his death in 1928, his funeral in Haarlem was attended by leading scientists including Ernest Rutherford and Marie Curie. The Lorentz Institute at Leiden and the Lorentz Medal are named in his honour.
