Stanley Milgram

Stanley Milgram (1933–1984).

Stanley Milgram (August 15, 1933 – December 20, 1984) was an American social psychologist at Yale University and the City University of New York. He is best known for his obedience experiments, which showed that ordinary people are surprisingly willing to follow orders from an authority figure to inflict what they believed were painful, even dangerous, electric shocks on another person.

The obedience experiments

Beginning in 1961, shortly after the trial of Adolf Eichmann, Milgram recruited volunteers for a supposed study of learning. Participants assigned the role of "teacher" were instructed to administer escalating shocks to a "learner" (an actor) for wrong answers. Despite the learner's protests, 65% of participants in the baseline condition continued to the maximum 450 volts when prompted by the experimenter. The results, published in 1963 and in Milgram's 1974 book Obedience to Authority, provoked intense debate about ethics in psychological research.

Other work

Milgram also originated the small-world experiment, which suggested that people are connected by short chains of acquaintances—the basis of the "six degrees of separation" idea—and studied the lost-letter technique and urban overload. His work is frequently compared with the Stanford prison experiment of Philip Zimbardo, his former schoolmate.

Legacy

Milgram's studies transformed social psychology's understanding of authority and led to stricter ethical standards, including informed consent and debriefing requirements, in human-subjects research.
