Southern dynasties

The Southern dynasties (南朝) were a series of four Chinese dynasties that ruled southern China during the era of the Northern and Southern dynasties (420–589). They were the Liu Song (420–479), the Southern Qi (479–502), the Liang (502–557), and the Chen (557–589). Their capital was Jiankang, near modern Nanjing.

Background

The Southern dynasties succeeded the Eastern Jin dynasty, whose court had fled south after the loss of the north to non-Han peoples in the early fourth century. Large numbers of northern aristocrats and commoners migrated across the Yangtze River, bringing northern Chinese culture to the south and accelerating the development of the region around the lower Yangtze.

Society and culture

The southern courts were dominated by great aristocratic clans, many of émigré origin, alongside older southern families. The period saw the flourishing of Buddhism, particularly under Emperor Wu of Liang, a devout patron who built temples and sponsored translations of sutras. Literature, calligraphy and landscape poetry flourished; the poet Xie Lingyun is often credited as a founder of Chinese landscape poetry. The official histories of the period include the Book of Song, Book of Southern Qi, Book of Liang and Book of Chen, three of which were compiled by the historian Yao Silian in the early Tang dynasty.

Decline and reunification

The Liang dynasty was devastated by the rebellion of the general Hou Jing (548–552), which destroyed Jiankang and weakened the south. The shorter-lived Chen dynasty that followed controlled a reduced territory. In 589 the northern Sui dynasty conquered Chen, reunifying China after nearly three centuries of division.
