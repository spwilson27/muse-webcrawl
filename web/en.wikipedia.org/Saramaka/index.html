Saramaka

The Saramaka are one of the six Maroon peoples of Suriname and French Guiana. Descended from Africans who escaped enslavement on Dutch plantations in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, they established autonomous communities in the rainforest interior along the upper Suriname River. In 1762 the Saramaka concluded a peace treaty with the Dutch colonial government, one of the earliest such treaties with a Maroon society in the Americas, recognising their freedom and territorial rights.

Society and culture

Saramaka society is matrilineal: descent, inheritance of ritual offices, and rights to land are traced through the maternal line, organised into clans (lo) and lineages (bee). Villages are led by headmen (kapiten) under a paramount chief (gaama). Their language, Saramaccan, is an English-based creole with strong Portuguese and African influences, regarded by linguists as one of the most African of the Atlantic creoles. Saramaka art, especially elaborately carved and painted woodwork and colourful textile arts, is internationally renowned.

Modern history

In the 1960s, construction of the Afobaka Dam and the Brokopondo Reservoir flooded roughly half of Saramaka territory, displacing thousands. In 2007, the Inter-American Court of Human Rights ruled in Saramaka People v. Suriname that the state had violated the people's rights to property and judicial protection by granting logging and mining concessions on their territory without consent — a landmark judgment for Indigenous and tribal peoples' land rights worldwide. Saramaka today live in dozens of villages in Suriname, with additional communities in French Guiana, many formed after Suriname's Interior War (1986–1992), and in the Netherlands.
