Noh

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Noh (Japanese: 能, "skill" or "talent") is a major form of classical Japanese musical drama that has been performed since the 14th century. Developed by Kannami and perfected by his son Zeami Motokiyo under the patronage of the shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu, Noh combines dance, chant, music, stylized gesture and elaborate masks and costumes in slow, austere performances on a bare roofed stage with a painted pine backdrop. It is recognized by UNESCO as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Structure and roles

A Noh play typically centres on the shite, the masked main actor, who often appears first in disguise and returns in a second act in true form — frequently the ghost of a famous warrior, woman or deity. The waki, an unmasked supporting actor (often a travelling priest), questions the shite and frames the story. Comic kyōgen actors appear in interludes between acts and in separate farces performed alongside Noh. Music is provided by the hayashi ensemble of flute and three drums, and a chorus (jiutai) seated at the side of the stage narrates and voices the shite's thoughts.

Repertoire and aesthetics

The repertoire of about 250 plays in the current canon is classified into five categories: god, warrior, woman (wig), miscellaneous or madness, and demon plays; a traditional full-day programme presented one of each. Zeami's treatises articulated the aesthetic principles of monomane (imitation) and yūgen, a profound and subtle grace, and the structural rhythm of jo-ha-kyū (beginning, development, rapid finale). Actors train within hereditary schools, and masks — carved for types such as the young woman, old man, deity and demon — are treasured objects.
