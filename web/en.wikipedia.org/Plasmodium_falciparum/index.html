Plasmodium falciparum

Plasmodium falciparum is a unicellular protozoan parasite and the deadliest of the Plasmodium species that cause malaria in humans. It is transmitted by the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes and is responsible for the great majority of malaria deaths, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa.

Life cycle

Sporozoites injected by a mosquito first infect liver cells, then emerge as merozoites that invade red blood cells, multiplying in roughly 48-hour cycles that produce the characteristic fevers. Some parasites develop into gametocytes, which continue the cycle when taken up by another mosquito. Infected red cells adhere to blood vessel walls — sequestration — which can cause cerebral malaria and organ failure.

Treatment and control

Artemisinin-based combination therapies are the standard treatment; resistance has emerged in parts of Southeast Asia and Africa. Control relies on insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor spraying, chemoprevention and the RTS,S and R21 malaria vaccines.
