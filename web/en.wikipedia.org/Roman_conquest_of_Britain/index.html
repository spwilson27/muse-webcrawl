The Roman conquest of Britain was the process by which the Roman Empire incorporated the island of Great Britain into the province of Britannia, beginning with the Claudian invasion of AD 43 and continuing for roughly four decades. Earlier, Julius Caesar had raided Britain in 55 and 54 BC, but no permanent occupation followed until the emperor Claudius sought military glory nearly a century later.

The Claudian invasion

In AD 43 four legions — about 40,000 men in total, the fighting core of the Roman legion system — crossed under Aulus Plautius, defeated the Catuvellauni kings Togodumnus and Caratacus at the Medway and the Thames, and received the submission of tribes at Camulodunum (Colchester), where Claudius himself arrived with elephants to accept it. Roman forces, including the future emperor Vespasian with Legio II Augusta, overran the south and southwest, taking hillforts such as Maiden Castle.

Revolt and consolidation

Expansion into Wales and the north met fierce resistance. In AD 60–61 the Iceni queen Boudica revolted after Roman abuses, burning Colchester, London and St Albans and slaughtering their inhabitants before Suetonius Paulinus crushed her army in the Midlands. Under a series of able governors the frontier advanced; Gnaeus Julius Agricola campaigned deep into Scotland and defeated the Caledonians at Mons Graupius (AD 83 or 84), while a Roman fleet circumnavigated the island. Rome nevertheless never subdued the far north: Hadrian's Wall (from AD 122) and briefly the Antonine Wall marked the effective limits.

Consequences

Roman Britain lasted about 370 years, bringing towns, roads, villas, written Latin — ancestor, through later contact, of vocabulary in the Romance languages family tree recorded across Europe — coinage, and eventually Christianity, before Roman administration collapsed around AD 410.
