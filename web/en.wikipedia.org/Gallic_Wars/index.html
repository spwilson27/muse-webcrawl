Gallic Wars

The Gallic Wars were a series of military campaigns waged by the Roman proconsul Julius Caesar against the peoples of Gaul between 58 and 50 BC. They culminated in the decisive Roman victory at the Battle of Alesia in 52 BC, after which the Roman Republic annexed Gaul — roughly modern France, Belgium, and parts of neighboring countries — transforming the political map of Western Europe and greatly advancing Caesar's wealth and power.

Course of the wars

Caesar intervened first against the migrating Helvetii (58 BC) and the Germanic king Ariovistus, then subdued the Belgae in the north (57 BC) and campaigned in Armorica and Aquitania. In 55 and 54 BC he launched expeditions across the Rhine and to Britain. A general Gallic revolt in 52 BC, led by Vercingetorix of the Arverni, nearly undid the conquest; Caesar trapped Vercingetorix at Alesia, defeating both the besieged army and a huge relief force. Mopping-up operations continued through 51–50 BC.

Sources and casualties

The principal source is Caesar's own Commentarii de Bello Gallico, a masterpiece of Latin prose that is also a work of political self-promotion. Plutarch reported figures of a million Gauls killed and another million enslaved over the wars — numbers historians treat with caution but which indicate a demographic catastrophe for Gaul.

Consequences

Gaul became a group of Roman provinces, was gradually Romanized, and remained part of the empire for five centuries. The conquest's riches and veteran legions enabled Caesar to cross the Rubicon in 49 BC and win the ensuing civil war.
