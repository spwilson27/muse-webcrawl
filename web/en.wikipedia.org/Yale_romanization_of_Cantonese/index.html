Yale romanization of Cantonese

The Yale romanization of Cantonese is a system for transcribing the Cantonese language into the Latin alphabet, developed in 1943 by Yale University scholars Parker Po-fei Huang and Geraldine P. Kok for teaching Cantonese to American soldiers during the Second World War. It was refined in Huang and Kok's textbooks and became the dominant romanization in North American and much English-language teaching of Cantonese.

Design

The system was designed to approximate Cantonese sounds using ordinary English spelling conventions, minimizing diacritics. It writes tones with diacritics over vowels (or an optional h marking the low-register tones in some versions). Unlike the later Jyutping system, Yale uses letters such as j, ch, and y in ways familiar to English readers (e.g., Jyutping zung1 = Yale jūng, "middle").

Initials and finals

Yale distinguishes the initials b, p, m, f, d, t, n, l, g, k, ng, h, gw, kw, j, ch, s, y, and w. It preserves the distinction between the vowels written a and aa and represents the rounded front vowels with yu and eu spellings (e.g., jyū for 朱, "pig/red").

Usage

Yale long dominated Cantonese textbooks in the United States and is used in widely used dictionaries such as those based on the work of Sidney Lau (whose own system is similar). In Hong Kong, the Linguistic Society of Hong Kong's Jyutping (1993) has largely superseded Yale in academic and digital contexts, but Yale remains common in Western classrooms.
