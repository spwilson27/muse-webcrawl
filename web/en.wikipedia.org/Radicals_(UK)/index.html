Radicals (UK)

19th-century reform movement
Active periodLate 18th – late 19th century
Key figuresJohn Wilkes, Thomas Paine (influence), William Cobbett, John Bright, Joseph Hume
GoalsParliamentary reform, universal male suffrage, free trade, civil liberties

The Radicals were a loose parliamentary and extra-parliamentary political movement in early- to mid-19th-century Great Britain, and an informal label for reformers who demanded sweeping democratic and social change. The term had earlier been applied to late-18th-century advocates of reform inspired by the American and French Revolutions.

Origins

Radicalism drew on figures such as John Wilkes, Major John Cartwright and later Thomas Paine, whose Rights of Man (1791) was widely read. After the Napoleonic Wars, mass radical meetings and publications such as William Cobbett's Political Register pressed for annual parliaments and manhood suffrage; the movement met repression at events such as the Peterloo Massacre of 1819.

In Parliament

Following the Reform Act 1832, Radicals such as Joseph Hume and John Arthur Roebuck formed an identifiable bloc in the House of Commons, campaigning on the "six points" later embodied in Chartism, for free trade alongside Richard Cobden and John Bright, and against the Corn Laws through the Anti-Corn Law League. By the later 19th century most Radicals were absorbed into the Liberal Party, where the radical wing influenced leaders such as William Ewart Gladstone and Joseph Chamberlain.

Legacy

Radical demands — secret ballot, abolition of property qualifications for MPs, equal electoral districts — were progressively enacted over the following century.
