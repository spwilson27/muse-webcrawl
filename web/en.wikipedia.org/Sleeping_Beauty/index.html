Sleeping Beauty (French: La Belle au bois dormant) is a classic fairy tale about a princess cursed to sleep for a hundred years, to be awakened by a prince. It is classified as Aarne–Thompson type 410 and has been retold in literature, ballet, opera and film.

The tale

In Charles Perrault's version, a princess is cursed at her christening by an offended fairy: she will prick her finger on a spindle and die. A good fairy softens the curse to a sleep of one hundred years. Despite the king's ban on spindles, the princess finds one at sixteen, falls asleep, and the whole castle sleeps with her, surrounded by a growing forest of thorns. A century later a prince makes his way through, and the princess awakens. Perrault added a second half in which the prince's ogress mother attempts to eat the couple's children.

Sources and variants

The tale has roots in the medieval romance Perceforest and in Giambattista Basile's "Sun, Moon, and Talia" (1634), a darker story. The Brothers Grimm published a gentler version, "Little Briar Rose" (Dornröschen), ending with the awakening kiss.

Adaptations

Tchaikovsky's ballet The Sleeping Beauty (1890), choreographed by Marius Petipa, is a cornerstone of classical ballet. Disney's animated film Sleeping Beauty (1959) fixed the princess Aurora and the villain Maleficent in popular culture; its imagery — including the castle that became Disneyland's icon — drew on Tchaikovsky's music. Later retellings, including the film Maleficent (2014), have reimagined the story from the fairy's perspective.
