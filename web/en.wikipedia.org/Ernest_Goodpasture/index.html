Ernest William Goodpasture (17 October 1886 – 20 September 1960) was an American pathologist and physician at Vanderbilt University whose work transformed the study and production of vaccines. He is best remembered for developing a practical method of cultivating viruses in fertilized chicken eggs, and for his 1919 description of the disease now known as Goodpasture syndrome.

Career

Goodpasture received his medical degree from Johns Hopkins University in 1912 and, after service in the First World War and posts at Harvard and the University of Pennsylvania, became professor of pathology at Vanderbilt in 1924, a chair he held for three decades. He served as dean of the Vanderbilt School of Medicine and later as scientific director of the Armed Forces Institute of Pathology.

Chick embryo technique

In 1931, working with Alice Miles Woodruff, Goodpasture showed that fowlpox virus could be grown on the chorioallantoic membrane of a developing chick embryo. The technique, extended with his "Goodpasture's method" of sterile egg inoculation, made it possible for the first time to grow many viruses in large quantities cheaply and in relative purity. It underpinned the mass production of vaccines against influenza, yellow fever, typhus, and smallpox, and remains a foundation of vaccine manufacture: much of the world's influenza vaccine is still produced in eggs.

Goodpasture syndrome

In 1919, during the influenza pandemic, Goodpasture published an autopsy report of a young man who died with haemoptysis and kidney failure. The combination of pulmonary haemorrhage and glomerulonephritis was later recognized as an autoimmune disease in which antibodies attack the basement membranes of the lungs and kidneys — anti-glomerular basement membrane disease, known eponymously as Goodpasture syndrome.
