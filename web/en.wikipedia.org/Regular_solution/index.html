Regular solution

In chemistry, a regular solution is a solution whose entropy of mixing equals that of an ideal solution — that is, the components mix randomly — but whose enthalpy of mixing is nonzero. The concept was introduced by Joel H. Hildebrand in the 1920s and underlies the Hildebrand solubility parameter used to predict miscibility of liquids and polymers.

Model

For a binary mixture with mole fractions x1 and x2, the excess Gibbs energy is written GE = Ω x1 x2, where Ω is an interaction parameter related to the difference between like and unlike molecular pair energies. If Ω is positive and large enough, the model predicts partial miscibility and a miscibility gap with an upper critical solution temperature; if Ω is negative the components attract one another more strongly than themselves.

The activity coefficients follow as RT ln γ1 = Ω x22, so deviations from Raoult's law are symmetric in composition. Although simplified — it ignores differences in molecular size and specific interactions — the regular solution model remains a useful first approximation in solution thermodynamics and materials science, for example in CALPHAD modelling of alloys.
