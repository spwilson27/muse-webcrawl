Fiction
DefinitionInvented narrative content, not presented as fact
FormsNovels, short stories, drama, film, poetry
Contrasts withNon-fiction
Major genresLiterary fiction, mystery, science fiction, fantasy, romance, horror

Fiction is any creative work — chiefly narrative — that presents invented characters, events or worlds rather than statements of fact. Although fiction is typically understood not to be true, it frequently draws on real people, places and history, and readers standardly treat fictional statements as true within the frame of the work. The term derives from the Latin fictio, "the act of making or fashioning." Fiction contrasts with non-fiction, which presents its content as factual, though hybrid forms such as historical fiction, docudrama and creative nonfiction blur the boundary.

Forms and formats

Written fiction is classified by length: the short story (under about 7,500 words), the novelette and novella, and the novel, generally over 40,000 words. Fiction also encompasses drama and screenplays, narrative poetry, comics and graphic novels, film, television, radio drama, video games and interactive fiction. Prose fiction in the form of the novel rose to cultural dominance in the 18th and 19th centuries with authors such as Daniel Defoe, Samuel Richardson, Jane Austen and Charles Dickens.

Genres

Fiction is commonly divided into literary fiction, emphasising style, character and theme, and genre fiction, organised by subject and convention: mystery and crime, science fiction, fantasy, romance, horror, the Western, thrillers and historical fiction, among others. Genre categories guide publishing, marketing and readership, though many works cross or subvert them.

Elements

Analyses of fiction commonly identify elements including plot, character, setting, theme, point of view, style and tone, and literary devices such as metaphor, irony, foreshadowing and symbolism. Narratology studies how such elements combine to produce narrative effects, and theories of make-believe (notably Kendall Walton's) examine how audiences engage imaginatively with fictional worlds.

History

Storytelling with invented narratives is ancient: epic poems such as the Epic of Gilgamesh and Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, ancient romances, medieval tales such as The Tale of Genji (often called the world's first novel) and Don Quixote are landmarks. Printing, rising literacy and the circulating library made fiction a mass medium, and digitisation has added fan fiction, web serials and interactive formats to its continuing evolution.
