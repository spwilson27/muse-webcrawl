Dielectric heating

Dielectric heating
Also calledElectronic heating; radio-frequency (RF) heating; microwave heating
FrequenciesRF: c. 13.56, 27.12, 40.68 MHz; Microwave: c. 915 MHz, 2.45 GHz
MechanismsDipolar rotation; ionic conduction
Best-known applicationMicrowave oven

Dielectric heating, also known as electronic heating, radio-frequency heating, and high-frequency heating, is the process in which a radio-frequency (RF) alternating electric field, or radio wave / microwave electromagnetic radiation, heats a dielectric material. The material is placed in a rapidly oscillating field; polar molecules continually attempt to align themselves with the field, and the resulting molecular rotation and collisions dissipate the field energy as heat throughout the volume of the material — not merely at its surface.

Mechanism

Two mechanisms dominate. In dipolar polarisation, molecules possessing a permanent dipole moment — water is the classic example — rotate back and forth as the field alternates, and intermolecular friction converts the motion into internal energy. In ionic conduction, dissolved ions oscillate under the field and collide with neighbouring species, again producing heat. The power dissipated per unit volume increases with frequency and with the material's loss factor (ε″), a measure of how effectively it converts field energy into heat. Materials with very low loss factors, such as many dry plastics and ceramics, are nearly transparent to the field and remain cool, which is why a microwave oven can heat food in a plastic container.

Radio-frequency heating

At lower frequencies (ISM bands around 13.56, 27.12 and 40.68 MHz) the material is commonly placed between electrodes, forming a lossy capacitor. RF heating penetrates more deeply than microwaves and is used industrially for drying timber and textiles, curing and welding plastics (dielectric sealing of PVC), preheating, gluing wood, and pasteurising or tempering foods, as well as in medicine for deep-tissue diathermy.

Microwave heating

Microwaves (commonly 2.45 GHz in domestic ovens and 915 MHz in some industrial systems) heat more shallowly, with a penetration depth of only a few centimetres in most foods, but respond very rapidly. The microwave oven — developed after Percy Spencer's 1945 observation that a magnetron melted a chocolate bar in his pocket, and commercialised by Raytheon from 1947 — made dielectric heating a household technology. The common claim that microwaves heat "from the inside out" is inaccurate: energy is absorbed within the outer layers and the interior warms largely by conduction, though volumetric RF/microwave heating is still far more uniform than surface heating of a cold, poorly conducting object.

Applications and hazards

  • Food: cooking, defrosting/tempering, drying, pasteurisation, pest control
  • Industry: plastic welding, wood gluing and drying, rubber vulcanisation, ceramics
  • Medicine: RF diathermy and ablation

Because metallic objects reflect microwaves and can concentrate fields, they may arc and damage equipment; sealed containers can burst from internal steam pressure, and superheated liquids can erupt violently when disturbed.
