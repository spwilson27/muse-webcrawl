Bandenbekämpfung

Bandenbekämpfung (German for "bandit fighting", commonly translated as anti-partisan warfare) was the term used by Nazi Germany for counter-insurgency operations against resistance movements and partisans in occupied Europe during the Second World War, particularly on the Eastern Front.

Doctrine and practice

The doctrine deliberately labelled all resistance fighters as "bandits" outside the laws of war, licensing extreme brutality. Operations were conducted by the Wehrmacht, SS, police battalions and local auxiliaries, and in practice involved mass reprisals against civilians, the burning of villages, forced labour deportations and mass killings. In regions such as Belarus, large "dead zones" were depopulated, and hundreds of thousands of civilians were murdered under the cover of anti-partisan sweeps.

Oversight

From 1942 the SS under Heinrich Himmler, through Erich von dem Bach-Zelewski as chief of anti-partisan formations, centralised control of these operations. After the war, the criminal character of Bandenbekämpfung was documented at the Nuremberg trials, although many perpetrators escaped prosecution during the Cold War.
