Political party

A political party is an organisation that coordinates candidates to compete in a particular country's elections. It is common for the members of a party to hold similar ideas about politics, and parties may promote specific ideological or policy goals. Political parties have become a major part of the politics of almost every country, as modern party organisations developed and spread around the world over the last few centuries.

Functions

Parties structure political choice by nominating candidates, aggregating interests, mobilising voters, organising government, and providing accountability through identifiable teams of officeholders. Political scientists distinguish party systems by the number and competitiveness of parties, from dominant-party and two-party systems to multiparty systems typical of proportional representation.

History

Modern parties are often traced to the factions of late seventeenth-century Britain (Whigs and Tories) and to the Federalist and Democratic-Republican parties of the early United States. Mass parties with dues-paying membership emerged in the nineteenth century alongside expanding suffrage; Maurice Duverger later distinguished cadre parties, mass parties, and their successors, including Otto Kirchheimer's "catch-all" party and the cartel party described by Richard Katz and Peter Mair.

Organisation and finance

Parties typically have a leader, an executive committee, local branches, and processes for selecting candidates, such as primary elections or selection by members. Their funding—private donations, membership dues, or state subsidies—is regulated to widely varying degrees across countries, and many democracies impose registration and ballot-access rules on parties.
