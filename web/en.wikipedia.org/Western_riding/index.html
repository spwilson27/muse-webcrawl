Western riding is a style of horse riding which evolved from the ranching and warfare traditions brought to the Americas by the Spanish conquistadors, and both equipment and riding style evolved to meet the working needs of the cowboy in the American West. American cowboys needed to work long hours in the saddle over rough terrain, sometimes needing to rope cattle with a lariat (or lasso). Because of the necessity to control the horse with one hand and use a lariat with the other, western horses were trained to change direction with a light pressure of the reins against the neck — a technique known as neck reining — and to stop, turn and sprint on command with minimal cueing.

Equipment

The most obvious distinction from English riding is the saddle. The western saddle is larger and heavier, with a prominent pommel topped by a horn (used for dallying a rope when roping cattle), a deep seat, and wide stirrups. It distributes the rider's weight over a larger area of the horse's back, making long days more comfortable. Western bridles typically use a curb bit with long shanks and split reins, and often lack a noseband. Riders traditionally wear jeans, cowboy boots, a long-sleeved shirt and a cowboy hat.

Gaits

Western horses are ridden at the walk, the jog (a slow, relaxed trot) and the lope (a slow, relaxed canter). In western pleasure classes, horses are expected to travel on a loose rein with low head carriage and calm, unhurried movement.

Disciplines

Competitive western events include reining — often described as the dressage of western riding, featuring sliding stops, spins and rollbacks; cutting, in which horse and rider separate a single cow from a herd; barrel racing and pole bending, timed speed events common at rodeos; working cow horse; team penning and ranch sorting; western pleasure; trail; and western horsemanship. The American Quarter Horse, American Paint Horse and Appaloosa are the breeds most associated with the style.

History

The tradition descends from the vaquero culture of colonial New Spain, itself adapted from Spanish and Moorish horsemanship. As ranching spread north into Texas and the Great Plains in the 19th century, the style absorbed influences from English foxhunting traditions in the eastern United States, producing the cowboy horsemanship recognised today.
