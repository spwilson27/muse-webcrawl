College of Pontiffs

The College of Pontiffs (Latin: Collegium Pontificum) was one of the four great priestly colleges of ancient Rome and the most important body in charge of the state religion. Its members, the pontiffs (pontifices), were headed by the pontifex maximus, the chief priest of Rome.

Composition and duties

Tradition ascribed the college's foundation to Numa Pompilius, Rome's second king. The college originally consisted of a small number of patrician priests chosen by co-optation; the Lex Ogulnia of 300 BC opened it to plebeians, and later reforms introduced election by a special assembly. Sulla set membership at fifteen, and Julius Caesar raised it to sixteen. The college supervised the religious calendar and festivals, regulated sacred law (ius divinum), advised magistrates and the Senate on religious questions, kept official records and annals, oversaw adoptions and wills, and supervised other priesthoods including the flamens, the Rex Sacrorum and the Vestal Virgins.

Pontifex maximus

The pontifex maximus was elected for life and resided in the Domus Publica near the Forum. From Julius Caesar onwards the office was held by leading statesmen, and under the Empire the emperor always held it, until it passed out of use in late antiquity; the title was later adopted by the popes of the Roman Catholic Church.
