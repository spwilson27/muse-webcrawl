The Rus' (Old East Slavic: Роусь; Greek: Ῥῶς, Rhōs) were a people in early medieval Eastern Europe. The scholarly consensus is that they originally consisted of Norse peoples — chiefly originating from what is now Sweden — who settled among the East Slavs, ruling and trading along the river routes between the Baltic Sea, the Black Sea and the Caspian Sea.

Origins and trade

The Rus' are first attested in 839 in the Annals of St Bertin, describing envoys of a ruler titled khagan. From bases at Staraya Ladoga, Novgorod and later Kyiv, they traded furs, slaves, wax and honey for silver dirhams from the Islamic world and luxury goods from Byzantium, travelling the Volga and Dnieper routes. Rus' merchants appear in Arabic sources such as Ibn Fadlan, who gave a famous account of a Rus' ship burial on the Volga in 922.

Kievan Rus'

According to the Primary Chronicle, the Slavic and Finnic tribes invited the Varangian Rurik to rule them in 862; his successor Oleg seized Kyiv in 882, founding the state known as Kievan Rus'. Over the 10th century the Norse elite assimilated into Slavic culture, adopting Slavic names and, under Vladimir the Great in 988, Orthodox Christianity. The ethnonym "Rus'" gave rise to the names Russia and Belarus.

Normanist controversy

The question of the Rus' origins was politically charged for centuries: the "Normanist" theory of Scandinavian origin was contested by "anti-Normanist" scholars, especially in the Soviet era. Most modern historians accept a Scandinavian origin for the early ruling group combined with rapid Slavicisation.
