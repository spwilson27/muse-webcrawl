The South Downs are a range of chalk hills in the south-eastern coastal counties of England that extends for about 110 kilometres (68 mi) across the south-eastern part of Hampshire, through West Sussex, to the chalk cliffs of Beachy Head in East Sussex. They are characterised by rolling chalk downland, dry valleys, and steep scarp slopes, and much of the area is protected as the South Downs National Park, established in 2010.

Geology

The Downs consist of chalk deposited during the Cretaceous period, when the area lay beneath a shallow sea. Subsequent uplift and erosion shaped the rounded hills, with flint nodules abundant in the chalk. The scarp face overlooks the Weald to the north, while gentler dip slopes run south toward the English Channel.

History

The Downs have been inhabited since the Neolithic period, with evidence including long barrows, hill forts such as Cissbury Ring and the Devil's Dyke, and the route of the South Downs Way, which follows an ancient ridgeway track. Chalk grassland was traditionally maintained by sheep grazing, giving rise to the distinctive short turf and wildflowers.

South Downs Way

The South Downs Way is a long-distance footpath of about 160 kilometres (100 mi) running the length of the Downs from Winchester to Eastbourne. It is one of Britain's National Trails and is popular with walkers, cyclists, and horse riders.
