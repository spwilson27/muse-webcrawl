Flash flood

A flash flood is a rapid flooding of low-lying areas such as washes, rivers, dry lakes and depressions. It may be caused by heavy rain associated with a severe thunderstorm, hurricane or tropical storm, or by meltwater from ice or snow. Flash floods are distinguished from ordinary floods by their short timescale — typically occurring within six hours, and often within minutes, of the causative rain — and by the depth and speed of the water, which can sweep away vehicles and people.

Causes and hazards

Flash floods occur when the ground becomes saturated or cannot absorb rainfall, when rain falls on impermeable urban surfaces, or when steep terrain funnels water into narrow channels. In deserts, dry riverbeds (arroyos or wadis) can fill with a wall of water from storms many kilometres away. The force of just 15 cm of fast-moving water can knock a person down, and 60 cm can float most vehicles. Other causes include dam and levee failures, glacial lake outbursts and the sudden release of water blocked by ice or debris.

Notable events

The 1976 Big Thompson Canyon flood in Colorado killed 144 people after intense rain fell over the canyon. The 2010 Pakistan floods, the 2011 Queensland floods and repeated deadly events in Texas Hill Country and European valleys such as the Ahr in 2021 show the phenomenon's global reach. In the United States, flash floods are among the deadliest weather hazards, and the National Weather Service promotes the guidance "Turn Around, Don't Drown".

Forecasting and safety

Meteorological agencies issue flash flood watches and warnings based on radar rainfall estimates and soil conditions. Safety advice stresses never driving through flooded roads, moving to high ground immediately, and avoiding canyon bottoms and dry washes when storms threaten.
