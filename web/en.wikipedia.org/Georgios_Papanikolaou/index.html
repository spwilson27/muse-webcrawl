Georgios Nikolaou Papanikolaou (Greek: Γεώργιος Παπανικολάου; 13 May 1883 – 19 February 1962) was a Greek physician, zoologist and microscopist who pioneered cytopathology and early cancer detection. He is best known as the inventor of the Pap test (Papanicolaou test), a method of screening for cervical cancer that has saved the lives of millions of women.

Early life and education

Papanikolaou was born in Kymi on the island of Euboea, the son of a physician who later became mayor and a member of the Greek parliament. He studied medicine at the University of Athens, graduating in 1904, and earned a doctorate in zoology from the University of Munich in 1910 after research at Jena, Freiburg and Munich. Returning to Greece, he met his wife Andromachi Mavroyenis, who became his lifelong laboratory collaborator.

Career in America

In 1913 the couple emigrated to the United States, where Papanikolaou joined the pathology department of New York Hospital and then the anatomy department of Cornell University Medical College. Studying vaginal smears in guinea pigs to track the oestrous cycle, he extended the method to women and recognised that abnormal cells in smears could reveal cancer of the uterus. He presented his findings in 1928, but they received little attention until his 1943 monograph with Herbert Traut, Diagnosis of Uterine Cancer by the Vaginal Smear, established cytological screening.

The Pap test and legacy

The Pap test — in which cells scraped from the cervix are stained (using the Papanicolaou stain) and examined microscopically — allowed cervical cancer to be detected at a precancerous, treatable stage. Its adoption as a routine screening programme from the 1950s onward dramatically reduced cervical-cancer mortality in countries where it was implemented. Papanikolaou spent his final years at the Papanicolaou Cancer Research Institute in Miami, which he directed from 1961 until his death from heart failure in 1962. Despite his contribution, he never received the Nobel Prize, a frequently noted omission.
