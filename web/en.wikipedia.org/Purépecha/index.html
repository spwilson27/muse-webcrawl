Purépecha

The Purépecha (historically known as the Tarascan) are an Indigenous people of the Mexican state of Michoacán, centred on the highlands around Lake Pátzcuaro and the city of Uruapan. Their language, also called Purépecha, is a language isolate unrelated to any other known language, and is spoken by well over 100,000 people. Before the Spanish conquest, the Purépecha state was one of the great powers of Mesoamerica and the principal rival of the Aztec Empire, which never succeeded in conquering it.

History

The Purépecha state emerged in the fourteenth century around the Lake Pátzcuaro basin, with its capital at Tzintzuntzan. Under rulers known by the title cazonci, the state expanded to control most of modern Michoacán and parts of neighbouring states. The Purépecha were accomplished metalworkers, producing copper and bronze tools, weapons, and ornaments, and their armies repeatedly defeated Aztec invasions, notably in the late fifteenth century. After the Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire, the last cazonci, Tangaxoan II, submitted to Spanish authority but was executed by Nuño de Guzmán in 1530. Under the influence of Bishop Vasco de Quiroga, Purépecha communities were reorganized into craft-specialized towns, many of which retain their specialties — copper work at Santa Clara del Cobre, guitars at Paracho, pottery at Tzintzuntzan — to this day.

Culture

Purépecha culture is noted for its distinctive cuisine, including corundas and uchepos, its lacemaking, woodwork, and copper-smithing traditions, and festivals such as the Night of the Dead (Noche de Muertos) observances around Lake Pátzcuaro, in which families hold vigils in cemeteries on the island of Janitzio and in towns such as Tzurumútaro. The pirekua, a traditional Purépecha song form, was inscribed by UNESCO on its list of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2010.

Language

Purépecha is an agglutinative language with a rich system of suffixes, including numerous locative and body-part suffixes. Despite centuries of contact with Spanish, it remains vigorous in many rural communities, though it is considered vulnerable as younger speakers shift to Spanish.
