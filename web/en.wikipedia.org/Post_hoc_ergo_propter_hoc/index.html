Post hoc ergo propter hoc

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The rooster crows before sunrise, but does not cause the sun to rise.

Post hoc ergo propter hoc (Latin: "after this, therefore because of this") is an informal fallacy that states: "Since event Y followed event X, event Y must have been caused by event X." It is often shortened simply to post hoc fallacy or post hoc reasoning. The error lies in concluding a causal relationship from nothing more than the order in which events occurred — temporal succession is a necessary condition for causation, but not a sufficient one.

Post hoc ergo propter hoc

Type: Informal fallacy; questionable-cause fallacy

Latin: "after this, therefore because of this"

Related: Correlation does not imply causation; cum hoc ergo propter hoc

Pattern

The fallacious argument typically follows the pattern: X occurred, then Y occurred; therefore X caused Y. Classic illustrations include the rooster that supposedly makes the sun rise by crowing, a rain dance followed by rain, and a sports fan's "lucky" ritual credited with a team's victories. Superstitions frequently rest on post hoc reasoning, as do many claims for ineffective medical remedies: a patient takes a remedy and then recovers (as most patients do anyway), and the recovery is attributed to the remedy.

Distinctions

The post hoc fallacy differs from cum hoc ergo propter hoc ("with this, therefore because of this"), which concerns events that occur simultaneously rather than in sequence. Both are instances of the questionable-cause fallacy family and relate to the statistical caution that correlation does not imply causation. Establishing a genuine causal claim requires more than sequence or correlation: controlled experiments, plausible mechanisms, and the exclusion of confounding variables and coincidence.
