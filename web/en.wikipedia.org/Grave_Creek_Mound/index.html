The Grave Creek Mound in Moundsville, West Virginia, is one of the largest conical burial mounds in the United States. Built by the prehistoric Adena culture during the Woodland period, roughly between 250 and 150 BCE, the earthwork is 62 feet (19 m) high and 240 feet (73 m) in diameter and originally contained an estimated 60,000 tons of earth. It stands near the Ohio River, at the mouth of Grave Creek, and gives the city of Moundsville its name.

Construction and excavation

The mound was built in stages, with burials placed in log tombs at successive levels. In 1838 the landowner Jesse Tomlinson and partners tunnelled into the mound, discovering two burial vaults with human remains and ornaments of shell, copper and mica; the excavation, conducted by antiquarian methods, destroyed much of the internal structure. A small inscribed stone, the so-called Grave Creek Stone, was reportedly found in the mound and became the subject of a long controversy; most scholars regard the stone's inscription as a hoax.

Preservation

After passing through various private hands — it was at times used as a saloon site, a fairground and a Civil War-era landmark — the mound was purchased by the state of West Virginia in 1909, an early success of the American preservation movement driven by the Wheeling chapter of the Daughters of the American Revolution. It was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1964 and is now interpreted by the adjacent Delf Norona Museum, operated by the West Virginia Division of Culture and History.
