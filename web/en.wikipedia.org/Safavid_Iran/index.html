Safavid Iran (also called the Safavid Empire or Safavid Persia) was an Iranian empire ruled by the Safavid dynasty from 1501 to 1736. It is regarded as the beginning of modern Iranian history and one of the so-called "gunpowder empires" alongside the Ottoman Empire and the Mughal Empire. The Safavids established Twelver Shia Islam as the official religion of Iran — a decision that permanently shaped the country's identity and forms the foundation of the modern Iranian state.

Origins and foundation

The dynasty originated in the Safaviyya, a Sufi religious order founded by Sheikh Safi al-Din in Ardabil in north-western Iran. By the fifteenth century the order had become militant and won devoted followers among the Turkmen tribes of Anatolia and Syria, known as the Qizilbash ("red heads") for their crimson headgear. In 1501 the order's young leader, Ismail I, captured Tabriz, proclaimed himself Shah, and declared Twelver Shi'ism the state religion despite ruling a population that was then largely Sunni.

Conflict with the Ottomans

Safavid expansion collided with the Sunni Ottoman Empire to the west. After the decisive Ottoman victory at the Battle of Chaldiran in 1514, the frontier between the two empires became one of the most contested in the Islamic world. Under Tahmasp I the capital moved east to Qazvin, and repeated Ottoman invasions were weathered until the Peace of Amasya (1555) temporarily stabilised the border.

Reign of Abbas the Great

The empire reached its zenith under Abbas I (reigned 1588–1629). Abbas reformed the army with the help of the English brothers Robert and Anthony Sherley, creating standing regiments of musketeers, artillery and ghulams (slave-soldiers) to counterbalance the Qizilbash. He reconquered territory lost to the Ottomans and Uzbeks, retook Baghdad in 1623, and in 1598 moved the capital to Isfahan, which he rebuilt into one of the grandest cities of the age, centred on the Naqsh-e Jahan square. Trade flourished: Persian silk was exported through the new port of Bandar Abbas, and diplomatic and commercial ties were opened with European powers.

Decline and fall

Later shahs, raised in the seclusion of the harem and dominated by court factions, proved weak rulers. Religious orthodoxy hardened under the influence of the ulama, and the economy suffered as trade routes shifted. In 1722 an Afghan rebel army under Mahmud Hotak besieged Isfahan and forced the abdication of Shah Sultan Husayn, ending effective Safavid rule. A Safavid claimant was briefly restored by Nader Khan, who finally deposed the last shah in 1736 and founded the Afsharid dynasty.

Legacy

The Safavids' conversion of Iran to Shi'ism created an enduring religious boundary with the Sunni world and gave Iran a distinct national character that survives today. Their era also produced masterpieces of Persian art and architecture — miniature painting, carpet weaving, tilework and poetry — and the administrative and cultural framework of the modern Iranian nation-state.
