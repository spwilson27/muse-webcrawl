Diego Rodríguez de Silva y Velázquez (1599–1660) was a Spanish painter, the leading artist at the court of King Philip IV of Spain and Portugal, and one of the most important painters of the Spanish Golden Age. His individualistic Baroque style, marked by striking naturalism and virtuoso brushwork, profoundly influenced later artists including Édouard Manet, who called him "the painter of painters".

Velázquez's self-portrait within Las Meninas (1656).

Life and career

Velázquez trained in Seville under Francisco Pacheco, painting bodegones — kitchen scenes with figures — such as the Old Woman Frying Eggs. Summoned to Madrid in 1623, he became court painter to Philip IV, a position he held for nearly four decades, rising through court offices while producing portraits of the royal family, court dwarfs and buffoons, and historical and mythological works. Two journeys to Italy (1629–31 and 1649–51) deepened his art.

Major works

His masterpiece Las Meninas (1656), a complex scene of the Infanta Margarita's household with the artist himself at the easel, is among the most analysed paintings in Western art. Other celebrated works include The Surrender of Breda, Las Hilanderas, the Rokeby Venus, Christ Crucified, and his searching late portraits, including that of Pope Innocent X, famed for its psychological intensity.

Legacy

Most of Velázquez's roughly 120 surviving works are held by the Museo del Prado in Madrid. His loose, painterly technique anticipated Impressionism, and he remains a central reference point for realist painting.
