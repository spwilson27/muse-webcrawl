TypeErosional residual landform
SynonymInselberg
NamesakeMount Monadnock, New Hampshire

A monadnock or inselberg is an isolated hill, knob, ridge, or small mountain that rises abruptly from a gently sloping or virtually level surrounding plain. Monadnocks are erosional residuals: they survive because their rock is harder and more resistant to erosion than the surrounding material, which has been worn away.

The term monadnock comes from the Abenaki name of Mount Monadnock in southwestern New Hampshire, a prominent isolated peak of resistant quartzite. Inselberg, German for "island mountain", was coined by the geologist Wilhelm Bornhardt in 1900 for such features in southern Africa.

Formation

Monadnocks form where differential erosion exposes a body of resistant rock — often granite, gneiss, or quartzite — surrounded by less durable rock. They are characteristic of peneplains in the late stage of the erosion cycle and are common in the Piedmont of the eastern United States, in Africa south of the Sahara (such as the kopjes of Zimbabwe), and in Australia, where Uluru is a famous, though compositionally distinct, example.

Examples

Examples include Stone Mountain in Georgia, Pilot Mountain in North Carolina, Sugarloaf Mountain in Brazil, and the many inselbergs of the African savanna. Ecologically, isolated monadnocks often support distinctive plant communities and act as refuges for species not found on the surrounding plain.
