Human rights in the Soviet Union

Overview

Period: 1922–1991

Key documents: Constitutions of 1936 and 1977

Notable movements: Moscow Helsinki Group, dissident movement

Human rights in the Soviet Union were severely restricted throughout the existence of the USSR. Although Soviet constitutions formally guaranteed freedoms such as speech, assembly, and religion, in practice the Communist Party maintained tight control over public life, and these rights were subordinate to the interests of the state and the party.

Political repression

From the Red Terror of the Russian Civil War through the Great Purge of the 1930s, the Soviet state used mass arrests, executions, and the Gulag system of forced-labour camps against real and perceived opponents. Under Joseph Stalin, millions were imprisoned or executed. After Stalin's death in 1953, Nikita Khrushchev's "Secret Speech" denounced the cult of personality, and many prisoners were released, but political repression continued in milder forms, including the use of punitive psychiatry against dissidents.

Dissidents and human rights movement

In the 1960s and 1970s a small but influential dissident movement emerged, demanding that the Soviet government honour its own laws and international commitments. Figures such as Andrei Sakharov, Alexander Solzhenitsyn, and Natan Sharansky drew international attention. The Chronicle of Current Events, a samizdat publication, documented abuses. The Moscow Helsinki Group, founded in 1976, monitored Soviet compliance with the Helsinki Accords.

Freedom of religion and emigration

The Soviet state was officially atheist and persecuted religious believers, closing churches, mosques, and synagogues, though the Russian Orthodox Church was partially rehabilitated during the Second World War. Jewish citizens who sought to emigrate to Israel, known as refuseniks, were often denied exit visas and faced harassment. Emigration restrictions eased under Mikhail Gorbachev's policies of glasnost and perestroika in the late 1980s.

Final years

Under Gorbachev, political prisoners were released, censorship loosened, and contested elections were held in 1989. The Soviet Union dissolved in 1991, and its successor states inherited varied human rights records.
