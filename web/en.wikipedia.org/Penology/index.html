Penology

Penology (from Latin poena, "punishment", and Ancient Greek -logia, "study of") is a sub-component of criminology that deals with the philosophy and practice of various societies in their attempts to repress criminal activities and satisfy public opinion via an appropriate treatment regime for persons convicted of criminal offences.

Scope

Penology studies the theories, purposes, and methods of punishment, including imprisonment, probation, parole, community sanctions, and capital punishment, as well as prison management and the rehabilitation of offenders. It examines the effectiveness of sanctions through measures such as recidivism, and the social and moral justification of penal practices.

History

Classical penology emerged with Cesare Beccaria's On Crimes and Punishments (1764), which argued for proportionate, certain, and public punishment, and John Howard's prison inspections in the 1770s. Jeremy Bentham designed the panopticon and developed utilitarian justifications of punishment. In the 19th century, rival "Pennsylvania" (solitary) and "Auburn" (silent congregate) systems of imprisonment competed internationally. The rehabilitative ideal dominated much of the 20th century until "nothing works" scepticism in the 1970s coincided with a turn to retribution, deterrence, and incapacitation, followed by mass incarceration in the United States.

Theories of punishment

Penology analyses the classic justifications: retribution, deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, and restoration. Michel Foucault's Discipline and Punish (1975) reframed the prison as an institution of social power and surveillance, profoundly influencing modern penological scholarship.
