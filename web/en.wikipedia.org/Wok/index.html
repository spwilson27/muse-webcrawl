Wok

TypeCooking vessel
Place of originChina
Main materialsCarbon steel, cast iron, stainless steel
UsesStir frying, deep frying, steaming, braising, smoking

A wok is a deep, round-bottomed cooking pan of Chinese origin, traditionally with a single long handle or two side handles. It is one of the most versatile tools in East and Southeast Asian cooking, used for stir frying, deep frying, pan frying, boiling, braising, steaming, and even smoking food. Its bowl shape concentrates intense heat at the base while providing cooler zones up the sides, allowing ingredients to be seared quickly and moved aside as needed.

History

Wok-like vessels have been used in China for over two thousand years; clay models of stoves with wok-shaped pots appear in Han dynasty tombs. The design suited fuel scarcity: a small fire could cook food quickly in the thin metal pan. Stir frying in woks became widespread by the Ming dynasty, and the wok spread throughout Asia and, with emigration, to the rest of the world.

Characteristics

Traditional woks are made of carbon steel or cast iron and are "seasoned" by repeated heating with oil, forming a dark patina that releases food easily — the source of wok hei, the prized smoky flavour of food cooked over very high heat in Cantonese cooking. Cantonese-style woks have two short handles, while northern (Mandarin) woks typically have one long handle. Flat-bottomed versions have been developed for use on Western stoves, including electric and induction cooktops.

Use

The wok is used with utensils such as the hoak (ladle) and wok chuan (spatula), and its high, sloping sides make tossing ingredients easy. In stir frying, small pieces of food are cooked rapidly in a little very hot oil, preserving texture and colour.
