Midnight movie

Era1970s–1980s (peak); continuing tradition
Emblematic titlesEl Topo, Pink Flamingos, Eraserhead, The Rocky Horror Picture Show, Night of the Living Dead, The Harder They Come
Key venueElgin Theater, New York City

The term midnight movie refers both to low-budget films shown on late-night American television from the 1950s onward and, more famously, to the phenomenon of unconventional films screened at midnight in theaters during the 1970s and 1980s, which acquired devoted cult audiences through word of mouth and repeat attendance.

The circuit

The modern midnight movie era began at New York's Elgin Theater, where Alejandro Jodorowsky's El Topo (1970) ran for months at late screenings promoted by Ben Barenholtz, who went on to champion David Lynch's Eraserhead (1977). The circuit spread to repertory and arthouse cinemas across the United States and included John Waters's Pink Flamingos (1972), George A. Romero's Night of the Living Dead (1968), Perry Henzell's The Harder They Come (1972), and the rock musical The Rocky Horror Picture Show (1975).

Rocky Horror and audience participation

Rocky Horror flopped in initial release but became the defining midnight movie: from 1976, audiences at New York's Waverly Theater began talking back to the screen, and the practice evolved into elaborate rituals of callbacks, props, costume, and shadow-cast performances. The film has remained in continuous limited release for decades, the longest-running theatrical release in cinema history.

Decline and legacy

Home video in the 1980s largely ended the midnight circuit by making cult films available on demand, but revival screenings, quote-alongs, and annual Rocky Horror events continue, and the term still denotes the cult-film sensibility — transgressive, offbeat works kept alive by fan devotion rather than mainstream distribution.
