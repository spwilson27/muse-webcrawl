Khazar Khaganate

The Khazar Khaganate at its height controlled the steppe between the Black and Caspian seas.

Khazar Khaganate
Flourished: c. 650 – 969 CE
Capital: Atil (Itil)
Peoples: Khazars (Turkic) and many subject peoples
Religions: Judaism (ruling elite), Tengrism, Christianity, Islam

The Khazar Khaganate was a major commercial empire created by the Khazars, a semi-nomadic Turkic people, in the Pontic–Caspian steppe from about 650 to 969 CE. Successor to the Western Turkic Khaganate, it grew into one of the great powers of the early medieval world, controlling trade routes linking Eastern Europe with Central Asia and the Islamic world, from its capital Atil at the mouth of the Volga.

History

The Khazars checked Arab expansion north of the Caucasus in the Arab–Khazar wars of the 7th and 8th centuries, and served as a buffer ally of the Byzantine Empire. Tributaries included Slavic tribes of the forest zone — the source of slaves, furs, wax and honey that fed the khaganate's trade — while the Rus' traded through Khazar territory to the Caspian. In the late 8th and 9th centuries the khaganate was weakened by internal reorganisation and the rise of the Pechenegs and Rus'; in 965 the Kievan prince Sviatoslav I destroyed Atil and the Khazar state, though Khazar communities lingered in the Caucasus and Crimea for centuries.

Religion: the conversion to Judaism

The Khazar ruling class famously adopted Judaism, probably in stages during the 8th and 9th centuries — a unique case among the steppe empires. Sources including the Khazar Correspondence between the Spanish Jewish courtier Hasdai ibn Shaprut and King Joseph of Khazaria, and the Cambridge Document, describe the conversion and the legendary disputation at which the king chose Judaism. The extent of conversion among ordinary Khazars remains debated; the empire was religiously plural, with mosques, churches and synagogues in its cities and judges for each faith. Later speculation that Ashkenazi Jews descend substantially from Khazars — popularised by Arthur Koestler's The Thirteenth Tribe — is not supported by mainstream historical or genetic scholarship.

Government and society

The khaganate had a dual kingship: a sacred khagan, largely a ritual figure, and a deputy (the beg or shad) who commanded armies and administered the empire. Its wealth rested on tolls on transit trade, tribute, and the commerce of Atil and the Crimean ports.
