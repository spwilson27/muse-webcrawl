The Kingdom of Gwynedd (Welsh: Teyrnas Gwynedd) was one of the principal Welsh kingdoms of the post-Roman and medieval periods, occupying north-west Wales — the lands between the River Dyfi and the River Dee, including the island of Anglesey (Ynys Môn) and the mountainous mainland of Snowdonia (Eryri). It endured from the 5th century until the Edwardian conquest of Wales in 1282–83.

Origins

Welsh tradition attributes the foundation of Gwynedd to Cunedda Wledig, said to have migrated with his sons from Manaw Gododdin in the "Old North" in the late 4th or 5th century to expel Irish settlers. The early kingdom was ruled by the dynasty of Cunedda — including Maelgwn Gwynedd in the 6th century — before passing, through Cadwallon ap Cadfan (who allied with Penda of Mercia and fell at Heavenfield in 634), to later ruling houses.

Height and decline

Rhodri the Great (r. 844–878) united Gwynedd with Powys and Seisyllwg, and his grandson Hywel Dda codified Welsh law. In the 13th century, Llywelyn the Great (r. c. 1195–1240) made Gwynedd the dominant power in Wales, and his grandson Llywelyn ap Gruffudd was recognised by the Treaty of Montgomery (1267) as Prince of Wales. The title and kingdom ended with the wars of Edward I: Llywelyn was killed in 1282, his brother Dafydd was executed in 1283, and the Statute of Rhuddlan (1284) absorbed the principality's lands into the English crown. Edward's ring of castles — Conwy, Caernarfon, Harlech, Beaumaris — enforced the settlement.

Legacy

The name Gwynedd survives in the modern county of Gwynedd, created in 1974 and covering much of the old kingdom's territory, and its court sites — Aberffraw on Anglesey above all — remain central to Welsh national memory.
