British Indian Army

Indian troops on the Western Front, 1914–15

The British Indian Army, commonly called the Indian Army, was the principal military force of British India until 1947. It originated in the armies of the East India Company, which were brought under the Crown after the Indian Rebellion of 1857. By the Second World War it had become the largest volunteer army in history, reaching some 2.5 million men, before being divided between India and Pakistan at Partition.

Organisation

The army was organised into regiments recruited heavily from groups the British designated "martial races" — Sikhs, Punjabi Muslims, Pathans, Gurkhas (recruited from Nepal), Rajputs, and others. Indian soldiers (sepoys in infantry, sowars in cavalry) served under Indian officers holding Viceroy's commissions (subedars and jemadars) and British King's commissioned officers; full commissions for Indians opened only slowly after 1917. Reforms by Lord Kitchener in 1903 unified the three presidency armies into a single Indian Army.

Service

Indian troops served across the empire: in the Anglo-Afghan and Anglo-Burmese wars, on the North-West Frontier, and in China and Abyssinia. In the First World War over 1.3 million served, in France, Gallipoli, Mesopotamia, Egypt, and East Africa; more than 70,000 died. In the Second World War the army fought in North Africa, Italy, and Burma, where the Fourteenth Army — largely Indian — defeated the Japanese; the Indian National Army of Subhas Chandra Bose fought on the other side. Some 87,000 Indian soldiers died in the two world wars, and Indian troops won numerous Victoria Crosses.

Legacy

At Partition in 1947 the army was split roughly two-thirds to India and one-third to Pakistan; its regiments, battle honours, and traditions continue in the modern Indian Army and Pakistan Army.
