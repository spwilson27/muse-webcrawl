United States military rations

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: An MRE (Meal, Ready-to-Eat) pouch and its contents laid out — placeholder]

United States military rations are the food provisions issued to members of the United States Armed Forces for consumption in the field, on deployments, and in other situations where organized food facilities are not available. The modern ration system is built around the Meal, Ready-to-Eat (MRE), supplemented by group rations such as the Unitized Group Ration (UGR) and specialized items including the First Strike Ration and cold-weather rations.

History

Military feeding in the United States dates to the Revolutionary War, when Congress prescribed a ration of beef, peas, rice, and spruce beer for Continental soldiers. During the Civil War, soldiers relied heavily on salt pork, hardtack, and coffee. The Spanish–American War exposed serious problems with canned rations, leading to reforms and eventually to the canned C-ration of the Second World War era.

The C-ration, introduced in 1939, combined canned meat units with bread and dessert units. It was followed by the MCI (Meal, Combat, Individual) in 1958, which remained standard through the Vietnam War. Dissatisfaction with the weight and palatability of the MCI led the Department of Defense to develop the MRE, which entered service in the early 1980s and has been continuously improved since.

The MRE

The MRE is a self-contained, individual field ration in lightweight packaging designed to sustain a service member in the field. Each MRE provides roughly 1,250 kilocalories and contains an entrée, side dish, crackers or bread, a spread, dessert, candy, a beverage mix, and an accessory packet with items such as a spoon, condiments, and a flameless ration heater. Menus rotate annually and include options for vegetarian diets as well as kosher and halal certified meals.

Development and testing

Rations are developed by the U.S. Army Natick Soldier Research, Development and Engineering Center in Massachusetts, which conducts extensive field testing with soldiers. Acceptability scores from troops determine whether new menu items are retained. Shelf life is a key requirement: MREs are designed to remain safe and palatable for at least three years at 27 °C (81 °F).
