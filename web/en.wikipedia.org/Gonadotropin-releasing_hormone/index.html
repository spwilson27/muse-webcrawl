Gonadotropin-releasing hormone

Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) is a releasing hormone responsible for the release of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) from the anterior pituitary gland. GnRH is a tropic peptide hormone synthesised and released from GnRH neurons within the hypothalamus. It is the initial step in the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, the system controlling reproduction in vertebrates.

Structure and synthesis

GnRH is a decapeptide, processed from a 92-amino-acid precursor encoded by the GNRH1 gene. It is released in pulses into the hypophyseal portal blood, and the frequency of these pulses differentially regulates LH and FSH synthesis: faster pulses favour LH, slower pulses favour FSH. The pulsatile pattern is essential — continuous exposure to GnRH paradoxically suppresses gonadotropin secretion by downregulating pituitary receptors, a phenomenon exploited pharmacologically.

Function

GnRH release begins to increase at puberty under the influence of kisspeptin neurons, initiating sexual maturation. In females, GnRH pulse patterns vary across the menstrual cycle, and the mid-cycle surge of LH it drives triggers ovulation. In males, steady pulsatility maintains spermatogenesis and testosterone production. Deficiency of GnRH or its migration pathway of GnRH neurons causes Kallmann syndrome, characterised by failure of puberty and loss of the sense of smell.

Clinical uses

Synthetic GnRH (gonadorelin) is used diagnostically. Long-acting GnRH agonists such as leuprorelin initially stimulate, then suppress, LH and FSH, and are used in prostate cancer, endometriosis, uterine fibroids, central precocious puberty, and assisted reproduction. GnRH antagonists such as cetrorelix and ganirelix suppress the axis immediately and are used in fertility treatment and advanced prostate cancer.

History

The hypothalamic control of pituitary gonadotropins was demonstrated in the 1960s–70s, and the structure of GnRH was characterised by the groups of Roger Guillemin and Andrew Schally, who shared the 1977 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their work on brain peptide hormones.
