Scotland Yard

Scotland Yard is a metonym for the headquarters of London's Metropolitan Police, by extension often used to refer to the police force itself. The name derives from the location of the original headquarters, at Great Scotland Yard in Whitehall, a street whose name commemorates a medieval palace that once served as the residence of Scottish kings visiting London and later as a lodging for Scottish ambassadors.

History

The Metropolitan Police was founded in 1829 by Home Secretary Sir Robert Peel, and its first headquarters opened at 4 Whitehall Place, with a rear entrance on Great Scotland Yard — the address by which the office quickly became known. The force moved its headquarters several times: to embankment buildings in 1890 ("New Scotland Yard" designed by Norman Shaw), to Broadway in Westminster in 1967, and in 2016 back to a refurbished building on the Victoria Embankment, again named New Scotland Yard.

The famous revolving sign outside the Broadway-era headquarters, installed in 1968, became an enduring symbol of British policing and was moved to the Embankment site.

Origin of the name

Great Scotland Yard takes its name from Scotland Yard, a building believed to have been used by the kings of Scotland, such as Kenneth III and Malcolm II, when they visited the English court. After the Union of the Crowns the building hosted Scottish officials, and the street retained the name.

In popular culture

Scotland Yard has appeared in countless detective stories, from the works of Arthur Conan Doyle (whose Sherlock Holmes frequently dealt with inspectors such as Lestrade) to modern crime drama. The name is also used colloquially for the Metropolitan Police's Criminal Investigation Department (CID).
