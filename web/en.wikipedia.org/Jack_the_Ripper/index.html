Jack the Ripper

Jack the Ripper was an unidentified serial killer active in the impoverished Whitechapel district of London in 1888. At least five women — the "canonical five" — are generally accepted as his victims, all murdered in the autumn of 1888, most with their throats cut and their bodies mutilated. The name originates in the "Dear Boss" letter, signed "Jack the Ripper", sent to the Central News Agency during the murders; it is widely believed to have been a journalist's hoax, but it fixed the killer's identity in the public imagination forever.

The canonical five

The five victims generally attributed to the Ripper are:

  • Mary Ann Nichols, found 31 August 1888 in Buck's Row.
  • Annie Chapman, found 8 September 1888 in Hanbury Street.
  • Elizabeth Stride, found 30 September 1888 in Berner Street.
  • Catherine Eddowes, found the same night, 30 September, in Mitre Square — the "double event".
  • Mary Jane Kelly, found 9 November 1888 in Miller's Court, the most extensively mutilated victim, and the only one killed indoors.

All five were poor women, most involved in casual prostitution, killed at night in or near Whitechapel. A Metropolitan Police file covers eleven Whitechapel murders between 1888 and 1891, but how many beyond the canonical five belong to the same hand remains disputed.

Investigation and letters

The murders provoked one of the largest police investigations of the Victorian era, involving the Metropolitan Police and the City of London Police, thousands of interviews and the Whitechapel Vigilance Committee. Hundreds of letters claiming to be from the murderer flooded in; the "From Hell" letter, accompanied by part of a human kidney, is among the few that may be genuine. The killer's apparent anatomical knowledge led contemporaries to suspect a surgeon or butcher. No one was ever charged.

Suspects and legacy

More than a hundred suspects have been proposed over 130 years of "Ripperology", including the barrister Montague Druitt, the Polish barber Aaron Kosminski, the American quack Francis Tumblety, the artist Walter Sickert and even members of the royal circle in elaborate conspiracy theories. Modern DNA claims have been advanced and strongly contested. The case became a founding myth of true crime: it exposed the misery of the Victorian East End, helped spur social reform, and has generated an unending literature of books, films, tours and theories — while the killer's identity remains unknown.
