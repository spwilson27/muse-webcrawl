Quantum decoherence


Entanglement with the environment suppresses interference between states.

Quantum decoherence is the process by which a quantum system loses its coherence — the phase relations between components of a superposition — through interaction and entanglement with its environment. Decoherence explains why macroscopic objects do not display quantum interference in practice and provides the mechanism by which the classical world of definite outcomes appears to emerge from quantum mechanics, without requiring any modification of the Schrödinger equation.

Mechanism

When a system interacts with its surroundings, system and environment become entangled; the environment effectively "measures" certain preferred states (pointer states), selected by the interaction — a process sometimes called environment-induced superselection, or einselection, developed by Wojciech Zurek and others building on H. Dieter Zeh's pioneering work from 1970. Tracing over the environmental degrees of freedom, the system's reduced density matrix rapidly loses its off-diagonal (interference) terms, on decoherence timescales that for macroscopic objects are fantastically short — far shorter than thermal relaxation times.

Relation to interpretations

Decoherence is central to several approaches to the measurement problem. In the many-worlds interpretation, decoherence explains the apparent branching into quasi-classical worlds; in consistent-histories and some Copenhagen-style accounts it explains why interference between macroscopically distinct outcomes is unobservable. Decoherence by itself does not select a single outcome — it converts a pure superposition into an improper mixture — so interpretational questions about why one particular outcome is experienced remain disputed.

Experimental studies and technology

Experiments with cavity quantum electrodynamics (notably Serge Haroche's group in the 1990s), ion traps, and superconducting circuits have directly observed the progressive loss of interference as systems are coupled to controlled environments. Conversely, decoherence is the principal obstacle to quantum computing: quantum error correction, dynamical decoupling, and decoherence-free subspaces are all strategies for protecting quantum information from it. Typical coherence times range from microseconds in superconducting qubits to much longer times in trapped ions and nuclear spins.
