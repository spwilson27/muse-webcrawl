Thermostat

A thermostat automatically regulates heating or cooling equipment to maintain a desired temperature.

A thermostat is a regulating device component which senses the temperature of a physical system and performs actions so that the system's temperature is maintained near a desired setpoint. Thermostats are used in any device or system that heats or cools to a setpoint temperature. Examples include building heating, central heating, air conditioners, HVAC systems, water heaters, ovens, refrigerators and medical and scientific incubators.

A thermostat operates as a "closed loop" control device, as it seeks to reduce the error between the desired and measured temperatures. Sometimes a thermostat combines both the sensing and control action elements of a controlled system, such as in an automotive thermostat.

History

Early temperature controls date to the 17th century, when Cornelis Drebbel built a thermostatically controlled incubator and furnace regulator. In the 1830s Andrew Ure developed a bimetallic thermostat, and in 1883 Warren S. Johnson patented an electric room thermostat that signalled building staff when a room was too warm or cold. Albert Butz's 1885 "damper flapper" patent is often credited as the origin of the modern thermostat industry and led to the founding of the company that became Honeywell. Programmable electronic thermostats appeared in the later 20th century, followed in the 2010s by learning "smart" thermostats such as the Nest.

Construction and types

Mechanical thermostats commonly use a bimetallic strip, two bonded metals with different thermal expansion rates that bend with temperature to make or break an electrical contact, sometimes via a mercury tilt switch. Wax pellet thermostats regulate coolant flow in internal combustion engines. Modern digital thermostats sense temperature with a thermistor or semiconductor sensor and control equipment through relays, often with programmable schedules, remote access and occupancy detection.

Applications

Thermostats are integral to heating, ventilation and air conditioning, refrigeration, water heating, cooking appliances, engine cooling systems, laboratory incubators and industrial processes. In control theory the thermostat is a classic example of bang-bang (on–off) control, and its hysteresis band prevents rapid cycling of equipment.
