Indian Act

The Indian Act (French: Loi sur les Indiens) is a Canadian Act of Parliament, first enacted in 1876, that concerns registered Indians (First Nations), their bands, and the system of Indian reserves. It defines who is recognized as a "status Indian," governs band councils and reserve lands, and historically served as the principal instrument of Canada's assimilationist policy toward Indigenous peoples.

History

The Act consolidated earlier colonial legislation, including the Gradual Civilization Act (1857) and the Gradual Enfranchisement Act (1869). Amendments over the decades imposed sweeping controls: the potlatch and other ceremonies were banned (1884–1951), traditional governance was displaced by elected band councils, attendance at residential schools was made compulsory, and "enfranchisement" stripped status from those who earned degrees, served in the military, or — for women — married non-status men.

Amendments and reform

A major 1951 revision removed some of the most repressive provisions. Bill C-31 (1985) responded to sex-discrimination challenges by restoring status to many women and their children, though "second-generation cut-off" rules continue to limit transmission of status. A 1969 government White Paper proposing to repeal the Act entirely was rejected by Indigenous leaders who feared the loss of legal recognition and treaty rights. The Act remains in force and controversial — criticized as paternalistic and discriminatory, yet also defended as the legal basis of rights and protections — and is a recurring subject of reform debate alongside the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's calls to action.
