The Confederate States of America (CSA), commonly referred to as the Confederate States or the Confederacy, was an unrecognized breakaway republic in the Southern United States that existed from February 8, 1861, to May 9, 1865. It was formed by eleven U.S. states that declared secession and faced the Union in the American Civil War; their secession was declared illegal by the U.S. federal government and no foreign nation officially recognized the Confederacy as a country, although the United Kingdom and France granted it belligerent status.

Secession

After the election of Abraham Lincoln in November 1860, South Carolina seceded, followed by six other Deep South states; they formed the Confederacy at Montgomery, Alabama, adopting a constitution closely modelled on the U.S. Constitution but explicitly protecting slavery. After Confederate forces fired on Fort Sumter in April 1861 and Lincoln called for troops, four more states, led by Virginia, joined, and the capital moved to Richmond.

War and collapse

The Confederacy fielded armies commanded by generals including Robert E. Lee, but was progressively strangled by the Union blockade, inferior industrial capacity, and inflation that destroyed its currency. Lee's surrender at Appomattox Court House on April 9, 1865, effectively ended the war; President Jefferson Davis was captured in May, and the remaining Confederate forces surrendered by June 1865. During Reconstruction the former Confederate states were readmitted to the Union.

Government and society

The Confederacy's economy rested on enslaved labour and cotton exports; about one-third of its population was enslaved. Vice President Alexander H. Stephens's "Cornerstone Speech" identified white supremacy and slavery as the new government's foundation.
