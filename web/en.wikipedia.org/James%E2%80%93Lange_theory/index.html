James–Lange theory

ProponentsWilliam James, Carl Lange
Proposed1884–1885
FieldPsychology of emotion

The James–Lange theory is a hypothesis on the origin and nature of emotions, independently proposed by psychologist William James and physician Carl Lange in the 1880s. It holds that emotions occur as a result of physiological reactions to events, rather than causing them.

Theory

According to the theory, an external stimulus triggers a physiological response — such as trembling, a racing heart, or sweating — and the emotion is the person's perception of that response. In James's famous formulation, we do not tremble because we are afraid; we feel afraid because we tremble.

Criticism

The theory was challenged by the Cannon–Bard theory, which argued that physiological arousal and emotion occur simultaneously, and that similar bodily states accompany different emotions. Later two-factor (Schachter–Singer) theories combined arousal with cognitive appraisal. Modern affective neuroscience has revived some elements of the James–Lange view, particularly the role of bodily feedback (interoception) in emotional experience.
