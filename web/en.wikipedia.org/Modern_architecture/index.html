Modern architecture

Modern architecture, also called modernist architecture, was an architectural movement and style that was prominent in the 20th century, especially between the 1920s and the 1970s. It was based on new technologies of construction — particularly steel, reinforced concrete and glass — and on the principle that form should follow function, with ornament rejected as unnecessary.

Origins

Precursors appeared in the late 19th century: Louis Sullivan's steel-frame skyscrapers in Chicago, Frank Lloyd Wright's prairie houses, and the glass-and-iron engineering of railway stations and exhibition halls. In Europe the Deutscher Werkbund (1907) and Peter Behrens's factory designs linked architecture with industrial production.

The International Style and the Bauhaus

After the First World War, Walter Gropius founded the Bauhaus school in Weimar (1919), uniting art, craft and technology. Le Corbusier proclaimed the house "a machine for living in" and codified his Five Points of Architecture — pilotis, free plan, free facade, ribbon windows and roof gardens — exemplified by the Villa Savoye (1931). Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, director of the Bauhaus before it closed under Nazi pressure in 1933, pursued minimalist clarity ("less is more"). The 1932 exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art in New York, curated by Henry-Russell Hitchcock and Philip Johnson, gave the movement its label: the International Style.

Post-war developments and criticism

Emigré architects brought modernism to the United States, where it produced the glass towers of corporate America (Mies's Seagram Building, 1958) and influenced mass housing, universities and new capitals such as Brasília and Chandigarh. By the 1960s critics such as Jane Jacobs attacked modernist planning for destroying street life, and the movement's reputation suffered from the failure of some large public housing projects. Postmodern architecture emerged as a reaction from the late 1960s, reintroducing ornament, colour and historical reference.
