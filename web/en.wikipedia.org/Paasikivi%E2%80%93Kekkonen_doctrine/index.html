Paasikivi–Kekkonen doctrine

The Paasikivi–Kekkonen doctrine was the foreign policy doctrine that guided Finland during the Cold War. Named after Presidents Juho Kusti Paasikivi and Urho Kekkonen, it held that Finland's survival as an independent, democratic state required friendly relations and a working accommodation with the Soviet Union, while preserving Finland's Western social and political institutions.

Background

After the Continuation War ended in 1944 with the Moscow Armistice, Finland — unlike the Baltic states — retained its independence but had to cede territory, pay reparations, and accept limits on its foreign policy. The 1948 Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation and Mutual Assistance (FCMA Treaty) with the USSR formalised the arrangement, obliging Finland to resist attacks "by Germany or its allies" through its territory.

Policy in practice

Finland maintained neutrality, stayed out of NATO and (for decades) the European Community, restrained public criticism of the Soviet Union, and cultivated trade with both East and West. Critics coined the term "Finlandization" to describe the influence the USSR could exert over Finnish affairs. Supporters argued the doctrine preserved Finland's independence and prosperity. It effectively ended with the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991.
