Chose

A chose (pronounced "showze"; from the French for "thing") is a term used in common law tradition to refer to rights in tangible and intangible property which are valuable by reason of the rights they confer, rather than by reason of any physical possession. In use, the term denotes an item of property which is not in the possession of its owner but which must be recovered or enforced through legal action — traditionally contrasted with a thing in actual possession.


A bond or debt is a classic chose in action: valuable only through the right to sue for payment.

Classification

Choses are traditionally divided into two classes:

  • Chose in action (also called a thing in action) — a known legal category of property describing all personal rights to property which can only be claimed or enforced by action, and not by taking physical possession. Examples include debts, shares in a company, rights under a contract, insurance policies, patents and the benefit of a trust. A chose in action must be sued upon; it has no tangible embodiment.
  • Chose in possession — a tangible item of personal property (a chattel) which the owner actually possesses or may take into possession, such as a car, furniture or a piece of jewellery.

History and terminology

The word derives from the Norman French chose, meaning "thing". Historically, English law distinguished between things one could physically seize and mere rights of action, which early common law regarded as non-assignable — a rule intended to prevent maintenance and champerty. Statutory reforms, culminating in the Law of Property Act 1925 in England and Wales, made choses in action generally assignable in writing, and equitable assignment had long been recognised by the courts of equity.

Modern usage

Today the term is most often encountered in the phrase "chose in action" in property, insolvency and commercial law. In bankruptcy, choses in action form part of the bankrupt's estate vesting in the trustee. In some jurisdictions the term also appears in tax and secured-transactions legislation. The parallel civil-law concept is the incorporeal right or ius in personam.
