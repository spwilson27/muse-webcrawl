Dred Scott v. Sandford

Dred Scott v. Sandford, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 393 (1857), was a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court that held that the U.S. Constitution did not extend American citizenship to people of Black African descent, whether enslaved or free, and therefore they could not sue in federal court. The Court also ruled that the Missouri Compromise of 1820 was unconstitutional because Congress lacked authority to prohibit slavery in federal territories.

Background

Dred Scott was an enslaved man whose owner, an army surgeon named John Emerson, had taken him from Missouri, a slave state, to posts in Illinois and the Wisconsin Territory, where slavery was prohibited. After Emerson's death, Scott sued his widow for his freedom in Missouri state court in 1846, arguing that his residence in free territory had made him free. The case eventually reached the Supreme Court after passing to John F. A. Sanford of New York (whose name was misspelled "Sandford" in the court records).

Decision

Chief Justice Roger B. Taney delivered the opinion of the Court on March 6, 1857. The Court held, 7–2, that people of African descent "had no rights which the white man was bound to respect," that Scott remained enslaved, and that the Missouri Compromise's ban on slavery in the territories exceeded Congress's constitutional power because it deprived slaveholders of property without due process.

Reaction and legacy

The decision was denounced in the North and celebrated in much of the South. It inflamed sectional tensions, helped propel the new Republican Party, and is widely regarded as one of the worst Supreme Court decisions in American history and a significant cause of the Civil War. The ruling was superseded by the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and effectively overturned by the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments, the latter of which guarantees citizenship to all persons born or naturalized in the United States.
