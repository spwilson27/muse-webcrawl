A prime meridian is the line of longitude defined as 0° in a geographic coordinate system. Together with its antimeridian at 180°, it forms a great circle dividing the Earth into the Eastern and Western hemispheres. Unlike lines of latitude, which are fixed by the equator, the position of a prime meridian is arbitrary and chosen by convention.

History

Different nations historically used their own prime meridians, often passing through their capitals or national observatories; at one point more than a dozen were in use, including meridians through Paris, Ferro (El Hierro), and Washington, D.C. In October 1884, delegates from 25 nations met at the International Meridian Conference in Washington, D.C., and voted to adopt the meridian passing through the Royal Observatory in Greenwich, London, as the common zero of longitude, largely because most nautical charts already used it and the United States had adopted it for its railroad time zones.

The modern meridian

The modern reference meridian maintained for satellite geodesy, the IERS Reference Meridian, passes about 102 metres east of the historic Greenwich marker, because modern measurements define the meridian relative to the Earth's centre of mass rather than to the local direction of gravity at Greenwich. GPS receivers at the historic marker therefore read a longitude of about 0°00′05″ west.

Geography

The prime meridian passes through the United Kingdom, France, Spain, Algeria, Mali, Burkina Faso, Ghana, and Antarctica. Its intersection with the equator in the Gulf of Guinea is occasionally marked by a buoy nicknamed "Null Island", a fictitious location used as a repository for geocoding errors at coordinates 0°, 0°.
