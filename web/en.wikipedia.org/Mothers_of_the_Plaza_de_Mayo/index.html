Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo

[Image: The Mothers marching around the May Pyramid in Buenos Aires]

The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo (Spanish: Madres de Plaza de Mayo) is an Argentine human rights association formed by mothers whose children were "disappeared" during the National Reorganization Process, the military dictatorship that ruled Argentina from 1976 to 1983. On 30 April 1977, fourteen mothers led by Azucena Villaflor began gathering in the Plaza de Mayo in Buenos Aires, in front of the Casa Rosada, demanding to know the whereabouts of their missing children. Ordered by police to keep moving, they marched in pairs around the May Pyramid - the Thursday marches they continue to this day.

Resistance and repression

The Mothers became the first major movement to openly confront the junta, wearing white headscarves embroidered with their children's names. In December 1977 Villaflor and two other founding Mothers, along with two French nuns who supported them, were themselves abducted and murdered by the regime. Despite the danger, the movement grew, gained international attention during the 1978 World Cup and after, and pressed the cause of an estimated 30,000 disappeared persons.

After the dictatorship

With the return of democracy in 1983, the Mothers supported the investigation and trial of the juntas, and related groups - the Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo - worked to identify children stolen from detained parents. In 1986 the Mothers split into two factions (the Association, led by Hebe de Bonafini, and the Founding Line). The weekly Thursday marches in the Plaza de Mayo remain an emblem of resistance to impunity, and the white headscarf has become an international symbol of human rights protest.
