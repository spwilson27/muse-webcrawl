Euler–Lagrange equation

In the calculus of variations, the Euler–Lagrange equation is a second-order partial differential equation whose solutions are the functions for which a given functional is stationary. It was developed by the Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler and the Italian mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange in the 1750s.

Statement

Given a functional that is the integral of a Lagrangian function L depending on position x, a function f(x), and its derivative f′(x), the function that makes the functional stationary must satisfy:

∂L/∂f − d/dx (∂L/∂f′) = 0

Because a differentiable functional has a stationary point at its local extrema, the Euler–Lagrange equation is the key tool for solving optimization problems in which one seeks the curve that minimizes or maximizes an integral quantity.

Applications

The equation is fundamental in classical mechanics, where, applied to the Lagrangian of a physical system (kinetic minus potential energy), it yields Lagrange's equations of motion, equivalent to Newton's laws. It is also used in optics (deriving Fermat's principle of least time), in finding geodesics and minimal surfaces, and in field theory, where a field-theoretic version governs the dynamics of fields in physics.

Examples

Classic applications include finding the shortest path between two points (a straight line), the brachistochrone curve of fastest descent, and the catenary shape of a hanging chain.
