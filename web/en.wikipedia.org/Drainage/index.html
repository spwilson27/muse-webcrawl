Drainage

An urban storm drain

Drainage is the natural or artificial removal of a surface's water and sub-surface water from an area with excess water. The internal drainage of most agricultural soils is good enough to prevent severe waterlogging (anaerobic conditions that harm root growth), but many soils need artificial drainage to improve or restore production. In urban and civil engineering, drainage systems collect rainwater and wastewater to prevent flooding, protect structures and control pollution.

Types of drainage

Surface drainage

Surface drainage removes water ponding on the land surface using open ditches, channels, land grading and bunds. In cities, road gullies, kerbs and storm sewers convey runoff to outfalls.

Subsurface drainage

Subsurface (tile) drainage uses buried perforated pipes, historically clay tiles, laid below the water table to lower it in agricultural land. French drains — gravel-filled trenches — serve similar purposes around buildings and playing fields.

Building and highway drainage

Roofs drain via gutters and downspouts; highways use camber, edge drains and culverts to keep water off and beneath the pavement, preventing frost heave and hydroplaning.

Environmental considerations

Draining wetlands destroys habitat and releases stored carbon; agricultural drainage can carry nutrients and pesticides into waterways. Modern sustainable drainage systems (SuDS) aim to slow runoff with swales, detention ponds, permeable paving and green roofs, mimicking natural drainage and improving water quality.

History

Drainage is ancient: the Indus Valley civilisation had covered drains by 3000 BC, and Roman cloacae drained Rome. Large-scale land drainage reclamation shaped the Netherlands (polders), the English Fens, and the Pontine Marshes in Italy.
