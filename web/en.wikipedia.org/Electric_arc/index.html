Electric arc

An electric arc (or arc discharge) is an electrical breakdown of a gas that produces a prolonged electrical discharge: current flows through a normally non-conductive medium such as air, ionising it into a luminous plasma channel. The arc is characterised by high current density, a bright emission of light, and extremely high temperatures — the plasma column can exceed 6,000 K, hotter than the surface of the Sun.

Characteristics

An arc forms when the electric field across a gap exceeds the dielectric strength of the gas, or when two conductors carrying current are separated. Unlike a spark, which is transient, an arc is self-sustaining: heat maintains thermionic emission from the cathode and keeps the gas ionised. Arcs exhibit negative differential resistance — as current increases, voltage across the arc falls — so circuits require ballast or current limiting to prevent runaway.

Applications

Electric arcs underpin arc welding, the electric arc furnace for steelmaking, arc lamps (historically the first practical electric lighting, developed by Humphry Davy around 1802–1809 and commercialised with the Yablochkov candle), plasma cutting, carbon-arc film projection and some types of electric propulsion. The scientist Hertha Ayrton's The Electric Arc (1902) was a landmark study of its physics.

Hazards

Unintentional arcs — arc faults in switchgear and wiring — cause fires and explosive arc flash events that are a serious industrial hazard. Arc-fault circuit interrupters (AFCIs) are used in buildings to detect and stop dangerous arcing.
