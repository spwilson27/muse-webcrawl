Lake Poets

The Lake Poets were a group of English poets who lived in the Lake District of England in the first part of the nineteenth century. The core members were William Wordsworth, Samuel Taylor Coleridge, and Robert Southey, whose association with the region gave English Romanticism one of its defining settings.

The group

Wordsworth settled at Dove Cottage in Grasmere in 1799 and later at Rydal Mount. Coleridge lived at Keswick, near Derwentwater, from 1800 to 1803, within walking distance of Wordsworth across the fells. Southey moved to Greta Hall in Keswick in 1803 and remained there for forty years. Though the label "Lake School" was coined derisively by critics in the Edinburgh Review, the poets' shared landscape and friendship shaped works including the Lyrical Ballads (1798), Wordsworth's Prelude, and Coleridge's conversation poems.

Wider circle

Other writers associated with the Lakes included Dorothy Wordsworth, whose Grasmere Journal inspired some of her brother's best-known poems; Thomas De Quincey, who took over Dove Cottage in 1809; John Wilson ("Christopher North"); and Hartley Coleridge. Later in the century the region also drew John Ruskin, who lived at Brantwood on Coniston Water.

Legacy

The Lake Poets' writing helped establish the Lake District as a landscape of national cultural significance, contributing to the region's eventual protection as a national park (1951) and UNESCO World Heritage Site (2017).
