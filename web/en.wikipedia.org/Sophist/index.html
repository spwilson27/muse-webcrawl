A sophist (Greek: σοφιστής, sophistēs) was a teacher in ancient Greece in the 5th and 4th centuries BC who specialized in the humanities — above all rhetoric — and taught, for a fee, the skills needed for success in public and political life. In the democratic city-states, especially Athens, their instruction in persuasive speech was in high demand.

The sophists

The leading sophists included Protagoras of Abdera, famous for the dictum "man is the measure of all things"; Gorgias of Leontini, a master of ornate rhetoric whose Encomium of Helen defended an indefensible client for display; Hippias of Elis, a polymath; and Prodicus of Ceos, noted for precision in the use of words. They debated the contrast between nature (physis) and convention (nomos), the existence of the gods, and whether virtue can be taught.

Criticism by Plato

Plato's dialogues portray the sophists as the great adversaries of Socrates: mercenary purveyors of mere opinion and clever fallacies (whence the words "sophistry" and "sophism") rather than seekers of truth. Aristotle similarly condemned making the weaker argument the stronger. Because nearly all surviving accounts come from their critics, modern scholarship has partly rehabilitated the sophists as serious thinkers — pioneers of relativism, linguistics, and democratic education.

Second Sophistic

The term was revived for the "Second Sophistic," a flowering of Greek rhetorical display under the Roman Empire (1st–3rd centuries AD), described by Philostratus in his Lives of the Sophists, whose stars such as Herodes Atticus performed improvised declamations before huge audiences.
