Demarcation problem

The demarcation problem is the question of how to distinguish science from non-science in philosophy of science. It involves the difficulties of stating necessary and sufficient conditions for deciding when a theory or its proponents should be considered scientific. The debate has roots in the early 20th century with the work of the logical positivists and Karl Popper, and was dramatically reshaped by Thomas Kuhn's account of scientific revolutions.

Logical positivism and verificationism

The Vienna Circle's logical positivists proposed that a statement is meaningful only if it can be verified empirically or is true by definition. On this criterion, metaphysics, theology and much ethics were literally meaningless, while science was the paradigm of meaningful discourse. Verificationism proved difficult to sustain: universal scientific laws cannot be conclusively verified by any finite set of observations.

Popper: falsifiability

Karl Popper proposed in The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934) that a theory is scientific if and only if it is falsifiable — that is, it forbids some observable state of affairs and could be shown false by a potential test. Confirmations count, he argued, only when they are risky predictions. By this standard, Darwinian evolution and relativity were science, while Adlerian psychology and vulgar Marxism, which could accommodate any outcome, were not. Popper stressed that falsifiability is a methodological proposal, not a criterion of meaning.

Kuhn, Lakatos and Laudan

Thomas Kuhn argued that normal science is puzzle-solving within a paradigm and that astrology failed not for lack of falsifiability but for lack of a puzzle-solving tradition. Imre Lakatos proposed judging research programmes by whether they predict novel facts (progressive) or merely accommodate them (degenerating). In 1983 Larry Laudan famously declared the demarcation problem dead, arguing that no criterion had succeeded and that the important question is whether a belief is well or poorly supported by evidence. Philosophers have since debated whether "pseudoscience" remains a useful category, often appealing to clusters of features rather than one bright line.

Practical stakes

Demarcation matters outside philosophy: courts have used it to keep creationism and intelligent design out of public school science classes in the United States (notably McLean v. Arkansas, 1982, and Kitzmiller v. Dover, 2005), and funding bodies and journals must continually distinguish research from pseudoscience such as astrology, homeopathy and perpetual motion schemes.
