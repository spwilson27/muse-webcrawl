Serrano pepper

The serrano pepper (Capsicum annuum) is a type of chili pepper that originated in the mountainous regions of the Mexican states of Hidalgo and Puebla. The name of the pepper is a reference to the mountains (sierras) of those regions. Unripe serranos are green; they ripen through orange to red or brown. The fruit is small, slender, and firm, typically 5–10 cm (2–4 in) long.

Heat

Serrano peppers rate between 10,000 and 25,000 on the Scoville scale, substantially hotter than a jalapeño but milder than a habanero. Their heat is crisp and immediate, and unlike many chilies they are rarely dried; they are eaten raw, roasted, or pickled.

Culinary use

Serranos are a staple of Mexican cuisine, used in salsa verde, pico de gallo, guacamole, and sauces where a bright, biting heat is wanted without the fruity depth of hotter peppers. They may be charred on a comal before blending, or finely minced raw as a table condiment. The pepper is also popular in Thai-influenced and Southwestern cooking as a substitute for Thai bird's eye chilies.

Cultivation

The serrano plant grows 0.5–1.5 m tall and produces up to 50 peppers per plant in warm, frost-free conditions. Mature serranos can be red, brown, orange, or yellow, depending on the variety.
