Ciclosporin

Ciclosporin, also spelled cyclosporine and cyclosporin A, is a calcineurin inhibitor used as an immunosuppressant medication. It is taken orally or by injection for rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, Crohn's disease, nephrotic syndrome, and after organ transplantation to prevent rejection. It is also used as eye drops for keratoconjunctivitis sicca (dry eyes).

Medical uses

Ciclosporin's introduction in the early 1980s revolutionized organ transplantation by sharply reducing acute rejection rates, particularly in kidney, liver, and heart transplantation. It is also used in severe atopic dermatitis, severe psoriasis, aplastic anaemia, and graft-versus-host disease. In ophthalmology, ciclosporin eye drops increase tear production in dry-eye disease.

Mechanism of action

Ciclosporin binds to the intracellular protein cyclophilin in T lymphocytes. The resulting complex inhibits the phosphatase calcineurin, preventing the dephosphorylation of NFAT and thereby reducing the transcription of interleukin-2 and other lymphokines, which suppresses T-cell activation and proliferation.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include kidney dysfunction, hypertension, tremor, headache, excess hair growth, and gum overgrowth. Ciclosporin levels require monitoring because of its narrow therapeutic index and many drug interactions mediated by CYP3A4.

History

The compound was isolated in 1971 from the fungus Tolypocladium inflatum, found in soil samples collected in Norway. Jean-François Borel at Sandoz discovered its immunosuppressive properties, and it was approved for clinical use in 1983.
