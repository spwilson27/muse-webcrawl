Click chemistry

Click chemistry is a class of highly reliable, high-yielding chemical reactions used to join molecular building blocks quickly and selectively, in the manner of a buckle clicking shut. The concept was introduced by K. Barry Sharpless and co-workers in 2001, who argued that synthesis should favour reactions that are modular, wide in scope, give very high yields, generate only inoffensive by-products, and work under simple conditions. Sharpless shared the 2022 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Carolyn Bertozzi and Morten Meldal for the development of click chemistry and bioorthogonal chemistry.

The azide–alkyne cycloaddition

The archetypal click reaction is the copper-catalysed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC), reported independently by the Sharpless and Meldal groups in 2002. It joins an azide with a terminal alkyne to form a stable 1,2,3-triazole ring, proceeding reliably in water at room temperature. The ruthenium-catalysed variant gives the complementary regioisomer. Because azides and alkynes are small, stable, and largely absent from biological molecules, the reaction became a general tool for tagging and assembling molecules.

Bioorthogonal chemistry

Bertozzi extended the idea to reactions that proceed inside living organisms without disturbing native biochemistry. Her strain-promoted azide–alkyne cycloaddition replaces the toxic copper catalyst with a bent cyclooctyne ring whose strain drives the reaction, enabling the labelling of sugars and other molecules on living cells.

Applications

Click reactions are now used across drug discovery, polymer and materials science, DNA sequencing technologies, radiochemistry, and bioconjugation, wherever two components must be joined rapidly, cleanly, and predictably.
