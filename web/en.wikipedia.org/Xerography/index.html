Xerography

InventorChester Carlson
First demonstration22 October 1938
EtymologyGreek xeros (dry) + graphein (to write)
Used inPhotocopiers, laser printers

Xerography is a dry photocopying technique that forms the basis of nearly all photocopiers and laser printers. It was invented by American physicist and patent attorney Chester Carlson, who made the first successful copy — reading "10-22-38 ASTORIA" — in Astoria, Queens, on 22 October 1938. The name combines Greek words for "dry" and "writing", since the process uses no liquid chemicals.

Process

A photoconductive drum or belt is given a uniform electrostatic charge. Exposure to the image discharges the illuminated areas, leaving a latent image in charge. Fine toner particles, oppositely charged, cling to the charged regions and are transferred to paper, where heat and pressure fuse them permanently. The drum is then cleaned and the cycle repeats.

Commercialization

Carlson struggled for years to interest companies before the Battelle Memorial Institute and then the Haloid Company — later renamed Xerox — took up development. The Xerox 914 of 1959, the first plain-paper copier, was a landmark success and transformed office work; Carlson's patents ultimately earned him a fortune.
