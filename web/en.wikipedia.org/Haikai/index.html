Haikai

Haikai (Japanese: 俳諧, "comic, unorthodox") is a broad term for a family of linked-verse poetry forms that developed in Japan from the medieval renga tradition. Haikai includes the haikai no renga (comic linked verse), its opening verse the hokku, and related forms such as senryū and haibun. The standalone short poem known today as the haiku derives from the hokku of haikai.

Origins

Linked verse (renga) had been composed at the imperial court for centuries, usually in a refined, classical register. In the 16th century, poets such as Yamazaki Sōkan and Arakida Moritake loosened the conventions, admitting humour, wordplay, everyday subjects and contemporary references. The resulting comic linked verse became known as haikai no renga — "unorthodox" or "playful" linked verse.

Development

Under Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694) and his school in the Edo period, haikai was elevated from light entertainment to a serious literary art, characterised by the aesthetic qualities of sabi (loneliness, patina) and karumi (lightness). Bashō's disciples spread haikai throughout Japan, and later masters such as Yosa Buson and Kobayashi Issa renewed the tradition. In the Meiji period, Masaoka Shiki reformed haikai, coining the term haiku for the independent opening verse and advocating realism drawn from life.

Characteristics

Haikai verse traditionally uses the 5-7-5 pattern of on (sound units) and includes a kigo (seasonal word) and a kireji (cutting word). In linked verse, each stanza responds to the previous one by "link and shift", creating a sequence that moves through seasons, places and moods. Haikai also embraces prose-poetry mixtures: haibun combines prose with haiku, as in Bashō's travel journal Oku no Hosomichi (The Narrow Road to the Deep North).
