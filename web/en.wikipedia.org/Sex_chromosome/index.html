Sex chromosome

Human sex chromosomes

A sex chromosome is a chromosome that participates in sex determination, in contrast to an ordinary autosome. In mammals, including humans, the sex chromosomes are the X chromosome and the Y chromosome: individuals with two X chromosomes (XX) develop as female, and those with one X and one Y (XY) develop as male under the influence of genes on the Y, principally SRY, which triggers testis development.

Systems

Several sex-chromosome systems exist in nature. In the XY system, males are the heterogametic sex (producing X- and Y-bearing gametes). In the ZW system of birds, some reptiles, butterflies, and some fish, the female is heterogametic (ZW) and the male homogametic (ZZ). Some insects use an X0 system, in which males have a single X and no second sex chromosome. Many other organisms determine sex without sex chromosomes at all, including haplodiploid insects and temperature-dependent reptiles. In flowering plants, sex chromosomes have evolved independently many times, as in Silene and papaya.

Evolution and dosage compensation

Sex chromosomes are thought to evolve from ordinary autosomes when one acquires a sex-determining gene; recombination between the pair is then suppressed, allowing the Y (or W) chromosome to degenerate and lose most of its genes — the human Y retains only a few dozen protein-coding genes, while the X carries roughly a thousand. Because females have two X chromosomes and males one, mammals equalise gene dosage by X-inactivation, silencing one X in each female cell early in development; calico cat coat patterns are a visible consequence. The small regions where X and Y still pair in meiosis are called pseudoautosomal regions.

Disorders

Abnormal numbers of sex chromosomes (aneuploidies) include Turner syndrome (X0), Klinefelter syndrome (XXY), and triple X syndrome. Many traits encoded on the X chromosome, such as red–green colour blindness and haemophilia, show sex-linked inheritance patterns.
