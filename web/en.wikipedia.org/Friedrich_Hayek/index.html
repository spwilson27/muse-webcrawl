Friedrich August von Hayek (8 May 1899 – 23 March 1992) was an Austrian-born British economist and political philosopher, a leading figure of the Austrian School and one of the most influential defenders of classical liberalism in the twentieth century. He shared the 1974 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for his work on money and economic fluctuations and on the interdependence of economic, social and institutional phenomena.

Life

Hayek was born in Vienna and served as an artillery officer in the First World War before studying law and political science at the University of Vienna. He worked with Ludwig von Mises, directed the Austrian Institute for Business Cycle Research, and in 1931 moved to the London School of Economics, where his debates with John Maynard Keynes made him the chief academic rival of Keynesian economics. He later taught at the University of Chicago (1950–1962) and the University of Freiburg.

Ideas

Hayek's best-known book, The Road to Serfdom (1944), argued that central economic planning leads inevitably toward totalitarianism. In "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945) he contended that the knowledge needed for economic coordination is dispersed among millions of individuals and can be aggregated only through the price system — the core of his critique of socialist calculation. Later works, including The Constitution of Liberty (1960) and Law, Legislation and Liberty (1973–79), developed his theory of spontaneous order: beneficial social institutions such as markets, law and language evolve without central design.

Hayek founded the Mont Pelerin Society in 1947, a forum that helped revive liberal thought and influenced the market-oriented policies of the 1980s.
