Telescope

A telescope is a device used to observe distant objects by their emission, absorption, or reflection of electromagnetic radiation. Originally an optical instrument using lenses, curved mirrors, or both to observe distant objects, the word now also covers instruments detecting other regions of the spectrum — radio, infrared, ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma-ray telescopes — and even particle detectors for neutrinos and cosmic rays. Telescopes increase the apparent angular size of distant objects as well as their apparent brightness, collecting far more light (or other radiation) than the unaided eye.

History

The first known practical telescopes were refractors built in the Netherlands in 1608; Hans Lippershey applied for a patent, and Jacob Metius and Zacharias Janssen made similar devices. Galileo improved the design in 1609 and turned it skyward, discovering Jupiter's moons, lunar mountains, sunspots, and the phases of Venus — observations that transformed astronomy. Kepler proposed the convex-eyepiece (Keplerian) design in 1611. Because refractors suffered from chromatic aberration, Newton built the first practical reflector in 1668, using a curved mirror. Reflectors dominated thereafter: Herschel's great telescopes, the 100-inch Hooker (1917, used by Hubble to establish the expansion of the universe), the 200-inch Hale (1948), and today's segmented-mirror giants such as Keck (10 m) and the Extremely Large Telescope under construction in Chile (39 m).

Optical designs

Refractors use a front objective lens; reflectors use a primary mirror, in configurations including Newtonian, Cassegrain, and Ritchey–Chrétien; catadioptric designs (Schmidt–Cassegrain, Maksutov) combine a corrector plate with mirrors. Performance is governed by aperture (light grasp and resolution, limited by the diffraction limit θ ≈ 1.22λ/D), focal ratio, and — for ground-based telescopes — atmospheric seeing, mitigated by adaptive optics. Mounts are altazimuth or equatorial, increasingly computer-controlled.

Beyond visible light

Radio telescopes such as Arecibo (1963–2020), FAST, and interferometer arrays like ALMA and the Very Large Array opened the non-optical sky. Space telescopes — Hubble (1990), Chandra (X-ray), Spitzer (infrared), and the James Webb Space Telescope (2021) — observe above the atmosphere's absorption and turbulence.
