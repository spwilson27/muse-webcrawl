Winfield Scott

Winfield Scott (June 13, 1786 – May 29, 1866) was an American military commander and political candidate. He served as Commanding General of the United States Army from 1841 to 1861 — longer than any other officer — and fought in the War of 1812, the Mexican–American War, and the opening of the Civil War. Nicknamed "Old Fuss and Feathers" for his insistence on military formality, he was the Whig Party's nominee for president in 1852.

War of 1812

Scott became a brigadier general in 1814 and won distinction at the battles of Chippewa and Lundy's Lane on the Niagara frontier, where his trained regulars proved American troops could stand against British veterans. He later professionalized the Army with standardized drill manuals and camp hygiene.

Mexican–American War

In 1847 Scott conducted the first major American amphibious landing at Veracruz and marched inland, winning battles at Cerro Gordo, Contreras, Churubusco, and Chapultepec before capturing Mexico City — a campaign the Duke of Wellington praised as unsurpassed. The victory added vast territory to the United States.

Civil War and the Anaconda Plan

At 74, Scott remained in command at the outbreak of the Civil War in 1861. His strategic concept — a naval blockade of Southern ports combined with control of the Mississippi River, derided as the "Anaconda Plan" — anticipated the Union's eventual winning strategy. Infirm and at odds with younger generals, he retired in November 1861. He died at West Point in 1866, having served under fourteen presidents.
