HMS Birkenhead (1845)

Launched30 December 1845
TypeIron-hulled steam frigate / troopship
Tonnage1,918 tons (bm)
FateWrecked 26 February 1852

HMS Birkenhead, also referred to as HM Troopship Birkenhead or Steam Frigate Birkenhead, was one of the first iron-hulled ships built for the Royal Navy. She was designed as a steam frigate but was converted to a troop transport before being commissioned. Her wrecking in 1852, with the loss of hundreds of soldiers who stood fast on deck while women and children escaped in the boats, made her famous and established the "women and children first" protocol in maritime disasters.

The wreck

In January 1852 Birkenhead sailed from Simon's Town, South Africa, carrying troops to the Eighth Frontier War. In the early hours of 26 February 1852 she struck an uncharted rock off Danger Point, near Gansbaai, some 140 km from Cape Town. The ship broke up rapidly. Of the approximately 643 people aboard, only 193 survived; more than 440 soldiers and crew perished, many taken by sharks.

"Women and children first"

As the ship sank, the soldiers of the 73rd and other regiments were drawn up on deck by their officers and stood in ranks, refusing to rush the boats carrying the women and children or to swim ashore in a way that might swamp them. Their discipline — celebrated in Rudyard Kipling's poem "Soldier an' Sailor Too" ("to stand and be still to the Birken'ead drill") — gave rise to the "Birkenhead drill" as a byword for selfless discipline in disaster, and the wreck is often credited as the origin of the "women and children first" evacuation custom later seen on the Titanic.
