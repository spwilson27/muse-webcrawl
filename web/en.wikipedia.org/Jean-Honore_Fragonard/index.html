Jean-Honoré Fragonard

Jean-Honoré Fragonard (5 April 1732 – 22 August 1806) was a French painter and printmaker whose late Rococo manner was distinguished by remarkable facility, hedonism, and virtuoso brushwork. One of the most prolific artists of the ancien régime, he produced more than 550 paintings (not counting drawings and etchings). His most famous works include The Swing (c. 1767), the series The Progress of Love painted for Madame du Barry, and innumerable garden scenes, portraits, and decorative panels celebrating love and pleasure in the last decades before the French Revolution.

Life and training

Fragonard was born in Grasse, in Provence, the son of a glover and perfumer. The family moved to Paris when he was a child. After a brief placement in a notary's office, his artistic talent was recognised and he was apprenticed — first to Chardin, briefly, and then to François Boucher, whose exuberant style decisively shaped him. In 1752 he won the Prix de Rome with Jeroboam Sacrificing to the Idols, studied under Carle Vanloo, and spent 1756–1761 at the French Academy in Rome. In Italy he absorbed Tiepolo, the ruins and gardens of Rome, and — during a celebrated tour with his patron the Abbé de Saint-Non — developed the luminous landscape drawing that marks his maturity.

Mature career

Back in Paris, Fragonard's Coresus and Callirhoë was acclaimed at the Salon of 1765 and purchased by the Crown; he was agréé of the Academy. Rather than pursue official history painting, however, he turned to the private market of wealthy financiers and courtiers, painting mythologies, pastorals, portraits, and erotic cabinet pictures with unmatched brio. The Swing, commissioned (according to anecdote) by a courtier who wished to see his mistress from below as she swung, epitomises this frivolous, virtuoso manner. For Madame du Barry, Louis XV's mistress, he painted the four great canvases of The Progress of Love (1771–72) for the pavilion at Louveciennes — rejected by her in favour of neoclassical work by Joseph-Marie Vien, a sign of changing taste, and now the glory of the Frick Collection in New York.

Later years

Fragonard married Marie-Anne Gérard, herself a miniaturist, in 1769; his sister-in-law Marguerite Gérard became his pupil and collaborator. A second Italian journey (1773–74) produced superb drawings. As neoclassicism eclipsed the Rococo and the Revolution destroyed his clientele, Fragonard's fortunes collapsed; he served on the museum commission that safeguarded confiscated art, protected by Jacques-Louis David, but he died in Paris in 1806 in relative obscurity. His reputation revived in the nineteenth century through collectors such as the Wallace and Rothschild families and the advocacy of the Goncourt brothers, and he is now regarded as among the supreme painters of eighteenth-century France.

Style and legacy

Fragonard's art is characterised by sketch-like, scintillating brushwork, pearly flesh tones, feathery foliage, and compositions swirling with movement — virtuosity that contemporaries sometimes faulted as mere facility. His oeuvre spans altarpieces and history paintings, the intimate figures de fantaisie (rapid portrait-like studies), landscapes, and illustrations to Ariosto, Don Quixote, and La Fontaine's Contes. His paintings hang in the Louvre, the Wallace Collection, the Frick Collection, the National Gallery of Art in Washington, and many other major museums.
