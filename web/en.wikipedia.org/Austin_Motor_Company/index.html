Austin Motor Company

The Austin Motor Company Limited was a British manufacturer of motor vehicles, founded in 1905 by Herbert Austin in Longbridge near Birmingham. It merged with Morris Motors Limited in 1952, forming the British Motor Corporation (BMC), and its cars were thereafter produced as a marque within BMC, British Leyland and the Rover Group until the Austin name was retired in 1988.

The Austin Seven brought motoring to a mass market in Britain.

Early years

Herbert Austin had managed the Wolseley Sheep Shearing Machine Company's car venture before founding his own company at a former print works at Longbridge in 1905. Early Austin cars were large and expensive, but the firm's fortunes were transformed by the Austin Seven of 1922, a small, affordable car that dominated the British market, was built under licence abroad (as the basis of the first BMW car and of early Datsun and Rosengart models), and remained in production until 1939.

War and merger

Austin produced military vehicles, aircraft and munitions in both world wars; the Longbridge "shadow factory" built aircraft including the Fairey Battle and Lancaster. In 1952 Austin merged with its rival Morris to form BMC, then Britain's largest car maker. Famous Austin-badged products of the BMC era included the A30 and A40, the Austin-Healey sports cars built with the Donald Healey Motor Company, and from 1959 the revolutionary front-wheel-drive Mini, also sold as the Morris Mini-Minor.

Decline

Under British Leyland, Austin models included the 1100/1300, the Allegro and the Metro, the last a major success of the 1980s. The Austin marque was dropped in 1988 in favour of Rover branding. The Austin trademark later passed to Chinese owners of the MG Rover assets; the Longbridge plant ceased volume production in 2005.
