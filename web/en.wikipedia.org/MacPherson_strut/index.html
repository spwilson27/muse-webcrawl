The MacPherson strut is a type of automotive suspension system that uses the top of a telescopic damper as the upper steering pivot, combining the damper, coil spring and steering pivot in a single unit. It was developed by American engineer Earle S. MacPherson at General Motors in the 1940s and introduced on the French-built 1949 Ford Vedette; it became the most widely used front suspension on modern passenger cars, particularly front-wheel-drive designs.

Design

In its classic form, the strut runs from a body-mounted top bearing down to the steering knuckle, which it clamps or bolts to rigidly. The coil spring sits around the damper between spring seats, so the strut carries both spring and damping loads and also serves as a structural link locating the top of the wheel. Lateral location at the bottom is provided by a single lower control arm (wishbone or L-arm) with a ball joint at the knuckle; an anti-roll bar often doubles as a fore-and-aft locating link. Steering tie rods attach to arms on the strut or knuckle.

Because the strut must resist bending loads from cornering and braking, its piston rod is thicker than in a simple damper, and friction in the strut ("stiction") can slightly compromise ride quality. The geometry gives less favourable camber change in roll than a double-wishbone suspension, but it is compact (leaving room for transverse engines), light, cheap to manufacture, and leaves the wheel well unobstructed by an upper arm.

History

MacPherson developed the design at Chevrolet in the mid-1940s, drawing on aircraft oleo-strut practice, patenting it in 1947 (US Patent 2,624,592). After GM declined to adopt it for his light-car project, MacPherson moved to Ford, which introduced it on the 1949 Vedette in France and the 1951 Consul and Zephyr in Britain. It spread through the industry in the 1960s–70s (BMW, Mercedes-Benz, Volkswagen, Toyota and many others) and became near-universal on front-wheel-drive cars from the 1980s. Colin Chapman's simplified rear-suspension version on the Lotus Elite (1957) is known as the Chapman strut. Porsche used MacPherson struts on the 911 for decades, and high-end variants now employ refined "double-pivot" strut designs that separate the steering and suspension axes for better feel.

Advantages and disadvantages

  • Advantages: few parts and low cost; low unsprung mass relative to beam axles; compact packaging, especially across the car; good isolation when top mounts are tuned.
  • Disadvantages: camber gain in body roll is limited, capping ultimate cornering grip; loads feed directly into the body structure at the strut towers; damper friction and stiction; not suited to very heavy vehicles.
