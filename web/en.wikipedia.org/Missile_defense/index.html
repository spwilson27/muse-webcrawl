Missile defense

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Missile defense is a system, weapon, or technology involved in the detection, tracking, interception, and destruction of attacking missiles. Originally conceived as a system for countering nuclear-armed intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs), missile defense has expanded to include interception of shorter-range tactical and theatre missiles.

Categories

Missile defense can be classified by the phase of the target's flight in which interception occurs:

  • Boost phase – interception shortly after launch, while the missile's rockets are still firing.
  • Midcourse phase – interception in space during the ballistic arc, the longest phase for ICBMs.
  • Terminal phase – interception as the missile re-enters the atmosphere and approaches its target.

Defences are also divided by range into strategic systems (against ICBMs), theatre systems (against medium- and intermediate-range missiles), and tactical systems such as Patriot, which defend smaller areas against short-range missiles, aircraft and drones.

History

Early missile defense research began during the Cold War. The United States developed the Nike Zeus, Sentinel and Safeguard programs, while the Soviet Union deployed the A-35 system around Moscow. The 1972 Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty sharply limited strategic defences, fearing they would undermine deterrence. In 1983 President Ronald Reagan announced the Strategic Defense Initiative, popularly called "Star Wars", which funded research into space-based and directed-energy interceptors.

The United States withdrew from the ABM Treaty in 2002 and has since deployed the Ground-Based Midcourse Defense system in Alaska and California, ship-based Aegis ballistic missile defense, and THAAD batteries. Other states, including Russia, China, India and Israel, operate or develop their own systems; Israel's Iron Dome and Arrow systems have seen extensive combat use against rockets and ballistic missiles.

Effectiveness and criticism

Missile defence is technically demanding: intercepting a warhead travelling at many kilometres per second is often compared to "hitting a bullet with a bullet". Critics argue that countermeasures such as decoys and multiple warheads can overwhelm defences, that tests are often scripted, and that extensive defences may provoke arms races. Supporters argue that even imperfect defences complicate an attacker's planning and protect against limited or accidental launches.
