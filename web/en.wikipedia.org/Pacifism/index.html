Pacifism

Pacifism is the opposition or resistance to war, militarism (including conscription) or violence. Pacifists generally reject the use of violence as a means of achieving political or social goals, and many advocate for the peaceful resolution of conflicts and the abolition of war as an institution.

Terminology

The term was coined by the French peace campaigner Émile Arnaud (1864–1921) and adopted by other peace activists at the tenth Universal Peace Congress in Glasgow in 1901. In modern usage it ranges from absolute rejection of all violence, even in self-defence, to more pragmatic positions opposing particular wars.

History

Pacifist ideas appear in many religious traditions: the principle of ahimsa in Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism; the teachings of Jesus in the Sermon on the Mount; and the "peace testimony" of the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers). Historic peace churches such as the Mennonites, Amish and Church of the Brethren have maintained pacifist commitments since the Reformation era.

In the 19th and 20th centuries, figures such as Leo Tolstoy, Mahatma Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr. developed theories of nonviolent resistance that influenced civil rights and independence movements worldwide. During the First and Second World Wars and the Vietnam War, conscientious objectors asserted a legal and moral right to refuse military service.

Criticism

Critics argue that pacifism may be ineffective against aggressive regimes, that it can amount to moral free-riding on the protection of others, and that some violence — for example in resisting genocide or tyranny — may be justified under just war theory. Pacifists respond that nonviolent movements have historically succeeded at comparable or better rates than violent ones, and that the costs of war fall overwhelmingly on civilians.
