Anne Brontë

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Anne Brontë (1820–1849), portrait by Branwell Brontë

Anne Brontë (17 January 1820 – 28 May 1849) was an English novelist and poet, the youngest member of the Brontë literary family.

Anne Brontë was the daughter of Patrick Brontë, a poor Irish clergyman in the Church of England. Anne lived most of her life with her family at the parish in Haworth on the Yorkshire moors. In school, she also attended a boarding school in Mirfield between 1836 and 1837, and between 1839 and 1845 she worked as a governess for two English families, first the Inghams and then later the Robinsons, in Thorp Green Hall. In 1846 she published a book of poems with her sisters Charlotte and Emily titled Poems by Currer, Ellis and Acton Bell, under the pen name Acton Bell.

Novels

Her first novel, Agnes Grey, was published in 1847 at the same time as Emily's Wuthering Heights, drawing on her experiences as a governess. Her second and final novel, The Tenant of Wildfell Hall, which is considered to be one of the first feminist novels, appeared in 1848. It attacked the institution of marriage and depicted the alcoholism of Arthur Huntingdon with a frankness that scandalised some readers; Charlotte prevented its republication after Anne's death, which long damaged its author's reputation.

Like her poems, both her novels were also published under the name Acton Bell. Anne's life was cut short when she died of what is now suspected to be pulmonary tuberculosis — the same disease that killed at least four of her siblings — at the age of 29. She died at Scarborough, where she had gone hoping the sea air would help her, and is buried in St Mary's churchyard there.

Legacy

Modern scholarship has restored Anne Brontë's standing: The Tenant of Wildfell Hall is now studied for its moral realism, its defence of women's independence, and its influence on later feminist writing.
