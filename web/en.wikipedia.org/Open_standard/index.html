Open standard

Open standard
DefinitionA standard that is publicly available and developed or maintained through an open, consensus-based process
ExamplesHTML, TCP/IP, USB, PDF (ISO 32000), SQL, POSIX
Key bodiesIETF, W3C, ISO, IEEE, OASIS, Ecma International
Related conceptsFree and open-source software, open data, interoperability, FRAND licensing

An open standard is a standard that is openly accessible and usable by anyone. It is also a prerequisite for interoperability: systems built to the same open standard can work together without proprietary gatekeepers. There is no single universally accepted definition, but most definitions share core criteria — the specification is publicly available, the development process is open to participation, and implementation is permitted on royalty-free or fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory (FRAND) terms.

Definitions

The Internet Society and the IETF emphasise rough consensus, running code and voluntary adoption. The World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) requires a transparent, participatory process and royalty-free patent commitments for its Recommendations. The European Union's European Interoperability Framework stresses availability at nominal or no cost and irrevocable royalty-free access to essential intellectual property, while standards bodies such as ISO and ITU allow standards that are merely "available" and licensable on FRAND terms, prompting continuing debate about how open such standards are.

Characteristics

Commonly cited characteristics include: open participation in development; consensus-based decision-making; public review of drafts; transparent finances and governance; availability of the final specification for free or at low cost; no discrimination against persons, groups or fields of endeavour; and the right to implement the standard without royalty payments for essential patents, or under FRAND terms.

Examples and impact

The internet itself rests on open standards: the TCP/IP protocols, DNS, HTTP and HTML are all maintained in open processes, and their royalty-free character is widely credited with enabling the web's explosive growth. Other major examples include Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11), USB, Bluetooth, the PDF format (standardised as ISO 32000) and office document formats such as ODF. Open standards are also mandated in many government procurement policies to avoid vendor lock-in.

Criticism and tensions

Disputes arise over patent ambush (concealing essential patents during standardisation), the compatibility of FRAND licensing with free and open-source software, and the cost of ISO standards documents. Some proprietary formats become de facto standards through market dominance without an open process, a situation open-standards advocates criticise as anti-competitive.
