Neologism

A neologism (from Greek neo- "new" and logos "word") is a relatively recent term, word or phrase that may be in the process of entering common use but has not yet been fully accepted into mainstream language. Neologisms arise from popular culture, technology, politics, marketing and literature, and are often directly attributable to a specific person, publication or event.

Formation

New words are created by many processes: compounding (blogosphere), blending or portmanteau (brunch, smog, Brexit), derivation with affixes, borrowing from other languages, abbreviation and acronymy (laser, scuba — now so established their coinage is forgotten), and semantic shift, where an old word takes a new sense (mouse, viral).

Life cycle

Lexicographers sometimes distinguish stages: protologisms freshly coined by an individual, neologisms that have spread but remain novel, and established words fully absorbed into the lexicon. Many coinages flare briefly and vanish; others, such as robot (from Karel Čapek's 1920 play R.U.R.), quark (Murray Gell-Mann, borrowing from Joyce), or Shakespeare's numerous coinages, become permanent. Dictionaries monitor corpora and add neologisms once they show sustained, widespread use — a process tracked publicly through "word of the year" selections by bodies such as the Oxford English Dictionary and Merriam-Webster.

In psychiatry and theology

In psychiatry, "neologism" denotes idiosyncratic words invented by a patient, a feature sometimes associated with schizophrenia-spectrum thought disorder. In theology, the term has been used for new doctrines or interpretations regarded as innovations.
