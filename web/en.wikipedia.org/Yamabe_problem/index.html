The Yamabe problem asks for constant scalar curvature within a conformal class.

The Yamabe problem is a celebrated question in differential geometry, named after the Japanese mathematician Hidehiko Yamabe. It asks whether, given a smooth compact Riemannian manifold of dimension at least three, every conformal class of Riemannian metrics contains a metric with constant scalar curvature. The answer is yes.

History of the solution

Yamabe proposed the problem in 1960 and claimed a solution that was found to contain a gap, repaired by Neil Trudinger in 1968 for the case of non-positive Yamabe invariant. Thierry Aubin in 1976 settled most remaining cases by comparison with the round sphere. The final, most difficult case was completed by Richard Schoen in 1984 using the positive mass theorem from general relativity — a landmark interaction between geometric analysis and mathematical physics.

The Yamabe equation

The problem reduces to solving a nonlinear elliptic partial differential equation, the Yamabe equation, for a positive conformal factor. The analytic difficulty comes from a critical Sobolev exponent, which causes a loss of compactness illustrated by "bubbling" phenomena; much of modern geometric analysis grew out of techniques developed for the Yamabe problem and its relatives, including work on the prescribed scalar curvature problem and the Yamabe flow.
