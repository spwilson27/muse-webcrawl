East Palestine, Ohio, train derailment

The East Palestine, Ohio, train derailment was a railway accident that occurred on February 3, 2023, when a Norfolk Southern freight train derailed in the village of East Palestine, Ohio, United States, near the border with Pennsylvania. The train included tank cars carrying hazardous materials, including vinyl chloride, butyl acrylate, ethylhexyl acrylate, and ethylene glycol monobutyl ether. Several cars burned for more than two days, and on February 6 emergency crews conducted a controlled release and burn of the vinyl chloride from five tank cars to prevent a catastrophic explosion, sending a large plume of black smoke over the area.

Background

East Palestine is a village of about 4,700 residents in Columbiana County, Ohio, located roughly 50 miles (80 km) northwest of Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. The derailed train, Norfolk Southern train 32N, was traveling from Madison, Illinois, to Conway, Pennsylvania, and consisted of two locomotives, 141 loaded cars, and nine empty cars. Twenty of the cars carried hazardous materials.

Derailment

At approximately 8:55 p.m. on February 3, 2023, 38 cars of the train derailed near the center of the village. Surveillance video from a business about 20 miles (32 km) before the derailment site showed a wheel bearing on one of the cars glowing and throwing sparks. A wayside defect detector passed by the train had transmitted an alarm about an overheated bearing shortly before the derailment, and the crew applied the brakes, but the axle failed as the train slowed. Eleven of the derailed cars contained hazardous materials, and a fire ignited among the wreckage.

Response and controlled burn

Residents within a one-mile radius of the crash site were evacuated. Fearing that the vinyl chloride cars could explode and send shrapnel over a wide area, Ohio governor Mike DeWine ordered an expanded evacuation, and on February 6 responders breached the five vinyl chloride tank cars and burned off their contents in a controlled manner, producing a dense column of dark smoke visible for miles. The evacuation order was lifted on February 8 after air monitoring indicated readings below levels of concern.

Environmental and health concerns

In the weeks after the derailment, residents reported symptoms including headaches, rashes, and sore throats, and several thousand fish and other aquatic animals were found dead in nearby creeks. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency ordered Norfolk Southern to conduct and pay for the cleanup, and monitoring of air, soil, and water continued under federal and state oversight. The incident prompted national debate over railway safety, the classification of hazardous-materials trains, and the adequacy of bearing defect detectors.

Aftermath and litigation

Norfolk Southern removed contaminated soil and water from the site and provided financial assistance to residents and the village. In 2024 the company agreed to a settlement of approximately $600 million to resolve class-action claims from residents and businesses, and a separate consent agreement with the federal government covering cleanup costs and penalties. The National Transportation Safety Board's final investigation attributed the derailment to the overheated wheel bearing and criticized the decision to vent and burn the vinyl chloride as unnecessary, concluding the cars were cooling and posed a lower explosion risk than responders had been told.
