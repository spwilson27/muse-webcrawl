Attitude (psychology)

In psychology, an attitude is a psychological construct — a mental and emotional entity that inheres in, or characterizes, a person — expressed by how favorably or unfavorably a person evaluates a particular entity (a person, object, idea, or event). Attitudes are a core topic in social psychology.

Components

The influential ABC model describes attitudes as having three components: affective (feelings toward the object), behavioral (tendencies to act), and cognitive (beliefs and knowledge). Attitudes may be explicit (deliberate and reportable) or implicit (automatic and unconscious), the latter often measured with the Implicit Association Test.

Formation and change

Attitudes form through experience, social learning, and persuasion. Theories of attitude change include Leon Festinger's cognitive dissonance theory, which holds that inconsistency between attitudes and behaviour produces discomfort that motivates change, and the elaboration likelihood model, which distinguishes central (argument-based) from peripheral (cue-based) routes to persuasion.

Attitudes and behaviour

Research such as the theory of planned behavior (Icek Ajzen) shows attitudes predict behaviour best when combined with subjective norms and perceived behavioural control, and when measured at the same level of specificity as the behaviour.
