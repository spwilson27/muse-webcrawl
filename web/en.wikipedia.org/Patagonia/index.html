Patagonia is a vast, sparsely populated region at the southern end of South America, shared by Argentina and Chile. It comprises the southern section of the Andes Mountains, with its lakes, fjords and glaciers, and the deserts, steppes and grasslands of the Argentine plateau to the east. Its conventional northern boundary is formed by the Colorado and Barrancas rivers in Argentina and the Reloncaví Estuary in Chile; to the south it reaches Tierra del Fuego and Cape Horn.

Geography and climate

The Andes dominate the west, with peaks such as Monte San Valentín and the volcanoes of the Southern Patagonian Ice Field — the largest expanse of ice in the Southern Hemisphere outside Antarctica — feeding glaciers including the Perito Moreno Glacier. The mountains wring moisture from Pacific winds, so Chilean Patagonia is wet and densely forested, while Argentine Patagonia, in the rain shadow, is arid steppe cut by river valleys and the plateaus called mesetas.

History

The region was inhabited for more than ten thousand years before Europeans arrived, by peoples including the Tehuelche of the steppe and the Mapuche of the northwest. Ferdinand Magellan's expedition of 1520 gave the region its name, traditionally explained as "land of the big feet" — the patagones, supposedly a race of giants. In the nineteenth century both Argentina and Chile moved to occupy the region: Argentina's "Conquest of the Desert" campaigns of the 1870s–80s destroyed the independence of the indigenous peoples of the pampas and northern Patagonia, while Welsh immigrants settled in the Chubut Valley from 1865, leaving a lasting Welsh-speaking community. Sheep ranching brought great estancias, and oil was later discovered near Comodoro Rivadavia.

Economy and wildlife

Today Patagonia's economy rests on sheep and cattle ranching, petroleum and gas, hydroelectricity, fishing and a growing tourism industry centred on national parks such as Los Glaciares and Torres del Paine. Its wildlife includes guanacos, rheas, pumas, Andean condors, right whales breeding at the Valdés Peninsula, and Magellanic penguins — a fauna that makes the region one of the world's great nature destinations.
