Gear

A gear or gearwheel is a rotating machine part with teeth that mesh with another toothed part to transmit torque and motion. Gears are used to change the speed, torque and direction of rotation between shafts, and form the basis of gearboxes and transmissions in machines from clocks to automobiles to wind turbines.

Principles

When two gears mesh, the ratio of their rotational speeds is the inverse ratio of their numbers of teeth. A small gear (pinion) driving a larger gear reduces speed while multiplying torque; the reverse arrangement increases speed. Because the teeth engage positively, there is no slip, unlike in belt or friction drives. The gear ratio, pitch circles, module and pressure angle are the fundamental parameters of gear design.

Types of gears

  • Spur gears: straight teeth parallel to the axis; the simplest and most common type.
  • Helical gears: teeth cut at an angle, giving smoother, quieter running at the cost of axial thrust.
  • Bevel gears: conical gears that transmit motion between intersecting shafts.
  • Worm gears: a screw-like worm driving a wheel, giving large reductions in one stage.
  • Rack and pinion: converts rotation into linear motion, as in car steering.
  • Planetary (epicyclic) gears: sun, planet and ring gears used in automatic transmissions and hub gears.
  • Internal gears, hypoid gears, herringbone gears and other specialised forms.

History

Toothed wheels appear in ancient China and in Hellenistic engineering — the Antikythera mechanism (c. 100 BC) contains an elaborate train of bronze gears. Leonardo da Vinci sketched many gear arrangements, and gears became central to clocks, mills and steam engines during the industrial revolution. Modern practice favours involute tooth profiles, standardised since the nineteenth century, which tolerate small errors in shaft spacing while maintaining a constant velocity ratio.

Manufacture and materials

Gears are made from steels, cast iron, brass and engineering plastics by hobbing, shaping, milling, broaching, powder metallurgy and, increasingly, additive manufacturing. Wear, lubrication, noise and tooth failure modes — pitting, bending fatigue and scuffing — govern design.
