Analogy (biology)

In biology, an analogy is a functional similarity between structures in different organisms that is not due to shared ancestry but rather to convergent evolution. Analogous structures perform similar functions but evolved independently in separate lineages. The term contrasts with homology, in which similar structures arise from a common ancestor.

Examples

The wings of birds, bats, and insects are classic analogous structures: all are used for flight, yet they evolved independently and have entirely different anatomical constructions. Similarly, the streamlined bodies and fins of dolphins (mammals) and sharks (fish) are analogous adaptations to aquatic life. The camera-like eyes of vertebrates and cephalopods such as the octopus are another frequently cited example.

Convergent evolution

Analogous traits arise through convergent evolution, in which unrelated organisms facing similar environmental pressures evolve similar solutions. Marsupial and placental mammals provide many examples, such as the marsupial sugar glider and the placental flying squirrel, which independently evolved gliding membranes.

Importance

Distinguishing analogy from homology is essential in phylogenetics and cladistics, because only homologous characters reliably indicate evolutionary relationships. Analogous characters, if mistaken for homologies, can mislead reconstructions of the tree of life.
