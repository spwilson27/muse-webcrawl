Giorgio Agamben

Giorgio Agamben (born 22 April 1942) is an Italian philosopher best known for his work investigating the concepts of the state of exception, form-of-life, and the figure of homo sacer. His work draws on and engages with thinkers including Aristotle, Michel Foucault, Walter Benjamin, Carl Schmitt, Martin Heidegger, and Hannah Arendt.

Agamben studied law and philosophy at the University of Rome, where he wrote a thesis on Simone Weil, and participated in Heidegger's seminars at Le Thor. He edited Italian editions of Benjamin's works and taught at several universities, including the University of Verona, the University of Macerata, and the Università Iuav di Venezia, as well as holding visiting positions abroad.

The Homo Sacer project

His best-known work is the multi-volume Homo Sacer project, begun with Homo Sacer: Sovereign Power and Bare Life (1995). In it Agamben revives the archaic Roman legal figure of the homo sacer — a person who may be killed but not sacrificed — to analyse how sovereign power operates by including life in the juridical order precisely through its exclusion, and he argues that the concentration camp, rather than the city, is the hidden paradigm of modern politics. Later volumes include State of Exception (2003), The Kingdom and the Glory (2007), Opus Dei (2012), and The Use of Bodies (2014), which conclude the series.

Other work and reception

Agamben's earlier books include The Man Without Content (1970), Stanzas (1977), Language and Death (1982), The Coming Community (1990), and Profanations (2005). His ideas have been highly influential in political theory, literary studies, and the humanities. During the COVID-19 pandemic, his critical writings about emergency measures and the Italian government's response attracted wide attention and controversy.
