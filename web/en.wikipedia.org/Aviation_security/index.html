Aviation security

ScopeAirports, aircraft, passengers, cargo
International bodyICAO (Annex 17)
US authorityTransportation Security Administration (TSA)

Aviation security is the combination of measures and human and material resources intended to safeguard civil aviation against acts of unlawful interference, including terrorism, sabotage, hijacking, and other criminal acts.

History

Security screening expanded sharply after the hijacking wave of the late 1960s and early 1970s, and again after the bombing of Pan Am Flight 103 over Lockerbie in 1988. The September 11 attacks of 2001 transformed the field: in the United States the Transportation Security Administration was created and screening was federalized, while cockpit doors were hardened and air marshal programs expanded worldwide.

Measures

Common measures include passenger and baggage screening with metal detectors, X-ray and CT scanners, and trace explosive detection; identity verification; airport access control and perimeter security; cargo screening; and intelligence-led programs such as watch lists and passenger name record analysis. The International Civil Aviation Organization sets global standards in Annex 17 to the Chicago Convention.

Criticism

Aviation security is debated over cost-effectiveness, privacy, passenger inconvenience, and profiling concerns; critics label some visible measures "security theatre", while officials argue layered, intelligence-driven systems deter and detect attacks.
