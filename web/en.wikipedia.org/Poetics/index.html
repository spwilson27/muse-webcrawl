Aristotle's Poetics founded the systematic study of literary form.

Poetics is the study of the theory of literary discourse, examining the structures, devices and conventions by which literature — originally poetry and drama, now literature generally — produces its effects. Distinct from practical criticism of individual works, poetics asks general questions: what makes a text literary, how genres work, and how narrative, metre, figure and voice generate meaning.

Aristotle

The discipline begins with Aristotle's Poetics (c. 335 BCE), a treatise analysing tragedy and epic. Aristotle defined poetry as mimesis (imitation of action), identified the six elements of tragedy — plot (mythos), character, thought, diction, melody and spectacle — ranked plot first, and described catharsis, the purging or clarification of pity and fear. His concepts of reversal (peripeteia), recognition (anagnorisis) and the flaw (hamartia) have structured dramatic theory ever since. The surviving text, focused on tragedy, was immensely influential on Renaissance and neoclassical drama and rules such as the three unities.

Later traditions

Indian poetics developed its own sophisticated theory in Sanskrit treatises on rasa (aesthetic emotion), and classical Arabic, Persian, Chinese and Japanese traditions elaborated distinct poetics. In the West, Horace's Ars Poetica, medieval rhetoric, and Romantic expressive theory followed. Twentieth-century movements reshaped the field: Russian Formalism emphasised "literariness" and defamiliarisation (Shklovsky); structuralist poetics (Todorov, Genette) systematised narrative; and reader-response, deconstructive and cognitive poetics turned attention to how readers construct meaning.

Usage

The term is also used more loosely for the distinctive principles of a particular poet, period or genre — "the poetics of modernism" — and, beyond literature, for the poetics of film, space or everyday life.
