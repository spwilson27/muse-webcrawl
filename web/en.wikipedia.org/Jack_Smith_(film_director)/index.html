Jack Smith (November 14, 1932 – September 25, 1989) was an American filmmaker, actor, and pioneer of underground cinema. He is widely acknowledged as a founding figure of American underground film and an important influence on the camp aesthetic, performance art, and the work of later artists including Andy Warhol and John Waters.

Flaming Creatures

Smith's best-known film, Flaming Creatures (1963), is a 43-minute black-and-white collage of costumed, sexually ambiguous performers in exotic, improvised scenes. The film was seized by police at screenings and became the center of a celebrated obscenity case; copies were confiscated and screenings banned in many jurisdictions. Critic Jonas Mekas and others defended the film as a work of art, and it became a landmark in debates over censorship and freedom of expression.

Later work

Smith's other major film, Normal Love (1963), featured performers including Mario Montez, a frequent collaborator. In later years Smith increasingly worked in live performance and slide-show pieces in his New York loft, resisting the completion and commercial distribution of films. He died of AIDS-related pneumonia in 1989.

Legacy

Smith's aesthetic — glamorous, threadbare, deliberately "trashy" spectacle — helped define camp and influenced generations of experimental filmmakers, drag performers, and visual artists.
