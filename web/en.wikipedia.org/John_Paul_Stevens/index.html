John Paul Stevens (April 20, 1920 – July 16, 2019) was an American lawyer and jurist who served as an associate justice of the Supreme Court of the United States from 1975 to 2010. Nominated by Republican President Gerald Ford, he became the de facto leader of the Court's liberal wing during his later years and was, at retirement, the third-longest-serving justice in its history.

Early life and career

Stevens was born in Chicago into a wealthy family that built the Stevens Hotel. He studied at the University of Chicago and, during the Second World War, served as a Navy intelligence officer working on code-breaking, for which he received a Bronze Star. After graduating first in his class from Northwestern University School of Law in 1947, he clerked for Justice Wiley Rutledge, practised antitrust law in Chicago, and in 1970 was appointed to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit.

Supreme Court

President Ford appointed Stevens in 1975 to succeed William O. Douglas; the Senate confirmed him 98–0. Over 35 years he wrote notable opinions across the law, including the majority in Chevron U.S.A. v. Natural Resources Defense Council (1984), establishing deference to agency interpretations, and in Clinton v. Jones (1997). His major dissents included Texas v. Johnson (1989) on flag burning and Citizens United v. FEC (2010), and he dissented in Bush v. Gore (2000) with the famous closing line that the loser was "the Nation's confidence in the judge as an impartial guardian of the rule of law."

Retirement and death

Stevens retired on June 29, 2010, succeeded by Elena Kagan. In retirement he wrote three books, including a memoir, The Making of a Justice (2019), and proposed six constitutional amendments in Six Amendments (2014). He died in Fort Lauderdale in 2019 at age 99.
