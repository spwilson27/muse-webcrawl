Siberia (/saɪˈbɪəriə/; Russian: Сибирь, Sibir') is an extensive geographical region comprising all of North Asia, from the Ural Mountains in the west to the Pacific Ocean in the east. It has formed part of Russia since the latter half of the 16th century, after the Russians conquered lands east of the Urals. Siberia is vast, accounting for about 77% of Russia's total area but only about a quarter of its population.

The name derives from the Tatar word Sibir, the name of a fortress at the confluence of the Tobol and Irtysh rivers. The region is famed for its long, harsh winters, with January averages around −25 °C (−13 °F), and for extremes such as −67.7 °C recorded in the Sakha Republic village of Oymyakon, one of the coldest inhabited places on Earth.

Geography

Siberia stretches from the Arctic Ocean in the north to the borders of Kazakhstan, Mongolia and China in the south. It encompasses the West Siberian Plain, one of the largest lowland plains in the world; the Central Siberian Plateau; and the mountainous regions of the south and east. Major rivers — the Ob, Yenisey, Lena and Amur — drain northward to the Arctic Ocean. Lake Baikal, in southern Siberia, is the world's deepest and oldest freshwater lake and holds roughly a fifth of the world's unfrozen surface fresh water.

Most of Siberia is covered by taiga (boreal coniferous forest); to the north lies treeless tundra, and to the south, steppe. Permafrost underlies much of the region.

History

Siberia was inhabited by various nomadic peoples — including the Enets, Nenets, Huns, Scythians and Uyghurs. In 630 the area around modern Tobolsk was the capital of the Khanate of Sibir. Russian expansion began in 1580–1582, when Cossack leader Yermak Timofeyevich defeated the khanate. Within eighty years Russian traders and Cossacks had reached the Pacific. The region later became a destination for exile under both the Tsarist katorga system and the Soviet Gulag.

The construction of the Trans-Siberian Railway (1891–1916) opened Siberia to settlement and industry. In the 20th century the Soviet Union developed Siberian oil, gas, coal and mineral resources, building industrial cities such as Norilsk, Yakutsk and Krasnoyarsk.

Economy

Siberia is extraordinarily rich in natural resources: it contains most of Russia's oil and natural gas (West Siberian fields such as Samotlor), diamonds (Yakutia), nickel (Norilsk), coal (Kuznetsk Basin), timber, gold and aluminium smelting powered by huge hydroelectric dams on the Yenisey and Angara rivers.

Cities

Major cities include Novosibirsk (the largest), Omsk, Krasnoyarsk, Barnaul, Irkutsk, Khabarovsk, Vladivostok (in the Far East, sometimes counted separately), Tyumen, Yakutsk and Novokuznetsk.
