Dover

Dover is a town and major ferry port in Kent, England, at the closest point of England to continental Europe: the Strait of Dover here separates England from France by only about 21 miles (34 km). It is famous for its white chalk cliffs, its medieval castle — one of England's largest — and its role as a historic gateway between Britain and Europe. Dover was one of the original Cinque Ports.

History

Dover's sheltered valley of the River Dour, at the narrowest crossing to Gaul, made it a port from prehistory: a Bronze Age boat found preserved in the town dates to about 1500 BC. The Romans developed Dubris as a major port with two lighthouses and a Saxon Shore fort; the surviving Roman lighthouse (pharos) in the castle grounds is among the oldest buildings in Britain. The town became a founding member of the Cinque Ports confederation. Henry II began the great stone keep of Dover Castle, and the castle later withstood a famous siege in the First Barons' War (1216–17). During the Second World War Dover, in "Hellfire Corner", was shelled from France, and the evacuation of troops from Dunkirk in 1940 was directed from tunnels beneath the castle.

Port and transport

The Port of Dover is one of the busiest ferry ports in the world, handling millions of passengers and much of the UK's trade in goods with the EU on ferries to Calais and Dunkirk; the Channel Tunnel terminal lies nearby at Folkestone/Cheriton. The A2 and A20 roads and rail services connect Dover to London and Canterbury.

Landmarks

Dover Castle, the largest castle in England, crowns the cliffs above the port. The White Cliffs of Dover, sheer chalk faces rising over 100 metres, are an iconic symbol of Britain. Other sites include the Roman Painted House, St Mary's Church, the Maison Dieu, and the Western Heights fortifications.
