Georgia Totto O'Keeffe (15 November 1887 – 6 March 1986) was an American modernist artist. She was known for her paintings of enlarged flowers, New York skyscrapers, and New Mexico landscapes, and has been called the "Mother of American modernism".

Early life and training

O'Keeffe was born on a farm near Sun Prairie, Wisconsin, the second of seven children. She studied at the School of the Art Institute of Chicago and the Art Students League of New York, and was influenced by the ideas of her teacher Arthur Wesley Dow, who encouraged art as personal expression rather than imitation. In 1915, teaching in South Carolina, she produced a set of abstract charcoal drawings that marked a decisive break with her academic training.

New York years

Photographer and gallery owner Alfred Stieglitz exhibited O'Keeffe's drawings in 1916 and gave her a solo show in 1917; they married in 1924. In New York she painted both the city's skyscrapers and, most famously, large-scale close-ups of flowers such as Black Iris (1926) and Oriental Poppies (1927), which made her one of the best-known American artists of the 1920s.

New Mexico

O'Keeffe first visited New Mexico in 1929 and was captivated by its desert landscapes, adobe architecture and bleached animal bones, which became central subjects — in paintings such as Cow's Skull: Red, White, and Blue (1931) and Ram's Head White Hollyhock and Little Hills (1935). After Stieglitz's death in 1946 she settled permanently at Abiquiú and Ghost Ranch, painting the Cerro Pedernal mountain again and again.

Later life and legacy

In her seventies O'Keeffe travelled widely and painted cloudscapes seen from aeroplane windows. Failing eyesight curtailed her painting in the 1970s; she died at Santa Fe in 1986 at the age of 98. The Georgia O'Keeffe Museum opened in Santa Fe in 1997. In 2014 her Jimson Weed/White Flower No. 1 sold for $44.4 million, then a record for a work by a woman artist.
