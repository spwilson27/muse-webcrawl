Tachykinin peptides

Tachykinin peptides are one of the largest families of neuropeptides, found from invertebrates to mammals. Their name derives from their ability to rapidly (Greek tachys, swift) induce contraction of intestinal smooth muscle, in contrast to the slower-acting bradykinin. All tachykinins share a conserved C-terminal sequence, Phe–X–Gly–Leu–Met–NH2, which is essential for receptor activation.

Substance P, an 11-amino-acid tachykinin, was the first member of the family isolated.

Members and discovery

The prototype, substance P, was detected in 1931 by von Euler and Gaddum in extracts of equine brain and gut, and sequenced in 1971. Subsequent members include neurokinin A and neurokinin B, the frog-skin peptides kassinin and physalaemin, and the invertebrate tachykinin-related peptides. In mammals, the TAC1 gene encodes substance P and neurokinin A; TAC3 encodes neurokinin B; TAC4 encodes hemokinin-1 and the endokinins.

Receptors and physiology

Tachykinins act on three G protein–coupled receptors: NK1 (preferring substance P), NK2 (neurokinin A), and NK3 (neurokinin B). They function in pain transmission, neurogenic inflammation, smooth-muscle contraction, salivation, mood regulation, and the control of vomiting. Substance P released from sensory nerve endings is a central mediator of nociception and plasma extravasation.

Clinical relevance

Antagonists of the NK1 receptor — aprepitant and its prodrug fosaprepitant — are established antiemetics for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting. NK3 antagonism (fezolinetant) has been developed for menopausal vasomotor symptoms arising from neurokinin B signalling in the hypothalamus. Tachykinin pathways have also been investigated in depression, asthma, and inflammatory bowel disease.
