Galicia (Galician: Galicia or Galiza; Spanish: Galicia) is an autonomous community of Spain and a historic nationality under Spanish law. Located in the north-west Iberian Peninsula, it comprises the provinces of A Coruña, Lugo, Ourense and Pontevedra, borders Portugal to the south and the Atlantic Ocean to the west and north, and has its capital at Santiago de Compostela.

Geography

Galicia is characterised by its rías — flooded river valleys that cut deeply into the Atlantic coast, divided into the Rías Altas and Rías Baixas — and by a green, hilly interior often compared with Ireland or Brittany. The Miño is the principal river; the highest point is Pena Trevinca (2,127 m). The climate is oceanic and notably wet, and the region is prone to forest fires in dry summers.

History

The Roman province of Gallaecia took its name from the Celtic Gallaeci. Galicia later formed a Suebic kingdom, then part of Visigothic and later the Asturian-Leonese realms. The discovery of the tomb attributed to the apostle Saint James in the 9th century made Santiago de Compostela one of medieval Christendom's greatest pilgrimage destinations; the Camino de Santiago still ends there. In the 20th century Galicia experienced heavy emigration to the Americas and Europe; its statute of autonomy was approved in 1981.

Language and culture

Galician, a Romance language closely related to Portuguese, is co-official and spoken by most of the population, though Spanish predominates in cities. Galician culture is marked by Celtic resonances — the gaita (bagpipes) is the emblematic instrument — and by a literary revival (the Rexurdimento) led by Rosalía de Castro. The economy relies on fishing and seafood, dairy and beef farming, shipbuilding, textiles (Inditex, owner of Zara, is headquartered at Arteixo), and tourism.
