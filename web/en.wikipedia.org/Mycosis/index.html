Mycosis

Mycosis, also known as a fungal infection, is an infectious disease caused by pathogenic fungi that invades the tissues, causing superficial, subcutaneous, or systemic disease. An example of a mycosis caused by an opportunistic pathogen is candidiasis, which can occur in immunocompromised hosts.

Classification

Mycoses are commonly classified by the level of tissue initially colonised. Superficial mycoses, such as athlete's foot and ringworm, affect the skin and its appendages. Cutaneous mycoses invade the keratinised layers of skin, hair, and nails. Subcutaneous mycoses involve the dermis and deeper tissues, often following traumatic inoculation. Systemic mycoses originate primarily in the lungs and can spread to other organ systems; examples include histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, blastomycosis, and invasive aspergillosis.

Causes and risk factors

Pathogenic fungi include dermatophytes, yeasts such as Candida and Cryptococcus, and moulds such as Aspergillus. Risk factors include immunosuppression (HIV/AIDS, chemotherapy, organ transplantation), prolonged antibiotic use, diabetes, and environmental exposure to fungal spores.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis relies on microscopy, culture, histopathology, and increasingly molecular methods. Treatment uses antifungal medications — including azoles, polyenes such as amphotericin B, and echinocandins — chosen according to the site and severity of infection.
