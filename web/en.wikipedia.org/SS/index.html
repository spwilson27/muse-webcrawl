The Schutzstaffel (SS; stylised ᛋᛋ) was a major paramilitary organisation under Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party in Nazi Germany. Founded in 1925 as a small bodyguard unit for Hitler, it grew under Heinrich Himmler into one of the most powerful and feared organisations in the Third Reich, encompassing the regime's police apparatus, the concentration camp system and the military Waffen-SS.

Rise to power

Himmler, appointed Reichsführer-SS in January 1929, expanded the SS from fewer than 300 men to hundreds of thousands by the outbreak of war. The SS played a decisive role in the Night of the Long Knives (1934), in which the rival SA leadership was murdered, and thereafter absorbed control of the German police, including the Gestapo. Membership was screened on racial criteria, and the organisation cultivated an elite self-image bound by the motto Meine Ehre heißt Treue ("My honour is loyalty").

Role in the Holocaust

The SS was the principal agency responsible for the Holocaust. Its SS-Totenkopfverbände administered the concentration and extermination camps; its Einsatzgruppen mobile killing squads murdered more than a million people, mostly Jews, in occupied Eastern Europe; and the SS Main Economic and Administrative Office exploited slave labour across the camp system. Under Reinhard Heydrich, head of the Reich Security Main Office, the SS coordinated the "Final Solution" formalised at the Wannsee Conference in January 1942.

Structure

The SS comprised three main elements: the Allgemeine SS (general SS), the political and administrative corps; the Waffen-SS, armed military formations that fought alongside the Wehrmacht and grew to some 38 divisions; and the SS-Totenkopfverbände, which ran the camps. The SS also controlled a large business empire and, through the Volksdeutsche Mittelstelle and allied offices, Germanisation programmes in occupied Europe.

Aftermath

At the Nuremberg trials, the International Military Tribunal declared the SS a criminal organisation. Himmler committed suicide in British custody in May 1945. Many SS members were prosecuted for war crimes, although others escaped through ratlines or were recruited during the early Cold War.
