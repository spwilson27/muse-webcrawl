The Lockheed U-2, nicknamed the "Dragon Lady", is an American single-engine, high-altitude reconnaissance aircraft operated by the United States Air Force and previously flown by the Central Intelligence Agency. Developed in secrecy by Lockheed's Skunk Works under Kelly Johnson in the 1950s, it provides day-and-night, all-weather intelligence gathering at altitudes above 70,000 feet (21,300 m).

Development

The U-2 was designed to photograph the Soviet Union from altitudes then believed to be beyond the reach of Soviet fighters and missiles. It first flew in August 1955 at Groom Lake (Area 51), Nevada, and began operational overflights in 1956. Its long, glider-like wings make it efficient at extreme altitude but difficult to fly: at cruising height the margin between stall speed and maximum speed — the "coffin corner" — can be only a few knots, and the aircraft's bicycle landing gear and delicate handling make landings famously demanding, assisted by chase cars on the runway.

1960 incident

On May 1, 1960, a U-2 flown by Francis Gary Powers was shot down over the Soviet Union by an S-75 surface-to-air missile. Powers was captured, the incident wrecked a Paris summit meeting, and the Eisenhower administration's cover story collapsed when the Soviets displayed the wreckage. Powers was exchanged for the Soviet spy Rudolf Abel in 1962.

Later service

U-2s photographed Soviet missiles in Cuba during the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 (Major Rudolf Anderson Jr. was killed when his U-2 was shot down), and later variants — the enlarged U-2R and the modernised U-2S — have served in conflicts and monitoring missions from Vietnam to Iraq, Afghanistan and Ukraine-era surveillance. NASA flies the civilian ER-2 variant for high-altitude Earth science. Despite repeated plans to retire it in favour of the unmanned RQ-4 Global Hawk and new classified systems, the U-2 remains in service into the 2020s, decades after its introduction.
