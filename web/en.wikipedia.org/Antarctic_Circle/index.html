Antarctic Circle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The Antarctic Circle marked on a map of Antarctica

The Antarctic Circle is the most southerly of the five major circles of latitude that mark maps of Earth. The region south of this circle is known as the Antarctic, and the zone immediately to the north is called the Southern Temperate Zone. South of the Antarctic Circle, the Sun is above the horizon for 24 continuous hours at least once per year (and the centre of the Sun is visible even at midnight) and the Sun is below the horizon for 24 continuous hours at least once per year; this is also true within the Arctic Circle, the Antarctic Circle's counterpart in the Northern Hemisphere.

Position

The position of the Antarctic Circle is not fixed and currently runs 66°33′48.8″ south of the Equator. Its latitude depends on the Earth's axial tilt, which fluctuates within a margin of more than 2° over a 41,000-year period, owing to tidal forces resulting from the orbit of the Moon. Consequently, the Antarctic Circle is currently drifting southward at a speed of about 14.5 m (48 ft) per year.

Day and night

At the circle itself, the sun is continuously visible for one day at the December solstice (midnight sun) and continuously below the horizon for one day at the June solstice (polar night); the periods lengthen toward the South Pole, where they reach six months each.

Geography

Unlike the Arctic Circle, which crosses many countries, the Antarctic Circle passes almost entirely over the Southern Ocean and the continent of Antarctica, briefly touching several parts of the coastline and offshore islands and the Antarctic Peninsula. No permanent population lives south of the circle, though research stations operate there year-round.
