Differential geometry

Differential geometry studies smooth shapes — curves, surfaces and manifolds — using calculus.

Differential geometry is a mathematical discipline that uses the techniques of differential and integral calculus, as well as linear and multilinear algebra, to study geometric problems. It deals with smooth objects — curves, surfaces, and their higher-dimensional generalisations, manifolds — and with quantities defined on them, such as curvature, that often have direct physical meaning.

History

The theory of plane and space curves goes back to antiquity, but the modern subject began with the application of calculus to curves and surfaces by Leibniz, the Bernoullis and Euler. Carl Friedrich Gauss's Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas (1827) founded the intrinsic geometry of surfaces: his theorema egregium showed that Gaussian curvature depends only on measurements made within the surface itself. Bernhard Riemann's 1854 habilitation lecture generalised these ideas to spaces of any dimension, introducing the metric tensor and Riemannian manifolds.

Main concepts

  • Curves — studied via arc length, curvature and torsion; the Frenet–Serret formulas describe a space curve completely.
  • Surfaces — first and second fundamental forms; principal, Gaussian and mean curvature.
  • Manifolds and tensors — smooth manifolds equipped with metrics, connections (such as the Levi-Civita connection) and curvature tensors.
  • Geodesics — the shortest or straightest paths on a curved space, extremising the length functional.
  • Differential forms and bundles — the modern language of the subject, developed by Élie Cartan and others.

Applications

Riemannian geometry provides the mathematical framework of Einstein's general relativity, in which spacetime is a four-dimensional Lorentzian manifold whose curvature encodes gravity. Differential geometry also underlies much of modern theoretical physics (gauge theory, string theory), as well as computer graphics, robotics, and the analysis of shape in computer vision.
