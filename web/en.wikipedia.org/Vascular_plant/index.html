Vascular plants (from Latin vasculum, "duct"), also called tracheophytes, are land plants that have lignified tissues — the xylem and phloem — for conducting water and minerals throughout the plant. Vascular plants include the clubmosses, ferns, gymnosperms (including conifers), and flowering plants (angiosperms).

Vascular bundles in a plant stem.

Characteristics

  • The dominant generation is the sporophyte, which develops true roots, stems, and leaves.
  • Xylem cells (tracheids and vessel elements) are dead at maturity and reinforced with lignin, providing structural support that lets vascular plants grow tall.
  • Unlike bryophytes (mosses and liverworts), vascular plants are not tied to moist habitats for reproduction — especially the seed plants.

Evolution

Vascular plants appeared in the Silurian period, over 420 million years ago; early forms such as Cooksonia were small and leafless. By the Devonian they had diversified into forests, transforming terrestrial ecosystems and atmospheric chemistry.
