Dog breeding

Dog breeding is the practice of mating selected dogs with the intention of maintaining or producing specific qualities and characteristics. When dogs reproduce without such human intervention, their offspring's characteristics are determined by natural selection, while "dog breeding" refers specifically to the artificial selection of dogs, in which dogs are intentionally bred by their owners. Breeding relies on the science of genetics, so a breeder who is knowledgeable about canine genetics, health, and the intended purpose of the dogs attempts to breed suitable characteristics.

History

Humans have maintained populations of useful animals around their places of habitat since pre-historic times. Dogs were selectively bred for hunting, herding, guarding and companionship, producing landraces adapted to local conditions. Over the last two centuries, formalised breeding with closed stud books and written breed standards — administered by kennel clubs founded in the 19th century, such as The Kennel Club (UK, 1873) and the American Kennel Club (1884) — has produced most of the several hundred recognised modern breeds.

Breeding practices

Responsible breeders screen breeding stock for hereditary conditions (hip dysplasia, eye disorders, cardiac disease), consider temperament and conformation to the breed standard, and track pedigrees to manage genetic diversity. Methods include outcrossing to unrelated lines, line breeding, and — more controversially — inbreeding, which increases homozygosity and can concentrate both desired traits and harmful recessive mutations.

Health and welfare concerns

Selection for extreme conformation has caused serious welfare problems in some breeds, including breathing difficulties in brachycephalic breeds (such as pugs and bulldogs), spinal disease, and syringomyelia. Closed stud books and small founder populations can reduce genetic diversity and raise the incidence of inherited disease. Regulators and kennel clubs in several countries have introduced rules or breeding restrictions for extreme traits, and crossbreeding programmes (such as the Dalmatian–Pointer backcross project) have been used to eliminate specific genetic defects.

Commercial breeding and law

Large-scale commercial breeding operations, sometimes called "puppy mills", are criticised for poor welfare and are regulated to varying degrees; many jurisdictions require licensing of breeders and restrict pet-shop sales in favour of adoption or direct breeder sales. Designer crossbreeds (e.g. Labradoodles) occupy a contested space between purebred and mixed-breed traditions.
