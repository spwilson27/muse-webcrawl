Aneurysm

Aneurysm

An aneurysm is an abnormal localised bulging or ballooning in the wall of a blood vessel, usually an artery, caused by weakening of the vessel wall. Aneurysms may occur in any artery but are most clinically important in the aorta (abdominal and thoracic aortic aneurysms), the cerebral arteries of the circle of Willis, and the popliteal artery. The word derives from the Greek aneurysma, meaning a widening.

Classification

Aneurysms are classified by shape and cause. True aneurysms involve all three layers of the vessel wall and may be saccular (a pouch on one side of the vessel) or fusiform (a symmetrical spindle-shaped dilatation). A false aneurysm (pseudoaneurysm) is a contained leak of blood outside the vessel wall, held in by surrounding tissue, often following trauma or arterial puncture. Mycotic (infective) aneurysms arise from infection of the wall, and dissecting aneurysms involve blood tracking within the wall layers.

Causes and risk factors

Most aneurysms develop gradually through degeneration of the tunica media, associated with atherosclerosis, chronic hypertension, smoking and ageing. Genetic and connective-tissue disorders — notably Marfan syndrome, vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome and autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease — predispose to aneurysm formation, particularly of the intracranial arteries. Cerebral ("berry") aneurysms occur at arterial branch points, most often in the circle of Willis.

Signs, symptoms and complications

Many aneurysms are asymptomatic and found incidentally on imaging. Large abdominal aortic aneurysms may cause back or abdominal pain or a palpable pulsatile mass. The most feared complication is rupture: rupture of a cerebral aneurysm causes subarachnoid haemorrhage, typically presenting as a sudden "thunderclap" headache, while rupture of an abdominal aortic aneurysm causes catastrophic internal bleeding and is frequently fatal without emergency surgery. Aneurysms may also thrombose and embolise, or compress neighbouring structures.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by ultrasound (the standard screening test for abdominal aortic aneurysm), CT angiography, MR angiography or catheter angiography. Management depends on size, site and rupture risk: small aneurysms may be monitored with periodic imaging and treated with smoking cessation and blood-pressure control, while larger or symptomatic aneurysms are repaired by open surgery (replacement of the segment with a graft) or endovascular techniques (stent-grafting for aortic aneurysms, coiling or flow diversion for cerebral aneurysms). Screening of men aged 65 and over for abdominal aortic aneurysm is offered in several countries.
