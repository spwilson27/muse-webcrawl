Ulster loyalism

Ulster loyalism is a strand of Ulster unionism associated mainly with working-class Protestants in Northern Ireland. Like other unionists, loyalists support the continued political union between Great Britain and Northern Ireland, and oppose a united Ireland. The term is also used for the paramilitary movement associated with some of its adherents during the conflict known as the Troubles.

History

Loyalism's roots lie in the Plantation of Ulster in the seventeenth century, which settled Scottish and English Protestants in the north of Ireland, and in resistance to Irish Home Rule in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The Ulster Covenant of 1912 and the original Ulster Volunteers expressed mass unionist opposition to Home Rule. After partition in 1921, Northern Ireland remained in the United Kingdom with a devolved unionist government.

The Troubles

During the Troubles (c. 1968–1998), loyalist paramilitary organisations such as the Ulster Volunteer Force (UVF) and the Ulster Defence Association (UDA) carried out campaigns of violence, primarily against the nationalist and Catholic community, killing several hundred people. Mainstream loyalist political identity was represented chiefly by the Democratic Unionist Party and the Ulster Unionist Party, while smaller loyalist parties, the Progressive Unionist Party and the Ulster Democratic Party, were linked to the paramilitaries. Loyalist groups declared ceasefires in 1994, and the Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement of 1998 reshaped Northern Ireland's politics, though loyalist communities have continued to debate their place within the Union, most recently over post-Brexit trading arrangements.

Culture

Loyalist identity is expressed through the Orange Order and its marching season, Apprentice Boys commemorations, murals, flags and bands, and attachment to symbols such as the Union Flag and the Red Hand of Ulster.
