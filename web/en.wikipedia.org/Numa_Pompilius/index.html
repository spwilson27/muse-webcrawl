Numa Pompilius

Numa Pompilius (traditionally reigned 715–673 BC) was the legendary second king of Rome, succeeding Romulus. Where Romulus was remembered as a warlike founder, Numa was credited with establishing most of Rome's religious institutions and giving the city its legal and cultic foundations during a long, peaceful reign.

Legend

According to Livy and Plutarch, Numa was a Sabine from Cures, renowned for his wisdom and piety, who accepted the throne only reluctantly after the death of Romulus and an interregnum. He was said to have been advised by the nymph Egeria, whom he met in a sacred grove. Later tradition even connected him with Pythagoras, though chronology makes this impossible, a point ancient authors themselves noted.

Institutions

Ancient sources attribute to Numa the reorganisation of the calendar into twelve lunar months, the creation of priesthoods including the flamens, the Vestal Virgins, the Salii, and the office of pontifex maximus; the building of the Temple of Janus, whose doors stood open in war and closed in peace; and the division of the populace into guilds of craftsmen. He supposedly banned human sacrifice and images of the gods.

Death and legacy

Numa was said to have died of old age after a reign of 43 years and was succeeded by Tullus Hostilius. In 181 BC, stone coffers purportedly containing his books were unearthed on the Janiculum; the Senate ordered the philosophical volumes burned as subversive to religion. Numa remained a symbol of pious statesmanship in Roman memory.
