Biofilm

A biofilm is a syntrophic consortium of microorganisms — most often bacteria, but also fungi, algae and other microbes — in which cells stick to each other and to a surface, embedded in a self-produced matrix of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) composed of polysaccharides, proteins, lipids and extracellular DNA. Biofilms form on virtually any wet surface, from river stones and ship hulls to teeth and medical implants.

Formation

Development proceeds in stages: reversible attachment of free-floating (planktonic) cells to a surface, irreversible adhesion and microcolony formation, maturation into structured communities with water channels, and finally dispersal of cells to colonise new sites. Cell-to-cell chemical signalling known as quorum sensing coordinates gene expression across the community, switching on matrix production and other collective behaviours.

Properties and significance

Cells within a biofilm are far more tolerant of antibiotics and disinfectants than the same cells growing planktonically — sometimes by factors of a hundred or more — because the matrix impedes penetration, nutrient gradients create dormant persister cells, and genes transfer readily between neighbours. Biofilms cause persistent infections (on catheters, prosthetic joints and in the lungs of cystic fibrosis patients), dental plaque and caries, and industrial biofouling of pipes, membranes and ship hulls. Beneficial biofilms are exploited in wastewater treatment, bioremediation and microbial fuel cells.

History

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek observed microbial communities on his own teeth in the 17th century, but the modern concept and the term "biofilm" developed only in the 1970s, notably through the work of J. William Costerton, who championed the view that the biofilm — not the free-swimming cell — is the predominant mode of bacterial life in nature.
