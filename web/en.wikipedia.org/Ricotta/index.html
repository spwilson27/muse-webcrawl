Ricotta

Ricotta (Italian for "recooked") is an Italian whey cheese made from the whey left over from the production of other cheeses, such as mozzarella and provolone, from the milk of sheep, cows, goats, or Italian water buffalo. Unlike most cheeses, which are made from curdled milk casein, ricotta is produced by heating whey and precipitating its remaining proteins — chiefly albumin and globulin — which form a delicate curd. Its texture is soft, white, and slightly grainy, with a mild, faintly sweet flavor.

Production

Whey is heated to near boiling, often with added acid (such as citric acid or vinegar) and sometimes with a portion of whole milk mixed in. As the temperature rises, the whey proteins denature and coagulate into fine curds that float to the surface, where they are skimmed or ladled into perforated containers to drain. Traditional ricotta uses whey alone; much commercial ricotta, particularly in the United States, is made wholly or partly from milk, producing a moister, milder product.

Varieties

  • Ricotta salata — pressed, salted, and aged; firm enough to grate, characteristic of Sicily and Sardinia.
  • Ricotta affumicata — smoked ricotta.
  • Ricotta infornata — baked ricotta, with a browned crust.
  • Ricotta forte — a sharp, fermented ricotta from Apulia.

Culinary uses

Ricotta is a staple of Italian cooking, used in lasagne, stuffed pastas such as ravioli and cannelloni, Sicilian cannoli, cassata, pastiera napoletana, gnudi, cheesecakes, and pancakes. It is also eaten fresh with honey or fruit, or spread on bread.

History

Making cheese from recooked whey is ancient; ricotta-like production is documented in Sicily and southern Italy from the medieval period, and the method made economical use of the by-product of hard-cheese manufacture.
