Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, most commonly known as NMR spectroscopy or magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), is a spectroscopic technique based on re-orientation of atomic nuclei with non-zero nuclear spin in an external magnetic field. This re-orientation occurs with absorption of electromagnetic radiation in the radio frequency region, roughly 4 to 900 MHz, which depends on the strength of the magnetic field and the magnetic properties of the isotope of the atoms.

Principle

Many nuclei possess spin; when placed in a strong magnetic field, their spin states split into energy levels (the Zeeman effect). Radiofrequency pulses can excite nuclei between levels, and the signals emitted as spins relax are recorded as a free induction decay, which is Fourier-transformed into a spectrum. A nucleus's resonance frequency — its chemical shift — is exquisitely sensitive to its local electronic environment, so NMR reveals the connectivity and three-dimensional structure of molecules. Spin–spin (J) coupling splits signals into multiplets that report on neighbouring nuclei.

Applications

NMR is the principal tool for structure elucidation of organic compounds in chemistry, and it is widely used in biochemistry to determine the structures of proteins and nucleic acids in solution (work recognised by Nobel Prizes to Richard Ernst in 1991 and Kurt Wüthrich in 2002). Multidimensional techniques such as COSY, HSQC, and NOESY correlate signals across nuclei. The same physical phenomenon underlies magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in medicine. NMR is also applied in quality control, metabolomics, and the study of dynamics and folding.

Common nuclei studied include 1H, 13C, 15N, 19F, and 31P.
