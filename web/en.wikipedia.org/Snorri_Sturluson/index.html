Snorri Sturluson, the Icelandic chieftain whose writings preserved Norse mythology.

Snorri Sturluson (1179 – 23 September 1241) was an Icelandic historian, poet and politician. He is the presumed author of the Prose Edda, the principal source for Norse mythology, and of the Heimskringla, a history of the Norwegian kings; he may also have written Egils saga. Twice elected lawspeaker of the Althing, he became one of the richest and most powerful chieftains of the Icelandic Commonwealth — and a central figure in its violent Age of the Sturlungs.

Life

Raised at Oddi under Jón Loftsson, Snorri received one of the finest educations in Iceland. Through an advantageous marriage he acquired the estate of Reykholt and built up great wealth and influence. He served as lawspeaker 1215–1218 and 1222–1231. In 1218–1220 he visited Norway and Sweden; King Haakon IV of Norway enlisted him to promote the submission of Iceland to the Norwegian crown, an ambition that made him enemies and undermined him at home.

Snorri's later years were consumed by feuds within the Sturlungar clan and with rivals such as Gissur Þorvaldsson. Having disobeyed the king's summons to Norway, he was marked as a traitor; on the night of 23 September 1241, Gissur's men attacked Reykholt and killed Snorri in his cellar — according to tradition, his last words were "Eigi skal höggva!" ("You shall not strike!").

Works

The Prose Edda (c. 1220) is a handbook of Norse myth and poetic diction: its Gylfaginning retells the myths of Odin, Thor and Loki, and its Skáldskaparmál and Háttatal preserve skaldic lore and verse forms. The Heimskringla traces the Norwegian kings from the legendary Yngling dynasty to 1177. Without Snorri's writings, much of what is known of pre-Christian Scandinavian religion would have been lost.
