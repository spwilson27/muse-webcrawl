Kampala Convention

The Kampala Convention, formally the African Union Convention for the Protection and Assistance of Internally Displaced Persons in Africa, is a regional treaty adopted by the African Union at a special summit in Kampala, Uganda, on 23 October 2009. It is the world's first legally binding regional instrument devoted to internally displaced persons (IDPs) — people forced to flee their homes but who have not crossed an international border.

Contents

The Convention obliges states parties to prevent arbitrary displacement, to protect and assist displaced people during displacement, and to promote durable solutions such as voluntary return, local integration or relocation. Notably, it addresses displacement caused not only by conflict and persecution but also by natural disasters, climate change effects, and development projects, and it assigns duties to armed groups, multinational corporations and other non-state actors. It also guarantees the right of displaced persons to personal documentation and to participation in decisions affecting them.

Status

The Convention entered into force on 6 December 2012, thirty days after the fifteenth ratification (by Swaziland, now Eswatini). Dozens of African Union member states have since signed or ratified it, and countries such as Niger have adopted national laws to implement it. Africa hosts one of the largest internally displaced populations in the world, numbering in the tens of millions.
