New wave is a music genre and umbrella term that encompasses a broad range of pop and rock styles that emerged in the mid-to-late 1970s in the United Kingdom and the United States, initially overlapping with punk rock before diverging into a more melodic, eclectic, and commercially successful movement. The term was promoted by labels such as Sire Records to describe acts that inherited punk's do-it-yourself energy and break with rock tradition while embracing synthesisers, art-school aesthetics, and pop craftsmanship.

The launch of MTV in 1981 turned many new wave acts — with their striking visual style and music videos — into major stars, especially British groups of the so-called Second British Invasion.

Characteristics

New wave music typically combines quirky, angular guitar playing, jerky or danceable rhythms, prominent synthesisers, and ironic or anxious lyrics. Compared with punk, it is more polished and experimental, drawing on sources as varied as krautrock, reggae, funk, disco, and 1960s pop. Subgenres include synth-pop (Depeche Mode, The Human League), the New Romantic movement (Duran Duran, Spandau Ballet), and post-punk's darker offshoots.

History

American pioneers such as Talking Heads, Blondie, Devo, and The Cars emerged from the CBGB scene in New York, while in Britain artists including Elvis Costello, The Police, XTC, Squeeze, and Joe Jackson turned punk's momentum into chart success. By the mid-1980s the label had largely dissolved into mainstream pop and alternative rock, though its aesthetics continue to be revived periodically.
