Reverse mortgage

A reverse mortgage is a mortgage loan, usually secured by a residential property, that enables the borrower to access the home equity of the property. The loans are typically promoted to older homeowners and typically do not require monthly mortgage payments. Borrowers are still responsible for property taxes or homeowner's insurance. Unlike a conventional mortgage, the loan balance increases over time as interest accrues, and the loan generally becomes due when the borrower dies, moves out permanently, or sells the home.

United States

In the United States, the dominant product is the Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM), insured by the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) and available to homeowners aged 62 or older. The first HECM was issued in 1989 under a programme authorised by the Housing and Community Development Act of 1987. Borrowers must receive counselling from an approved agency before obtaining a HECM. Proprietary (non-FHA) reverse mortgages are also offered by private lenders, sometimes for higher-value homes.

Reverse mortgages are non-recourse loans in the HECM programme: neither the borrower nor their estate owes more than the value of the home when the loan is repaid, with the FHA insurance fund covering any shortfall.

How it works

The amount a borrower can obtain depends on the borrower's age, the value of the home, and prevailing interest rates. Funds may be taken as a lump sum, a line of credit, regular monthly payments ("tenure" or "term" payments), or a combination. Because the loan balance grows over time while the home's value may not, equity available to heirs declines; for this and other reasons, consumer advocates advise caution and attention to fees, which have historically been relatively high.

Other countries

In the United Kingdom, similar products are called lifetime mortgages, a form of equity release regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority; industry standards through the Equity Release Council include a no-negative-equity guarantee. In Australia, reverse mortgages are regulated under the National Consumer Credit Protection Act, which provides a statutory no negative equity guarantee. In Canada, products such as the CHIP Reverse Mortgage are offered to homeowners aged 55 and over and are not government-insured.

Criticism

Reverse mortgages have been criticised for high fees, complexity, and the risk that borrowers who fail to pay property taxes or insurance may face foreclosure. Regulators have at times acted against misleading advertising. Supporters argue the products allow older people to remain in their homes and supplement retirement income, and safeguards such as mandatory counselling and non-recourse terms mitigate the risks.
