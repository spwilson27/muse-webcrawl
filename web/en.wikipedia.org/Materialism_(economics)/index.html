Materialism (economics)

Attitude prioritising material possessions and wealth

Economic materialism, in consumer research and social psychology, is the tendency to regard the acquisition of material goods and wealth as a central life goal, a marker of success, and a source of happiness. It is distinct from the philosophical doctrine of materialism (the view that matter is the fundamental substance of reality) and from historical materialism in Marxist theory, though critics sometimes connect the phenomena.

Measurement and research

Psychologists measure materialism with scales such as the Material Values Scale developed by Marsha Richins and Scott Dawson (1992), which assesses the centrality of acquisition, the belief that possessions signal success, and the belief that acquiring things brings happiness. A large body of research — summarised in meta-analyses such as Dittmar et al. (2014) — finds a consistent negative association between strong materialistic values and personal well-being, life satisfaction and prosocial behaviour, and positive associations with compulsive buying, debt and anxiety.

Causes and critique

Studies link materialistic orientations to insecurity in childhood, social comparison (now amplified by social media), and exposure to advertising. Critics of consumer culture, from Thorstein Veblen's account of conspicuous consumption onward, have treated materialism as a social pathology; defenders respond that rising material standards have delivered real gains in health, comfort and opportunity, and that moderate acquisitiveness is a normal human motive.
