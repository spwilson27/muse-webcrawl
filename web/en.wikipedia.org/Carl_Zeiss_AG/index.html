Carl Zeiss AG, branded as ZEISS, is a German manufacturer of optical systems and optoelectronics, founded in Jena in 1846 by optician Carl Zeiss. Together with Ernst Abbe and Otto Schott, Zeiss transformed lens-making from craft into science, and the company became a world leader in microscopes, camera lenses, binoculars, planetariums, ophthalmic instruments, and medical technology. The Zeiss Group is wholly owned by the Carl Zeiss Foundation (Carl-Zeiss-Stiftung), a structure created by Abbe in 1889 that remains unusual among large industrial companies.

History

Carl Zeiss opened his workshop for precision mechanics and optics in Jena in 1846, initially making microscopes. From 1866 he collaborated with physicist Ernst Abbe, whose scientific theory of image formation — including the Abbe sine condition and Abbe number — enabled systematic lens design; glass chemist Otto Schott joined to develop new optical glasses, founding the Jena glassworks. After Zeiss's death in 1888, Abbe transferred the company to the Carl Zeiss Foundation. Following the Second World War, the firm was split between East and West Germany (VEB Carl Zeiss Jena and the western company in Oberkochen), a division that lasted until reunification in 1991.

Business areas

Modern Zeiss operates in semiconductor manufacturing technology (including the lithography optics supplied through its stake in, and partnership with, ASML for EUV systems), industrial quality and research microscopy, medical technology (Carl Zeiss Meditec), and vision care and consumer products such as camera and cinema lenses, sports optics, and planetarium projectors. Famous Zeiss lens designs include the Tessar, Planar, Sonnar, Biogon, and Distagon.
