Valence (chemistry)

In chemistry, the valence of an atom is a measure of its combining capacity with other atoms when it forms chemical compounds or molecules. It is usually understood as the number of chemical bonds the atom can form, related to the number of electrons in its outermost shell.

Definition and examples

Hydrogen has a valence of one, oxygen of two, nitrogen commonly three, and carbon four — a fact central to organic chemistry. Many transition metals show variable valence, as in iron(II) and iron(III) compounds. The concept underlies empirical formulas and was historically used to define equivalent weight.

Historical development

The idea emerged in the 1850s through the work of Edward Frankland, August Kekulé and Archibald Scott Couper, who recognised fixed combining powers and self-linking carbon chains. With the advent of quantum mechanics, valence was explained through valence electrons and formalised in valence bond theory and the concept of oxidation state, which is related but distinct: oxidation state is a bookkeeping formalism, while valence counts bonds.

IUPAC usage

The IUPAC Gold Book defines valence as the maximum number of univalent atoms (originally hydrogen or chlorine atoms) that may combine with an atom of the element under consideration. Modern usage often prefers terms such as covalence, electrovalence and coordination number for precision in different bonding situations.
