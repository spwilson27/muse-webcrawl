The University of Oxford is a collegiate research university in Oxford, England. There is evidence of teaching as early as 1096, making it the oldest university in the English-speaking world and the world's second-oldest university in continuous operation. It grew rapidly from 1167, when Henry II banned English students from attending the University of Paris.

Organization

Oxford is a federation of self-governing constituent colleges — 39 in total, including Balliol, Christ Church, Magdalen, and Merton — together with central departments and the Bodleian Library, one of the oldest libraries in Europe. Students apply to a college, live as members of it, and receive small-group tuition known as tutorials, the signature of the Oxford teaching system.

History

The university's early centuries were marked by religious and political conflict, including the St Scholastica Day riot of 1355. During the Renaissance and the English Civil War, Oxford was a Royalist stronghold. In the 19th century the Oxford Movement reshaped Anglicanism, and reforms gradually professionalized research. Women were admitted to full membership only in 1920, and the last men's colleges became mixed in the 1970s and 1980s.

Reputation and alumni

Oxford consistently ranks among the world's top universities. Its alumni and faculty include dozens of British prime ministers, numerous heads of state, and many Nobel laureates. The Rhodes Scholarship, established in 1902, brings international postgraduate students to Oxford. The university's rivalry with Cambridge — collectively "Oxbridge" — is most publicly embodied in the annual Boat Race on the Thames.
