Gram-negative bacteria

Gram-negative bacteria are bacteria that do not retain the crystal violet stain used in the Gram stain method of bacterial differentiation. They are characterised by a cell envelope consisting of a thin peptidoglycan layer sandwiched between an inner cytoplasmic membrane and an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide (LPS).

Characteristics

In the Gram staining procedure, Gram-negative cells lose the crystal violet–iodine complex during alcohol decolourisation because their peptidoglycan layer is only a few nanometres thick; they are then counterstained pink or red with safranin or fuchsine. The outer membrane provides an extra barrier that makes many Gram-negative bacteria more resistant to antibiotics, detergents, and lysozyme: drugs must cross the membrane through porin channels. LPS, also called endotoxin, can trigger strong immune responses, including fever and septic shock, when released into the bloodstream.

Classification and examples

Most bacteria are Gram-negative in structure. Important groups include the Proteobacteria (such as Escherichia coli, Salmonella, Pseudomonas, and the cholera bacterium Vibrio cholerae), the cyanobacteria, spirochaetes (including the agents of syphilis and Lyme disease), and the Bacteroidetes. Medically significant pathogens include Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Haemophilus influenzae, Helicobacter pylori, and Klebsiella pneumoniae.

Medical significance

Gram-negative infections, particularly in hospitals, are a major public-health concern because of rising antibiotic resistance, including carbapenem-resistant strains. The World Health Organization's priority pathogens list is dominated by resistant Gram-negative species such as Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. The outer membrane's structure also makes developing new antibiotics difficult, since candidate molecules must penetrate both membranes.
