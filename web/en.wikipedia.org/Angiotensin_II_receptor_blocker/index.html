Angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), formally angiotensin II receptor type 1 (AT1) antagonists, also known as sartans, are a group of pharmaceuticals that modulate the renin–angiotensin system, the body's principal blood-pressure regulating system. They block the activation of angiotensin II AT1 receptors, preventing angiotensin II from exerting its vasoconstrictor and aldosterone-secreting effects.

Medical uses

ARBs are used principally in the treatment of hypertension, diabetic nephropathy, and heart failure, particularly in patients who cannot tolerate ACE inhibitors because of cough — a side effect ARBs largely avoid, since they do not inhibit the breakdown of bradykinin. Losartan, the first ARB, was approved in the 1990s. Some agents have additional properties: telmisartan is a partial PPAR-γ agonist, and losartan has a mild uricosuric effect.

Adverse effects

ARBs are generally well tolerated. Possible adverse effects include dizziness, hyperkalaemia, and impaired renal function, especially in patients with bilateral renal artery stenosis. Like ACE inhibitors, they are fetotoxic and contraindicated in pregnancy. Combining an ARB with an ACE inhibitor or the direct renin inhibitor aliskiren is generally not recommended because of increased adverse events without added benefit in most trials, such as ONTARGET and ALTITUDE.
