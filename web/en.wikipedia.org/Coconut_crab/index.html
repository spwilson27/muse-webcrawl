The coconut crab is the largest terrestrial arthropod

The coconut crab (Birgus latro) is a species of terrestrial hermit crab, also known as the robber crab or palm thief. It is the world's largest terrestrial arthropod, with a weight of up to 4.1 kg (9 lb). It can grow to 1 m (3 ft 3 in) in length from leg to leg. Adult coconut crabs live on islands throughout the Indian Ocean and parts of the Pacific Ocean, roughly coinciding with the distribution of the coconut palm.

Biology

Unlike most hermit crabs, only juveniles use salvaged snail shells for protection; adults develop a tough exoskeleton of their own. Coconut crabs have evolved organs called branchiostegal lungs that allow them to breathe on land; they will drown if submerged for long. Their huge claws can crack coconuts, and they climb palm trees to cut fruit down. The diet is mostly fruit, nuts, seeds and carrion.

Life cycle and conservation

Mating occurs on land, but females release fertilized eggs into the sea, where the larvae drift as plankton for several weeks before settling on the seabed. Coconut crabs are considered a delicacy in parts of their range and have been hunted to extinction on some inhabited islands; they are listed as Vulnerable. Charles Darwin, who encountered them at the Cocos (Keeling) Islands, described their strength and coconut-opening behaviour in his voyage accounts.
