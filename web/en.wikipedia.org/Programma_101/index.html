Programma 101

The Olivetti Programma 101, also known as the Perottina, is a printing programmable calculator designed by Pier Giorgio Perotto and launched by the Italian company Olivetti in 1964. Marketed at around US$3,200, it was designed for general-purpose tasks such as statistics and engineering calculations, and is often regarded as one of the first all-electronic desktop programmable calculators — and, by some accounts, the first personal computer.

Design

Unlike earlier calculators, the Programma 101 could store programs — sequences of arithmetic and conditional-jump instructions — on magnetic cards that users could keep in libraries and swap for different tasks. It featured a built-in printer, an algebraic keyboard, ten 22-digit registers built from a magnetostrictive delay-line memory, and conditional branching, making it genuinely programmable rather than merely a four-function calculator. Its elegant casing, designed by the young architect Mario Bellini, won design recognition.

Reception and use

Presented at the 1964 New York World's Fair, it surprised American observers; about 44,000 units were sold, mostly in the United States. NASA bought multiple units, reportedly used for calculations connected with Apollo missions. Hewlett-Packard's similar HP 9100A (1968) led to a patent dispute in which HP paid Olivetti royalties.

Legacy

The Programma 101 demonstrated that computing power could sit on an individual's desk years before the microcomputer era; historians of computing frequently cite it as a landmark of Italian industrial design and as an ancestor of the personal computer. A working unit is held by major museums, and it remains an icon of Olivetti's design culture.
