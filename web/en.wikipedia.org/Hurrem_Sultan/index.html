Hurrem Sultan (Turkish: Hürrem Sultan, Ottoman Turkish: خرم سلطان; c. 1504 – 15 April 1558), also known as Roxelana, was the chief consort and legal wife of the Ottoman sultan Suleiman the Magnificent. She became one of the most powerful and influential women in Ottoman history, and a prominent figure of the period popularly known as the Sultanate of Women.

Born in Ruthenia (then an eastern region of the Kingdom of Poland) to a Ruthenian Orthodox priest, Hurrem was captured by Crimean Tatars during a slave raid and eventually taken to Constantinople, the Ottoman capital. She entered the imperial harem, rose through the ranks and became the favourite of Sultan Suleiman. Breaking Ottoman tradition, he married Hurrem, making her his legal wife; sultans had previously married only foreign freeborn princesses. She was the first imperial consort to receive the title Haseki Sultan.

Early life

Hurrem was born around 1504 in the town of Rohatyn, then part of the Kingdom of Poland and now in Ukraine. Her birth name is uncertain; she is often called Aleksandra or Anastazja Lisowska in later traditions. Her father was a Ruthenian Orthodox priest. In the 1510s or early 1520s, Crimean Tatars captured her during a slave raid and she was taken to the Crimean city of Kaffa, a major centre of the slave trade, and then to Constantinople.

Rise in the harem

Hurrem entered the harem of Suleiman, probably around 1520, shortly after his accession. Her cheerful temperament earned her the Turkish name Hürrem, "the cheerful one". She quickly became Suleiman's favourite, displacing his earlier favourite Mahidevran. In 1533 or 1534 Suleiman broke with two centuries of Ottoman tradition and married her in a formal ceremony, making her the first former slave to become the legal wife of a sultan.

Influence

As Haseki Sultan, Hurrem exercised considerable influence over state affairs. She acted as Suleiman's adviser, corresponded with foreign rulers including King Sigismund II Augustus of Poland, and is thought to have influenced Ottoman foreign policy. Historians have debated her role in the falls of Grand Vizier Pargalı Ibrahim Pasha (executed 1536) and Grand Vizier Kara Ahmed Pasha (executed 1555), and in the execution of Suleiman's eldest son Şehzade Mustafa in 1553, which cleared the path for her own sons.

Charitable works

Hurrem commissioned numerous public works, including the Haseki Hürrem Sultan Hamamı (bathhouse) near Hagia Sophia, mosques, soup kitchens (imarets) in Constantinople, Mecca, Medina and Jerusalem, and a hospital for women near the slave market in Constantinople. The Haseki Sultan Imaret in Jerusalem, founded in 1552, provided food for hundreds of the poor daily.

Death and legacy

Hurrem died on 15 April 1558 in Constantinople and was buried in an domed mausoleum (türbe) in the Süleymaniye Mosque complex, beside the mausoleum later built for Suleiman. Her son Selim succeeded Suleiman as Selim II in 1566. Her life has inspired numerous works of art, literature and television, including the Turkish series Muhteşem Yüzyıl (Magnificent Century).
