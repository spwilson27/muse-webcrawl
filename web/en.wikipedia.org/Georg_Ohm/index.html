Georg Simon Ohm (16 March 1789 – 6 July 1854) was a German physicist and mathematician. He is best known for Ohm's law, the fundamental relationship between voltage, current, and electrical resistance, and the SI unit of electrical resistance, the ohm (Ω), is named in his honour.

Life

Ohm was born in Erlangen, the son of a locksmith, and largely educated himself in mathematics and physics before briefly studying at the University of Erlangen. Financial difficulties led him to teach in Switzerland and at schools in Bamberg and Cologne, where, at the Jesuit Gymnasium, he had access to a good physics laboratory. He later held posts at the Polytechnic School of Nuremberg and, from 1852, a professorship of experimental physics at the University of Munich.

Ohm's law

In his 1827 book Die galvanische Kette, mathematisch bearbeitet (The Galvanic Circuit Investigated Mathematically), Ohm presented a complete theory of electricity, showing that the current through a conductor is proportional to the applied voltage and inversely proportional to its resistance: V = IR. His experiments, using thermocouples as a stable voltage source and measuring current by the deflection of a magnetic needle, also established how resistance depends on a wire's length and cross-section. The work was initially received coldly in Germany, but was recognised abroad; the Royal Society awarded him the Copley Medal in 1841.

Other work and legacy

Ohm also proposed an acoustic law concerning how the ear perceives complex tones as combinations of pure sinusoidal components. Beyond the unit named for him, his name is commemorated by a lunar crater and by the Georg Simon Ohm University of Applied Sciences in Nuremberg.
