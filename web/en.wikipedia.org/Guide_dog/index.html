Guide dog

A guide dog wearing the rigid-handled harness used by blind handlers.

Guide dogs (colloquially known in the US as seeing-eye dogs) are assistance dogs trained to lead blind or visually impaired people around obstacles. Although dogs can be trained to navigate various obstacles, they cannot distinguish the colours of traffic lights, so the handler must judge when it is safe to cross. Guide dogs learn "intelligent disobedience" — refusing a command that would put the team in danger.

History

References to service animals date to the 16th century, but systematic training began after the First World War, when many soldiers were blinded by gas attacks. The first guide dog school was founded in Germany in 1916; in the United States, The Seeing Eye was founded in Nashville in 1929 by Dorothy Harrison Eustis after her Saturday Evening Post article about Swiss training methods. Guide Dogs for the Blind Association in Britain followed in 1931.

Training and breeds

Common breeds include the Labrador Retriever, Golden Retriever, and German Shepherd, chosen for temperament, health and working drive. Puppies are raised by volunteers for about a year, then undergo several months of formal training before being matched with a handler. Working life is typically eight to ten years. In many jurisdictions guide dogs have legal access rights to public places where pet dogs are excluded.
