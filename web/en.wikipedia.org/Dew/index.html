Dew is water in the form of droplets that appears on thin, exposed objects in the morning or evening due to condensation. As a surface exposed to the sky cools by radiating its heat (radiative cooling), atmospheric moisture condenses on it at a rate greater than that at which it can evaporate, if the surface temperature falls to or below the dew point of the surrounding air.

Formation

Dew forms most readily on clear, calm nights when objects such as grass blades, leaves, and car roofs lose heat efficiently to the cold sky while warm, moist air remains near the ground. The surface must be a poor conductor of heat from below, so that its temperature can drop below that of the air. If the surface cools below freezing, the deposit is frost rather than liquid dew.

Significance

Although dewfall amounts are small, dew is an important moisture source in arid and semi-arid ecosystems, sustaining insects, small animals, and plants — some, like the Namib Desert beetle, harvest dew and fog actively. Dew also wets crops and can promote fungal diseases; gardeners and farmers distinguish it from guttation, the exudation of xylem sap from plant leaves themselves, which is often mistaken for dew.

Measurement and harvesting

Dew is measured with a drosometer or estimated from leaf-wetness sensors. Traditional and modern dew condensers — from ancient stone piles to radiative foil condensers — collect dew for drinking water in dry regions, and dew appears throughout literature and religion as a symbol of freshness, transience, and blessing.

Dew point

The dew point is the temperature to which air must be cooled, at constant pressure, for saturation to occur; the difference between air temperature and dew point indicates how close the air is to saturation and is a practical measure of humidity.
