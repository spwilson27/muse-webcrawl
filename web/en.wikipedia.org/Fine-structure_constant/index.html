In physics, the fine-structure constant, also known as the Sommerfeld constant, commonly denoted by α (the Greek letter alpha), is a fundamental physical constant which quantifies the strength of the electromagnetic interaction between elementary charged particles.

[Image placeholder: The fine structure splitting of hydrogen spectral lines]

It is a dimensionless quantity, independent of the system of units used, which is related to the strength of the coupling of an elementary charge e with the electromagnetic field. Its numerical value is approximately 1/137. The constant was introduced in 1916 by Arnold Sommerfeld to explain the fine structure of atomic spectra within the old Bohr model of the atom.

Definition

α = e² / (4πε0ℏc)

where e is the elementary charge, ε0 is the vacuum permittivity, ℏ is the reduced Planck constant, and c is the speed of light in vacuum. In natural units, α = e²/(4π).

Measurement

The most precise measurements of α come from the anomalous magnetic moment of the electron (its g-factor) compared against quantum electrodynamics calculations, and from atom interferometry measurements of the recoil of atoms such as caesium and rubidium. Since the 2019 redefinition of SI base units fixed the values of e, h, and c, α is determined experimentally through the vacuum permittivity. Independent measurement methods agree to within parts per billion, confirming quantum electrodynamics to extraordinary precision.

Physical interpretation

The fine-structure constant sets the scale of the splitting of spectral lines (the fine structure) in atoms, determines the size of the Bohr radius relative to the Compton wavelength of the electron, and acts as the coupling constant of quantum electrodynamics: the probability amplitude for an elementary electromagnetic vertex is proportional to α. Richard Feynman famously called 1/137 "one of the greatest damn mysteries of physics: a magic number that comes to us with no understanding by man."

Is it constant?

Quantum field theory predicts that the effective value of α grows slowly with energy scale (a phenomenon called running), reaching about 1/128 at the mass scale of the Z boson. Whether α has varied over cosmological time is an open question; observations of quasar absorption spectra have produced contested claims of variation, while laboratory and natural constraints (such as the Oklo natural nuclear reactor) place tight limits on any change.
