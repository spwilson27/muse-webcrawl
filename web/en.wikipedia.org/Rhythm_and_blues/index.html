Rhythm and blues, often abbreviated as R&B, is a genre of popular music that originated in African-American communities in the 1940s. The term was coined by Jerry Wexler at Billboard magazine in 1948 as a marketing label replacing "race music", and originally described electrified blues, jump blues, and uptempo dance music.

Origins and classic era

R&B grew out of the blues, boogie-woogie, jazz, and gospel traditions, shaped by the Great Migration of Black Americans to northern and western cities. Artists such as Louis Jordan, Ruth Brown, Big Joe Turner, LaVern Baker, and the vocal groups of the doo-wop era defined the sound: strong backbeats, saxophone riffs, electric guitar, and emotive singing. Independent labels — Atlantic, Chess, Sun, Specialty — recorded and distributed the music, which by the early 1950s was reaching white teenage audiences and mutating into rock and roll under performers like Chuck Berry, Little Richard, and Fats Domino.

Soul, funk, and later developments

In the 1960s, R&B fused with gospel intensity to produce soul music (Ray Charles, Aretha Franklin, Sam Cooke; the Motown and Stax sounds), which in turn gave rise to funk in the hands of James Brown and others. In the late 1980s and 1990s, "contemporary R&B" combined soul singing with hip-hop production (new jack swing, then artists such as Mary J. Blige, Beyoncé, and Usher), becoming one of the dominant commercial styles worldwide. The abbreviation R&B is today usually applied to this modern style, while the original music is often called "classic R&B" or "rhythm and blues" in full.

Legacy

R&B is foundational to virtually all subsequent Anglo-American popular music: rock, soul, funk, disco, hip-hop, and modern pop all descend substantially from it, and the Rhythm and Blues Foundation and the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame commemorate its pioneers.
