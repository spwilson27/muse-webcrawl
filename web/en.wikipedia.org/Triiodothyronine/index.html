Triiodothyronine

Structure of triiodothyronine (T3)

Names3,3′,5-triiodo-L-thyronine
FormulaC15H12I3NO4
Molar mass650.98 g/mol
Other namesT3, liothyronine

Triiodothyronine, also known as T3, is a thyroid hormone. It affects almost every physiological process in the body, including growth and development, metabolism, body temperature, and heart rate.

Production of T3 and its prohormone thyroxine (T4) is activated by the pituitary hormone thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), which is released in response to thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) from the hypothalamus. This pathway is regulated by a closed-loop negative feedback system: increased concentrations of T3 and T4 inhibit the production of TSH and TRH.

Production

Synthesis in the thyroid

Iodide is taken up by thyroid follicular cells and oxidised by thyroid peroxidase; the resulting iodine is incorporated into tyrosine residues of thyroglobulin, forming monoiodotyrosine and diiodotyrosine, which couple to form T4 and smaller amounts of T3. About 20% of circulating T3 is secreted directly by the thyroid gland.

Conversion from T4

T3 is the more metabolically active hormone, roughly four times as potent as T4. About 80% of T3 is produced outside the thyroid by removal of one iodine atom from T4 — deiodination — catalysed by iodothyronine deiodinase enzymes in tissues such as the liver, kidneys, and brain.

Mechanism of action

T3 binds to nuclear thyroid hormone receptors, which act as transcription factors that regulate gene expression. In almost every tissue, T3 increases the basal metabolic rate, protein synthesis, sensitivity to catecholamines, and the activity of membrane ion pumps.

Measurement and clinical use

Serum T3 is measured in the evaluation of hyperthyroidism, in which it may be elevated earlier than T4. Pharmaceutical T3 is sold as liothyronine (for example, Cytomel) and is used in the treatment of hypothyroidism and myxoedema coma; an inactive stereoisomer, reverse T3 (rT3), is produced by an alternative deiodination pathway.
