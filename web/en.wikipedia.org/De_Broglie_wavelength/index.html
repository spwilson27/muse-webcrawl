De Broglie wavelength

The de Broglie wavelength is the wavelength associated with a massive particle (that is, matter), proposed by French physicist Louis de Broglie in his 1924 PhD thesis. It expresses wave–particle duality: a particle with momentum p behaves as a wave with wavelength

λ = h / p

where h is Planck's constant. De Broglie's hypothesis extended to matter the wave–particle duality Albert Einstein had established for light.

History

De Broglie suggested that electrons in atoms occupy only orbits whose circumferences contain whole numbers of wavelengths, offering a physical explanation of the Bohr model's quantized energy levels. The idea was confirmed experimentally in 1927, when Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer observed electron diffraction from a nickel crystal, and George Paget Thomson independently observed diffraction through thin films. De Broglie received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1929, and Davisson and Thomson shared it in 1937.

Applications

Because the wavelength of macroscopic objects is vastly smaller than any measurable scale, wave behavior matters only at atomic and subatomic scales. Electron diffraction underlies the electron microscope and neutron diffraction studies of materials, while de Broglie waves are fundamental to quantum mechanics, the Schrödinger equation, and matter-wave interferometry with atoms and molecules.
