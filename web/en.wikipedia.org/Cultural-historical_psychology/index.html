Cultural-historical psychology

Cultural-historical psychology is a branch of psychology founded by Lev Vygotsky in the late 1920s and developed by his students, notably Alexander Luria and Alexei Leontiev. It holds that psychological processes are shaped by the cultural and historical context in which people develop, and that higher mental functions are mediated by tools and signs, above all language.

Core concepts

Central ideas include the zone of proximal development — the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with guidance — mediation by cultural artifacts, and internalization, the process by which social interactions become individual mental functions. Vygotsky argued that thought and speech have separate roots that intertwine during development.

Development of the school

Vygotsky's work was suppressed in the Soviet Union after his death in 1934 and became widely known in the West only after the English publication of Thought and Language and the 1978 collection Mind in Society. His ideas strongly influenced developmental psychology, education, and later cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT), developed by Yrjö Engeström and others.

Influence

The approach underlies research on scaffolding in education, literacy practices, and situated cognition. It contrasts with purely individualist or behaviorist accounts by treating the social world as constitutive of mind, not merely an influence on it.
