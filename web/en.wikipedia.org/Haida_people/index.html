Haida people

The Haida (Haida: X̱aayda) are an Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest Coast of North America. Their traditional territory is the archipelago of Haida Gwaii (formerly the Queen Charlotte Islands) off the coast of British Columbia, Canada, and the southern part of Prince of Wales Island in Alaska, where they are known as the Kaigani Haida.

Culture

Haida society was organised into matrilineal moieties — Raven and Eagle — subdivided into crest groups whose inherited crests (gyaahlgal) were displayed on monumental cedar carvings, including totem poles and longhouse frontal poles. The Haida are renowned for their art, including argillite carving, weaving, and the works of artists such as Charles Edenshaw and Bill Reid. The potlatch (gyaahlgal feasts) validated rank, marriages, and memorials. The Haida language (X̱aad Kíl) is a language isolate, today the focus of revitalisation efforts.

History

Haida Gwaii has been inhabited for at least 10,000 years. Maritime fur trade contact from the 1770s brought wealth and then devastating smallpox epidemics in the 19th century, which reduced the population from many thousands to a few hundred survivors consolidated at Masset and Skidegate. In the modern era the Council of the Haida Nation governs Haida affairs in Canada; landmarks of Haida self-assertion include the 1985 Lyell Island blockade against logging, which led to the creation of Gwaii Haanas National Park Reserve and Haida Heritage Site, co-managed by the Haida and Parks Canada.
