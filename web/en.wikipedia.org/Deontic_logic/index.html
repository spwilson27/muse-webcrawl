Deontic logic

Deontic logic is the field of philosophical logic concerned with obligation, permission, and related concepts. It is a type of modal logic, typically formalised with operators such as O ("it is obligatory that"), P ("it is permitted that") and F ("it is forbidden that"). The name derives from the Greek déon, meaning "what is binding or proper".

History

Early work on norms and imperatives was done by Ernst Mally in the 1920s. The modern discipline is usually traced to Georg Henrik von Wright's 1951 paper "Deontic Logic", which applied the apparatus of modal logic to normative concepts.

Standard deontic logic

The standard system, SDL, treats O as a normal modal operator analogous to necessity. It validates principles such as: if something is obligatory, then it is permitted; and obligations are closed under logical consequence. SDL is axiomatised with the K and D axioms, the latter expressing that obligations are consistent — nothing can be both obligatory and forbidden.

Paradoxes

Deontic logic is known for a family of puzzles, including Ross's paradox ("you ought to mail the letter" seemingly implying "you ought to mail the letter or burn it"), the Good Samaritan paradox, and contrary-to-duty puzzles such as Chisholm's paradox, which challenge simple systems.
