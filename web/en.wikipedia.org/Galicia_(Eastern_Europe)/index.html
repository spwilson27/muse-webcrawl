Galicia (Ukrainian: Галичина, Halychyna; Polish: Galicja) is a historical and geographic region spanning what is now south-eastern Poland and western Ukraine, north of the Carpathian Mountains. The name derives from the medieval principality of Halych. As the Kingdom of Galicia and Lodomeria, it was a crown land of the Habsburg monarchy from the First Partition of Poland in 1772 until the dissolution of Austria-Hungary in 1918 — for most of that period the empire's largest and most populous province.

History

In the Middle Ages the region was divided between the principalities of Halych and Volhynia (together "Galicia–Volhynia"), contested by Poland, Hungary and the Mongols, before passing to the Polish crown in the 14th century. Austria annexed it in 1772 and added western districts (including Kraków, briefly) over the following decades; its capital was Lemberg (Polish Lwów, Ukrainian Lviv). After 1867, Polish elites enjoyed extensive autonomy within Cisleithanian Austria, while the Ukrainian (Ruthenian) national movement also flourished there — Galicia has been called the "Ukrainian Piedmont".

After the First World War, Poles and Ukrainians fought over the region (1918–19); it was assigned to Poland, and its eastern part was organised as the Lwów, Stanisławów and Tarnopol voivodeships. The Second World War brought Soviet annexation (1939), Nazi occupation and the Holocaust, which annihilated Galicia's large Jewish population, and in 1945 the region was divided along roughly the Curzon Line: the west remained Polish, the east became part of Soviet Ukraine. Population transfers expelled Poles from the east and Ukrainians from the west (Operation Vistula, 1947).

Society and culture

Historical Galicia was a multi-ethnic borderland of Poles, Ukrainians, Jews (about 10 percent of the population, with a rich Yiddish and Hasidic culture centred on towns such as Brody and Belz), Armenians, Germans and others. It was famously poor — "Galician misery" was a byword — driving mass emigration to the Americas. Oil extraction around Borysław and Drohobych made it one of the world's earliest petroleum regions. Writers associated with Galicia include Joseph Roth, Bruno Schulz, Stanisław Lem and Sholem Aleichem's contemporaries in the shtetl world it evokes.

Today

The name survives in the Polish sub-Carpathian region and, in Ukraine, as a cultural identity for the oblasts of Lviv, Ivano-Frankivsk and Ternopil; a short-lived proposal for a Euroregion and occasional "Galician" regionalism keep the term in use, though it has no administrative status on either side of the border.
