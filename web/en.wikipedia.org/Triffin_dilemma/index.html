Triffin dilemma

The Triffin dilemma or Triffin paradox is a conflict of economic interests that arises between short-term domestic and long-term international objectives for countries whose currencies serve as the world reserve currency. It was identified in the 1960s by the Belgian-American economist Robert Triffin, who testified before the United States Congress in 1959–60.

The dilemma

Under the Bretton Woods system, the US dollar was convertible into gold at a fixed rate and other currencies were pegged to the dollar, so the world's growing trade required ever-larger dollar reserves abroad. To supply them, the United States had to run persistent balance-of-payments deficits, letting dollars accumulate overseas. But the more dollars accumulated relative to US gold stocks, the less credible the promise to convert them into gold at $35 an ounce became. Restricting the deficits to defend convertibility, however, would starve the world of liquidity. The system thus contained a contradiction that would undermine itself either way.

Resolution and aftermath

The dilemma played out as Triffin foresaw: US gold reserves fell sharply through the 1960s, the London Gold Pool collapsed in 1968, and in August 1971 President Richard Nixon suspended dollar convertibility — the "Nixon shock" — leading to the end of Bretton Woods and the era of floating exchange rates by 1973.

Modern relevance

Economists continue to invoke the dilemma in debates about the dollar's continuing role as the dominant reserve currency, global imbalances, and the stability of the international monetary system, as well as in discussions of alternative arrangements such as expanded Special Drawing Rights or a multipolar reserve system.
