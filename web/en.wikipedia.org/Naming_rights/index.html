Naming rights

Naming rights are a financial transaction and form of advertising whereby a corporation or other entity purchases the right to name a facility or event, typically for a defined period of time. The transaction is most common with sports stadiums and arenas, but also applies to theatres, transit stations, university buildings and charitable events.

History

Early examples include Boston's Fenway Park (named for the owner's realty company) and Chicago's Wrigley Field (named for the chewing-gum magnate William Wrigley Jr.). The practice expanded rapidly from the 1970s and 1980s as publicly financed stadium construction made naming deals an important revenue stream; by the 2000s the majority of major-league venues in North America carried corporate names.

Value and notable deals

Deals for major venues commonly run for 10–30 years and can be worth hundreds of millions of dollars; among the largest are the agreements for SoFi Stadium in Los Angeles and the naming of university facilities and transit stations. Naming rights can change when a sponsor merges, rebrands or goes bankrupt — famously, Houston's Enron Field was renamed after Enron's collapse in 2001. Critics argue that selling names erodes civic identity and tradition, and some cities and teams (such as those preserving names like Lambeau Field) have resisted the practice.
