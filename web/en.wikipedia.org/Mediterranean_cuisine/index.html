Mediterranean cuisine is the food and methods of preparation used by the peoples of the Mediterranean Basin. Despite national and regional differences, it is united by the climate and by shared staple ingredients — above all the "Mediterranean triad" of wheat (bread and pasta), grapes (wine) and olives (olive oil).

Common elements

Throughout the region, meals emphasise vegetables, legumes, grains, fruits, nuts and olive oil, with fish and seafood near the coast, moderate amounts of cheese, yogurt, poultry and eggs, and relatively little red meat. Herbs such as oregano, basil, thyme and parsley, together with garlic, lemon and tomatoes (a post-Columbian addition), provide characteristic flavours. Flatbreads, grilled meats, stuffed vegetables and slow-cooked stews recur from Spain to the Levant.

Regional cuisines

The umbrella covers many distinct cuisines: Italian, Provençal and Catalan in the northwest; Greek and Turkish around the Aegean; Levantine cooking (hummus, falafel, tabbouleh) in the east; and the couscous- and tagine-based cuisines of the Maghreb in North Africa. Centuries of trade and empire — Phoenician, Roman, Arab, Ottoman and Venetian — spread ingredients and dishes across the whole sea.

The Mediterranean diet

In the 1950s–60s the physiologist Ancel Keys and colleagues linked the traditional diet of Crete and southern Italy to low rates of heart disease. The resulting "Mediterranean diet" became a model of healthy eating, and in 2010 UNESCO inscribed the Mediterranean diet on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, recognising also its social dimensions of communal eating.
