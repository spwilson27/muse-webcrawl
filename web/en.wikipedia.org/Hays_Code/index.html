Hays Code

The Motion Picture Production Code was a set of industry guidelines for the self-censorship of content that was applied to most motion pictures released by major studios in the United States from 1934 to 1968. It is also popularly known as the Hays Code, after Will H. Hays, president of the Motion Picture Producers and Distributors of America (MPPDA) from 1922 to 1945. Under Hays's leadership, the MPPDA, later the Motion Picture Association of America (MPAA), adopted the code in 1930 and began strictly enforcing it in 1934.

Background and content

The code was a response to Hollywood scandals of the 1920s and to pressure from religious and civic groups, notably the Catholic Legion of Decency, threatening boycotts and government censorship. Written largely by publisher Martin Quigley and Jesuit priest Daniel A. Lord, the code set out general principles — that no picture should "lower the moral standards of those who see it", that correct standards of life be presented, and that law should not be ridiculed nor sympathy created for its violation — followed by detailed "Particular Applications" concerning crime, brutality, sex, profanity, costumes, religion, and "national feelings".

Under the code, crime could not be shown to profit, adultery and "sex perversion" (a category used to exclude any depiction of homosexuality) were barred, miscegenation was expressly forbidden, profanity was tightly limited, and clergy could not be villains or comic figures. Films required a certificate of approval from the Production Code Administration, headed from 1934 by Joseph Breen, before release.

Decline and replacement

The code weakened in the postwar era under pressure from television competition, foreign films, independent producers, and court decisions extending First Amendment protection to film, notably Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson (1952). Otto Preminger released successful films without certificates (The Moon Is Blue, 1953; The Man with the Golden Arm, 1955), and works such as Some Like It Hot (1959) and Psycho (1960) stretched the rules. Under MPAA president Jack Valenti, the code was replaced in 1968 by the MPAA film rating system (G, M/R, R, X), shifting the industry from prohibition to classification and parental guidance.

The era before strict enforcement, 1930–1934, is known as "pre-Code Hollywood" and is celebrated for its frank treatment of sexuality, crime and social themes.
