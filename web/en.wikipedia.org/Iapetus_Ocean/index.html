Iapetus Ocean

The Iapetus Ocean was an ancient ocean that existed in the late Neoproterozoic and early Paleozoic eras of the geologic timescale (between roughly 600 and 400 million years ago). It lay between the paleocontinents of Laurentia (roughly, North America and Scotland), Baltica (Scandinavia and eastern Europe) and Avalonia (including parts of what became England, Newfoundland and New England). The ocean was named for the titan Iapetus, who in Greek mythology was the father of Atlas; the Atlantic Ocean, which later opened in approximately the same place, is named for Atlas.

History

The Iapetus began to open in the late Neoproterozoic as the supercontinent Rodinia broke apart, with rifting between Laurentia and the continents that formed Gondwana beginning around 600 million years ago. During the Cambrian the ocean widened to several thousand kilometres, developing passive margins, island arcs and subduction zones around its edges.

From the Ordovician onwards, subduction began to consume the ocean floor and the bordering continents converged. The ocean closed in stages during the Silurian and Devonian as part of the Caledonian orogeny: the collision of Avalonia with Baltica and then with Laurentia welded together the pieces that later formed Europe and North America. The suture where the Iapetus finally closed runs through the British Isles — along a line from the Solway Firth to Berwick — and through Ireland, Newfoundland and the northern Appalachians; the rocks of the Appalachian, Caledonian and related mountain belts are the scar tissue of the ocean's destruction. The closure assembled, with the contemporaneous closing of the Tornquist Sea, the "Old Red Sandstone continent" of Euramerica (Laurussia).

Evidence

Evidence for the Iapetus includes distinct trilobite and brachiopod faunas on its former shores — the "Atlantic" and "Pacific" faunal provinces found today in Scotland and England, only a short distance apart — ophiolites (slices of oceanic crust) such as those of Newfoundland and Norway, and palaeomagnetic reconstructions of the ancient continents. The term "Iapetus" was proposed by W. B. Harland and R. A. Gayer in 1972, as evidence mounted in the early years of plate tectonic theory for a former ocean where the Atlantic now lies.
