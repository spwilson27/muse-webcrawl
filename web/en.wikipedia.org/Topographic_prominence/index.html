Topographic prominence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Contour diagram showing prominence measured from the key col of a peak]

Topographic prominence (also autonomous height, relative height, or shoulder drop in Britain) measures the height of a mountain or hill summit relative to the lowest contour line encircling it but containing no higher summit within it. It is the minimum vertical descent one must make from the summit before one can ascend to any higher terrain, and it is a standard numerical measure of a peak's independence.

Definitions

The key col (or key saddle) of a peak is the highest point on the lowest contour that encircles the peak and no higher ground. Prominence equals the summit's elevation minus the elevation of its key col. The peak reached by climbing from the key col toward higher ground is the parent peak; several conventions exist for choosing it, including the prominence parent and the encirclement parent.

Examples

Mount Everest has, by definition, a prominence equal to its elevation, 8,849 m, since no higher land exists. By contrast, a high but subsidiary summit such as Mont Blanc de Cheilon, or a dramatic subsidiary peak of a larger massif, may have a prominence of only a few hundred metres despite a great elevation. Mount Kilimanjaro's prominence of about 5,895 m — nearly its full height — reflects its isolation as a free-standing volcano. Prominence is the criterion used to define lists of independent peaks, for example the "ultras" (peaks with prominence of at least 1,500 m) and the distinction between the Scottish Munros and their subsidiary "Tops".

Oceanic prominence

If sea level is taken as the surrounding datum, the concept extends to islands: the prominence of an island high point is its elevation, since the encircling contour is the coastline at sea level.
