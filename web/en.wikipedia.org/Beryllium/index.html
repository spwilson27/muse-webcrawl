Beryllium

Beryllium is a chemical element with the symbol Be and atomic number 4. It is a steel-grey, strong, lightweight, and brittle alkaline earth metal. Cosmically rare — it is not formed in stellar nucleosynthesis in significant quantities and is instead produced mainly by cosmic ray spallation — beryllium occurs naturally only in combination with other elements in minerals such as beryl and chrysoberyl.

Properties

Beryllium has one of the highest melting points of the light metals (1,287 °C) and an exceptional stiffness-to-weight ratio: its Young's modulus (about 287 GPa) makes it roughly 50 percent stiffer than steel while being far less dense (1.85 g/cm³). It is relatively transparent to X-rays and is non-magnetic. The element was discovered in 1798 by Louis Nicolas Vauquelin as a component of beryl and emerald, and was first isolated in 1828 by Friedrich Wöhler and Antoine Bussy independently.

Applications

Because of its low density, stiffness, and thermal stability, beryllium is used in aerospace components, satellites, and the mirrors of the James Webb Space Telescope. Beryllium-copper alloys (about 2% beryllium) are hard, non-sparking, and widely used for tools and electrical contacts. Pure beryllium serves as a neutron reflector and moderator in nuclear reactors and in neutron sources; thin beryllium foils form the windows of X-ray tubes.

Health hazards

Beryllium and its compounds are highly toxic. Inhalation of beryllium dust can cause chronic beryllium disease (berylliosis), a potentially fatal granulomatous lung disease, and beryllium is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the IARC. Occupational exposure is therefore tightly regulated, which, along with scarcity, keeps production small — only a few hundred tonnes per year worldwide, chiefly from the United States, Kazakhstan, and China.
