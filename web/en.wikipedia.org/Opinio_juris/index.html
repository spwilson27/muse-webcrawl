Opinio juris

Opinio juris, in full opinio juris sive necessitatis ("an opinion of law or necessity"), is the belief that an action was carried out because it was a legal obligation. In international law it is one of the two elements required for a practice to become customary international law, the other being consistent state practice.

FieldPublic international law
Related conceptCustomary international law
SourceStatute of the ICJ, Article 38(1)(b)

Definition

Article 38(1)(b) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice refers to "international custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law". The subjective element — acceptance as law — is opinio juris: states must engage in the practice out of a sense of legal duty, not merely courtesy, habit or convenience.

Evidence

Opinio juris may be inferred from states' official statements, diplomatic correspondence, treaty preambles, votes on resolutions of the UN General Assembly, national legislation and the reasoning of international courts. In the North Sea Continental Shelf cases (1969), the International Court of Justice emphasised that the acts concerned must "amount to a settled practice" carried out "in such a way as to be evidence of a belief that this practice is rendered obligatory".

Debates

Scholars debate the so-called "chronological paradox": how a belief that a rule already binds can precede the rule's existence. Others question how opinio juris is proven for new customs and whether resolutions alone can evidence it. The International Law Commission's 2018 conclusions on identification of customary international law treat the two elements — practice and acceptance as law — as separately ascertainable.
