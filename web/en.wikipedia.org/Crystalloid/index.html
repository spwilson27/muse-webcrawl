Crystalloid

A crystalloid is a substance that, when dissolved, forms a true solution and can pass through a semipermeable membrane, in contrast to a colloid, whose particles are too large. The distinction was introduced by Thomas Graham in the 19th century, who observed that crystalloids diffuse readily and can be crystallised.

In medicine

In clinical practice, "crystalloids" are aqueous solutions of mineral salts and other small, water-soluble molecules used for intravenous fluid therapy — for example normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride), Ringer's lactate (Hartmann's solution) and dextrose solutions. Because their solutes cross capillary membranes, only a fraction of an infused crystalloid volume remains in the blood vessels, the rest distributing into the interstitial and intracellular spaces, unlike colloid solutions which were designed to stay intravascular.

Usage

Crystalloids are the first-line fluids for resuscitation in hypovolaemia, dehydration and shock in most modern guidelines, with balanced solutions often preferred over saline for large volumes.
