Psychology


Psi (Ψ), the symbol of psychology.

Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behaviour. Its goals are to describe, explain, predict, and — in applied settings — help change behaviour and mental processes. Psychologists study topics ranging from the neural basis of perception to personality, development, social interaction, and mental disorder, using methods from controlled experiments and psychometric testing to case studies, surveys, and computational modelling.

History

Questions about the mind are ancient, but psychology emerged as an experimental discipline in the late 19th century: Wilhelm Wundt founded the first psychology laboratory at Leipzig in 1879, and William James's Principles of Psychology (1890) defined the young field in America. Early schools included structuralism, functionalism, Gestalt psychology, and psychoanalysis — Sigmund Freud's theory of the unconscious. Behaviourism, championed by John B. Watson and B. F. Skinner, dominated mid-20th-century American psychology by restricting study to observable behaviour; the "cognitive revolution" of the 1950s–60s, aided by the computer metaphor and work by figures such as Ulric Neisser and Noam Chomsky, restored mental processes to the centre of the discipline.

Subfields

Major branches include cognitive psychology (perception, memory, reasoning, language), developmental psychology, social psychology, personality psychology, biological psychology and neuroscience, and quantitative psychology. Applied branches include clinical and counselling psychology, health psychology, industrial-organisational psychology, educational psychology, and forensic psychology. Clinical practice — assessment and psychotherapy for mental disorders, classified by manuals such as the DSM-5 and ICD-11 — employs the largest share of psychologists in many countries.

Methods and debates

Psychological research is subject to research-ethics rules shaped by controversial classics such as Milgram's obedience experiments and the Stanford prison experiment. Since the 2010s the field has confronted a "replication crisis": large-scale attempts to replicate published findings, such as the Open Science Collaboration's 2015 project, succeeded for only a minority of studies, prompting reforms including preregistration, open data, and larger samples. Cross-cultural psychologists also criticise the field's heavy reliance on WEIRD (Western, educated, industrialised, rich, democratic) participants.
