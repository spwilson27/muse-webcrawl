Air conditioning

Air conditioning, often abbreviated as A/C (US) or air con (UK), is the process of removing heat and controlling the humidity of air in an enclosed space to achieve a more comfortable interior environment. It typically uses a vapor-compression refrigeration cycle, in which a refrigerant is compressed, condensed, expanded, and evaporated to transfer heat from indoors to outdoors.

History

Modern air conditioning was invented by Willis Haviland Carrier in 1902, initially to control humidity in a Brooklyn printing plant whose paper expanded and contracted with moisture. Carrier's 1906 patent for an "Apparatus for Treating Air" and his rational psychrometric formulas (1911) founded the industry; the Carrier Corporation commercialized systems for theaters, department stores, and eventually homes and automobiles. Air conditioning transformed architecture, settlement patterns in the American Sun Belt and Gulf states, and industries from cinema to computing.

Types and operation

Systems range from window units and split systems (with separate indoor and outdoor units, common worldwide) to central ducted systems, chilled water plants for large buildings, and evaporative coolers suited to dry climates. Efficiency is rated by measures such as SEER and EER. Heat pumps are air conditioners that can reverse the cycle to provide heating.

Environmental impact

Air conditioning consumes roughly a tenth of global electricity and is a growing share of energy demand as incomes and temperatures rise. Refrigerants have been regulated successively: chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) were phased out under the Montreal Protocol for ozone depletion, and their HFC replacements are being phased down under the Kigali Amendment because of their high global warming potential. Waste heat from air conditioners also intensifies urban heat islands.
