Middle ear

NerveFacial nerve branches; glossopharyngeal (Jacobson's nerve)

The middle ear is the portion of the ear medial to the eardrum, and distal to the oval window of the cochlea, in the tympanic cavity of the temporal bone. It is an air-filled chamber, connected to the nasopharynx by the Eustachian tube, that houses the three smallest bones in the human body — the auditory ossicles.

Anatomy

The ossicular chain consists of the malleus (hammer), attached to the tympanic membrane; the incus (anvil); and the stapes (stirrup), whose footplate seals the oval window of the inner ear. Two small muscles modulate the chain: the tensor tympani (innervated by the trigeminal nerve) and the stapedius (innervated by the facial nerve), the smallest skeletal muscle in the body. The Eustachian tube equalizes pressure across the eardrum and drains the cavity; the mastoid air cells connect posteriorly through the aditus ad antrum.

Function

Airborne sound is far less dense than the fluid of the cochlea, and without compensation most sound energy would reflect off the inner ear. The middle ear provides impedance matching through the area ratio between the eardrum and the stapes footplate and the lever action of the ossicles, yielding a pressure gain of roughly 22-fold (about 26–30 dB). The acoustic reflex — contraction of the stapedius in response to loud sounds — partially protects the inner ear and improves speech perception in noise.

Clinical significance

Middle ear infection (otitis media) is among the most common childhood illnesses and can cause conductive hearing loss from fluid (otitis media with effusion). Other disorders include otosclerosis (fixation of the stapes), cholesteatoma, tympanic membrane perforation, and barotrauma from pressure changes during flight or diving when the Eustachian tube fails to open.
