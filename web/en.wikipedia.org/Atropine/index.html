Structure of atropine

Atropine is a tropane alkaloid and anticholinergic medication naturally occurring in plants of the nightshade family, including deadly nightshade (Atropa belladonna), jimsonweed and mandrake. It blocks the action of acetylcholine at muscarinic receptors and is used to treat certain types of slow heart rate (bradycardia), to reduce saliva production during surgery, and as an antidote for organophosphate and nerve-agent poisoning. As eye drops it dilates the pupil (mydriasis) and paralyses accommodation.

Medical uses

In emergency medicine, atropine is a first-line treatment for symptomatic bradycardia. Because organophosphate insecticides and nerve agents such as sarin kill by overwhelming the cholinergic system, large doses of atropine — often given by military autoinjectors together with pralidoxime — are life-saving antidotes. Low-dose atropine eye drops are also used to slow the progression of myopia in children, and the drug appears on the WHO List of Essential Medicines.

Effects and toxicity

Atropine decreases secretions, relaxes smooth muscle (relieving bronchospasm and gut spasm), increases heart rate and dilates the pupils. Overdose produces the classic anticholinergic syndrome summarised as "dry as a bone, red as a beet, blind as a bat, mad as a hatter, hot as a hare": dry mouth and skin, flushing, blurred vision, delirium and raised temperature. Physostigmine can be used as a specific antidote in severe cases. The plant name belladonna ("beautiful lady") recalls the historical cosmetic use of its juice to dilate women's pupils.

History

Atropine was first isolated in pure form in 1833 by the German chemist Philipp Lorenz Geiger, and extracts of nightshade had been used as poisons and medicines since antiquity. It was first synthesised in 1901 by Richard Willstätter.
