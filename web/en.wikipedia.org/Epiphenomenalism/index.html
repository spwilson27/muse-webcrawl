Epiphenomenalism

In epiphenomenalism, mental events are caused by physical events but have no causal effects themselves

Epiphenomenalism is a position in the philosophy of mind on the mind–body problem. It holds that subjective mental events are completely dependent for their existence on underlying physical and biochemical processes in the body, yet themselves have no influence over those processes. In other words, mental events are caused by physical events, but mental events do not cause anything. The mental realm is, in this view, a by-product or "epiphenomenon" of the physical realm.

History

The doctrine emerged in the 19th century alongside scientific materialism. Thomas Henry Huxley defended a version of it in his 1874 essay "On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata," comparing consciousness to the steam whistle of a locomotive — a by-product of the machinery that contributes nothing to its operation. The term derives from the Greek epi ("in addition") and phainomenon ("appearance").

Arguments

Supporters argue that epiphenomenalism avoids the difficulties of interactionist dualism, such as explaining how an immaterial mind could affect the physical world without violating the causal closure of physics. If every physical event already has a sufficient physical cause, then there appears to be no causal work left for mental events to do, even though mental events undeniably occur.

Criticism

Critics object that epiphenomenalism is counterintuitive: it seems obvious that a feeling of pain causes a person to wince or withdraw their hand. Philosophers such as Karl Popper and John Eccles defended interactionism in response. Others argue that if mental events have no effects, it is hard to explain how we could ever know about them or talk about them, since our reports of our experiences would themselves be physical events uncaused by the experiences. Evolutionary objections also ask why natural selection would produce consciousness if it conferred no causal advantage.
