Polynesian Triangle

The Polynesian Triangle is a region of the Pacific Ocean with three island groups at its corners: Hawaii, Easter Island (Rapa Nui), and New Zealand (Aotearoa). It is often used as a simple way to define the Polynesian peoples and the extent of their settlement across the central and southern Pacific Ocean.

Geography

The triangle covers an enormous area of ocean, roughly 26 million square kilometres (10 million square miles), making it one of the largest cultural regions on Earth relative to its land area. Within it lie the island groups of Samoa, Tonga, the Cook Islands, French Polynesia (including Tahiti and the Marquesas), Tuvalu, Tokelau, Niue, Wallis and Futuna, and many smaller islands and atolls. The three corner islands are among the most remote inhabited places on Earth: Hawaii in the far north, Rapa Nui in the southeast, and New Zealand in the southwest.

Settlement

Polynesian settlement of the triangle was achieved through remarkable feats of open-ocean voyaging in double-hulled canoes, navigated by stars, swell patterns, winds, and bird flight. Archaeological and linguistic evidence suggests Polynesian ancestors spread eastward from the Samoa–Tonga region beginning roughly 3,000 years ago, reaching the far corners of the triangle — Hawaii, Rapa Nui, and New Zealand — by about 1000–1300 CE. New Zealand was among the last major landmasses on Earth to be settled by humans.

Culture

The peoples within the triangle share closely related languages, all belonging to the Polynesian branch of the Austronesian language family, as well as broadly similar social structures, religious traditions, tattooing practices, and material culture such as the tiki figure. Despite the vast distances, oral traditions across the region preserve shared ancestral homelands, notably the legendary Hawaiki.
