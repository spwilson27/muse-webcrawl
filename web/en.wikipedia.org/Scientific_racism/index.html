Scientific racism

Scientific racism, sometimes called biological racism, is the pseudoscientific belief that empirical evidence supports the classification of humans into biologically distinct races ranked by superiority and inferiority. It flourished in Europe and the Americas from the late 18th century into the mid-20th century, lending an appearance of scientific authority to slavery, colonialism, segregation and eugenics. Modern genetics and anthropology reject its premises: human genetic variation is continuous, mostly occurs within rather than between populations, and does not support ranked racial hierarchies.

History

Early racial typologies by Linnaeus and Blumenbach assigned temperaments and capacities to continental types. In the 19th century, polygenists such as Samuel Morton claimed cranial measurements proved separate origins and unequal intellects; Arthur de Gobineau's Essay on the Inequality of the Human Races (1853–55) supplied a theory of racial decline through mixture. After Darwin, distorted versions of evolution justified "social Darwinism", and Francis Galton founded the eugenics movement, which influenced immigration restrictions, forced sterilisation laws in the United States and elsewhere, and Nazi racial policy, which drew on German racial hygiene to catastrophic ends.

Repudiation

From the early 20th century, anthropologists such as Franz Boas showed that supposed racial traits changed with environment within a generation. After the Second World War the scientific consensus hardened: UNESCO issued statements on race in 1950 and 1951, an international panel of geneticists and anthropologists reaffirmed human unity, and works such as Ashley Montagu's Man's Most Dangerous Myth popularised the critique. The American Association of Physical Anthropologists and other bodies have formally stated that race is a social construct without the claimed biological hierarchy, while race-realist arguments survive only on the fringes of science.

Legacy

Scientific racism left durable harms, including medical abuses, discriminatory law and the misuse of intelligence testing. Historians and scientists continue to study it as a cautionary case of bias shaping research questions and interpretation, and institutions have revisited collections (such as Morton's skull collection) and honours derived from that era.
