Judicial review

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Judicial review is a process under which executive, legislative, and administrative actions are subject to review by the judiciary. In a judicial review, a court may invalidate laws, acts, and governmental actions that are incompatible with a higher authority. For example, an executive decision may be invalidated for being unlawful, or a statute may be invalidated for violating the terms of a written constitution. Judicial review is one of the checks and balances in the separation of powers: the power of a court to supervise the legislative and executive branches when the latter exceed their authority.

Constitutional review

The best-known form is review of legislation against a constitution. In the United States, the Supreme Court asserted this power in Marbury v. Madison (1803), in which Chief Justice John Marshall held that a federal court may declare a statute unconstitutional. In Fletcher v. Peck (1810) and Martin v. Hunter's Lessee (1816) the Court extended review to state laws and state court judgments. The US model ("diffuse" review) allows any court to refuse to apply an unconstitutional law.

Many other countries use a "centralised" (Kelsenian) model with a specialised constitutional court that alone can strike down legislation — pioneered by Austria's Constitutional Court of 1920 and adopted, in variations, by Germany, Italy, Spain, and many post-1989 democracies. France's Constitutional Council historically conducted review before promulgation, expanded in 2008 by the question prioritaire de constitutionnalité.

Administrative review

Judicial review also covers the legality of administrative action: whether public bodies acted within their powers (ultra vires), followed fair procedures, and acted rationally. In the United Kingdom, where Parliament is sovereign, courts cannot strike down primary legislation but review executive action on grounds of illegality, irrationality, and procedural impropriety (the grounds set out in the GCHQ case, 1985), and since the Human Rights Act 1998 may issue declarations of incompatibility with the European Convention on Human Rights.

Debate

Judicial review is praised for protecting rights and the rule of law, and criticised as counter-majoritarian — allowing unelected judges to override elected legislatures. Political scientists and legal theorists debate its democratic legitimacy, its effects on policy, and differences between strong-form review (courts have the final word, as in the US) and weak-form review (legislatures may respond, as in the UK, Canada with its notwithstanding clause, and New Zealand).
