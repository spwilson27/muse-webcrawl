The Battle of Metz was fought from September to December 1944 between the United States Third Army under General George S. Patton and German forces holding the fortified city of Metz in Lorraine, France. Metz, ringed by two belts of forts built under German rule before and after the First World War, had never been taken by storm in its history. Repeated American attacks in September and October 1944 — including the costly failure against Fort Driant — made little progress amid supply shortages and autumn rains. A renewed offensive in November enveloped the city, which was declared secure on 22 November, although isolated forts held out into December; the last, Fort Jeanne d'Arc, surrendered on 13 December.

Background

In early September 1944, Patton's Third Army, outrunning its fuel supplies after the pursuit across France, reached the Moselle River. Metz anchored the German defence of the approaches to the Saar and the Siegfried Line. The fortress zone (the Feste system) comprised dozens of concrete forts, observation posts and underground barracks, many still serviceable and garrisoned by German officer-cadet and fortress units under General Heinrich Kittel.

The battle

September–October attacks

XX Corps crossed the Moselle south of Metz in September, but counter-attacks and the fortress guns stalled the advance (among them the armoured clash at Arracourt). In early October the 11th Infantry Regiment attacked Fort Driant, a massive work south-west of the city; after days of fighting on and inside the fort, hampered by tunnels and casemates the Americans could not crack, the attack was abandoned on 12 October with heavy casualties.

November offensive

On 8 November, with supplies restored and supported by massive artillery and air preparation, XX Corps attacked again, crossing the Moselle both north and south of Metz. The 90th Infantry Division's northern crossing, made during severe flooding, trapped the city from the rear. American infantry reduced the forts one by one, using direct fire, demolition and siege methods. U.S. troops entered Metz on 18 November and the city was cleared by 22 November; Kittel was captured. The outlying forts surrendered piecemeal, the last on 13 December.

Aftermath

The three-month struggle cost the Third Army thousands of casualties and delayed Patton's advance toward the Saar until the German Ardennes offensive intervened. The battle is studied as an example of the difficulty of attacking modern fortress systems and of the logistical limits of rapid pursuit. Metz, French again, retained its ring of forts as historical sites.
