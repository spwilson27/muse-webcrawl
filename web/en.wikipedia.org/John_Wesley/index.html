John Wesley (28 June 1703 – 2 March 1791) was an English cleric, theologian and evangelist who founded Methodism, a movement within the Church of England that grew into a worldwide family of Methodist churches.

Early life

Wesley was born at Epworth, the son of the rector Samuel Wesley and his wife Susanna. He was educated at Charterhouse and Christ Church, Oxford, where he and his brother Charles led a devout study group nicknamed the "Holy Club" — its members were mockingly called "Methodists" for their methodical habits.

Ministry

After a formative religious experience on 24 May 1738, when he felt his heart "strangely warmed" at a meeting in Aldersgate Street, London, Wesley began preaching outdoors to working people excluded from parish churches. He travelled some 250,000 miles on horseback across Britain and Ireland, preaching tens of thousands of sermons, organizing converts into societies and classes, and ordaining ministers — steps that led, after his death, to Methodism's separation from the Church of England.

Theology and legacy

Wesley emphasized justification by faith, Christian perfection, and the universal offer of grace (Arminianism). He opposed slavery, promoted education and prison reform, and published hymns, sermons and a popular medical handbook. Today the worldwide Methodist communion numbers tens of millions of adherents.
