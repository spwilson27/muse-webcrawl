Diastole

Diastole is the phase of the cardiac cycle in which the heart muscle relaxes and the chambers fill with blood, in contrast to systole, when the muscle contracts and ejects blood. The term derives from the Greek diastolē, meaning dilation or expansion. During ventricular diastole, the atrioventricular valves (mitral and tricuspid) open and the ventricles fill, mostly passively, with a final contribution from atrial contraction.

The cardiac cycle: diastole (relaxation and filling) alternates with systole.

Physiology

Diastole begins with the closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves, marked by the second heart sound. It includes isovolumetric relaxation, rapid ventricular filling, a period of reduced filling (diastasis), and atrial systole. The coronary arteries receive most of their blood flow during diastole, because contracting myocardium compresses them during systole.

Clinical significance

The lower number of a blood-pressure reading is the diastolic pressure — the arterial pressure during ventricular relaxation; a normal value is around 80 mmHg. Impaired relaxation and increased stiffness of the ventricle cause diastolic dysfunction, which can lead to heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). As heart rate rises, diastole shortens disproportionately compared with systole.
