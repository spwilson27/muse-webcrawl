Shiro Ishii (石井 四郎, Ishii Shirō, 25 June 1892 – 9 October 1959) was a Japanese microbiologist and army medical officer who served as the director of Unit 731, a covert biological and chemical warfare research and development unit of the Imperial Japanese Army. Under his leadership, the unit conducted lethal human experimentation on thousands of prisoners, mostly Chinese civilians and prisoners of war, at its facility near Harbin in Japanese-occupied Manchuria.

Biography

Ishii was born in Kamo village (now part of Shibayama, Chiba Prefecture). He studied medicine at Kyoto Imperial University, graduating in 1920, and joined the Imperial Japanese Army as a surgeon. A protégé of the influential army physician Koizumi Chikahiko, Ishii rose quickly through the ranks of the army medical corps.

In the late 1920s, Ishii undertook a two-year tour of Western countries to study chemical and biological warfare research. Convinced that Japan needed its own biological weapons program, he lobbied successfully for funding, arguing that such weapons would be cheap and effective. In 1932 he established a research facility in Harbin, and in 1936 took command of what became Unit 731, based at the Zhongma Fortress and later at the large Pingfang complex.

Unit 731

At Pingfang, Ishii directed experiments that included infecting prisoners with plague, anthrax, cholera and other pathogens; vivisection without anesthesia; frostbite experiments; and field tests of biological weapons on Chinese cities and villages. Estimates of the number of people killed in the unit's experiments range into the tens of thousands, with additional deaths from field attacks.

Immunity and later life

After Japan's surrender in 1945, Ishii and his colleagues negotiated immunity from prosecution as war criminals in exchange for providing the United States with the data from their experiments. He was never tried for his crimes. Ishii spent his later years in Japan and died in Tokyo in 1959 of throat cancer, aged 67.
