Runes are the letters in a set of related alphabets known as runic alphabets, which were used to write various Germanic languages before the adoption of the Latin alphabet and for specialised purposes thereafter. The Scandinavian variants are also known as futhark (or fuþark, from the first six letters ᚠ ᚢ ᚦ ᚨ ᚱ ᚲ), and the Anglo-Saxon variant as futhorc.

The Elder and Younger Futhark

The earliest runic inscriptions date to around 150 CE and use the 24-letter Elder Futhark, which spread with Germanic peoples across northern Europe. In Scandinavia it developed, by about 800 CE, into the 16-letter Younger Futhark of the Viking Age, while in Anglo-Saxon England the alphabet expanded into the 28–33 letter Futhorc. Medieval runes remained in use in parts of Scandinavia into the late Middle Ages, and runes survived in Swedish Dalarna as a local script into the early 20th century.

Uses

Runes were carved on memorial stones, weapons, tools, coins and amulets, and were associated in Norse mythology with Óðinn, who in the Hávamál wins the runes by hanging wounded on the world tree for nine nights. Christianisation replaced runes with the Latin alphabet for most purposes, though they persisted for calendars, markers and magic. Today runes are studied by runologists and used in neo-pagan and popular-cultural contexts.
