Tick-borne disease

SpecialtyInfectious disease
VectorTicks (hard and soft ticks)
Pathogen typesBacteria, viruses, protozoa
PreventionProtective clothing, repellents, tick checks, prompt removal

Tick-borne diseases, or tick-borne illnesses, are diseases transmitted by the bite of infected ticks. Ticks are among the most important vectors of human and animal disease worldwide, second only to mosquitoes for human infections. Because ticks feed on blood at several life stages and on many hosts, they can acquire pathogens from wild animal reservoirs and transmit them to people and livestock.

Major diseases

Bacterial tick-borne diseases include Lyme disease (caused by Borrelia burgdorferi and transmitted by Ixodes ticks), Rocky Mountain spotted fever and other rickettsioses, tularemia, ehrlichiosis, anaplasmosis, and tick-borne relapsing fever. Viral diseases include tick-borne encephalitis, Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever, Colorado tick fever, and Powassan virus disease. The protozoan disease babesiosis is caused by Babesia parasites that invade red blood cells. A bite from the lone star tick can also trigger alpha-gal syndrome, an allergy to red meat.

Transmission

A tick typically attaches and feeds for hours to days. Many pathogens require a period of attachment before transmission — for Lyme disease, generally at least 36–48 hours — so prompt detection and removal of ticks substantially reduces risk. A single tick bite can transmit more than one pathogen, leading to co-infections.

Signs and symptoms

Many tick-borne illnesses begin with nonspecific flu-like symptoms: fever, chills, headache, muscle aches, and fatigue. Some have characteristic features, such as the expanding “bull's-eye” rash (erythema migrans) of Lyme disease or the spotted rash of Rocky Mountain spotted fever. Untreated infections can progress to affect the joints, heart, and nervous system.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention focuses on avoiding tick bites: wearing long sleeves and trousers, using repellents such as DEET or permethrin-treated clothing, checking the body after outdoor activity, and removing attached ticks with fine-tipped tweezers. Most bacterial tick-borne diseases respond to antibiotics, especially doxycycline, when given early; viral infections are managed supportively, though a vaccine exists for tick-borne encephalitis in endemic regions.
