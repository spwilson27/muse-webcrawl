Daphni Monastery (Greek: Μονή Δαφνίου) is an 11th-century Byzantine monastery 11 kilometres west of downtown Athens, in the suburb of Haidari. It stands beside the Sacred Way to Eleusis, on the site of an ancient sanctuary of Apollo Daphnaios, destroyed by the Goths in 395 AD. The monastery is famous for its mosaics, regarded as among the finest surviving works of Middle Byzantine art, and was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1990 together with Hosios Loukas and Nea Moni of Chios.

History

A first monastery was founded in the 6th century within the precinct of the Apollo sanctuary, reusing its Ionic columns. The present katholikon (main church), an octagonal cross-in-square church, was built around 1080. After the Fourth Crusade, in 1205, the monastery was granted to Cistercian monks of Bellevaux Abbey, who added the cloister and exonarthex; it remained Cistercian until the Ottoman conquest around 1458, when it returned to Orthodox monks. The monastery was dissolved in the 1820s during the Greek War of Independence, later served various secular uses, and underwent major restorations after earthquake damage, notably in 1889–1897 and after the 1999 Athens earthquake.

Mosaics

The mosaics, made around 1100 with extensive gold ground, decorate the dome, squinches, and upper walls. In the dome, a stern Christ Pantocrator gazes down, surrounded by sixteen prophets; below are scenes from the life of Christ and the Virgin distinguished by classical balance and restrained, humanized expressions that mark the height of Komnenian art. They are considered a turning point toward a more naturalistic Byzantine style and one of the most important ensembles of medieval art in Greece.
