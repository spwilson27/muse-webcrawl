Burnout (psychology)

Burnout is a psychological syndrome arising from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. Psychologist Christina Maslach characterised it by three dimensions: overwhelming exhaustion, feelings of cynicism and detachment from the job (depersonalisation), and a sense of ineffectiveness or reduced professional accomplishment.

Recognition

The World Health Organization includes burnout in the ICD-11 as an "occupational phenomenon" — explicitly not classified as a medical condition — defined as resulting specifically from workplace stress. The term was introduced in the 1970s by psychologist Herbert Freudenberger, who observed it among staff at free clinics.

Causes and effects

Contributing organisational factors include excessive workload, lack of control, insufficient reward, unfairness, and conflicts between personal and organisational values. Burnout is associated with fatigue, insomnia, impaired concentration, and increased risk of depression and cardiovascular problems, and is especially studied among healthcare workers, teachers, and other caregiving professions.

Prevention and treatment

Research suggests organisational interventions — adjusting workload, increasing autonomy and fairness — are more effective than individual coping strategies alone, though rest, recovery, social support, and changes in job role can help affected individuals.
