The heliocentric model with the Sun at the centre of the planetary system.

The Copernican system is the model of the universe presented by the Renaissance astronomer Nicolaus Copernicus, in which the Sun, rather than the Earth, occupies the centre of the system of planets. It replaced the geocentric Ptolemaic system, which had dominated Western astronomy for more than fourteen centuries.

Background

Since antiquity, the motions of the planets against the fixed stars had been described by systems of circles centred on a stationary Earth. The Ptolemaic system, refined in the second century CE, required epicycles, deferents, and equants to reproduce observations such as retrograde motion. Copernicus, a Polish canon and scholar, sought a more harmonious arrangement and recognised that placing the Sun near the centre simplified the explanation of planetary phenomena.

The model

In the Copernican system, the sphere of the fixed stars remains outermost and stationary. The planets — Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn — revolve around the central Sun in circular orbits, retaining uniform circular motion as an ideal. The Moon revolves around the Earth. The daily rotation of the heavens is explained by the rotation of the Earth on its axis, and the annual motion of the Sun along the ecliptic by the Earth's orbital revolution. Retrograde motion arises naturally when the faster-moving Earth overtakes an outer planet.

Publication

Copernicus's work appeared in the treatise De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres), printed at Nuremberg in 1543, the year of his death. An unsigned preface by Andreas Osiander presented the model as a mathematical hypothesis rather than a physical claim, though Copernicus's own text treats the arrangement as real.

Reception and legacy

The system was initially taken up mainly by mathematical astronomers. Its physical vindication came through the work of Johannes Kepler, who replaced circles with elliptical orbits; Galileo Galilei, whose telescopic observations undermined the Ptolemaic scheme; and Isaac Newton, whose law of universal gravitation explained why the planets move as they do. The shift from an Earth-centred to a Sun-centred cosmos is often termed the Copernican Revolution and is regarded as a founding episode of modern science.
