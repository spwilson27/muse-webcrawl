Rice pudding

Rice pudding is a dish made from rice mixed with water or milk and commonly sugar, cinnamon, vanilla, and raisins. Combinations of sweeteners and flavourings vary across the world, and the dish may be served as a dessert or a meal. In many countries it is a traditional festive food, particularly associated with Christmas in Scandinavia.

When used as a dessert, it is commonly combined with a sweetener such as sugar. Such desserts are found on many continents, especially Asia, where rice is a staple. Some variants are thickened only with the rice starch; others include eggs, making them a kind of custard.

History

Rice puddings descend from ancient grain pottages. Sweet rice cooked in milk appears in medieval European cookery once rice, imported through Arab trade, became available; early English recipes in works such as The Forme of Cury (c. 1390) describe rice pottages. In India, the ancient dish kheer — rice simmered in milk with sugar and cardamom — has been made for millennia and is offered in temples. Persia and the Ottoman world produced their own milk-rice desserts, such as sütlaç, and Chinese cuisine developed sweet congee-like rice desserts. Because rice was long expensive in northern Europe, rice pudding was a luxury dish that gradually became a nursery and comfort food as prices fell.

Regional varieties

  • United Kingdom: short-grain pudding rice baked slowly in milk with sugar and nutmeg, forming a prized golden skin; long a school-dinner staple.
  • Scandinavia: risengrød (Denmark), risengrynsgrøt (Norway), risgrynsgröt (Sweden) — hot Christmas porridge with cinnamon; chilled leftovers whipped with cream become Danish risalamande, served with cherry sauce and a hidden whole almond.
  • Spain and Latin America: arroz con leche, flavoured with cinnamon and lemon zest.
  • Turkey: sütlaç, often baked (fırın sütlaç) for a browned top.
  • India and Pakistan: kheer, flavoured with cardamom, saffron, pistachios, and almonds; a denser set version is phirni.
  • Middle East: roz bel laban and related milk puddings, sometimes scented with rosewater or orange-blossom water.
  • Philippines: champorado, a chocolate rice porridge.
  • Portugal: arroz doce, enriched with egg yolks and decorated with cinnamon patterns.

Preparation

The simplest method simmers short- or medium-grain rice in milk with sugar until the grains swell and release starch, thickening the liquid to a creamy consistency; frequent stirring prevents sticking. Baked versions are cooked slowly in a low oven and develop a skin. Some recipes enrich the pudding with beaten egg or egg yolks, cream, butter, or condensed milk; others lighten it with whipped cream after chilling. Rice pudding can be served warm or cold, plain or with fruit sauces, jam, or spiced syrups.

In culture

Rice pudding occupies a homely place in English-language literature and idiom — A. A. Milne's poem "Rice Pudding" asks "What is the matter with Mary Jane?" over her refusal of it — while in Denmark the Christmas porridge is traditionally left out for the household nisse. Explorers and institutions adopted it as a cheap, filling, easily digested food, and canned rice pudding became a familiar convenience product in the 20th century.
