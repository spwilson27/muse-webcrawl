KISS principle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

KISS, an acronym for "keep it simple, stupid", is a design principle noted by the U.S. Navy in 1960. It states that most systems work best if they are kept simple rather than made complicated; therefore, simplicity should be a key goal in design, and unnecessary complexity should be avoided.

Origin

The phrase is generally attributed to Kelly Johnson, lead engineer at the Lockheed Skunk Works, who told his designers that the aircraft they built had to be repairable by an average mechanic in combat conditions with ordinary tools. Johnson's version is often rendered "keep it simple and stupid". The principle was documented in U.S. Navy usage by 1960 and spread through engineering, software development, and management culture.

Interpretation and use

KISS is invoked in software engineering (where it aligns with the Unix philosophy and the principle of least astonishment), in writing and communication, in product design, and in military planning. Variants of the expansion include "keep it simple, silly", "keep it short and simple", and "keep it simple and straightforward". The idea echoes older maxims such as Occam's razor and Albert Einstein's dictum that everything should be made as simple as possible, but not simpler.

Criticism

Critics note that simplicity pursued naively can strip out necessary functionality or hide real complexity rather than manage it. The principle is therefore usually understood as a call to avoid gratuitous complexity, not to prefer the simplest design regardless of requirements.
