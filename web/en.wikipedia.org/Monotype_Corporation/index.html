The Monotype Corporation was a company specialising in printing equipment and type design, built around the Monotype system of hot-metal typesetting invented by Tolbert Lanston and patented in the 1880s. Unlike the rival Linotype, which cast whole lines of type as slugs, the Monotype consisted of a keyboard producing a perforated paper tape and a separate caster that cast and assembled individual letters (sorts), making corrections easy — a system especially suited to book work.

The British company and its typefaces

The British arm, established in London in 1897 (and from 1931 the Monotype Corporation Ltd), became famous far beyond machinery for its typeface programme, directed for decades by Stanley Morison. Its revivals and originals shaped 20th-century typography: they include Times New Roman (1931–32, designed with Victor Lardent for The Times), Gill Sans by Eric Gill, Perpetua, Albertus, and elegant revivals of Bembo, Baskerville, Garamond, and Plantin. The Salfords works in Surrey employed a large drawing office — overwhelmingly staffed by women — whose precision underpinned the library's quality.

Decline and legacy

Hot-metal composition collapsed in the 1970s–80s as phototypesetting and then digital type took over. The machinery business wound down, but the type library endured: the rights passed through various owners to Monotype Imaging, which continued to license and extend the historic designs as digital fonts.
