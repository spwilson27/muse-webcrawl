End-of-life care

End-of-life care (EoLC) is health care provided in the final stages of a person's life, typically when they have an incurable or terminal condition, or are in the final days, weeks, or months of life. It encompasses medical, nursing, psychological, social, and spiritual support for the patient, and practical and bereavement support for their family and carers. The aims are to relieve suffering, manage symptoms, and help the person live as fully and comfortably as possible until death, rather than to cure the underlying disease.

Overview

End-of-life care may be provided in hospitals, in specialist hospices, in care homes, or in the person's own home. It is closely related to palliative care, which can begin earlier in the course of an illness, and to hospice care, which focuses on people thought to be in the last months of life. Common conditions include advanced cancer, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, dementia, and motor neurone disease.

Symptom management

Pain control is a central concern. Opioids such as morphine are commonly used, alongside medicines for breathlessness, nausea, anxiety, agitation, and excessive secretions. Good practice involves regular assessment, anticipatory prescribing, and adjusting treatment as the person's condition changes. In the final days of life, eating and drinking often diminish naturally, and artificial hydration is used selectively.

Planning and decision-making

Advance care planning allows people to record their wishes about treatment, place of care, and resuscitation while they still have capacity. Instruments include advance directives (living wills), lasting powers of attorney, and do-not-attempt-CPR decisions. When a person lacks capacity, clinicians act in their best interests, consulting those close to them. In several jurisdictions, debates continue about assisted dying and the legal boundaries of end-of-life decision-making.

Communication

Honest, sensitive communication about prognosis and dying is considered essential. Patients and families value clear information, the opportunity to ask questions, and involvement in decisions. Breaking bad news is a taught clinical skill, using frameworks that emphasise setting, perception, invitation, knowledge, empathy, and summary.

Spiritual and cultural aspects

Care at the end of life often addresses spiritual, religious, and cultural needs, including rituals around death, care of the body after death, and mourning practices, which vary widely between faiths and communities.
