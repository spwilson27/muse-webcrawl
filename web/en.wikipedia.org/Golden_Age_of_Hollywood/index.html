The Golden Age of Hollywood is the period of classical Hollywood cinema, conventionally dated from the arrival of sound films in the late 1920s to the early 1960s. It was dominated by the studio system, in which a handful of vertically integrated companies controlled production, distribution and exhibition, and employed actors, directors and writers under long-term contracts.

The studio system

The "Big Five" — MGM, Paramount, Warner Bros., RKO and 20th Century Fox — owned theatre chains and released a film a week at their peak. Stars such as Gloria Swanson, Clark Gable, Humphrey Bogart, Katharine Hepburn and Marilyn Monroe were manufactured and marketed as durable brands. From 1934 the industry enforced the Hays Code, a strict production code governing screen morality.

Golden age achievements

The era produced enduring genres — the gangster film, the screwball comedy, film noir, the western and the lavish musical — and canonical works including Gone with the Wind and Casablanca. The year 1939 is often cited as Hollywood's greatest, seeing the release of The Wizard of Oz, Stagecoach, Mr. Smith Goes to Washington and others.

Decline

The 1948 Supreme Court decision in United States v. Paramount Pictures forced the studios to divest their theatres; television then eroded weekly audiences, which had peaked in 1946. By the 1960s the contract system had collapsed and a freer "New Hollywood" generation emerged.
