Sayyid Qutb

Sayyid Qutb (1906–1966).

Sayyid Ibrahim Husayn Shadhili Qutb (9 October 1906 – 29 August 1966) was an Egyptian author, educator, poet, and Islamist theorist. A leading member of the Muslim Brotherhood in the 1950s and 1960s, he is regarded as the intellectual father of the ideology known as Qutbism and a major influence on modern Islamist and jihadist movements.

Life

Born in the village of Musha in Upper Egypt, Qutb studied in Cairo and worked for the Ministry of Education. His early writings were literary and secular in orientation. From 1948 to 1951 he studied in the United States, an experience that deepened his hostility to Western materialism and culture, described in his essay "The America I Have Seen". On returning to Egypt he joined the Muslim Brotherhood. After the 1952 revolution he briefly advised the new regime before breaking with Gamal Abdel Nasser.

Imprisonment and death

Following an assassination attempt on Nasser in 1954, Qutb was imprisoned for a decade, during which he wrote his Quranic commentary In the Shade of the Qur'an and the manifesto Milestones (1964). Released in 1964, he was rearrested the following year, convicted of conspiring against the state, and executed by hanging on 29 August 1966.

Ideas

Qutb argued that the modern world, including self-described Muslim societies, had relapsed into jahiliyya — the state of ignorance that preceded Islam — because sovereignty (hakimiyya) had been usurped by human rulers rather than belonging to God alone. He called for a committed vanguard to re-establish an Islamic order governed by sharia, by preaching and, where necessary, by jihad. His concepts of jahiliyya and of the illegitimacy of nominally Muslim rulers were later developed by militant groups, though their application remains contested among scholars and Islamists.
