Developing variation

Developing variation is a compositional technique in which a musical work is generated from a small basic idea — a motif or Grundgestalt ("basic shape") — that is continuously varied, reshaped, and developed rather than simply repeated. The concept was articulated by Arnold Schoenberg to describe the music of Johannes Brahms.

Origins of the term

Schoenberg developed the idea in essays such as "Brahms the Progressive" (1947), arguing against the view of Brahms as a conservative traditionalist. He showed how Brahms built whole movements from tiny motivic cells, transforming their rhythm, interval content, and phrasing so that apparent variety is unified by constant derivation — variation that is simultaneously development.

Technique

In developing variation, a motive may be inverted, extended, compressed, combined with itself, or reharmonized; phrase lengths shift asymmetrically as ideas grow organically out of earlier material. The approach connects to Schoenberg's own practice, both in his tonal works and in the twelve-tone technique, where a row can serve as the basic shape from which an entire piece is derived.

Influence

The concept became a central tool of music analysis, applied to Beethoven and other composers as well as to Brahms, and it influenced theorists such as Walter Frisch, who wrote extensively on Brahms and developing variation.
