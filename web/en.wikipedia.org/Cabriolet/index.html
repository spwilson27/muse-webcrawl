Cabriolet

A cabriolet is a term used in several senses. Originally it described a light, two-wheeled, one-horse carriage with a folding hood, popular in the late 18th and 19th centuries. In modern automotive usage — particularly in European markets — a cabriolet is a car with a retractable or removable roof, i.e. a convertible. The word derives from the French cabrioler, "to caper or leap", reflecting the carriage's springy ride.

The carriage

The horse-drawn cabriolet originated in France and spread to Britain in the early 1800s. It had two large wheels, a curved body carrying two passengers, a folding leather top, and often a platform at the back for a servant ("tiger"). In London, cabriolets for hire were licensed from 1823 and gave rise to the shortened name "cab", later transferred to motor taxis.

The automobile

Early motor cars borrowed carriage terminology, and "cabriolet" came to denote open touring models with a foldable soft top and roll-up windows. German manufacturers retained the term strongly: Porsche and Audi market their convertibles as "Cabriolet", and Volkswagen's Golf Cabriolet is a long-standing model line. Depending on marque and era, the term overlaps with "convertible", "roadster" (usually two seats, minimal weather equipment) and "drophead coupé" in British usage.

Other uses

Cabriole is also a term for a style of curved furniture leg — the cabriole leg, convex above and concave below, ending typically in a pad or ball-and-claw foot — and for a movement in ballet and a curved jump in dressage (the capriole, related in origin).
