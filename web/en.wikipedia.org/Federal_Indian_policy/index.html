Federal Indian policy

Federal Indian policy is the body of policies, laws and treaties through which the United States federal government has governed its relations with Native American tribes. Historians conventionally divide it into eras: a treaty-making and removal era (to 1871), allotment and assimilation (1887–1934), the Indian New Deal or reorganisation era (1934–1945), termination (1945–1961), and the self-determination era (from the 1960s to the present). Throughout, federal policy has rested on the constitutional status of tribes as "domestic dependent nations" and on the federal trust responsibility toward tribal lands and resources.

Early policy and removal

From the founding of the republic, the federal government claimed exclusive authority over Indian affairs under the Commerce Clause. The Trade and Intercourse Acts (1790–1834) regulated trade and barred private purchase of tribal land. Treaties recognised tribes as sovereign parties, but the policy of westward expansion culminated in the Indian Removal Act of 1830, under which tens of thousands of members of the Cherokee, Muscogee, Seminole, Chickasaw and Choctaw nations were forcibly relocated to Indian Territory — the Cherokee removal of 1838–39 becoming known as the Trail of Tears. Congress ended treaty-making with tribes in 1871.

Allotment and assimilation

The General Allotment Act of 1887 (the Dawes Act) divided communal tribal lands into individual parcels, with "surplus" land sold to non-Indians; tribal landholdings fell from about 138 million acres in 1887 to about 48 million by 1934. Federal boarding schools, exemplified by the Carlisle Indian Industrial School, sought to assimilate Native children by separating them from their languages and cultures. The Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 extended U.S. citizenship to all Native Americans born in the United States.

Reorganisation, termination and self-determination

The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, promoted by Commissioner of Indian Affairs John Collier, ended allotment and encouraged tribal self-government under written constitutions. After the Second World War policy swung to termination: between 1953 and the mid-1960s Congress terminated federal recognition of more than 100 tribes, with damaging economic consequences. The termination policy was repudiated in the late 1960s, and the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 allowed tribes to contract to run federal programmes themselves. Later milestones include the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988 and the recognition of tribal sovereignty in areas such as taxation, jurisdiction and child welfare.
