Curare

Curare is a common name for various plant-derived arrow poisons originating from indigenous peoples of Central and South America. Used on hunting arrows and blowgun darts, these poisons paralyse prey by blocking neuromuscular transmission. The active principles are alkaloids such as tubocurarine.

Curare
Type: Arrow poison / neuromuscular blocker
Main sources: Strychnos toxifera, Chondrodendron tomentosum
Active agent: Tubocurarine

History

European explorers encountered curare in the Amazon and Orinoco basins from the 16th century. In the 19th century Claude Bernard showed it acted at the junction between nerve and muscle. In 1942 a standardised preparation (Intocostrin) was introduced into anaesthesia, revolutionising surgery by allowing muscle relaxation at lighter anaesthetic depths.

Mechanism

Curare alkaloids are non-depolarising neuromuscular blockers: they competitively antagonise nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the motor end plate, causing flaccid paralysis. Because they are quaternary ammonium compounds, they are not absorbed from the gut, so game killed with curare can be eaten safely. Paralysis of the respiratory muscles causes death by asphyxiation unless ventilation is maintained.

Medical use

Tubocurarine was the first neuromuscular blocking agent used clinically; it has largely been replaced by synthetic agents such as atracurium, vecuronium, and rocuronium with fewer side effects.
