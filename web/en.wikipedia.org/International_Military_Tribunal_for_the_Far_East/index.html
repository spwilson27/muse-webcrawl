The International Military Tribunal for the Far East (IMTFE), also known as the Tokyo Trial or the Tokyo War Crimes Tribunal, was a military trial convened on 29 April 1946 to try leaders of the Empire of Japan for crimes against peace, conventional war crimes, and crimes against humanity committed during the Second Sino-Japanese War and the Second World War. It was modelled on the Nuremberg trials held in Germany.

Trial

The tribunal was established by a proclamation of General Douglas MacArthur, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, in January 1946. Eleven nations provided judges, with Sir William Webb of Australia as president and Joseph B. Keenan of the United States as chief prosecutor. Twenty-eight political and military leaders were indicted as Class-A criminals; proceedings ran from May 1946 to November 1948, producing a transcript of some 49,000 pages.

Judgment

Of the 28 defendants, two died during the trial and one was declared unfit; the remaining 25 were all convicted. Seven — including former prime minister Hideki Tojo — were sentenced to death and hanged at Sugamo Prison on 23 December 1948; sixteen received life imprisonment and two lesser terms. Emperor Hirohito was not indicted, a decision that remains controversial. The Indian judge Radhabinod Pal issued a famous dissenting judgment acquitting all defendants, arguing the trial was victors' justice; the Dutch judge Bert Röling also dissented in part.

Legacy

The Tokyo Trial, together with Nuremberg, laid foundations for modern international criminal law, later developed by the tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda and the International Criminal Court. Criticisms include its selective prosecution, the immunity granted to Unit 731 personnel in exchange for their research data, and the exclusion of Allied actions from scrutiny.
