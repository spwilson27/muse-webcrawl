Taiga

Taiga (Russian: тайга) or boreal forest is a biome characterised by coniferous forests consisting mostly of pines, spruces, and larches. It is the world's largest land biome, forming a circumpolar belt across northern North America and Eurasia, and in total makes up about 29% of the world's forest cover — some 17 million km² — lying between the tundra to the north and temperate forests and steppes to the south.

Climate and soils

The taiga has a subarctic climate with very large temperature ranges: winter means below −20 °C in much of Siberia, and the coldest inhabited place on Earth, Oymyakon, lies within it. Precipitation is modest (30–85 cm annually), falling mostly as summer rain. Soils are young, nutrient-poor, and acidic beneath conifer litter; discontinuous permafrost underlies much of the biome, producing bogs, fens, and vast peatlands.

Flora and fauna

Evergreen conifers dominate, with deciduous birch, aspen, and alder in disturbed areas. Larch, uniquely deciduous among conifers, dominates the coldest Siberian taiga. Characteristic animals include moose, brown bears, lynx, wolves, wolverines, snowshoe hares, and crossbills; many species migrate or hibernate. Huge populations of breeding migratory birds use the taiga's wetlands each summer.

Ecology and human use

The taiga stores enormous quantities of carbon in its soils and peat — estimated to hold more carbon than all tropical forests — making it critical to the global climate. Natural disturbance is dominated by wildfire and insect outbreaks (such as spruce bark beetle). Human uses include logging (much of the world's industrial softwood and paper pulp), hydroelectricity, mining, and oil and gas extraction; only a small fraction of the biome is formally protected, and climate warming is shifting its southern boundary northward.
