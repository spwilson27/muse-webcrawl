Atropa belladonna, commonly known as deadly nightshade.

Atropa bella-donna, commonly known as belladonna or deadly nightshade, is a poisonous perennial herbaceous plant in the nightshade family Solanaceae, which also includes tomatoes, potatoes, and eggplant (aubergine). It is native to Europe, North Africa, and Western Asia. The foliage and berries are extremely toxic when ingested, containing tropane alkaloids including scopolamine and hyoscyamine, which cause delirium and hallucinations; the plant's roots are generally the most poisonous part.

Description

Atropa bella-donna is a branching herbaceous perennial rhizomatous hemicryptophyte, often growing as a subshrub to around 2 metres tall. It bears dull purple bell-shaped flowers and sweet, black cherry-like berries about 1.5 cm wide, which are nonetheless dangerously poisonous to humans, though eaten without harm by some animals.

Toxicity and uses

The species name belladonna ("beautiful woman" in Italian) recalls its historical cosmetic use: drops of plant extract were used to dilate the pupils. The alkaloid atropine, derived from the plant, remains important in medicine — in ophthalmology, anaesthesia, and as an antidote to certain nerve agents and pesticide poisonings. Poisoning symptoms include dilated pupils, blurred vision, dry mouth, tachycardia, delirium, and potentially death.
