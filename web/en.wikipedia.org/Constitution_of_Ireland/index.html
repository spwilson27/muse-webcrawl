Constitution of Ireland

Bunreacht na hÉireann, adopted in 1937.

The Constitution of Ireland (Irish: Bunreacht na hÉireann) is the fundamental law of Ireland. It asserts the national sovereignty of the Irish people and establishes the institutions of the state: the President, the Oireachtas (parliament, comprising Dáil Éireann and Seanad Éireann), the Government and the courts. It also guarantees certain fundamental rights to citizens.

History

The constitution was drafted under the direction of Éamon de Valera and approved by plebiscite on 1 July 1937, coming into force on 29 December 1937. It replaced the 1922 Constitution of the Irish Free State. It may be amended only by referendum. Notable amendments include the removal of the "special position" of the Catholic Church (1972), accession to the European Communities (1972), the introduction and later removal of a constitutional ban on abortion (1983; repealed 2018), the legalisation of divorce (1995), marriage equality (2015) and the removal of the offence of blasphemy (2018).

Main provisions

Article 2 and 3, as amended by the Nineteenth Amendment following the Good Friday Agreement (1998), define the national territory in terms of an aspiration to unity by consent. Articles 40–44 set out fundamental rights, including equality before the law, personal liberty, freedom of expression, assembly and association, and the protection of the family.
