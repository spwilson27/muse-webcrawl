Ash'ari

Ash'arism (Arabic: الأشعرية, romanized: al-Ashʿariyya) is a school of Islamic theology (kalam) founded by the Arab Muslim scholar, shaykh and theologian Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. 936 CE). Alongside Maturidism, Ash'arism is one of the two orthodox schools of Sunni theology and historically the most influential.

Adherents of the school are known as Ash'aris or Ash'arites. The school emerged in Basra and Baghdad in the tenth century as a reaction both to the rationalism of the Mu'tazila and to the literalism of some Hanbali scholars, seeking a middle path that used rational argument to defend the doctrines of the Quran and hadith.

History

Al-Ash'ari was originally a Mu'tazilite theologian who, according to tradition, publicly renounced Mu'tazilism around the age of forty and set out to defend traditional Sunni doctrine with the Mu'tazila's own dialectical methods. His students spread his teachings, and under scholars such as al-Baqillani, al-Juwayni, and al-Ghazali the school became dominant in Shafi'i and Maliki lands, taught in institutions such as al-Azhar in Cairo and the Nizamiyya madrasas.

Doctrines

Characteristic Ash'ari positions include the doctrine that the Quran is uncreated, the affirmation of God's attributes "without asking how" (bila kayf), the occasionalist view that God directly creates all events including human acts while humans "acquire" (kasb) their deeds, and the position that reason can know God exists but revelation is required to know good and evil. Faith (iman) is defined chiefly as assent of the heart.
