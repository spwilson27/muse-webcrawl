X-ray telescope

OpticsGrazing-incidence (Wolter) mirrors
Major observatoriesChandra, XMM-Newton, NuSTAR
First X-ray satelliteUhuru (1970)

An X-ray telescope is a telescope designed to observe distant objects in the X-ray spectrum. Because Earth's atmosphere absorbs X-rays, such telescopes must be carried to high altitudes by rockets, balloons, or — for sustained observations — placed on satellites in orbit.

Optics

X-rays cannot be focused by ordinary lenses or normal-incidence mirrors, since they are absorbed or pass through materials rather than reflecting. X-ray telescopes therefore use grazing-incidence optics, typically nested Wolter mirror assemblies in which photons reflect at very shallow angles from polished paraboloid and hyperboloid surfaces, often coated with gold or iridium. Alternative designs for hard X-rays use multilayer coatings or coded-aperture masks, as on the NuSTAR and INTEGRAL missions.

History

The first cosmic X-ray source, Scorpius X-1, was detected by a sounding rocket in 1962 in an experiment led by Riccardo Giacconi. The Uhuru satellite (1970) performed the first all-sky X-ray survey. Later observatories included the Einstein Observatory (1978), ROSAT (1990), the Chandra X-ray Observatory (1999) with sub-arcsecond imaging, and ESA's XMM-Newton (1999) with large collecting area.
