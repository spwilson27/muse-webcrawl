Gatehouse

A gatehouse is a fortified structure built to guard and control the entrance to a castle, walled town, manor house or other enclosed complex. In medieval fortification the gate was the most vulnerable point of a defensive circuit, and gatehouses consequently became some of the strongest and most elaborate parts of castles.

Medieval gatehouses

Early medieval gates were simple openings defended by a single tower. From the thirteenth century, gatehouses typically consisted of a vaulted passage flanked by two strong round or D-shaped towers, defended by a portcullis, heavy doors, murder holes in the passage roof, and arrowslits. The passage could be bent or barriered to slow attackers. Rooms above and within the towers housed guards, the constable of the castle, and sometimes a chapel or the porter's lodging; in later castles gatehouse apartments could be grand enough for important guests.

Notable examples include the gatehouse of Harlech Castle in Wales and the Byward Tower entrance to the Tower of London. In Tudor and Jacobean England the gatehouse evolved into a primarily ceremonial and residential feature of great houses — as at Hampton Court and Burghley House — while retaining a fortified appearance.

Other uses of the term

"Gatehouse" also denotes a small lodge at the entrance to an estate, park or institution, housing a gatekeeper; and, in engineering, the structure at a dam or reservoir that houses the controls for sluice gates and outlet valves.
