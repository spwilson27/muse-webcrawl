Book of Optics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Book of Optics (Arabic: كتاب المناظر, Kitāb al-Manāẓir; Latin: De Aspectibus or Perspectiva) is a seven-volume treatise on optics and other fields of study composed by the medieval Arab scholar Ibn al-Haytham, known in the West as Alhazen or Alhacen (965 – c. 1040 AD). The treatise was written while Ibn al-Haytham was under house arrest in Egypt, between 1011 and 1021.

The book had an important influence on the development of optics and the scientific method in general because its experiments were devised using a controlled scientific method. It presented the first successful intromission theory of vision, arguing that vision occurs when light rays enter the eye, against the older extramission theory that the eye emits rays.

Contents

The first three books examine the nature of light and the structure and functioning of the eye, establishing that light travels in straight lines and that each point of an illuminated object sends rays in all directions. Books four through six treat reflection from plane, spherical, cylindrical, and conical mirrors. The seventh book is devoted to refraction, including the refraction of light through the atmosphere and its effects on astronomical observations.

Experimental method

Ibn al-Haytham used experiments with a camera obscura-like device, lenses, and apertures to test his hypotheses about light and vision. He insisted that physical theories must be supported by observation and experiment, an approach that has led many historians to call him the "first true scientist" and a founder of the modern scientific method.

Influence

Translated into Latin in the late 12th or early 13th century, the De Aspectibus influenced Roger Bacon, John Pecham, Witelo, and later Johannes Kepler, whose work on optics and the retinal image built directly on Alhazen's account. The book was printed in Friedrich Risner's Opticae Thesaurus of 1572.
