Ternary form

Ternary form, sometimes called song form, is a three-part musical form consisting of an opening section (A), a following contrasting section (B), and then a repetition of the first section (A). It is usually schematised as A–B–A. The return of A may be exact or varied (A′), and the middle section typically contrasts in key, melody, texture, or mood. Ternary form is contrasted with binary form (A–B) and with the strophic forms of songs.

Characteristics

In a typical ternary structure, each section is a self-contained musical period. The A section is usually in the tonic key and harmonically closed; the B section — sometimes called the trio in marches and dances, or the episode — contrasts, often moving to the dominant, relative major/minor, or another related key; the returning A restores the opening material and key, giving the listener a sense of symmetry and completion.

Uses

Ternary form underlies the da capo aria of Baroque opera and cantata, in which the singer repeats the first section after a contrasting middle, traditionally adding ornamentation. It is the standard form of the minuet and trio (and the later scherzo and trio) in symphonies and string quartets: the movement as a whole may nest ternary forms within a larger ternary design. Marches, mazurkas, nocturnes, and many short piano pieces — as well as many popular songs with a contrasting bridge — also use the form. When the middle section is short relative to the outer sections, the result approaches rounded binary form, and analysts differ over where one category ends and the other begins.
