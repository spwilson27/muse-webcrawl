Frederick Law Olmsted

Frederick Law Olmsted (April 26, 1822 – August 28, 1903) was an American landscape architect, journalist, social critic and public administrator, widely regarded as the founder of American landscape architecture. With Calvert Vaux he designed Central Park in New York City, and he went on to create park systems in Boston, Buffalo, Chicago, Milwaukee and many other cities, as well as the grounds of the World's Columbian Exposition of 1893.

Early life and career

Olmsted worked as a seaman, farmer and journalist — his accounts of the slaveholding South, beginning with A Journey in the Seaboard Slave States (1856), made his reputation — before becoming superintendent of the future Central Park site in 1857. During the Civil War he directed the U.S. Sanitary Commission.

Design work

Olmsted and Vaux's naturalistic "pastoral" and "picturesque" landscapes favoured winding paths, broad meadows and concealed boundaries. His works include Prospect Park in Brooklyn, Boston's Emerald Necklace, the U.S. Capitol grounds, the Biltmore Estate, and the campus of Stanford University. He also championed the preservation of Niagara Falls and Yosemite.

Legacy

Olmsted's firm, continued by his sons as the Olmsted Brothers, shaped American suburbs, campuses and park systems for a century. His Brookline home and office, Fairsted, is a National Historic Site.
