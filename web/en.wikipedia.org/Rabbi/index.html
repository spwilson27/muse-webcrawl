Rabbi

Rabbi
Hebrew: רַבִּי ("my master")
Role: Teacher of Torah, spiritual leader
Ordination: Semikhah

A rabbi (Hebrew: רַבִּי, "my master") is a spiritual leader and religious teacher in Judaism. One becomes a rabbi by being ordained (semikhah) after completing a rigorous course of study of Jewish texts, principally the Torah and the Talmud, usually at a yeshiva or rabbinical seminary.

History

The title emerged in the first centuries CE, when the rabbis of Roman Palestine — the sages of the Mishnah and Talmud — succeeded the priesthood and the Sanhedrin as the authorities of Jewish religious life after the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE. In medieval and early modern communities the rabbi typically served as judge in religious courts, teacher and interpreter of Jewish law (halakha).

Modern roles

Today rabbis lead congregations, preach and teach, officiate at weddings and funerals, provide pastoral counselling, and rule on questions of religious practice. Their exact role varies among Orthodox, Conservative, Reform and Reconstructionist movements; all major movements now ordain women, beginning with Reform Judaism in 1972, while Orthodox ordination of women remains contested. Some rabbis work outside synagogues as scholars, chaplains, or heads of schools and communal organisations.
