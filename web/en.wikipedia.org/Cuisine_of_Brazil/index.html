Brazilian cuisine is the set of cooking practices and traditions of Brazil, shaped by the country's indigenous peoples and by Portuguese colonisation, enslaved Africans, and waves of Italian, German, Japanese, Arab and other immigrants. It varies strongly by region: the Afro-Brazilian kitchens of Bahia, the Amazonian cooking of the north, the barbecue culture of the southern gaúchos, and the hearty farm food of Minas Gerais are all distinct traditions under one national umbrella.

Signature dishes

The national dish is feijoada, a slow stew of black beans with pork and beef, served with rice, collard greens, orange slices and farofa (toasted cassava flour). Other emblematic foods include acarajé (black-eyed-pea fritters fried in palm oil) and moqueca (fish stew with coconut milk and dendê oil) from Bahia; tacacá and açaí from the Amazon; pão de queijo (cheese bread) and tutu de feijão from Minas Gerais; and churrasco, the southern grilled-meat tradition served in rodízio restaurants. Snacks such as coxinha and brigadeiro sweets, and drinks from caipirinha to guaraná soda, are ubiquitous.

Staples

Cassava — eaten as flour, tapioca and the root itself — is the foundational indigenous staple, while the everyday plate across Brazil is rice and beans with meat, salad and farofa.
