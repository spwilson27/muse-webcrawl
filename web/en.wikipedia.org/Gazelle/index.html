A gazelle is one of many antelope species in the genus Gazella, together with the related genera Eudorcas and Nanger. Gazelles are known as swift animals, able to run at sustained speeds around 50 km/h and burst to nearly 100 km/h, and are found in the grasslands, savannas and deserts of Africa and Asia, from the Mongolian steppe to the Arabian Peninsula and the Indian subcontinent.

Characteristics

Gazelles are slender, graceful bovids, typically 60–110 cm at the shoulder and 12–75 kg in weight. Most have fawn-coloured coats with white underparts, dark flank stripes and facial markings; both sexes usually carry ringed, lyre-shaped horns, though in some species only males are horned. They live in herds that range from a few individuals to hundreds, with males defending territories during the rut. Their speed and agility, together with the bouncing gait called stotting or pronking seen in species such as the springbok, help them evade predators including cheetahs, lions, leopards, African wild dogs and hyenas.

Species

Well-known gazelles include Thomson's gazelle (Eudorcas thomsonii) and Grant's gazelle (Nanger granti) of East Africa; the dama gazelle (Nanger dama), the largest and now critically endangered; the goitered gazelle (Gazella subgutturosa) and Mongolian gazelle of Central Asia; the chinkara (Gazella bennettii) of India; and the mountain gazelle and Arabian sand gazelle of the Middle East. The springbok of southern Africa, though often compared, belongs to the separate genus Antidorcas.

Conservation

Several species have declined sharply through hunting and habitat loss. The dama gazelle and the addra are critically endangered; the Arabian gazelle and Cuvier's gazelle are vulnerable, and the Saudi gazelle was declared extinct. Others, like Thomson's gazelle, remain numerous across protected savannas of Kenya and Tanzania.
