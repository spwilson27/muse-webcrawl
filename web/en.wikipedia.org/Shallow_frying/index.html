Shallow frying

Shallow frying uses enough oil to partially submerge the food, typically in a frying pan or sauté pan.

Shallow frying is a cooking method in which food is cooked in a shallow layer of hot oil or fat, usually in a frying pan, skillet, or sauté pan, with the oil reaching roughly one-third to one-half of the way up the sides of the food. It sits between pan frying (a thin film of oil) and deep frying (complete immersion). Foods are typically turned once during cooking so that both sides brown evenly.

Shallow frying is used for foods such as schnitzel, breaded cutlets, fritters, latkes, fried chicken (in some traditions), fish fillets, and many stir-fry-adjacent preparations. Breading or batter coatings are common because they crisp in the oil and protect the interior; the oil is typically heated to about 160–190 °C (320–375 °F). Because the food is not fully submerged, a crisp crust forms mainly where food contacts the oil, and excess oil is drained on a rack or absorbent paper after cooking.

Comparison with other methods

Compared with deep frying, shallow frying uses less oil and simpler equipment but requires turning and gives less uniform browning. Compared with pan frying, it uses more oil and suits thicker items that need more cooking time. Temperature control is important: oil that is too cool produces greasy food, while oil that is too hot burns coatings before the interior cooks.
