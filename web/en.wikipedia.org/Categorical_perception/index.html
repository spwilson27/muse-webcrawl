Categorical perception

FieldCognitive psychology; psycholinguistics
Key phenomenaBoundary effect; within-category compression
Classic domainsSpeech sounds (phonemes); colour naming

Categorical perception is a phenomenon of perception of distinct categories when there is a gradual change in a variable along a continuum. Rather than being perceived as a smooth gradient, stimuli are sorted into discrete classes: differences between stimuli that fall within the same category are compressed (perceived as smaller than they are), while differences across a category boundary are expanded. The phenomenon is often summarised by the slogan "separation is seen within categories and assimilation across categories" — that is, between-category differences are amplified and within-category differences are minimised.

Speech perception

Categorical perception was first demonstrated for speech sounds in experiments at Haskins Laboratories in the 1950s. Along a synthetic continuum from /ba/ to /da/ to /ga/, or in voice onset time between /ba/ and /pa/, listeners identify intermediate sounds as belonging sharply to one phoneme or the other, and they can discriminate two sounds much better when the sounds straddle a phoneme boundary than when equal acoustic differences lie within one category. Such findings suggested that speech is perceived in terms of the phonemic categories of one's language.

Other domains

Similar effects occur in colour perception, where the continuous spectrum is divided according to the colour terms of one's language, in facial expressions of emotion, in musical intervals, and in learned categories of many kinds. Chinchillas and other animals show categorical boundaries for some speech contrasts, indicating that auditory mechanisms of general hearing contribute to the effect; training can also create new categorical boundaries in the laboratory.

Theoretical significance

Categorical perception has been invoked in debates about linguistic relativity (the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis), the innateness of phonetic categories, and the modularity of speech perception. Later research has complicated the picture, showing that perception is rarely perfectly categorical and that within-category detail is retained, while still confirming robust boundary effects.
