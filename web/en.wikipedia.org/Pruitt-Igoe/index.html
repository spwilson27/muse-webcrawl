Pruitt-Igoe

The Pruitt-Igoe complex in St. Louis, Missouri, shortly before demolition.

The Pruitt-Igoe housing project was a large urban housing complex in St. Louis, Missouri, United States. Designed by architect Minoru Yamasaki and completed in 1954–1956, it comprised 33 eleven-storey apartment blocks containing 2,870 units on a 57-acre (23 ha) site. Originally conceived as a modernist solution to the city's slum housing, the complex suffered from underfunding, declining occupancy, racial segregation, and deteriorating conditions, and it was demolished between 1972 and 1976. Its dramatic failure became a symbol in debates about modernist architecture, public housing policy, and urban renewal.

History

The complex was named for Wendell O. Pruitt, an African American fighter pilot from St. Louis, and William L. Igoe, a former US congressman. Under the segregated planning of the era, the Pruitt buildings were intended for black residents and the Igoe buildings for white residents; following the end of legal segregation in public housing, the complex was integrated but white residents largely departed. Federal funding covered construction but not operating costs, which were to be met from tenants' rents; as occupancy fell and St. Louis's population and industrial base shrank, maintenance collapsed. By the late 1960s large parts of the complex stood vacant and vandalised.

Demolition and symbolism

On 16 March 1972 the first building was demolished by controlled implosion, an event broadcast on television; demolition was completed in 1976. Architectural historian Charles Jencks famously declared that "Modern Architecture died in St. Louis, Missouri on July 15, 1972 at 3.32 p.m." — dating the supposed death of modernism to the demolition of another Pruitt-Igoe block. Later scholarship, notably the 2011 documentary The Pruitt-Igoe Myth, has emphasised that the project's failure owed more to funding structures, segregation, and deindustrialisation than to its architectural design alone.

The site today

The site remained largely vacant for decades, part of it occupied by schools and woodland that colonised the empty lots. In the 2020s the area was selected for redevelopment, including a new campus for the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency and associated projects.
