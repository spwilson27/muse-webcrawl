Period (music)

A typical eight-bar period: antecedent phrase ending with a half cadence, consequent ending with a perfect authentic cadence.

In music theory, the term period refers to a group of phrases consisting usually of at least one antecedent phrase and one consequent phrase, totalling about 8 to 16 bars in length, and forming a complete musical thought. The antecedent ends with a relatively weak cadence (commonly a half cadence), posing a musical "question", while the consequent ends with a stronger cadence (commonly a perfect authentic cadence), giving the "answer".

Types

A parallel period has antecedent and consequent phrases that begin with similar melodic material; a contrasting period uses different opening material in the consequent. A period is symmetrical when the phrases are of equal length (typically four bars each), and asymmetrical otherwise. In a double period, two antecedent–consequent pairs are combined.

History of the term

The concept descends from rhetoric, where a period is a complete sentence; eighteenth-century theorists such as Heinrich Christoph Koch applied phrase terminology drawn from grammar to musical form. The period is a foundational unit of structure in Classical-era themes and in song forms generally.
