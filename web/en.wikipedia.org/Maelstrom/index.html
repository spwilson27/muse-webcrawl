Maelstrom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A maelstrom is a powerful whirlpool formed in a body of water, typically where opposing tidal currents meet over an irregular seabed. The word comes from the Dutch maelstroom ("grinding stream"), popularised in English through descriptions of the Moskstraumen off the Lofoten Islands of Norway, one of the strongest tidal currents in the world. In common usage, "maelstrom" has become a general word for a violent whirlpool and, figuratively, for any turbulent or chaotic situation.

Famous maelstroms

The Moskstraumen, between the Lofoten islands of Moskenesøya and Værøy, is generated by strong tidal currents passing through shallow sounds, with currents reaching speeds reported up to 32 km/h in the wider system. Norway's Saltstraumen, near Bodø, is often cited as the world's strongest maelstrom, where up to 400 million tonnes of seawater force through a 3 km-long, 150 m-wide strait every six hours, forming whirlpools up to 10 metres across. Other notable examples include the Naruto whirlpools in Japan, the Old Sow between Maine and New Brunswick, and the Corryvreckan in Scotland.

In literature

The Moskstraumen entered literature through Edgar Allan Poe's short story "A Descent into the Maelström" (1841), whose narrator survives by observing which objects the vortex draws down slowest, and Jules Verne's Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Seas (1870), whose climax takes place in the maelstrom. Earlier, the whirlpool appeared in the 1555 Historia de Gentibus Septentrionalibus of Olaus Magnus, whose dramatic maps and descriptions fixed its fearsome reputation in European imagination.

See also

Related phenomena include the volcanic crater lakes of Maderas and the lochs and narrows described under Maeshowe and the Orkney Islands.
