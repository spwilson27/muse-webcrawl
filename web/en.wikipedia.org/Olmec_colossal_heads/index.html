The Olmec colossal heads are seventeen monumental stone sculptures carved from large basalt boulders by the pre-Columbian Olmec civilization of ancient Mesoamerica. Dating from at least 900 BCE, they are among the earliest and most famous works of monumental art in the Americas.

Characteristics

The heads range from about 1.5 to 3.4 metres in height and weigh between roughly 6 and 50 tons. Each depicts a mature man with fleshy cheeks, a flat nose, and slightly crossed eyes, wearing a distinctive close-fitting headdress resembling a helmet — features that have led most scholars to interpret them as portraits of individual Olmec rulers. No two heads are alike.

Production and transport

The basalt came from the Cerro Cintepec in the Tuxtla Mountains, some 80 to 150 kilometres from the sites where the heads were erected. How the Olmecs moved such masses — probably by raft along rivers and rolling on land — remains imperfectly understood and testifies to a large, organized labour force.

Discovery and fate

The first head was reported at Tres Zapotes in 1862; systematic excavation followed in the 20th century at La Venta and San Lorenzo. Many heads were deliberately buried, and some were mutilated — possibly through ritual "killing" of a ruler's monument, dynastic overthrow, or recycling attempts. Most originals are now in Mexican museums, particularly in Xalapa and Villahermosa, with replicas at some sites.
