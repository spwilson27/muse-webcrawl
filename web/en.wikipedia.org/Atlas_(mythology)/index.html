Atlas (Greek: Ἄτλας) is a Titan in Greek mythology who, after the defeat of the Titans by the Olympian gods, was condemned to stand at the western edge of the world and hold up the sky (or, in later depictions, the celestial sphere) for eternity. He is the father of the Pleiades and of Calypso, and in Plato's account the first king of Atlantis.

Mythology

Atlas was the son of the Titan Iapetus and the Oceanid Clymene, and brother of Prometheus, Epimetheus and Menoetius. In Hesiod's Theogony, Zeus assigned Atlas his burden as punishment for leading the Titans in the Titanomachy. Homer's Odyssey describes his daughter Calypso detaining Odysseus on the island of Ogygia.

In one of the twelve labours of Heracles, the hero offered to hold the sky while Atlas fetched the golden apples of the Hesperides. On returning, Atlas tried to leave Heracles beneath the burden, but Heracles tricked him into resuming it by asking him to take the weight briefly while he padded his shoulders. Another late myth, relating Atlas to the Atlas Mountains, tells how Perseus turned the Titan to stone with the head of Medusa.

Legacy

Atlas's image carrying the heavens became a standard motif in art, famously in the second-century Roman Farnese Atlas statue. Because Gerardus Mercator placed a picture of the Titan on the title page of his collected maps (1595), a book of maps has been called an "atlas" ever since. His name also survives in the Atlantic Ocean ("Sea of Atlas"), the Atlas Mountains of North Africa, and the first cervical vertebra, the atlas, which supports the skull.
