Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum

The Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM), alternatively the Eocene thermal maximum 1 (ETM1) or the Initial Eocene Thermal Maximum (IETM), was a period of rapid global warming that occurred approximately 55.8 million years ago, at the boundary between the Paleocene and Eocene epochs. Global temperatures rose by roughly 5–8 °C over a geologically brief interval (on the order of a few thousand to tens of thousands of years), with the warming lasting about 170,000–200,000 years in total.

Cause

The PETM is marked by a pronounced negative carbon isotope excursion, indicating a massive release of carbon depleted in carbon-13 into the ocean–atmosphere system. Proposed sources include the dissociation of methane clathrates on continental margins, volcanic activity associated with the North Atlantic Igneous Province, the burning of peat and coal deposits, and permafrost or soil carbon feedbacks. The total release is often estimated in the thousands of gigatonnes of carbon, comparable in magnitude (though slower) to potential future anthropogenic emissions.

Effects

The oceans warmed and acidified; carbonate dissolution horizons in deep-sea sediments record severe undersaturation, and 30–50% of benthic foraminiferal species went extinct — the largest deep-sea extinction of the past 90 million years. On land, mammals responded with dwarfing, range shifts toward the poles, and rapid evolutionary turnover, including the first appearances of several modern orders such as early primates and even-toed and odd-toed ungulates. Rainfall patterns changed, with some regions becoming wetter and others more seasonal.

Recovery and significance

Temperatures returned to background levels as carbon was sequestered by silicate weathering and organic burial over roughly 150,000–200,000 years. The PETM is widely studied as the closest geological analogue for rapid anthropogenic climate change, particularly for ocean acidification and carbon-cycle feedbacks.
