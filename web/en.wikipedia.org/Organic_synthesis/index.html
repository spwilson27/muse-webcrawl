Organic synthesis is a branch of chemical synthesis concerned with the intentional construction of organic compounds — molecules containing carbon — from simpler starting materials. It is a central activity of organic chemistry, enabling the preparation of pharmaceuticals, natural products, polymers, and novel materials not available from nature.

History

The field is traditionally dated to 1828, when Friedrich Wöhler synthesized urea from inorganic ammonium cyanate, undermining the doctrine of vitalism. Nineteenth-century milestones included the synthesis of dyes such as mauveine (Perkin, 1856). In the 20th century, Robert Burns Woodward's total syntheses of complex natural products — including vitamin B12 (completed with Albert Eschenmoser in 1973) — raised synthesis to an art form, and E. J. Corey formalized retrosynthetic analysis, earning the 1990 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Methods

Synthetic planning works backwards from the target molecule (retrosynthesis) by breaking bonds at strategic points into synthons and then available reagents. Key tools include functional-group interconversions, carbon–carbon bond-forming reactions (Grignard, aldol, Diels–Alder, and palladium-catalysed cross-couplings such as Suzuki and Heck reactions), protecting groups, oxidation and reduction methods, and stereoselective techniques for controlling chirality. Total synthesis of natural products remains a proving ground, while combinatorial chemistry, flow chemistry, and green chemistry address library production, scale-up, and sustainability.

Applications

Industrial organic synthesis produces most small-molecule drugs (aspirin, penicillin derivatives, statins), agrochemicals, fragrances, and specialty polymers. Synthetic routes must balance yield, cost, safety, and environmental impact at scale.
