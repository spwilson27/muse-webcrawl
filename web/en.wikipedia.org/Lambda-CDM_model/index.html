Lambda-CDM model

Type: Cosmological model (Big Bang)
Components: ~5% ordinary matter, ~27% dark matter, ~68% dark energy
Key parameters: Six basic parameters fit observations

The Lambda-CDM (ΛCDM) model is the standard model of Big Bang cosmology. In it, the universe contains a cosmological constant (denoted by the Greek capital letter lambda Λ, associated with dark energy), ordinary matter, and cold dark matter (CDM). It is frequently called the standard model of cosmology because it accounts for a wide range of observed phenomena with a small set of parameters.

Features explained

ΛCDM successfully describes the cosmic microwave background radiation and its anisotropies, the large-scale structure of galaxies, the abundances of hydrogen, helium and lithium from Big Bang nucleosynthesis, and the accelerating expansion of the universe discovered through distant supernovae in 1998. Space is taken to be nearly flat, and the model assumes the cosmological principle — large-scale homogeneity and isotropy.

Parameters and composition

Measurements, notably from the Planck satellite, indicate the present-day universe consists of roughly 5% ordinary (baryonic) matter, 27% dark matter, and 68% dark energy, with a Hubble constant near 67–68 km/s/Mpc and an age of about 13.8 billion years. Six parameters suffice to fit most cosmological data.

Open questions

The physical nature of dark matter and dark energy remains unknown, and discrepancies such as the "Hubble tension" between early- and late-universe measurements of the expansion rate have motivated refinements and alternative models. Nevertheless ΛCDM remains the benchmark against which cosmological theories are judged.
