Performance practice

Performance practice refers to the way in which music is performed, particularly the conventions and playing techniques characteristic of a given period, place or tradition, as distinct from what is written in the musical score. The study of performance practice is a branch of musicology and is central to the historically informed performance movement.

Scope

Performance practice research draws on evidence such as treatises on playing technique, historical instruments that survive in museums, iconography, accounts of concerts, and the notation itself, which in earlier eras assumed knowledge that performers of the time supplied automatically. Matters investigated include tempo, articulation, dynamics, ornamentation, improvisation, vibrato, tuning and temperament, the size and constitution of ensembles, and the pitch standards in use.

Historical development

In medieval and Renaissance music, much depended on unwritten conventions: singers added musica ficta (inflections not in the notation), and instrumentalists improvised divisions on repeated strains. Baroque performance expected the realisation of figured bass by continuo players, the addition of ornaments, and flexible rhythm in styles such as the French notes inégales. Classical and Romantic sources, from tutors by Quantz and C.P.E. Bach to early recordings, show that practices such as portamento, tempo rubato and sparse vibrato differed markedly from much twentieth-century playing.

Historically informed performance

From the mid-twentieth century, performers such as Nikolaus Harnoncourt, Gustav Leonhardt and Christopher Hogwood sought to recover period practices and to play on original instruments or copies, giving rise to the historically informed performance (HIP) movement, which reshaped the mainstream performance of early music and, increasingly, of classical and Romantic repertoire.
