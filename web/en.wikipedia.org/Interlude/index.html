Interlude

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An interlude is a short entertainment or piece of music performed between the parts of a larger work — between the acts of a play, during a concert programme, or within a religious service. The word derives from the Latin interludium (inter, "between", and ludus, "play").

Theatre

In medieval and Tudor England, interludes were brief dramatic entertainments performed at feasts and in noble households, often between courses of a banquet or between the parts of a longer performance. Distinct from the morality plays, interludes tended toward farce and secular comedy; John Heywood's The Play of the Weather (c. 1533) is a well-known example. These household dramas helped bridge the gap between medieval allegorical theatre and the professional playhouses of the Elizabethan era.

Music

In music, an interlude is a connecting passage or short independent piece. It appears as an intermezzo between movements or scenes of an opera, as organ interludes improvising between verses of a hymn or psalm in church services, and as instrumental passages linking songs in popular music albums and concept albums. In jazz and theatre organ performance, interludes fill transitions and scene changes. The concept overlaps with the entr'acte, intermezzo, and divertissement.

Other uses

More generally, "interlude" denotes any intervening period or episode that contrasts with what precedes and follows it — a brief pause in activity, a quiet passage in a narrative, or a temporary interruption in a sequence of events.
