Light cruiser

A light cruiser is a type of small- or medium-sized warship. The term is a shortening of "light armored cruiser", describing a small ship that carried armour in the same way as an armoured cruiser: a protective belt and deck. Smaller and lighter than other cruisers, light cruisers were intended for scouting, screening the battle fleet, leading destroyer flotillas and raiding commerce.

History

The type emerged in the early 20th century from protected cruisers; the Royal Navy's Town-class and Arethusa-class ships of the First World War were typical early examples. The 1922 Washington Naval Treaty and the 1930 London Naval Treaty formalised the category: "light cruisers" were defined as cruisers with guns of 6.1-inch (155 mm) calibre or less, as opposed to heavy cruisers with 8-inch guns.

Second World War

During the Second World War, light cruisers served in every major navy. Classes such as the American Cleveland class, the British Fiji class and the Japanese Agano class provided anti-aircraft cover, shore bombardment and surface action capability. Japan's Mogami class, initially built with 155 mm guns to exploit treaty definitions, blurred the line with heavy cruisers after re-arming.

Postwar

After 1945 the role was largely absorbed by destroyers and frigates equipped with guided missiles. A small number of gun-armed light cruisers remained in service into the late 20th century, notably in South American navies; the Peruvian Almirante Grau (ex-Dutch De Ruyter), retired in 2017, was the last in service.
