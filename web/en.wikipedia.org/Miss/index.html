Miss

Miss is an English-language honorific traditionally used for an unmarried woman, as in "Miss Jane Austen." It is a shortened form of "mistress," the older feminine counterpart of "master," and has been in use since the early 17th century. Unlike "Mrs.," which historically indicated a married woman, "Miss" traditionally carried no implication about age, though it was most often applied to younger women and girls.

History and usage

"Mistress" was once a general title of respect for women regardless of marital status. By the 18th and 19th centuries, usage crystallised so that "Miss" marked a never-married woman and "Mrs." a married or widowed one—a distinction that applied to women but not to men, whose title "Mr." reveals nothing about marriage. Twentieth-century feminists criticised this asymmetry, and the revival of "Ms."—a title that does not specify marital status—from the 1970s onward gave women an alternative; "Ms." was famously promoted by Ms. magazine, founded in 1971.

"Miss" remains standard address for schoolgirls in many Commonwealth schools (where female teachers are often called "Miss" regardless of status), in beauty pageants (Miss World, Miss Universe), in service and hospitality contexts, and in the southern United States, where "Miss" combined with a first name is a respectful form for women of any age or marital status. In modern business correspondence, "Ms." is often preferred unless a woman has indicated another preference.
