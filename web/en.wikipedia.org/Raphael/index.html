Raffaello Sanzio da Urbino (28 March or 6 April 1483 – 6 April 1520), now generally known in English as Raphael, was an Italian painter and architect of the High Renaissance. His work is admired for its clarity of form, ease of composition, and visual achievement of the Neoplatonic ideal of human grandeur. Together with Leonardo da Vinci and Michelangelo, he forms the traditional trinity of great masters of that period. Despite dying at only 37, Raphael was enormously productive and ran the largest workshop of his age.

Early life and training

Raphael was born in Urbino, a small but refined court city in the Marche. His father, Giovanni Santi, was court painter to Duke Federico da Montefeltro, and Raphael learned the basics of painting in his workshop before Giovanni's death in 1494. By about 1500 he was working with Pietro Perugino in Perugia, whose clear, sweet style shaped Raphael's early works such as The Marriage of the Virgin (1504). In Florence (1504–1508) he absorbed the innovations of Leonardo — pyramidal compositions, sfumato modelling — and of the young Michelangelo, producing a celebrated series of Madonnas, including Madonna del Prato and La Belle Jardinière.

Rome: the Vatican works

Summoned to Rome by Pope Julius II in late 1508, Raphael received the defining commission of his career: the fresco decoration of the papal apartments now called the Raphael Rooms (Stanze). In the Stanza della Segnatura he painted The School of Athens (1509–1511), a gathering of Greek philosophers beneath soaring classical architecture — Plato and Aristotle at its centre — which has become an icon of Renaissance humanism, alongside The Parnassus and the Disputa. Further rooms, the Stanza di Eliodoro and Stanza dell'Incendio, continued under Julius and Leo X. For the Sistine Chapel he designed the ten Raphael Cartoons (1515–16) for tapestries of the lives of Saints Peter and Paul. His easel paintings of this period include the Sistine Madonna (1512), the Galatea fresco in the Villa Farnesina, and many portraits, culminating in the visionary Transfiguration (1516–1520), left unfinished at his death.

Architecture and later years

After Bramante's death in 1514, Raphael was appointed architect of St Peter's Basilica, and he also designed palaces and the Villa Madama. He was made commissioner of antiquities for Rome, mapping the ancient city and lamenting the destruction of its ruins. Raphael died in Rome on 6 April 1520 — his 37th birthday, according to Vasari — after a short fever, and was buried with great honour in the Pantheon.

Legacy

Vasari's Lives presented Raphael as the embodiment of grace (grazia) and the culmination of art's progress. Academic art teaching revered him for centuries; the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood (founded 1848) rebelled precisely against the academic idealisation of his later style, naming themselves for art before Raphael. His harmonious, lucid compositions remain central to the Western canon.
