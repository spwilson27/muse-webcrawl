Organistrum

Organistrum
Classification: String instrument (wheel fiddle)
Period: Medieval, from c. 10th century
Players required: Two
Successor: Hurdy-gurdy

The organistrum is an early form of the hurdy-gurdy, a stringed instrument in which a hand-cranked, rosined wheel acts as a bow on the strings. The organistrum was generally played in religious settings in medieval Europe, from around the 10th to 13th centuries.

Description

The organistrum was large — around a metre and a half long — and required two players, one to turn the crank and the other to work the tangents (keys) that stopped the melody strings. It typically had three strings: two drones and one melodic string passing through the body. Its size made it suitable only for slow, solemn music, and it was used in monasteries and churches, probably to accompany chant.

History

The instrument is depicted in church sculptures, most famously on the Pórtico da Gloria of Santiago de Compostela Cathedral. As instrument making improved, smaller single-player versions emerged — called symphonie and later hurdy-gurdy — freeing the design from the church and spreading it into courtly and folk music.
