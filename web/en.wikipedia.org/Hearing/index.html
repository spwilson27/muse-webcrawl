Hearing

Hearing, or auditory perception, is the ability to perceive sounds through an organ, such as an ear, by detecting vibrations as changes in the pressure of the surrounding medium. It is one of the five traditional senses. The field concerned with hearing is auditory science. The inability to hear is called deafness or hearing loss.

Mechanism

In humans, sound waves are collected by the outer ear and funnelled through the ear canal to the eardrum (tympanic membrane), which vibrates. The vibrations are transmitted by the three ossicles of the middle ear — the malleus, incus, and stapes — to the oval window of the cochlea in the inner ear. Within the cochlea, sensory hair cells in the organ of Corti convert the mechanical vibrations into electrical signals, which travel along the auditory nerve to the brainstem and auditory cortex, where they are interpreted as sound.

Range and perception

A healthy young human can typically hear frequencies from about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Sensitivity is greatest in the range of human speech, roughly 1,000–4,000 Hz. As people age, high-frequency sensitivity declines (presbycusis). Many animals hear ranges far beyond the human one: dogs and bats detect ultrasound, while elephants and some fish perceive infrasound. Loudness is measured in decibels; sustained exposure above about 85 dB can damage hearing.

Hearing loss and testing

Hearing can be impaired by damage to the conductive pathway (conductive loss), to the cochlea or auditory nerve (sensorineural loss), or both. Hearing is assessed with audiometry, which plots an individual's threshold across frequencies on an audiogram. Treatments include hearing aids, and, for severe sensorineural loss, cochlear implants.
