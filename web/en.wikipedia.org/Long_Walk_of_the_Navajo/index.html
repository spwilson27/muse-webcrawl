Long Walk of the Navajo

The Long Walk of the Navajo, also called the Long Walk to Bosque Redondo (Navajo: Hwéeldi), was the forced deportation and ethnic cleansing of the Navajo people by the United States federal government between 1864 and 1866. Following a scorched-earth campaign led by Colonel Kit Carson, some 8,500–9,000 Navajo men, women and children were marched — in a series of journeys of roughly 300 miles (480 km) — from their homelands in northeastern Arizona and northwestern New Mexico to an internment camp at Bosque Redondo near Fort Sumner, New Mexico.

Background

Decades of reciprocal raiding between Navajo, New Mexican settlers, and other peoples preceded the American Civil War era. When the US Army under General James Carleton sought to remove the Navajo to Bosque Redondo — where the Mescalero Apache had already been interned — Carson's 1863–64 campaign destroyed crops, orchards and livestock, including the famous peach orchards of Canyon de Chelly, starving the Navajo into surrender. Leaders such as Manuelito and Barboncito held out longest; Barboncito was the last to surrender, in 1866.

The marches and internment

More than fifty separate marches took place, along several routes. Many Navajo died of disease, exposure and starvation on the way; oral histories record particular cruelty. At Bosque Redondo the experiment failed: alkaline water, poor soil, crop failures, disease and Comanche raids made the camp untenable, and the internment cost the government millions of dollars. Roughly 2,300 Navajo died during the four-year exile.

Treaty of 1868 and legacy

In 1868, Generals William Tecumseh Sherman and Samuel Tappan negotiated the Navajo Treaty (Naal Tsoos Saní, the "Old Paper"), signed on 1 June 1868. Unusually among removal treaties, it allowed the Navajo to return to a portion of their ancestral homeland — a reservation of some 3.5 million acres — rather than being removed to Indian Territory. The Long Walk remains a defining trauma and a symbol of resilience in Navajo collective memory, commemorated as Hwéeldi.
