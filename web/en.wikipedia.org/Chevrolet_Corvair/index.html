The Chevrolet Corvair is a compact car manufactured by Chevrolet for model years 1960–1969 in two generations. It was the only American-designed, mass-produced passenger car with a rear-mounted, air-cooled flat-six engine, and it pioneered turbocharging in production automobiles with the Corvair Monza Spyder of 1962.

Design

Developed under Ed Cole as GM's answer to the Volkswagen Beetle and the Rambler compacts, the Corvair used unibody construction and an aluminium flat-six mounted behind the rear axle. Body styles included sedans, coupes, convertibles, station wagons, and forward-control vans and pickups (the Corvair 95 series). The sporty Monza coupe helped popularise the compact sporty-car segment in America.

Safety controversy

The first generation's swing-axle rear suspension was criticised for unpredictable handling in Ralph Nader's 1965 book Unsafe at Any Speed, whose first chapter targeted the Corvair and helped spur US federal vehicle safety legislation. GM had fully redesigned the suspension by 1965, and a later government study found the early Corvair's handling comparable to its contemporaries, but sales collapsed and production ended in 1969.
