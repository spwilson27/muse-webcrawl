Reading Abbey is a large, ruined abbey in the centre of the town of Reading, in the English county of Berkshire. It was founded by Henry I in 1121, "for the salvation of my soul, and the souls of King William, my father, and of William, my brother, and Queen Maud, my wife, and all my ancestors and successors". In its heyday the abbey was one of the largest and richest monasteries in England.

History

The abbey was a Cluniac house, initially staffed by monks from Cluny in France and from the priory of St Pancras at Lewes. It stood at the confluence of the River Thames and the River Kennet, on land well placed for travellers on the road between London and the west. The abbey church was consecrated by Thomas Becket in 1164.

Henry I was buried in the abbey church in 1136, in front of the high altar, and the abbey became an important place of pilgrimage, possessing among its relics the hand of St James, which attracted offerings from across Europe. Henry III was a frequent visitor, and parliament met at the abbey on occasion. The song "Sumer Is Icumen In", the earliest known English round, was written down by a Reading monk in the thirteenth century.

Dissolution and after

The abbey was surrendered during the Dissolution of the Monasteries. In 1539 its last abbot, Hugh Faringdon, was tried for treason and hanged, drawn and quartered outside the abbey gateway after refusing to acknowledge Henry VIII's supremacy. The buildings were extensively plundered and much of the stone was reused elsewhere in the town. The abbey church was largely demolished under Edward VI.

The surviving ruins — including the chapter house, refectory and dormitory ranges, and the inner gateway — are a scheduled monument and Grade I listed. After a major conservation project, the ruins reopened to the public in June 2018 under the banner "Reading Abbey Revealed". Oscar Wilde wrote The Ballad of Reading Gaol after his imprisonment in the gaol built beside the abbey site, and Jane Austen attended school in the abbey gateway in 1785.
