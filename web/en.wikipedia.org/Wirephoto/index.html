Wirephoto

Wirephoto, also rendered telephotography or radiophoto in its various forms, is the transmission of photographs by telephone or telegraph wire — and later by radio — so that a picture taken in one place can be reproduced and printed by a newspaper in another. The best-known system was the Associated Press WirePhoto network, launched in 1935, which transformed the speed with which news photographs could appear in print.

History

Experiments in sending pictures electrically date to the early 20th century: Édouard Belin's bélinographe and AT&T's telephotography service of the 1920s used a rotating drum and a photoelectric cell to scan a photograph line by line, converting brightness into a varying electrical signal. At the receiving end, a synchronised drum exposed photographic paper or film through a modulated light source, recreating the image.

The Associated Press began its WirePhoto service on 1 January 1935, initially linking a network of American cities; the first image transmitted showed an aircrash in New York state. Portable transmitters allowed photographers to send pictures from the field over ordinary telephone lines, and wirephoto became a standard part of news-gathering for decades, alongside rival services from United Press and others.

Legacy

Wirephoto technology is an ancestor of the fax machine and of modern digital image transmission. Analogue wirephoto networks were displaced in the 1980s and 1990s by digital photography and satellite and internet transmission, but "wire photo" survived as a newsroom term for agency pictures distributed to subscribers.
