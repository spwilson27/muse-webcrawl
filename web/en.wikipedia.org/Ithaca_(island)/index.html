Ithaca (Greek: Ιθάκη, Itháki) is a Greek island in the Ionian Sea, northeast of Cephalonia, from which it is separated by a narrow strait. It is the second-smallest of the seven main Ionian Islands and forms its own regional unit within the Ionian Islands region. Ithaca is famous worldwide as the mythical home of Odysseus, whose ten-year return voyage from the Trojan War is the subject of Homer's Odyssey.

Geography

The island covers about 118 km² and consists of two mountainous lobes joined by a narrow isthmus, with Mount Nirito (806 m) in the north. Its deeply indented coast has many small bays; the main harbour, Vathy, sits in a sheltered, almost landlocked bay on the east coast and serves as the capital and principal port, with ferries to Cephalonia, Lefkada, and Patras. The landscape is dry and rocky, clothed in olive groves, cypresses, and maquis.

History

Archaeology shows habitation from the Neolithic period, and Mycenaean finds — including a cave sanctuary at Polis Bay where a tripod bore an inscription to Odysseus in later centuries — attest to an early cult of the hero. In antiquity the island belonged to the Cephalonian sphere; in medieval and early modern times it passed through Norman, Angevin, Venetian (1504–1797), French, and British rule, joining the Kingdom of Greece in 1864. Like its neighbours, Ithaca suffered severe damage in the great Ionian earthquake of August 1953, after which its villages were rebuilt. Many Ithacans emigrated in the 19th and 20th centuries, notably to Australia and South Africa, and diaspora remittances remain significant.

The Homeric question

Whether modern Ithaca is Homer's Ithaca has been debated since antiquity: the Odyssey describes Ithaca as the westernmost of its neighbouring islands, "low-lying" and rugged, which fits imperfectly. Alternative identifications have included Cephalonia and its Paliki peninsula (advanced in the 2005 book Odysseus Unbound) and Lefkada. Excavations on Ithaca itself, including a large Mycenaean-era complex at Agios Athanasios announced in 2010 as a possible palace of Odysseus, keep the traditional identification alive, and the island's identity is in any case firmly bound to the poem: "Ithaca" has become a universal symbol of homeland and the goal of a long journey, as in Constantine Cavafy's celebrated poem Ithaca (1911).
