Pollinator decline

Affected groupsBees, butterflies, moths, other insect pollinators
Major driversHabitat loss, pesticides, pathogens, climate change
At stakePollination of ~75% of leading food crops

Pollinator decline is the reduction in abundance and diversity of pollinating animals — especially insects such as bees and butterflies — documented in many parts of the world since the late twentieth century. Because roughly three-quarters of the world's leading food crops benefit from animal pollination, the trend is a significant concern for agriculture and ecosystems.

Causes

The drivers interact and include habitat loss and fragmentation, intensive agriculture and monocultures, pesticides (notably neonicotinoid insecticides), diseases and parasites such as the Varroa destructor mite in honey bees, invasive species, light pollution, and climate change disrupting the timing of flowering and insect emergence.

Evidence

Long-term monitoring shows sharp declines in bumblebee ranges, regional losses of butterfly species, and large drops in flying-insect biomass, such as the widely cited 2017 German study finding a decline of over 75 percent in protected areas over 27 years. Managed honey bee colonies have suffered episodes of colony collapse disorder, though global hive numbers have not fallen overall.

Responses

Responses include restrictions on neonicotinoids in the European Union, pollinator-friendly planting and habitat corridors, agri-environment schemes, and citizen-science monitoring. The IPBES 2016 assessment report on pollinators was the first global scientific assessment devoted to the issue.
