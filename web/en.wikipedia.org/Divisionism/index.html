Divisionism

Divisionism, also called chromoluminarism, is the characteristic style in Neo-Impressionist painting defined by the separation of colors into individual dots or patches which interact optically. By requiring the viewer to combine the colors optically instead of physically mixing pigments, Divisionists believed they could achieve a greater luminosity than by the conventional means. The scientific basis came from the color theories of Michel Eugène Chevreul, Ogden Rood, and Charles Henry, among others.

Origins

Divisionism was first developed by the French artists Georges Seurat and Paul Signac in the mid-1880s. Seurat's A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte (1884–86) is the canonical early example. When the individual strokes consist of small dots the technique is commonly called Pointillism, a term coined by critics; artists such as Henri-Edmond Cross and Maximilien Luce extended the method.

In Italy

In Italy, Divisionism (Italian: Divisionismo) became a major movement in its own right, practiced by artists including Giovanni Segantini, Giuseppe Pellizza da Volpedo, Angelo Morbelli, and Gaetano Previati. Italian Divisionists used longer, thread-like strokes and frequently combined the technique with symbolist and social themes, as in Pellizza's The Fourth Estate. The style strongly influenced the young Futurists, notably Umberto Boccioni and Gino Severini.

Technique and theory

Divisionist painters avoided blending pigments on the palette, instead placing touches of pure color side by side so that they fused in the viewer's eye — an application of simultaneous contrast and optical mixture. Because pigments mix subtractively and appear duller, optical mixing was expected to preserve luminosity, although critics have noted that the theoretical gains were often overstated.
