Cyclogenesis

A maturing extratropical cyclone showing the characteristic comma-shaped cloud pattern.

Cyclogenesis is the development or strengthening of cyclonic circulation in the atmosphere — the formation of a low-pressure system, or cyclone. The term most often refers to the birth and intensification of extratropical cyclones in the midlatitudes, but it also applies to tropical cyclones (tropical cyclogenesis) and to mesoscale systems. The opposite process, the weakening and dissipation of a cyclone, is cyclolysis.

Extratropical cyclogenesis

Midlatitude cyclones form along the polar front, the boundary between cold polar air and warm subtropical air. The classical Norwegian cyclone model, developed by Vilhelm Bjerknes and collaborators after the First World War, describes a wave forming on a stationary front, deepening as warm air is lifted ahead of the low and cold air undercuts behind it, and finally occluding as the cold front overtakes the warm front. The alternative Shapiro–Keyser model, common for intense maritime storms, features a bent-back front and frontal fracture rather than a classic occlusion.

In modern dynamical terms, cyclogenesis occurs where upper-level divergence — associated with a jet streak or an approaching short-wave trough — removes mass above a low-level zone of temperature contrast (a baroclinic zone), causing surface pressure to fall. Latent heat released in clouds and surface fluxes over warm oceans can greatly amplify development. When central pressure falls by at least 24 hPa in 24 hours (adjusted for latitude), the process is called explosive cyclogenesis, and the resulting storm is popularly termed a "bomb cyclone".

Tropical cyclogenesis

Tropical cyclones form over warm ocean water (typically above 26.5 °C) where the Coriolis effect is sufficient — usually at least 5° from the equator — given pre-existing disturbed weather, low vertical wind shear and moist air through the depth of the troposphere. Forecasting genesis in both basins relies heavily on numerical weather prediction and indices derived from these ingredients.

Stages

A typical extratropical lifecycle runs: incipient wave → deepening (mature) cyclone with distinct warm and cold fronts → occluded, slowly filling low. Satellite imagery reveals the development through cloud signatures such as the leaf, comma and dry-intrusion patterns.
