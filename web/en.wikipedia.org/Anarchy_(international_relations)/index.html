Anarchy (international relations)

In international relations theory, anarchy is the absence of any authority above the level of the sovereign state — there is no world government, no higher power that can reliably make and enforce rules among states. The concept is foundational to realism and especially to Kenneth Waltz's neorealism, which treats anarchy as the "ordering principle" of the international system, contrasted with the hierarchy that orders domestic politics.

Implications in theory

Because no higher authority guarantees their security, states in anarchy are said to inhabit a self-help system: each must provide for its own survival, which drives security competition, balancing of power, and the security dilemma, in which one state's defensive measures appear threatening to others. Liberals and institutionalists reply that international institutions, law, trade, and norms can mitigate the effects of anarchy, while constructivists — in Alexander Wendt's famous phrase, "anarchy is what states make of it" — argue that its consequences depend on how states understand their identities and relationships, which can range from enmity to friendship.

Usage

The term does not imply chaos or disorder; it denotes only the absence of central government. Debates about how constraining anarchy really is — and whether features such as international organizations, nuclear weapons, or shared culture modify it — remain central to the discipline.
