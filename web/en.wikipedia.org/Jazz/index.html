Jazz

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jazz is a music genre that originated in the African-American communities of New Orleans, Louisiana, in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, with roots in blues, ragtime, and West African and European musical traditions. It is characterised by swing rhythms, syncopation, call-and-response, and — above all — improvisation, in which soloists create spontaneous variations over a song's chord changes.

History

New Orleans jazz (or "Dixieland") developed around brass bands and pianists such as Buddy Bolden and Jelly Roll Morton; Louis Armstrong's recordings of the 1920s transformed jazz into a soloist's art. The music migrated to Chicago and New York, where the big-band swing of Duke Ellington, Count Basie, and Benny Goodman made it America's popular music in the 1930s–40s. In the 1940s Charlie Parker and Dizzy Gillespie created bebop — fast, harmonically complex small-group jazz — followed by cool jazz, Miles Davis's modal jazz (Kind of Blue, 1959), the free jazz of Ornette Coleman and John Coltrane, and the jazz fusion of the 1970s.

Characteristics

Standard ensembles combine a rhythm section (piano, bass, drums, guitar) with horns (trumpet, saxophone, trombone). Repertoire draws on the "standards" of the Great American Songbook, blues forms, and original compositions. Improvisation within a shared harmonic framework remains the music's core practice, making each performance unique.

Influence

Jazz profoundly shaped rock, R&B, hip-hop, and popular song worldwide, and its major figures — Armstrong, Ellington, Parker, Davis, Coltrane — are counted among the most influential musicians of the twentieth century. UNESCO marks 30 April as International Jazz Day.
