Lavrentiy Beria

Lavrentiy Pavlovich Beria (29 March 1899 – 23 December 1953) was a Soviet politician of Georgian origin who served as chief of the NKVD, the Soviet secret police, under Joseph Stalin from 1938 to 1946, and as a deputy premier and member of the Politburo. The longest-serving and most influential of Stalin's secret police chiefs, Beria supervised the later stages of the Great Purge, administered the Gulag camp system, directed wartime deportations of entire nationalities, and managed the Soviet atomic bomb project. After Stalin's death in March 1953 he became one of the most powerful men in the USSR, but was arrested that June by his colleagues, tried for treason, and executed.

Early life and rise

Born in Merkheuli, near Sukhum, to a Mingrelian peasant family, Beria studied in Baku and joined the Bolsheviks in 1917. He entered the Cheka in Azerbaijan and then Georgia, rising through the republic's security organs and becoming party chief of Georgia in 1931 and of the Transcaucasian federation soon after. In the Caucasus he built a reputation for ruthless efficiency and cultivated Stalin's favor with a flattering 1935 history of the Transcaucasian Bolshevik organizations.

Head of the NKVD

Summoned to Moscow in 1938 as deputy to Nikolai Yezhov, Beria replaced him that November as People's Commissar for Internal Affairs. He wound down the mass operations of the Great Terror while presiding over targeted purges, the execution of Yezhov himself, and the murder of some 22,000 Polish officers and prisoners in the Katyn massacre of 1940, which Beria proposed to Stalin in a memorandum of 5 March 1940. Through the NKVD he ran the Gulag's vast forced-labor economy.

War years

During the Second World War Beria sat on the State Defense Committee, oversaw defense production using prison labor, and directed the mass deportations of "punished peoples" — including Chechens, Ingush, Crimean Tatars and Volga Germans — to Central Asia and Siberia, in which hundreds of thousands died. After the war he directed the Soviet atomic bomb project, achieving a first test in August 1949, four years ahead of Western expectations, aided by espionage and Gulag labor.

After Stalin

On Stalin's death in March 1953, Beria merged the security ministries under himself, proclaimed an amnesty releasing over a million prisoners, and floated liberalizing measures, including in East Germany. His fellow leaders — Khrushchev, Malenkov and Molotov among them — conspired against him with Marshal Zhukov's help. Beria was arrested at a Presidium meeting on 26 June 1953, tried in secret in December, and shot on 23 December 1953.

Legacy

Beria is remembered as one of the principal organizers of Stalinist terror, implicated in the deaths and imprisonment of millions; he is also accused of numerous personal crimes, including sexual violence, documented by historians after the opening of Soviet archives. In 2000 the Military Collegium of the Russian Supreme Court refused to rehabilitate him.
