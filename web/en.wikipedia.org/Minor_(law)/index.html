Minor (law)

In law, a minor is a person under a certain age, usually the age of majority, which legally separates childhood from adulthood. The age of majority depends on the jurisdiction and application but is most commonly 18. A minor is also sometimes called an infant in older legal usage, and the concept contrasts with that of an adult.

Minors generally lack full legal capacity: they may be unable to vote, enter binding contracts, marry without consent, purchase alcohol or tobacco, or be held fully responsible for criminal acts. At the same time, the law affords minors special protections, including in education, employment and criminal procedure, where separate juvenile justice systems usually apply.

Legal status and exceptions

Most jurisdictions recognise exceptions and gradations. The doctrine of emancipation can release a minor from parental control and grant adult-like capacity, for example through marriage, military service or court order. Minors may typically enter contracts for necessities, and contracts a minor does sign are often voidable at the minor's option. Different ages may apply for different purposes: driving licences, consent to medical treatment, criminal responsibility and consent to sexual activity each have their own thresholds in many legal systems.

History

Under Roman law and later English common law, the age of majority was commonly 21, and guardianship arrangements protected the property of those under age. Over the twentieth century most countries lowered the age of majority to 18, often in step with lowering the voting age.
