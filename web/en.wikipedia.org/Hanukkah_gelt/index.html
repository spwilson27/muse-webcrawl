Hanukkah gelt

Hanukkah gelt (Yiddish: חנוכה געלט, khanuke gelt; Hebrew: דמי חנוכה, dmei ḥanukkah, "Hanukkah money") refers to money given as a gift during the Jewish festival of Hanukkah. In modern practice it is most commonly given as chocolate coins wrapped in gold or silver foil, often used by children in the game of dreidel.

History

The custom of giving small coins to children at Hanukkah has roots in early modern Europe. One tradition connects it to the coins minted by the Hasmoneans after their victory over the Seleucid Empire, commemorating Jewish independence. By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Eastern European Jewish families commonly gave children small amounts of cash, sometimes intended in part to reward Torah study or to be given to charity (tzedakah).

Chocolate gelt

In the twentieth century, American chocolatiers began producing foil-wrapped chocolate coins modelled on the money gifts; American producers popularised them in the 1920s. Today chocolate gelt is sold in small mesh bags and is a standard Hanukkah treat. Some families continue the older custom of giving real money, often encouraging children to donate a portion to charity.

Dreidel

Children frequently gamble their gelt — real or chocolate — in the dreidel game, in which a spinning four-sided top determines whether players win or lose coins from a central pot.
