Food studies

Food studies is an interdisciplinary field that examines food and its contexts — the social, cultural, historical, economic, political, and scientific dimensions of what people eat and how they eat it. Drawing on anthropology, sociology, history, geography, literary studies, and nutrition science, food studies treats food not merely as sustenance but as a medium of identity, power, memory, and meaning.

History

Anthropologists and historians long studied food customs, from Margaret Mead's work on food habits during the Second World War to Claude Lévi-Strauss's structuralist analysis of cooking. The field coalesced as a named discipline in the 1980s and 1990s, with the founding of journals such as Gastronomica (2001), scholarly associations like the Association for the Study of Food and Society, and graduate programmes at institutions including New York University and Boston University.

Themes

Major themes include the cultural construction of taste and disgust; food and national or ethnic identity; the globalisation of cuisines and the politics of "authenticity"; hunger, famine, and food security; industrial agriculture and alternative food movements; and the relationship between diet, health, and the environment. Scholars also study cookbooks and food media as texts that encode class, gender, and colonial histories.

Methods

Food studies research combines archival work, ethnography, oral history, and textual analysis. Its practitioners argue that everyday eating practices offer a revealing lens on large-scale processes such as migration, colonisation, and climate change.
