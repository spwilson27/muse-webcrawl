Pycnocline

In oceanography and limnology, a pycnocline is the layer within a body of water where the density gradient is greatest, that is, where density changes most rapidly with depth. The word derives from the Greek pyknos (dense) and klinein (to slope).

Formation

Seawater density is controlled mainly by temperature and salinity. In most of the open ocean, a warm, less dense surface layer rests above cold deep water, and the sharp density transition between them — driven largely by the thermocline (temperature) and, in some regions, the halocline (salinity) — forms a strong, stable pycnocline, typically at depths of a few hundred metres.

Significance

The pycnocline acts as a barrier to vertical mixing, limiting the exchange of heat, gases, and nutrients between surface and deep waters. This stratification shapes ocean circulation, nutrient supply to phytoplankton, and the propagation of internal waves. Pycnoclines also occur in lakes and estuaries, where they can trap low-oxygen water at depth.
