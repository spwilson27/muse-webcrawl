Philadelphia Convention

DateMay 25 – September 17, 1787
LocationPennsylvania State House, Philadelphia
Participants55 delegates from 12 states
OutcomeDrafting of the United States Constitution

The Philadelphia Convention, also known as the Constitutional Convention, took place from May 25 to September 17, 1787, in the old Pennsylvania State House (now Independence Hall) in Philadelphia. Although the convention was intended to revise the Articles of Confederation, many proponents — chiefly James Madison and Alexander Hamilton — sought from the outset to create a new frame of government rather than amend the existing one.

Background

The weaknesses of the Articles of Confederation had become apparent: the Confederation Congress could not levy taxes, regulate interstate commerce, or compel the states to meet their obligations. Economic turmoil and events such as Shays' Rebellion in Massachusetts convinced many leaders that a stronger national government was necessary. Congress called a convention "for the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles of Confederation."

Delegates and proceedings

Twelve of the thirteen states appointed delegates; only Rhode Island declined to send any. George Washington was unanimously elected president of the convention. Madison's Virginia Plan proposed a strong national government with a bicameral legislature based on population, while the New Jersey Plan favored equal state representation. The deadlock between large and small states was resolved by the Connecticut Compromise, which created a House of Representatives apportioned by population and a Senate with equal representation per state.

Compromises and signing

Other major compromises included the Three-Fifths Compromise on counting enslaved persons for representation and taxation, and agreements on the slave trade and presidential election through the Electoral College. On September 17, 1787, thirty-nine of the remaining delegates signed the Constitution, which was then transmitted to the states for ratification.

Legacy

The document produced at Philadelphia remains the framework of the government of the United States. Debate records, particularly Madison's notes, provide much of what is known about the convention's secret deliberations.
