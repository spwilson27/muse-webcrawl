Fanfare for the Common Man

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fanfare for the Common Man is a musical work by the American composer Aaron Copland, written in 1942 for the Cincinnati Symphony Orchestra under conductor Eugene Goossens. It is scored for brass and percussion — four horns, three trumpets, three trombones, tuba, timpani, bass drum and tam-tam — and lasts about four minutes. It is one of the most recognisable American concert works of the 20th century.

Background

During the 1942–43 season, Goossens commissioned fanfares from eighteen composers as patriotic contributions to the war effort, to open Cincinnati Symphony concerts. Copland's was the only one to enter the standard repertoire. The title was inspired by a 1942 speech by Vice President Henry A. Wallace proclaiming the arrival of the "Century of the Common Man". Copland wrote that he had chosen the title because "it was the common man, after all, who was doing all the dirty work in the war". The premiere took place on 12 March 1943 — scheduled, at Copland's suggestion, near the deadline for filing income tax returns, "in honour of the common man".

Music and legacy

The piece opens with dramatic percussion strokes and a slow, majestic brass theme built largely from the intervals of the perfect fourth and fifth, answered and expanded until the full ensemble sounds the theme in triumph. Copland reused the fanfare as the principal theme of the fourth movement of his Third Symphony (1946). The progressive rock group Emerson, Lake & Palmer adapted it on their album Works Volume 1 (1977); the recording, released with Copland's approval, reached number two on the UK singles chart and introduced the piece to a new audience. The fanfare has since been played at countless public ceremonies, sporting events and memorials.
