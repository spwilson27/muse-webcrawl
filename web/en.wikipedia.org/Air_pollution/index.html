Air pollution

Photochemical smog over a large city; vehicle exhaust and industrial emissions are leading sources.

Air pollution is the contamination of air by substances — including gases, particulates and biological molecules — that are harmful to the health of humans and other living beings, or cause damage to the climate or to materials. There are many sources of air pollution, including gases (such as ammonia, carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, nitrous oxides, methane and chlorofluorocarbons), particulates (both organic and inorganic), and biological molecules.

Air pollution can cause diseases, allergies, and even death to humans; it can also cause harm to other living organisms such as animals and crops, and may damage the natural environment or built environment. The World Health Organization estimates that air pollution is responsible for several million premature deaths each year, making it one of the largest environmental health risks worldwide.

Pollutants

  • Particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10) — fine particles that penetrate deep into the lungs.
  • Nitrogen oxides (NOx) and sulfur dioxide (SO2) — from combustion; precursors of acid rain.
  • Ground-level ozone — a secondary pollutant formed when NOx and volatile organic compounds react in sunlight.
  • Carbon monoxide (CO) — a colourless, odourless gas from incomplete combustion.
  • Greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide and methane, which drive climate change.

Sources

Anthropogenic sources include fossil-fuel power stations, industrial facilities, motor vehicles, biomass burning, and agriculture. Natural sources include dust storms, wildfires and volcanic eruptions. Indoor air pollution — from solid-fuel cooking, heating and tobacco smoke — is a major burden in low-income households. Land-use practices such as agroforestry can locally reduce particulate levels, while large-scale mitigation relies on emission standards, clean energy and public transport.

Effects and control

Exposure is linked to respiratory and cardiovascular disease, stroke, lung cancer and adverse birth outcomes. Ecosystems suffer acidification and eutrophication, and soot (black carbon) accelerates ice melt. Landmark legislation includes the United States Clean Air Act and the United Kingdom's Clean Air Act 1956, passed after the Great Smog of London in 1952 killed thousands. Air quality is commonly reported using the Air Quality Index (AQI).
