Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co

CourtCourt of Appeal (England and Wales)
Decided7 December 1892
Citation[1893] 1 QB 256
KeywordsOffer, acceptance, unilateral contract, advertisement

Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Company [1892] EWCA Civ 1 is an English contract law decision by the Court of Appeal, which held that an advertisement containing certain terms to get a reward constituted a binding unilateral offer that could be accepted by anyone who performed its terms. It is notable for its treatment of unilateral contracts and remains a foundational case taught to law students.

Facts

The company advertised its "smoke ball" remedy with a promise to pay £100 to anyone who caught influenza after using the product as directed, stating it had deposited £1,000 at a bank to show sincerity. Louisa Carlill used the smoke ball as directed, caught influenza, and claimed the reward; the company refused to pay, arguing the advertisement was a mere puff and could not be an offer to the whole world.

Judgment

The Court of Appeal held the advertisement was a unilateral offer to the world at large; Carlill accepted it by performing the stated conditions; the bank deposit showed intention to be bound; and consideration was present in the inconvenience of using the product and its boost to sales. The company was liable for the £100.

Significance

The case establishes principles about offers in advertisements, acceptance by conduct, and notification of acceptance in unilateral contracts, and is cited throughout common-law jurisdictions.
