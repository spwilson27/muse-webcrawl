Deferent and epicycle

In the Hipparchian and Ptolemaic systems of astronomy, the deferent and epicycle were geometric devices used to explain the apparent motions of the planets against the background of stars, including their periodic retrograde motion, while preserving the principle that heavenly bodies move in perfect circles at uniform speed around the Earth.

The model

A planet was imagined to move around a small circle, the epicycle, whose centre in turn moved around a larger circle, the deferent, centred near the Earth. By adjusting the sizes and speeds of the two circles, astronomers could reproduce a planet's looping path through the sky. Ptolemy's Almagest (2nd century AD) refined the system with additional devices — the eccentric and the equant — achieving good predictive accuracy.

History

The scheme originated with Apollonius of Perga and Hipparchus in the Hellenistic period and dominated astronomy for over a millennium, in Islamic and European astronomy alike. Copernicus retained epicycles in his heliocentric model, and they were only rendered unnecessary by Kepler's discovery that planetary orbits are ellipses.
