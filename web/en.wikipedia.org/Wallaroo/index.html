Wallaroo is a common name for medium-sized macropod marsupials of Australia, intermediate in size between the larger kangaroos and the smaller wallabies. The word is a portmanteau of "wallaby" and "kangaroo". Wallaroos are not a distinct taxonomic group, but the name is applied to several species:

  • The common wallaroo or euro (Osphranter robustus), widespread across mainland Australia, inhabiting steep rocky hills and escarpments.
  • The black wallaroo (Osphranter bernardus), restricted to the sandstone country of Arnhem Land in the Northern Territory.
  • The antilopine wallaroo (Osphranter antilopinus), also called the antilopine kangaroo, found across tropical northern Australia.

Characteristics

Wallaroos are stocky, heavily built macropods adapted to rugged terrain. The common wallaroo has shaggy fur, a bare black muzzle, and powerful hind limbs; males are considerably larger and darker than females. They are primarily nocturnal and solitary, sheltering in caves and under rock overhangs during the heat of the day, and grazing on grasses and shrubs.

Like other macropods, wallaroos are herbivores with chambered stomachs, and females carry a single joey in the pouch for several months. They can survive on poor-quality forage and little water, an adaptation to arid and semi-arid habitats.
