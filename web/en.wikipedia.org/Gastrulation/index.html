Gastrulation
PrecursorBlastula / blastocyst
ProductGastrula with three germ layers: ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm
Timing (humans)Third week of development
Key structurePrimitive streak and node

Gastrulation is a phase of early embryonic development in most animals during which the single-layered blastula is reorganised into a multilayered structure, the gastrula, establishing the three germ layers — ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm — from which all tissues and organs of the body derive. The developmental biologist Lewis Wolpert famously remarked that it is "not birth, marriage, or death, but gastrulation which is truly the most important time in your life."

Process in humans

In the third week after fertilisation, the bilaminar embryonic disc (epiblast and hypoblast) forms a midline thickening, the primitive streak, on its dorsal surface. Epiblast cells migrate toward the streak, slip through it (ingress) and displace the hypoblast to form the definitive endoderm; subsequent cells spread between the layers to form mesoderm, while the remaining epiblast becomes ectoderm. Cells passing through the primitive node form the notochord, a rod that patterns the body axis and induces the overlying ectoderm to form the neural plate. Signalling pathways including Nodal, Wnt and BMP govern these movements.

Variations

Modes of gastrulation vary across animals: invagination in sea urchins, involution in amphibians such as Xenopus (beginning at the blastopore and Spemann organiser), and delamination or ingression in other groups — but all achieve the same layered body plan.
