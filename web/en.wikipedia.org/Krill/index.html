Krill

OrderEuphausiacea
Species86 known
SizeTypically 1–2 cm; up to ~15 cm
HabitatAll oceans, from surface to deep water

Krill are small, shrimp-like crustaceans of the order Euphausiacea, found in all the world's oceans. The name comes from the Norwegian krill, "small fry of fish." There are 86 known species, most between 1 and 2 centimetres long as adults, though the largest reach about 15 cm. Krill are a cornerstone of marine food webs: they feed largely on phytoplankton — and, in the case of Antarctic krill, on algae growing beneath sea ice — and are themselves the principal food of many whales, seals, penguins, squid, and fish.

Biology

Krill have translucent bodies with conspicuous compound eyes, feathery thoracic limbs that form a feeding basket for filtering microscopic algae, and photophores — light-producing organs — that make many species bioluminescent. They typically occur in vast swarms that can extend for kilometres and include billions of individuals, tinting the sea surface red or orange. Most species perform daily vertical migrations, rising to feed near the surface at night and descending by day.

Antarctic krill and the fishery

Antarctic krill (Euphausia superba), up to 6 cm long, is among the most abundant animal species on Earth by biomass, with estimates in the hundreds of millions of tonnes. It underpins the Southern Ocean ecosystem, sustaining most of the region's large predators. A commercial fishery, regulated by the Commission for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources (CCAMLR), harvests krill for aquaculture feed, pet food, and krill oil supplements rich in omega-3 fatty acids. Scientists are concerned that ocean warming and sea-ice loss may reduce krill habitat in parts of the Antarctic.
