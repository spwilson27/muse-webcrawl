History of cartography

The history of cartography refers to the development and consequences of cartography, or mapmaking technology, throughout human history. Maps have been one of the most important human inventions, allowing humans to explain and navigate their way through the world. The earliest surviving maps date back to prehistoric cave paintings and Babylonian clay tablets; cartography matured in ancient Greece, flourished in the Islamic Golden Age and in China, was transformed by European exploration and printing in the Age of Discovery, and has been revolutionized by aerial photography, satellites and geographic information systems (GIS).

Earliest maps

A wall painting at Çatalhöyük (c. 6200 BC) is sometimes interpreted as a town plan. Babylonian clay tablets from the 6th century BC include the famous Imago Mundi, a schematic world map centered on Babylon. Egyptian and Chinese traditions also produced early maps; in China, silk maps of the Han dynasty (2nd century BC) show sophisticated surveying.

Greek and Roman cartography

Anaximander (c. 610–546 BC) is credited with one of the first Greek world maps. Hecataeus improved it; Herodotus criticized its symmetries. Eratosthenes (276–194 BC) coined the word "geography," drew parallels and meridians, and calculated the Earth's circumference with remarkable accuracy. Claudius Ptolemy's Geography (2nd century AD) codified mathematical mapping with coordinates for thousands of places and instructions for map projections; rediscovered in the Renaissance, it shaped European cartography for centuries. Roman cartography emphasized practical itineraries, as in the Peutinger Table of roads.

Medieval and Islamic cartography

Medieval European maps were often theological — the T-O maps and mappaemundi such as the Hereford Mappa Mundi placed Jerusalem at the center. Meanwhile, Islamic scholars preserved and advanced Ptolemaic knowledge: al-Khwarizmi revised Ptolemy's coordinates, and Muhammad al-Idrisi produced the Tabula Rogeriana (1154) for Roger II of Sicily, among the most advanced world maps of its time. Sailors of the Mediterranean developed portolan charts of striking coastal accuracy.

Age of Discovery and the scientific era

European voyages of exploration filled in coastlines worldwide; the Cantino planisphere (1502) and the maps of Martin Waldseemüller (1507, which first named "America") mark the transition. Gerardus Mercator's 1569 projection, designed for navigation, became the standard nautical chart. In the 17th–18th centuries, national surveys (Cassini's triangulation of France), marine chronometers for longitude, and explorers such as James Cook brought maps close to modern accuracy.

Modern cartography

The 20th century introduced aerial photogrammetry, thematic mapping, satellite remote sensing, and — from the 1960s — computer-based GIS. The Global Positioning System (GPS), open data projects such as OpenStreetMap, and web platforms like Google Maps have made interactive, continuously updated maps a ubiquitous part of daily life.
