Grammatical mood

Grammatical mood is a grammatical feature of verbs, used for signalling modality: the speaker's attitude toward what they are saying, such as whether they present it as a fact, a command, a wish, a possibility, or something contrary to fact. Mood is distinct from tense (when an event occurs) and aspect (how it unfolds in time). Not all languages mark mood by verb inflection; some use particles or auxiliary verbs instead.

Principal moods

  • Indicative — states facts or what the speaker presents as fact; the default mood in most languages.
  • Imperative — issues commands or requests ("Go!").
  • Subjunctive — expresses wishes, hypothetical or non-factual situations, and appears in subordinate clauses in many languages; vestigial in English ("if I were you").
  • Conditional — expresses events contingent on a condition ("I would go"), treated as a mood in Romance grammar.
  • Optative — expresses hopes and wishes, a distinct inflectional mood in Ancient Greek and Sanskrit.
  • Interrogative — asks questions; marked by word order or particles in most languages, occasionally by a dedicated form.

Mood across languages

Ancient Greek distinguished indicative, subjunctive, optative, and imperative. Latin had indicative, subjunctive, and imperative. Modern Romance languages make extensive use of the subjunctive after verbs of emotion, doubt, and volition. English expresses modality mainly through modal auxiliary verbs (can, may, must, would) rather than inflection. Some languages additionally grammaticalise evidentiality — marking whether the speaker witnessed an event or heard it reported — which interacts closely with mood.
