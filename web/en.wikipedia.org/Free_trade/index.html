Free trade

International trade at a container port

Free trade is a trade policy that does not restrict imports or exports; it can also be understood as the free market idea applied to international trade. In government, free trade is predominantly advocated by political parties that hold economically liberal positions, while economically left-wing and nationalist parties generally support protectionism, the opposite of free trade.

Most nations are today members of the World Trade Organization multilateral trade agreements. Regional examples of free trade areas include the European Union, the USMCA (United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement), and the African Continental Free Trade Area. However, most governments still impose some protectionist policies intended to support local employment, such as applying tariffs to imports or subsidies to exports.

Economics

The classical economic case for free trade rests on comparative advantage, formalised by David Ricardo in 1817: even if one country is more efficient at producing everything, both countries gain when each specialises in what it produces relatively most efficiently and trades. Free trade is argued to lower consumer prices, widen consumer choice, enlarge markets for exporters, encourage competition and innovation, and raise aggregate real income. Economists generally find that the gains are unevenly distributed, however: workers in import-competing industries may face job losses and long adjustment costs, which motivates compensation schemes and political opposition.

History

Adam Smith attacked mercantilism in The Wealth of Nations (1776), and in the 19th century Britain became free trade's leading champion, repealing the Corn Laws in 1846 and the Navigation Acts in 1849. A wave of liberal trade treaties followed, but protectionism returned from the late 19th century and reached an extreme in the 1930s with measures such as the US Smoot–Hawley Tariff. After the Second World War, the GATT (1947) and later the WTO (1995) institutionalised successive rounds of tariff reduction; regional free-trade agreements proliferated from the 1990s, alongside a growing political backlash against globalisation in the 21st century.

Criticism

Critics argue that free trade can depress wages and erode labour and environmental standards in a "race to the bottom", hollow out strategic industries, increase dependence on foreign suppliers, and harm developing countries whose infant industries cannot yet compete — an argument going back to Alexander Hamilton and Friedrich List. Defenders reply that protectionism typically raises consumer prices and shelters inefficiency, and that openness correlates strongly with long-run growth.
