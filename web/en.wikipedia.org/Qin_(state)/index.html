Qin (state)

Qin and its rivals in the Warring States period

Qin (Chinese: 秦; Wade–Giles: Ch'in) was an ancient Chinese state during the Zhou dynasty. Originating in the western Wei River valley of modern Gansu and Shaanxi, it rose from a minor frontier march to become, during the Warring States period, one of the Seven Warring States — and ultimately the strongest, conquering all the others and unifying China under the Qin dynasty in 221 BC.

Rise

The Qin ruling house traced its descent to the horse-breeder Feizi, enfeoffed by the Zhou king in the 9th century BC. Qin expanded eastward into the fertile Guanzhong plain after the Zhou court fled east in 771 BC. In the mid-4th century BC, Duke Xiao's chancellor Shang Yang enacted sweeping Legalist reforms — reorganising the population into mutual-responsibility groups, standardising measures, rewarding military merit over noble birth, and strengthening central administration — which transformed Qin's military and economy.

Conquest of China

From its capitals at Yong and later Xianyang, Qin absorbed the neighbouring states of Shu and Ba (modern Sichuan) in 316 BC, and over the following century defeated its rivals in turn. Between 230 and 221 BC, Ying Zheng, who took the title Qin Shi Huang ("First Emperor of Qin"), conquered Han, Zhao, Wei, Chu, Yan and Qi, ending the Warring States period and founding the Qin dynasty. Although the dynasty collapsed within fifteen years of unification, the state of Qin left China its name in many languages and a template for imperial rule.
