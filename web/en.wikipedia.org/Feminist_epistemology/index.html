Feminist epistemology

Feminist epistemology is an outgrowth of feminist philosophy that studies how gender influences our concepts of knowledge, the knowing subject, and practices of inquiry and justification. It identifies ways in which dominant conceptions and practices of knowledge systematically disadvantage women and other marginalized groups, and strives to reform these conceptions and practices so that they serve the interests of such groups.

Overview

Traditional Western epistemology often portrayed the ideal knower as a detached, disembodied, autonomous rational agent. Feminist epistemologists argue that this picture ignores the embodied and socially situated character of actual knowers, and that it can encode androcentric biases. The field emerged in the 1970s and 1980s alongside feminist work in the philosophy of science, which documented cases in which ostensibly neutral inquiry reproduced gender stereotypes — for example in primatology, archaeology, and reproductive biology.

Main approaches

Scholarly literature commonly distinguishes three broad approaches. Feminist empiricism holds that sexism and androcentrism are correctable biases, best addressed through stricter adherence to existing norms of scientific inquiry. Feminist standpoint theory, associated with thinkers such as Sandra Harding and Nancy Hartsock, argues that inquiry beginning from marginalized lives can produce less partial accounts, because the socially situated perspective of the oppressed reveals features of the social order invisible from dominant positions. Feminist postmodernism, exemplified by Donna Haraway's notion of "situated knowledges", rejects any single privileged standpoint and emphasizes the partial, located character of all knowledge claims. Other influential figures include Helen Longino, Lynn Hankinson Nelson, Lorraine Code, and Alison Wylie.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether standpoint approaches can justify epistemic privilege claims without circularity, and whether feminist postmodernism collapses into relativism. Feminist epistemologists reply that objectivity itself is better understood as a social achievement — secured by diverse critical communities, responsiveness to criticism, and shared standards — than as freedom from all social location.
