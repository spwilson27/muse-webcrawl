Bioterrorism

Bioterrorism is terrorism involving the intentional release or dissemination of biological agents — bacteria, viruses, toxins, or other harmful biological substances — to cause illness or death in people, animals, or plants, and to create fear and social disruption. It differs from state-level biological warfare mainly in the actor and scale, and it is criminalised under national laws and addressed internationally by the Biological Weapons Convention.

History

Deliberate use of disease predates germ theory, but documented modern bioterror incidents are comparatively rare and technically difficult. In 1984, followers of the Rajneeshee movement contaminated salad bars in The Dalles, Oregon, with Salmonella, sickening 751 people, in an attempt to influence a local election — the largest bioterror attack in US history by case count. The Japanese cult Aum Shinrikyo attempted several biological attacks in the early 1990s before turning to sarin gas. In 2001, letters containing anthrax spores were mailed in the United States shortly after the September 11 attacks, killing five people, infecting 17 others, contaminating postal facilities, and triggering a massive public-health and law-enforcement response; the FBI ultimately attributed the mailings to government scientist Bruce Ivins.

Agents of concern

Public-health agencies classify potential agents by risk. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's Category A list includes anthrax (Bacillus anthracis), smallpox (Variola major), plague (Yersinia pestis), botulism, tularaemia, and viral haemorrhagic fevers, because they can be disseminated or transmitted person-to-person and carry high mortality. Most listed agents are difficult to obtain, weaponise, and disperse effectively, which analysts cite as a key reason successful attacks have been few.

Preparedness

Bioterrorism preparedness emphasises disease surveillance and rapid laboratory diagnosis (since a covert attack may first appear as unusual illness), stockpiles of vaccines and antibiotics such as the US Strategic National Stockpile, hospital surge capacity, and international notification under the International Health Regulations. Exercises such as the 2001 "Dark Winter" smallpox simulation influenced policy and funding. Preparedness overlaps heavily with natural pandemic preparedness, a dual benefit often used to justify investment, although critics warn against over-securitising public health.
