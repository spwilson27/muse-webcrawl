Melanism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Melanism is the increased development of the dark-coloured pigment melanin in the skin or appendages of an organism, resulting from genetic variation. A melanistic individual appears black or nearly black. The term is most often used for animal coat colour; the opposite of melanism is leucism and albinism, involving reduced pigmentation.

Adaptive significance

Melanism can be adaptive through camouflage, thermoregulation or disease resistance. The classic example is industrial melanism in the peppered moth (Biston betularia), whose dark form became common in polluted industrial areas of Britain during the nineteenth century because it was better camouflaged against soot-darkened trees.

Examples

Melanistic leopards and jaguars are known as "black panthers". Melanic forms also occur in squirrels, wolves, reptiles and many insects. In the black jaguar and leopard, the underlying rosette pattern remains faintly visible.

Genetics

In many species melanism is controlled by a small number of genes, frequently involving the melanocortin 1 receptor and its antagonist, the agouti signalling protein.
