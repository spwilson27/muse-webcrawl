Executive functions

Executive functions (also known as cognitive control or supervisory attentional systems) are a set of cognitive processes that are necessary for the cognitive control of behavior: selecting and successfully monitoring behaviors that facilitate the attainment of chosen goals. They include basic cognitive processes such as attention control, inhibitory control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility, as well as higher-order functions such as planning, reasoning, and problem solving.

Core components

A widely used framework by Miyake and colleagues identifies three core executive functions: inhibition (suppressing prepotent responses and resisting interference), working memory updating (monitoring and revising the contents of working memory), and shifting (flexibly switching between tasks or mental sets). From these core processes, higher-level abilities such as planning and fluid intelligence are built. Executive functions depend heavily on the prefrontal cortex and its networks, and they develop gradually through childhood and adolescence, among the last cognitive capacities to mature.

Assessment and impairment

Common tests include the Stroop task, the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, the Tower of Hanoi/London, and trail-making tasks. Executive dysfunction ("dysexecutive syndrome") can follow frontal-lobe injury — the classic historical case being Phineas Gage — and features in many clinical conditions, including ADHD, depression, schizophrenia, autism, and dementia. Historical neuropsychological theorizing drew on Alexander Luria's work with brain-injured patients and on the notion of a "central executive" in Baddeley's model of working memory.
