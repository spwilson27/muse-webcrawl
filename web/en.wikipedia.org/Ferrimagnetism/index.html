Ferrimagnetism

Ferrimagnetism

Ferrimagnetism is a type of magnetic ordering in which the magnetic moments of atoms on different sublattices are aligned antiparallel to one another, as in antiferromagnetism, but the opposing moments are unequal in magnitude, so that a net spontaneous magnetisation remains. Ferrimagnetic materials therefore behave in many macroscopic respects like ferromagnets: they exhibit hysteresis, a Curie temperature above which the ordering disappears, and spontaneous magnetisation in zero applied field. The phenomenon was first explained theoretically by Louis Néel in 1948.

Description

In a ferrimagnet, neighbouring magnetic ions are coupled antiparallel by exchange interactions, but the ions on the two sublattices differ — in number, in their magnetic moment, or both — so the cancellation is incomplete. The classic example is magnetite, Fe3O4, the original lodestone known since antiquity. In magnetite's inverse spinel structure, Fe3+ ions occupy both tetrahedral and octahedral sites with opposing moments that cancel, while the Fe2+ ions on octahedral sites supply the net moment.

Temperature dependence and compensation point

Because the sublattice magnetisations generally vary with temperature at different rates, the net magnetisation of a ferrimagnet has a characteristic temperature dependence. In some materials the two sublattice moments exactly cancel at a temperature below the Curie point, called the compensation point: at this temperature the material has zero net magnetisation yet remains magnetically ordered, and coercivity typically peaks. This behaviour has no analogue in simple ferromagnets and is exploited in magneto-optical recording and spintronic devices.

Materials and applications

Important ferrimagnetic materials include the ferrites — ceramic oxides with the spinel structure, such as nickel ferrite and manganese-zinc ferrite — and the garnets, notably yttrium iron garnet (YIG). Because most ferrites are electrical insulators, they suffer far smaller eddy-current losses than metallic ferromagnets and are used extensively in high-frequency applications: transformer and inductor cores, antennas, circulators and isolators in microwave engineering, and magnetic recording media. Ferrimagnetic resonance and the low magnetic damping of YIG make garnets important in magnonics research.

History

The anomalous magnetic properties of magnetite and related oxides were a long-standing puzzle. Néel's 1948 molecular-field theory of ferrimagnetism, extending his earlier theory of antiferromagnetism, explained the characteristic curved inverse-susceptibility plots above the Curie temperature and the unusual temperature dependence of the spontaneous magnetisation. Néel received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1970 in part for this work.
