1700 Cascadia earthquake

The 1700 Cascadia earthquake was a megathrust earthquake that struck the Pacific Northwest coast of North America on January 26, 1700, at about 9 p.m. local time. It ruptured the Cascadia subduction zone along a fault roughly 1,000 kilometres long, from northern California to Vancouver Island, with an estimated magnitude of about 8.7–9.2.

Evidence

No written records exist from the region at the time, but the event was reconstructed from geological evidence: coastal marshes that subsided and were drowned, "ghost forests" of red cedar killed by salt water, sand layers deposited by the ensuing tsunami, and oral traditions of Indigenous peoples of the coast. The precise date was established from Japanese records of an "orphan tsunami" — a tsunami that arrived in Japan on January 27, 1700 (local time) with no accompanying felt earthquake — and from tree-ring dating of the ghost forests, which died between the 1699 and 1700 growing seasons.

The tsunami

The tsunami crossed the Pacific and was recorded in Japan, where waves of several metres damaged coastal villages. Along the Pacific Northwest coast, waves inundated low-lying communities; oral histories describe villages destroyed and canoes carried into trees.

Future risk

Geological studies indicate that great earthquakes of this kind recur on the Cascadia margin at intervals of roughly 300–500 years. The 1700 event is the most recent full-margin rupture, and modern risk planning in Oregon, Washington and British Columbia treats another such event as a serious planning scenario, prompting building-code changes and tsunami evacuation programmes.
