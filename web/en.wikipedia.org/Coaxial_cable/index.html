Coaxial cable

Coaxial cable, or coax, is a type of electrical cable consisting of concentric layers: a central inner conductor surrounded by a tubular insulating dielectric, a conductive outer shield (typically braided wire or foil), and a protective outer jacket. Because the electromagnetic field carrying the signal exists only in the space between the conductors, coaxial cable can be run next to metal objects without significant loss and is well shielded against external interference. It is widely used to carry radio-frequency signals, cable television, and broadband internet.

History

The geometry was patented by the English engineer Oliver Heaviside in 1880. Commercial coaxial systems emerged in the 1930s for telephone trunk lines and early television; AT&T's L-carrier systems and the first transatlantic telephone cable (TAT-1, 1956) employed coaxial designs. Coaxial cable later became the medium of early computer Ethernet networks (10BASE5 and 10BASE2) before twisted-pair wiring took over that role.

Construction and properties

The ratio of conductor diameters and the dielectric constant of the insulation set the cable's characteristic impedance — 50 ohms for radio and test equipment, 75 ohms for television distribution. Common types are designated by "RG" (Radio Guide) numbers: RG-58 (thin 50 Ω), RG-8, RG-6 and RG-11 (75 Ω for television), and low-loss semi-rigid and hardline cables for transmitter sites. Losses rise with frequency (the skin effect) and are expressed in decibels per unit length; foam dielectrics and larger diameters reduce them.

Connectors and applications

Common fittings include BNC connectors for instrumentation and older networks, type N and SMA connectors for radio work, and the F connector screwed onto RG-6 drops in homes. Cable television systems use coaxial distribution in hybrid fibre-coaxial (HFC) networks, in which fibre optic trunks feed coaxial neighbourhood segments that deliver television and DOCSIS broadband internet. Coax also serves as feedline between radios and antennas, and in CCTV, satellite LNB downleads, and laboratory test setups.
