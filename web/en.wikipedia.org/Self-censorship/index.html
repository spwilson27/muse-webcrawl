Self-censorship

Self-censorship is the act of censoring or classifying one's own discourse. This is done out of fear of, or deference to, the sensibilities or preferences (actual or perceived) of others and without overt pressure from any specific party or institution of authority. Self-censorship is often practiced by film producers, film directors, publishers, news anchors, journalists, musicians, and other kinds of authors including individuals who use social media.

Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights guarantees freedom of speech, but the exercise of that right may be curtailed by self-imposed restraint. As the Supreme Court justices have noted, the threat of legal or social sanction can produce a "chilling effect" on speech.

Reasons

Self-censorship may occur for moral reasons, to avoid offending religious or cultural sensibilities; for political reasons, to avoid punishment under an authoritarian regime or retaliation by employers; or for commercial reasons, to avoid alienating audiences, advertisers, or business partners. In journalism, editors may withhold stories to protect sources or to maintain access to officials.

In different contexts

Literature and art

Authors and artists throughout history have modified or suppressed their own work. Writers under repressive regimes, such as those in the Soviet Union, often anticipated the censor's objections and edited manuscripts in advance. In liberal societies, market pressures and anticipated public reaction play a similar role.

Media

News organisations may engage in self-censorship to preserve business interests, as described in the propaganda model of Noam Chomsky and Edward S. Herman. Opinion polls also show that journalists sometimes avoid controversial topics that might attract complaints or legal action.

Social media

On social media platforms, users may self-censor posts because of context collapse—the presence of multiple audiences including employers and family members—and fears of harassment or reputational damage.
