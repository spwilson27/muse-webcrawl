Revolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A revolution is a fundamental and relatively sudden change in political power and political organization which occurs when the population revolts against the government, typically due to perceived oppression (political, social, economic) or political incompetence. In political science, the term is used for a rapid, fundamental transformation of a society's class, state, culture, and economic social structure, beyond the established mechanisms of government. Revolutions have occurred through human history and vary widely in methods, duration, and motivating ideology.

Characteristics

Political scientist Jack Goldstone distinguishes four generations of scholarly research on revolutions. Early theorists such as Alexis de Tocqueville focused on the psychology of crowds and the role of rising expectations. Later structural approaches, exemplified by Theda Skocpol's States and Social Revolutions (1979), emphasized state breakdown, international pressure, and peasant organization as preconditions. A revolution is usually understood to involve mass mobilization and to produce significant institutional change, distinguishing it from a coup d'état, in which a small group seizes power, and from a revolt or rebellion, which may fail to transform the underlying order.

Types

Revolutions are often classified as political (changing the government), social (changing the structure of society, as in the French and Russian revolutions), or economic and technological (such as the Industrial Revolution and the Neolithic Revolution). The term is also used metaphorically for fundamental transformations in science and culture, as in the Copernican Revolution or the "digital revolution".

Famous revolutions

The American Revolution (1765–1783) created the United States. The French Revolution (1789–1799) abolished the privileges of the aristocracy and proclaimed the rights of citizens, reshaping European politics. The Russian Revolution of 1917 overthrew the Tsarist autocracy and brought the Bolsheviks to power. The Chinese Communist Revolution culminated in the founding of the People's Republic of China in 1949. The Iranian Revolution of 1979 established an Islamic republic.

Causes and outcomes

Common causes include fiscal crisis of the state, elite division, widespread grievances over inequality or repression, and the availability of an ideology that frames change as necessary and possible. Outcomes range from democratization to new forms of authoritarianism; many revolutions pass through phases of radicalization and reaction, a pattern observed as early as Crane Brinton's The Anatomy of Revolution (1938).
