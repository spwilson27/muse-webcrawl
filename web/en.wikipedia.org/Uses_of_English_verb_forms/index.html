Uses of English verb forms

English combines tense and aspect to situate events in time.

This article describes the uses of English verb forms. English verbs show contrasts of tense (past versus present), aspect (simple, progressive, perfect, and perfect progressive), mood, and voice (active versus passive). Combinations of these categories produce the forms traditionally presented as the "tenses" of English.

Present forms

The simple present is used for habits, general truths, and scheduled events ("The train leaves at six"). The present progressive describes actions in progress at the moment of speaking or temporary situations. The present perfect connects a past event or period with the present, as in "I have lived here for years," while the present perfect progressive emphasises duration or ongoing activity.

Past and future reference

The simple past reports completed events situated in past time; the past progressive provides background to them, and the past perfect places one event before another in the past. English has no single inflectional future tense: future time is commonly expressed with the modal will, with be going to, or with present forms used futuratively. Verb forms also serve non-temporal functions, including politeness ("I wanted to ask…"), hypothetical meaning in conditional clauses, and reported speech with backshifted tenses.
