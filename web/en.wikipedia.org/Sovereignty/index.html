Sovereignty

Sovereignty is the supreme authority within a territory — the defining characteristic of a state. In political theory, a sovereign is the ultimate overseer or authority in the decision-making process of the state, free from external interference in its internal affairs (de facto and de jure internal sovereignty) and recognised by other states as an equal member of the international system (external sovereignty).

History

The concept crystallised in early modern Europe. Jean Bodin, in Six Books of the Commonwealth (1576), defined sovereignty as the absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth, typically vested in a monarch. Thomas Hobbes argued in Leviathan (1651) that subjects surrender their freedom to a sovereign in exchange for security. John Locke and Jean-Jacques Rousseau instead grounded sovereignty in the people — the idea of popular sovereignty that underlies modern democracy. The Peace of Westphalia (1648), ending the Thirty Years' War, is traditionally treated as the birth of the "Westphalian system" of mutually recognising sovereign states, though historians debate how complete that break really was.

Types and dimensions

  • De jure vs de facto: legal right to rule versus effective control in practice.
  • Internal vs external: authority over domestic affairs versus independence from foreign control.
  • Popular, national and dynastic sovereignty: competing claims about where sovereignty resides — the people, the nation, or the ruler.
  • Pooled sovereignty: states voluntarily sharing authority, as in the European Union.

Contemporary challenges

Globalisation, international law and institutions, and human-rights norms all constrain classical sovereignty. The doctrine of the "responsibility to protect" (2005) holds that sovereignty does not shield governments abusing their own populations. Unrecognised or partially recognised states, separatist movements, supranational bodies and even claims to "digital sovereignty" continue to test the concept's boundaries.
