Network neutrality, or simply net neutrality, is the principle that Internet service providers (ISPs) must treat all Internet communications equally, offering users and online content providers consistent transfer rates regardless of content, website, platform, application, type of equipment, source address, destination address, or method of communication. The term was coined by Columbia University media law professor Tim Wu in 2003, as an extension of the longstanding concept of the common carrier, used to regulate the telephone network.

Arguments

Supporters argue neutrality protects free expression, competition and innovation by preventing ISPs from favouring their own services or charging content providers for faster delivery ("fast lanes"). Opponents — mainly large ISPs — argue that rules discourage investment in broadband networks and that reasonable network management and differentiated services should be permitted. Disputed practices at the centre of the debate include zero-rating, paid prioritisation, throttling and blocking, and deep packet inspection.

Regulation by country

Chile became the first country to legislate net neutrality in 2010, followed by the Netherlands (2011) and the European Union's 2015 regulation. India adopted strong rules in 2018 after the campaign against Facebook's Free Basics. In the United States, policy has swung repeatedly: the FCC's 2015 Open Internet Order classified broadband as a common-carrier service; it was repealed in 2017 under Chairman Ajit Pai; restored in 2024 under Chairwoman Jessica Rosenworcel; and in January 2025 a federal appeals court struck down the federal rules, holding the FCC lacked authority — leaving state laws such as California's in place.
