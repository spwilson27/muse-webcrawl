London (poem)

"London" is a poem by William Blake, first published in his Songs of Experience in 1794. It is one of the best-known poems of the collection, depicting a walk through the streets of London and lamenting the suffering, corruption, and oppression Blake saw in the city.

The poem

The speaker wanders through each "charter'd street" near the "charter'd Thames", seeing in every face "marks of weakness, marks of woe". He hears the "mind-forg'd manacles" in every voice, and describes the chimney-sweeper's cry, the hapless soldier's sigh, and the youthful harlot's curse that "blasts the new-born infant's tear" and "blights with plagues the marriage hearse".

Analysis

The poem's sixteen lines in four quatrains attack institutions Blake held responsible for misery — the monarchy, the Church, and the economic order of industrialising London. Its compact, repetitive structure and bitter final oxymoron have made it one of the most frequently anthologised and studied poems in English literature.
