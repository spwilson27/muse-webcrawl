Prebisch–Singer hypothesis

In economics, the Prebisch–Singer hypothesis (also called the Prebisch–Singer thesis) argues that the price of primary commodities declines relative to the price of manufactured goods over the long term, which causes the terms of trade of primary-product-dependent (developing) countries to deteriorate.

Origins

The hypothesis was developed independently in 1950 by the Argentine economist Raúl Prebisch of the UN Economic Commission for Latin America and by Hans Singer, then working at the United Nations in New York. Prebisch based his analysis on British trade statistics stretching back to the 1870s, which appeared to show a long-run decline in the relative price of primary products.

Theory

Explanations offered for the trend include the lower income elasticity of demand for food and raw materials compared with manufactures (Engel's law), the asymmetric structure of labour and product markets between industrial "centre" and agricultural "periphery", and the tendency of technical progress in manufacturing to raise incomes rather than lower prices. The thesis became a foundation of dependency theory and of policy proposals for import-substitution industrialisation in the developing world.

Evidence and criticism

Statistical evidence for a secular decline in commodity prices has been debated; long commodity price series show large cycles, including the 2000s commodity boom, alongside periods of decline. Critics question the quality-adjusted price data and the composition of the indices used.
