Heterodyne detection

Heterodyne detection (and the closely related heterodyning) is a signal-processing technique in which an input signal is combined (mixed) with a locally generated signal of a different frequency to produce new signals at the sum and difference of the two frequencies. The difference — or "beat" — frequency carries the same information as the original signal but at a frequency where it can be amplified, filtered, or measured more conveniently. The technique, invented by Canadian engineer Reginald Fessenden around 1901 and refined by Edwin Armstrong in the superheterodyne receiver, underlies virtually all radio receivers, and analogues of it are used across optics and acoustics.

Principle

Multiplying two sinusoids of frequencies f1 and f2 yields components at the sum and difference frequencies:

cos(2πf1t) · cos(2πf2t) = ½[cos(2π(f1+f2)t) + cos(2π(f1−f2)t)]

In a practical mixer — a diode, transistor, photodiode, or other nonlinear element — the unwanted component is removed by filtering. If the local oscillator is chosen so that the difference frequency is fixed (the intermediate frequency, IF), all stations can be processed by the same high-gain, sharply selective IF amplifier; this is the superheterodyne architecture that has dominated radio since the 1920s. When the two frequencies are equal, the technique is called homodyne or direct conversion, and the output is at baseband.

Applications

  • Radio: AM/FM receivers, radar, radio telescopes, mobile phones, and software-defined radio all down-convert signals by heterodyning.
  • Optics: In optical (coherent) heterodyne detection, a weak optical signal is mixed with a strong laser local oscillator on a photodetector; the photocurrent carries the signal at the difference frequency. This enables shot-noise-limited sensitivity and precise measurement of optical phase and frequency, and is used in coherent optical communications, Doppler lidar, laser spectroscopy, and interferometry.
  • Spectroscopy: Heterodyne receivers measure molecular rotational lines in astronomy at millimetre and submillimetre wavelengths, and terahertz spectroscopy uses photomixing.
  • Music and acoustics: The theremin uses heterodyning of two ultrasonic oscillators to produce audible tones.

Advantages and noise

Heterodyne detection preserves both amplitude and phase (the full complex field) of the input, unlike direct (square-law) detection which records intensity only, and it effectively amplifies weak signals with the local oscillator's power. In optics its sensitivity can reach the quantum (shot-noise) limit, at the cost of detecting only one spatial/polarisation mode and requiring frequency stability between signal and oscillator.
