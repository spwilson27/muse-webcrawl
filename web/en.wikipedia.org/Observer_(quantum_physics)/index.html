Observer (quantum physics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In quantum mechanics, an observer is anything that performs a measurement on a quantum system — typically a measuring instrument and its environment, not necessarily a conscious being. The interaction between system and observer is what produces a definite outcome from the range of possibilities described by the wavefunction, a process at the heart of the measurement problem.

The measurement problem

Quantum theory describes isolated systems evolving deterministically as superpositions, yet measurements yield single results with probabilities given by the Born rule. Different interpretations handle the observer differently: the Copenhagen interpretation treats measurement as a primitive classical process; Everett's many-worlds interpretation denies any special collapse, with observers becoming entangled with outcomes; von Neumann and Wigner once entertained a role for consciousness, an idea largely abandoned by physicists. Decoherence theory shows how interaction with an observing environment suppresses interference without invoking observers at all.

Observer effect

Popular accounts often conflate the observer with conscious attention disturbing a system. In practice the "observer effect" is simply physical interaction: to detect a particle, a probe must exchange energy with it, which changes its state — as dramatised in Heisenberg's microscope thought experiment.
