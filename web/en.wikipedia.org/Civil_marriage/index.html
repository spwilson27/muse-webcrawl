Civil marriage

A civil marriage is a marriage performed, recorded, and recognized by a government authority rather than by a religious institution. It may be entered into independently of any religious ceremony, or alongside one.

History

State registration of marriage grew as governments took over record-keeping from churches. The Netherlands introduced civil marriage in the 16th century; revolutionary France made it the sole legally valid form in 1792. In England and Wales, civil ceremonies at register offices were introduced in 1837. In many countries—including France, Germany, and much of Latin America—a civil ceremony is legally required, and any religious ceremony may only follow it.

Legal aspects

Civil marriage confers the legal rights and obligations of marriage: property and inheritance rights, tax status, parental presumptions, immigration sponsorship, and access to divorce through the courts. Requirements generally include a marriage license, minimum age, absence of a subsisting marriage, and a ceremony before an authorized official such as a registrar, judge, or notary.

Modern debates

Extension of civil marriage to same-sex couples became a major question in many countries from the late 20th century onward, achieved through legislation or court decisions in numerous jurisdictions beginning with the Netherlands in 2001. Civil marriage is distinct from a civil partnership or civil union, a separate legal status created in some places.
