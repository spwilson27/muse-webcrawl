Adriano Olivetti (11 April 1901 – 27 February 1960) was an Italian engineer, entrepreneur and social thinker. As president of Olivetti, the typewriter and office-machine company founded by his father Camillo in Ivrea, he turned it into one of Italy's most successful postwar industrial firms and an internationally celebrated model of humane, design-centred capitalism.

Career at Olivetti

Joining the firm in 1924 after studying chemical engineering in Turin and touring American factories, Adriano reorganised production, founded a development and advertising office, and from the 1930s made industrial design a hallmark of the company. Products such as the Lexicon 80 (1948), the Lettera 22 portable typewriter (1950), and later the Elea 9003 mainframe computer (1959) won international acclaim; the Lettera 22 entered the permanent collection of New York's Museum of Modern Art. Under his leadership Olivetti expanded worldwide and in 1959 acquired the American Underwood company.

Social thought

Inspired by personalist and communitarian ideas, Adriano envisioned the factory as a community: Olivetti provided workers with housing, libraries, nurseries, healthcare and above-average wages, and Ivrea's modernist factory and social buildings (now a UNESCO World Heritage site, "Ivrea, industrial city of the 20th century") embodied the ideal. He founded the Movimento Comunità (Community Movement), published the journal Comunità, and was elected to the Italian parliament in 1958. His writings, including L'ordine politico delle comunità (1945), proposed a federal state built from autonomous communities.

Death and legacy

Olivetti died suddenly of a cerebral haemorrhage on a train journey to Switzerland in February 1960, aged 58. His model of corporate social responsibility and design excellence remains influential, and his name is borne by the Fondazione Adriano Olivetti and the city of Ivrea's industrial heritage listing.
