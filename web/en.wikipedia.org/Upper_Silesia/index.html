Upper Silesia (Polish: Górny Śląsk; German: Oberschlesien; Silesian: Gōrny Ślōnsk) is the south-eastern part of the historical region of Silesia, lying mostly in modern Poland with a smaller portion in the Czech Republic. It is centred on the Upper Silesian Industrial Region around Katowice, one of Europe's great coal-mining and steel-making areas.

History

Originally part of Poland under the Piast dynasty, the region fragmented among Silesian Piast duchies and passed to the Bohemian Crown in the 14th century and to the Habsburgs in 1526. Prussia seized most of Silesia from Austria in the Silesian Wars of the 1740s. Industrialisation in the 19th century transformed Upper Silesia into a heavily German-Polish bilingual mining region.

After the First World War, the Treaty of Versailles ordered a plebiscite in 1921 to decide whether the region would belong to Germany or the reborn Poland. The vote, accompanied by the Silesian Uprisings, led the League of Nations to partition the territory, with the industrial east going to Poland. After 1945 nearly all of Upper Silesia was transferred to Poland and most of its German population was expelled westward, though a recognised German minority and the distinct Silesian language and identity persist.

Economy and culture

Coal mining long dominated the economy, and the conurbation of Katowice, Gliwice, Zabrze, and Bytom remains Poland's principal industrial heartland. Upper Silesians have a strong regional identity; hundreds of thousands declare Silesian nationality or language in Polish censuses.
