Porter's five forces analysis

Porter's Five Forces is a framework for analysing the competitive structure and attractiveness of an industry, developed by Harvard Business School professor Michael E. Porter and introduced in his 1979 Harvard Business Review article "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy" and his 1980 book Competitive Strategy. Porter argued that an industry's long-term profitability is determined not only by direct rivals but by five forces acting on firms within it.

The five forces

The forces are: (1) competitive rivalry among existing firms; (2) the threat of new entrants, governed by barriers to entry such as economies of scale, capital requirements, and regulation; (3) the threat of substitute products or services that meet the same need differently; (4) the bargaining power of buyers, who can force prices down; and (5) the bargaining power of suppliers, who can raise input prices. The stronger the collective forces, the lower the industry's profit potential; firms can use the analysis to find positions where the forces are weakest or can be influenced in their favour.

Use and criticism

The framework became a staple of strategic management teaching and corporate planning. Critics note that it presents a largely static picture, underplays cooperation, complementors, and government, and assumes a clear industry structure — objections that led to proposed extensions, such as adding a sixth force for complementors in the "value net" model of Brandenburger and Nalebuff. Porter revisited the framework in a 2008 Harvard Business Review article, reaffirming its relevance while clarifying common misuses.
