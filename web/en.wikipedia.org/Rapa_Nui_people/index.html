Rapa Nui people

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Rapa Nui are the indigenous Polynesian people of Easter Island (Rapa Nui), a special territory of Chile in the southeastern Pacific Ocean. They are Polynesians who settled one of the most remote inhabited islands on Earth, probably between about 800 and 1200 CE, and are renowned as the carvers of the moai, the monumental stone statues for which the island is famous. The Rapa Nui language, a member of the Eastern Polynesian branch of the Austronesian family, is still spoken on the island alongside Spanish, though UNESCO classifies it as severely endangered.

History

According to oral tradition, the first settlers arrived under the leadership of the chief Hotu Matu'a, following a dream that guided voyagers across the ocean from a homeland called Hiva. The settlers developed a sophisticated society organised in clans, erected ceremonial platforms (ahu) and carved nearly a thousand moai from the volcanic tuff of Rano Raraku, which were transported across the island and erected to embody ancestral chiefs.

From the 1720s European contact brought disease and conflict. The catastrophe came in 1862–63, when Peruvian slave raiders abducted roughly half the population, including the king and the learned class who were the only people able to read the undeciphered rongorongo script; smallpox spread by the few returnees reduced the population to just 111 people by 1877. Chile annexed the island in 1888, and for decades much of it was leased to a sheep-farming company while the Rapa Nui were confined to the village of Hanga Roa. The islanders received Chilean citizenship in 1966, and today several thousand people identify as Rapa Nui, with a political movement seeking greater autonomy and control over land and immigration.

Culture

The most famous expression of traditional Rapa Nui culture after the moai is the tangata manu ("birdman") cult centred on the village of Orongo, in which competitors raced to retrieve the first egg of the sooty tern from the islet of Motu Nui. Modern cultural revival is celebrated annually in the Tapati Rapa Nui festival, featuring traditional song, dance, body painting (takona) and sports. The island's archaeological landscape is protected as Rapa Nui National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
