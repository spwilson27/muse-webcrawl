Troy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Troy (Greek: Τροία, Troía; Latin: Troia; Hittite: Wilusa) was a city located in the northwest of Anatolia in what is now Türkiye, at the mound of Hisarlik near the Aegean coast and the Dardanelles. It is best known as the setting of the Trojan War described in Homer's Iliad. The archaeological site, excavated since the 19th century, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Archaeology

Heinrich Schliemann's excavations beginning in 1870 revealed a deep sequence of settlements, later classified into layers Troy I through Troy IX, spanning the Early Bronze Age (c. 3000 BC) to the Roman period. Troy VI and VIIa, destroyed around 1180 BC — a date close to the traditional fall of Troy — are the leading candidates for the city of the epic, though the scale and cause of the war remain debated. Letters in the Hittite archives mentioning Wilusa and a ruler named Alaksandu are widely identified with Troy and a figure corresponding to Alexandros (Paris).

The legend

In Greek legend, Troy was ruled by King Priam and defended by Hector; the Greeks, led by Agamemnon, besieged it for ten years to recover Helen, wife of Menelaus, who had eloped with Paris. The war narrative ends with the stratagem of the wooden horse, told most fully in Virgil's Aeneid. The site later hosted a Greek and Roman city, Ilium, visited by Alexander the Great and several Roman emperors.
