Animals in space

Animals in space originally served to test the survivability of spaceflight before human missions were attempted. Beginning in the 1940s, the United States and the Soviet Union launched fruit flies, mice, monkeys, dogs, and other animals on suborbital and orbital flights to study the effects of weightlessness, acceleration, and radiation on living organisms.

Early flights

The first animals intentionally sent into space were fruit flies aboard a U.S. V-2 rocket in 1947, recovered alive after reaching 109 km. The rhesus monkey Albert II became the first mammal in space in 1949, though he died on impact. The Soviet dog Laika, aboard Sputnik 2 in 1957, was the first animal to orbit the Earth; she died during the flight, as no recovery was planned. In 1960 the Soviet dogs Belka and Strelka orbited and returned safely. The American chimpanzee Ham (1961) and Enos (1961) flew ahead of the first American human orbital flight, and the French cat Félicette flew suborbitally in 1963.

Later missions

Animals continued to fly on dedicated biosatellite missions: the Soviet Bion programme, tortoises that circled the Moon aboard Zond 5 in 1968, spiders, fish, and nematodes flown on Skylab, the Space Shuttle, and the International Space Station. Animal experiments in orbit have studied bone and muscle loss, development, and radiation biology. Modern institutional animal-care rules have tightened over time, and the ethical debate surrounding the early flights — especially those from which animals could not return — continues.
