Temple Mount

The Temple Mount (Hebrew: הַר הַבַּיִת, Har haBayīt, "Mount of the House [of God]"; Arabic: الحرم الشريف, al-Ḥaram ash-Sharīf, "the Noble Sanctuary") is a hill in the Old City of Jerusalem that for thousands of years has been venerated as a holy site in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam alike. The walled compound sits atop an enormous elevated plaza built by Herod the Great in the 1st century BCE, its retaining walls including the Western Wall (Kotel). At its centre stands the Dome of the Rock (691–692 CE), and at its southern end the Al-Aqsa Mosque.

Religious significance

In Judaism this is the holiest site: the place of the First Temple of Solomon (destroyed by the Babylonians in 586 BCE) and the Second Temple (destroyed by Rome in 70 CE), and in tradition the site of the binding of Isaac and the Foundation Stone, the point from which the world was created. For Muslims it is the third holiest site in Islam: al-Haram al-Sharif, from where the Prophet Muhammad is believed to have ascended to heaven on the Night Journey (Isra and Mi'raj), and the direction of the first qibla before it was changed to Mecca. Christians revere it as the setting of many episodes in Jesus's life, including the presentation in the Temple and the cleansing of the Temple.

History

Herod expanded the temple platform to roughly 14 hectares, among the largest sacred precincts of the ancient world. After Rome destroyed the Second Temple in 70 CE, the site later held a Roman temple and then Byzantine churches before the Muslim conquest; the Umayyad caliphs built the Dome of the Rock (the oldest surviving monumental work of Islamic architecture) and the Al-Aqsa Mosque. Crusaders converted the Dome into a church and the mosque into a palace and Templar headquarters (hence "Temple Mount"); Saladin restored Muslim worship in 1187.

Status quo

Since Israel captured the Old City in 1967, a delicate "status quo" governs the compound: the Islamic Waqf administers the site, Israel controls security and access, non-Muslims may visit at set times through the Mughrabi Gate but may not pray there, and Jews pray at the Western Wall plaza below. The site's contested status makes it one of the most sensitive flashpoints in the Israeli–Palestinian conflict; visits by politicians and clashes at the compound have repeatedly sparked wider violence, as in 2000, 2017, and 2021.
