Faith and rationality

Faith and rationality — the philosophical study of how religious faith relates to reason and evidence.

Faith and rationality are two modes of belief that are sometimes held to be in tension, and sometimes held to be compatible or mutually supportive. Philosophers and theologians have long debated whether religious faith requires rational justification, can coexist with it, or properly operates independently of it.

Views

Rationalism

Strong rationalists hold that religious beliefs should be accepted only on the basis of sufficient evidence and argument. W. K. Clifford's essay "The Ethics of Belief" (1877) argued that it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence.

Fideism

Fideism holds that faith is independent of, and may even conflict with, reason. It is associated with thinkers such as Tertullian (often paraphrased as "I believe because it is absurd") and, in a more moderate form, with Søren Kierkegaard's "leap of faith".

Compatibility views

Many medieval thinkers, notably Thomas Aquinas, argued that faith and reason cannot genuinely conflict because both come from God; apparent conflicts arise from errors in reasoning or interpretation. Augustine's maxim "believe in order that you may understand" (credo ut intelligam) expresses the idea that faith precedes and aids understanding.

Evidentialism and Reformed epistemology

In the late 20th century, philosophers such as Alvin Plantinga developed Reformed epistemology, arguing that belief in God can be "properly basic" — rational without being based on evidence, in the way belief in other minds or the external world is basic.
