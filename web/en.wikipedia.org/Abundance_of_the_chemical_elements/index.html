Abundance of the chemical elements

The abundance of the chemical elements is a measure of the occurrence of the chemical elements relative to all other elements in a given environment. Abundance is measured in one of three ways: by mass fraction (or weight fraction), by mole fraction (fraction of atoms by numerical count, or sometimes fraction of molecules), or by volume fraction. In most cases the three give similar rankings for the most common elements.

Abundances vary enormously depending on the environment considered. The universe as a whole is dominated by hydrogen and helium left over from the Big Bang, while the Earth's crust is dominated by oxygen and silicon, and the human body is mostly oxygen, carbon and hydrogen.

Abundance in the universe

The elements formed during the Big Bang — hydrogen, helium and trace amounts of lithium — make up the overwhelming majority of ordinary matter in the universe. By mass, hydrogen is about 74% and helium about 24% of all ordinary matter. All heavier elements, produced by stellar nucleosynthesis and supernovae, together account for only about 2%. There is a general pattern in which elements with even atomic numbers are more abundant than their odd-numbered neighbours (the Oddo–Harkins rule), and iron is unusually abundant because of its high nuclear binding energy per nucleon.
