Introduction to quantum mechanics

Quantum mechanics is the fundamental theory of nature at the smallest scales, describing atoms and subatomic particles. Emerging in the 1920s from work by Planck, Einstein, Bohr, Heisenberg, Schrödinger, and Dirac, it replaced the deterministic picture of classical physics with one built on probabilities.

Key ideas

Particles exhibit wave–particle duality, demonstrated by the double-slit experiment: unobserved, they behave as waves passing through both slits; measured, they appear as particles. The state of a system is described by a wavefunction whose squared amplitude gives probabilities (the Born rule), evolving under the Schrödinger equation. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle limits the simultaneous precision of position and momentum, and systems can exist in superpositions of states and become entangled, so that measurements on distant particles are correlated in ways Einstein called "spooky action at a distance".

Applications

Quantum mechanics underlies chemistry, semiconductors and lasers, atomic clocks, and emerging technologies such as quantum computing and quantum cryptography.
