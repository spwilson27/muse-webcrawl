The stem supports the plant and transports water and nutrients between roots and leaves.

The stem is one of two main structural axes of a vascular plant, the other being the root. The stem is normally divided into nodes, where leaves and buds arise, and internodes, the segments between them. Stems elevate leaves toward light, transport water, minerals and sugars, and in many species store food and water or carry out photosynthesis.

Structure

A typical stem contains three tissue systems: dermal tissue (the epidermis, often with a waxy cuticle), ground tissue (pith and cortex), and vascular tissue arranged as bundles or cylinders of xylem, which conducts water upward, and phloem, which translocates sugars. In herbaceous dicots the vascular bundles form a ring; in monocots they are scattered through the ground tissue. Woody stems add secondary growth from the vascular cambium, producing annual rings of wood and an outer bark.

Growth

Primary growth in length occurs at shoot apical meristems protected by the terminal bud; axillary buds in leaf axils can form branches or flowers. Auxin produced at the tip suppresses lateral buds (apical dominance). In woody plants the cambium and cork cambium drive secondary growth in girth.

Modified stems

Stems are adapted into many forms: underground rhizomes (ginger), tubers (potato), stolons or runners (strawberry), bulbs (onion), corms, climbing tendrils, thorns, and the flattened photosynthetic cladodes of cacti. These modifications serve storage, asexual reproduction, climbing and defence.

Economic importance

Stems yield timber, paper pulp, fibres such as flax and hemp, sugar (sugarcane, maple sap), cinnamon bark, rubber latex and many vegetables including asparagus and kohlrabi.
