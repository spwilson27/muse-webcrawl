John Wycliffe (c. 1328 – 31 December 1384) was an English scholastic philosopher, theologian and Catholic priest who became a leading dissenter within the Church. Often called the "Morning Star of the Reformation", he argued that Scripture alone should guide the Church and inspired the first complete translation of the Bible into English.

Life and thought

Wycliffe studied and taught at the University of Oxford. Initially useful to the Crown in its disputes with the papacy over taxation, he developed increasingly radical positions: that dominion depends on grace, that the Church held too much wealth and temporal power, and that the doctrine of transubstantiation was unscriptural. His followers, the Lollards, spread his teachings across England.

Wycliffe's Bible

The Middle English Bible translations associated with Wycliffe (c. 1382–1395) were produced by his followers and circulated in manuscript; more copies survive than of any other Middle English work. The Church responded with laws against unlicensed translation and heresy.

Condemnation and legacy

Wycliffe died of a stroke in 1384. The Council of Constance condemned him posthumously in 1415, and in 1428 his bones were exhumed, burned and scattered into the River Swift. His ideas influenced Jan Hus and, through him, the later Protestant Reformation.
