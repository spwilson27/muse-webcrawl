Treasury of merit

The treasury of merit (Latin: thesaurus meritorum) is a doctrine of Roman Catholic theology according to which the infinitely valuable merits of Jesus Christ, together with the superabundant merits of the Virgin Mary and the saints, form a spiritual treasury belonging to the whole Church. Because the saints in earthly life did more good works than were required for their own salvation, their surplus merits are held to be available to benefit others, and the Church, through the power of the keys, administers this treasury — most notably through the granting of indulgences.


The doctrine underlies the medieval practice of indulgences

Development

The idea grew from the early Church's penitential discipline, in which the prayers and sufferings of martyrs and confessors could shorten the penance of others. Scholastic theologians such as Alexander of Hales and Thomas Aquinas developed it systematically in the 13th century: Christ's satisfaction was infinite, and the saints, united with him as members of one body, added merits that the Church could apply to the faithful. Pope Clement VI gave the doctrine its classic official statement in the bull Unigenitus (1343), issued for the Roman Jubilee of 1350.

Indulgences and controversy

Drawing on the treasury, the Church granted indulgences — remissions of the temporal punishment due to sins already forgiven — first for crusaders and later for prayers, pilgrimages and almsgiving. In practice the system was often abused, with indulgences effectively sold to raise funds, most notoriously in the campaign that provoked Martin Luther's Ninety-five Theses in 1517 and ignited the Protestant Reformation, in which the treasury of merit was rejected as unscriptural. The Council of Trent (1563) reaffirmed indulgences while condemning abuses, and the doctrine remains part of Catholic teaching, restated in modern form in Pope Paul VI's Indulgentiarum Doctrina (1967).
