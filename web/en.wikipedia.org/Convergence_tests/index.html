Convergence tests

In mathematics, convergence tests are methods for determining whether an infinite series converges or diverges, and in some cases for estimating the value to which it converges. Because the sum of a series is defined as the limit of its sequence of partial sums, the tests are criteria applied to the terms of the series, to ratios or roots of successive terms, or to comparisons with series whose behaviour is already known.

Term test

The simplest necessary condition is the nth-term test: if the terms of a series do not tend to zero, the series diverges. The converse is false — the harmonic series has terms tending to zero yet diverges — so the test can only establish divergence.

Comparison tests

The direct comparison test concludes that a series converges if its terms are dominated, in absolute value, by the terms of a known convergent series, and diverges if it dominates a known divergent series. The limit comparison test compares the asymptotic ratio of the terms of two series; if the limit is finite and positive, both series share the same behaviour. Standard benchmarks are the geometric series and the p-series, which converges exactly when p > 1.

Ratio and root tests

D'Alembert's ratio test examines the limit of the ratio of successive terms: a limit below 1 implies absolute convergence, above 1 divergence, and equal to 1 leaves the question open. Cauchy's root test applies the same reasoning to the nth root of the terms and is strictly stronger, succeeding in some cases where the ratio test is inconclusive.

Other tests

The integral test relates a series of decreasing positive terms to the convergence of an improper integral. Raabe's test and Kummer's test refine the ratio test for borderline cases. For alternating series, the Leibniz test guarantees convergence when the terms decrease monotonically to zero. Dirichlet's and Abel's tests handle series whose terms are products of a monotonic null sequence with a sequence having bounded partial sums.
