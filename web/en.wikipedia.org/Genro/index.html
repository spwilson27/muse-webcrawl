Genro

Genro (元老, genrō, "elder statesman") is the unofficial designation for a group of retired elder statesmen who served as informal, extraconstitutional advisors to the emperor in Imperial Japan from the 1890s until 1940. The institution developed out of the earlier genkun and the informal counsel given by the leading oligarchs of the Meiji Restoration. Though the genrō had no basis in the Meiji Constitution, they exercised decisive influence over Japanese politics, most notably by recommending to the emperor who should be appointed prime minister.

History

The men recognized as genrō were the surviving leaders of the Restoration generation, mostly from the former domains of Satsuma and Chōshū. The group is usually listed as Itō Hirobumi, Yamagata Aritomo, Matsukata Masayoshi, Inoue Kaoru, Saigō Tsugumichi, Ōyama Iwao, Katsura Tarō, and Saionji Kinmochi. As each died, the circle shrank; Katsura's status is sometimes debated because of his younger age.

During the Meiji and Taishō periods the genrō were consulted on the appointment of prime ministers, the conclusion of treaties, declarations of war, and other questions of the highest importance. Because they alone combined prestige, experience, and the emperor's confidence, their recommendation of a new premier was effectively binding.

Decline

The institution weakened as the original oligarchs died. After Yamagata's death in 1922, Saionji Kinmochi was the sole surviving genrō, and he alone shouldered the task of recommending prime ministers through the 1920s and 1930s. Saionji favored constitutional government and party cabinets, which brought him into conflict with the military. He escaped assassination during the February 26 Incident of 1936, and his influence waned thereafter. With Saionji's death in November 1940, the genrō institution came to an end; the function of recommending the prime minister passed to the jūshin, a more formal council of former prime ministers.

Legacy

Historians view the genrō as both stabilizing and anti-democratic: they provided experienced, pragmatic leadership during Japan's rapid modernization, but their informal and unaccountable authority sat uneasily with the development of parliamentary government and civilian control of the military.
