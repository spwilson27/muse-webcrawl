Mountaineering, also called alpinism in Europe, is the sport of climbing mountains. It encompasses a spectrum of activities including hiking, scrambling, rock climbing, ice climbing, and high-altitude expedition climbing, typically with the goal of reaching a summit. Unlike simple hill walking, mountaineering usually involves technical skills, specialised equipment, and the management of objective hazards such as rockfall, avalanches, crevasses, and severe weather.

History

People have climbed mountains for practical and religious reasons since antiquity, but mountaineering as a sport emerged in the Alps during the nineteenth century. The first ascent of Mont Blanc in 1786 by Jacques Balmat and Michel-Gabriel Paccard is often taken as a founding moment. The so-called Golden Age of Alpinism in the 1850s and 1860s saw British climbers, guided by local professionals, make first ascents of most major Alpine peaks; the era ended tragically with the 1865 Matterhorn disaster, in which four of Edward Whymper's party fell to their deaths after the first successful ascent.

In the twentieth century attention turned to the greater ranges, culminating in the first ascent of Mount Everest by Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay in 1953. All fourteen eight-thousand-metre peaks were climbed by 1964. Later decades emphasised alpine-style ascents — small teams, minimal fixed ropes and no supplementary oxygen — as exemplified by Reinhold Messner, the first person to climb all fourteen eight-thousanders without bottled oxygen.

Techniques and equipment

Mountaineers combine skills from several disciplines. On rock they use techniques of rock climbing; on snow and ice they employ crampons, ice axes, and snow anchors. Glacier travel requires roped movement and crevasse-rescue skills. Essential equipment includes ropes, harnesses, carabiners, helmets, layered clothing, boots, and navigation tools. At high altitude, acclimatisation is critical to avoid altitude sickness, which can progress to life-threatening pulmonary or cerebral oedema.

Hazards and ethics

Mountaineering carries inherent risks: falls, avalanches, hypothermia, and altitude illness kill climbers every year. Environmental concerns include litter and human waste on popular peaks, leading to clean-up expeditions and permit systems. Debates continue over commercial guiding, the use of fixed ropes and supplementary oxygen, and the ethics of risk on crowded routes such as Everest's standard lines.
