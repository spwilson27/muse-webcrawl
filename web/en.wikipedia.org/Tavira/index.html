Tavira is a town and municipality in the Algarve region of southern Portugal, in the district of Faro. Straddling the Gilão River near its mouth on the Atlantic coast, it is known for its castle, its many churches — it is sometimes called the "city of churches", with over twenty in the town — and its traditional pitched "scissor" roofs (telhados de tesoura) found nowhere else in Portugal.

History

The site has Phoenician and Roman antecedents (the Roman town of Balsa lay nearby). Under Moorish rule, from the eighth century to 1242, Tavira was an important port; it was conquered for Portugal by Paio Peres Correia, master of the Order of Santiago. In the Age of Discoveries it was a significant port for supplying Portuguese North African outposts and for trade in tuna, salt, wine, and dried fruits. The 1755 Lisbon earthquake severely damaged the town, destroying much of its castle and waterfront. Fishing — especially for tuna and later octopus — remained central to the economy into the twentieth century.

Geography and attractions

Opposite the town lies the Ilha de Tavira, a long barrier island of the Ria Formosa lagoon system, reachable by ferry and prized for its beaches. Landmarks include the medieval castle walls, the seven-arched "Roman" bridge (in fact largely of medieval and seventeenth-century construction), the Renaissance church of Misericórdia, and the old market hall. Tavira is generally quieter and less developed than the western Algarve resorts, and tourism, alongside agriculture and salt production, drives the modern economy.
