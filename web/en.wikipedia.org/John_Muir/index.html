John Muir (21 April 1838 – 24 December 1914), also known as "John of the Mountains" and "Father of the National Parks", was a Scottish-American naturalist, author, environmental philosopher, botanist, zoologist and glaciologist. His letters, essays and books describing his adventures in nature — especially in the Sierra Nevada of California — have been read by millions and helped shape the modern preservation movement.

Early life

Muir was born in Dunbar, Scotland, and emigrated with his family to Wisconsin in 1849, where he was raised on Fountain Lake Farm under a strict, Bible-centred regime. He studied at the University of Wisconsin–Madison without graduating, showing a passion for botany, geology and chemistry. After an industrial accident in Indianapolis in 1867 temporarily blinded him, he resolved to devote his life to nature, undertaking a celebrated thousand-mile walk from Indiana to the Gulf of Mexico, recorded in A Thousand-Mile Walk to the Gulf (published posthumously, 1916).

California and the Sierra Nevada

Muir arrived in San Francisco in 1868 and soon walked to Yosemite Valley, which became the centre of his life and work. Working as a shepherd and sawmill hand, he studied the valley's geology and advanced the (then controversial) theory that it had been carved by glaciers; his glacial studies in Alaska, where Muir Glacier is named for him, confirmed his expertise. In 1880 he married Louisa Strentzel and managed her family's fruit ranch in Martinez, California, while continuing to write.

Preservation work

Muir's magazine articles helped build support for the creation of Yosemite National Park in 1890, as well as Sequoia and other parks. In 1892 he co-founded the Sierra Club and served as its first president, a post he held until his death. His 1903 camping trip with President Theodore Roosevelt in Yosemite is often credited with strengthening Roosevelt's conservation programme. His final campaign, against the damming of Hetch Hetchy Valley inside Yosemite National Park, was defeated in 1913, but it galvanised the national parks movement and helped set the stage for the National Park Service Act of 1916.

Death and legacy

Muir died of pneumonia in Los Angeles on 24 December 1914. His writings, including The Mountains of California (1894), Our National Parks (1901), The Yosemite (1912) and Travels in Alaska (1915), remain classics of nature writing. The John Muir Trail, Muir Woods National Monument, and numerous schools and natural features bear his name; California celebrates John Muir Day on 21 April.
