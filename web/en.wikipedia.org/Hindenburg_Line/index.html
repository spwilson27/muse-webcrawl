Hindenburg Line

The Hindenburg Line (German: Siegfriedstellung, 'Siegfried Position') was a German defensive position built during the winter of 1916–1917 on the Western Front of the First World War, running from Arras to Laffaux, near Soissons on the Aisne. It was named by the British after Paul von Hindenburg, while the German name invoked the hero Siegfried of the Nibelungenlied.

Construction

Ordered after the costly battles of Verdun and the Somme in 1916, the line was built by German troops, prisoners of war, and conscripted labour using concrete blockhouses, deep dugouts, wide belts of barbed wire, and reverse-slope positions laid out for defence in depth. Some 50 divisions of field fortifications formed a system several kilometres deep, with outpost, battle, and rear zones.

Operation Alberich

In February–March 1917 the German armies withdrew to the line in Operation Alberich, shortening their front by some 40 km, freeing divisions, and devastating the evacuated territory — demolishing villages, felling trees, poisoning wells, and laying booby traps — while deporting much of the civilian population. Allied attempts to disrupt the withdrawal, and assaults such as the Battle of Arras and the Nivelle Offensive, met the full strength of the new defences.

Breaching the line

The line was attacked in the Battle of Cambrai (November 1917), where massed tanks briefly broke through. It was decisively breached during the Allied Hundred Days Offensive in autumn 1918: at the Battle of St Quentin Canal (29 September 1918), British, Australian, and American troops, supported by tanks and artillery, crossed the St Quentin Canal and broke the main position, precipitating Germany's final retreat and the Armistice of 11 November 1918.
