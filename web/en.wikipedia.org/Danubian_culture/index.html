Danubian culture

Linearbandkeramik pottery of the Danubian farmers

Danubian culture is a term coined by the Australian archaeologist V. Gordon Childe for the early Neolithic farming cultures of central and eastern Europe, spread along the Danube valley and its tributaries roughly between 5500 and 4500 BC. The term is used principally for the Linear Pottery culture (Linearbandkeramik, LBK) and its regional successors, the first agricultural societies to colonise the loess soils of temperate Europe.

The Linear Pottery culture

The LBK emerged in the middle Danube basin, in present-day Hungary, Slovakia and Austria, around 5500 BC, and spread with remarkable speed westward to the Rhine and the Paris Basin and eastward into Ukraine. Its hallmarks are longhouses of timber, characteristic band-decorated pottery, polished stone adzes ("shoe-last celts"), and a farming economy based on emmer and einkorn wheat, peas and lentils, and domesticated cattle, pigs, sheep and goats.

Successor cultures

Childe divided the Danubian sequence into phases, with later regional groups including the Stroke-ornamented ware (Stichbandkeramik), Rössen, Lengyel and Tisza cultures. These societies built larger and sometimes fortified or ditched-enclosure settlements, developed more elaborate pottery, and in the southeast came into contact with the copper-using cultures of the Balkans.

Significance

The Danubian cultures represent the principal route by which agriculture entered central and northern Europe. Ancient DNA studies have shown that LBK farmers were largely descendants of Anatolian early farmers who migrated up the Danube corridor, mixing only gradually with local hunter-gatherer populations.
