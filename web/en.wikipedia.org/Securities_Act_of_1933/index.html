Securities Act of 1933

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The Act was among the first major New Deal financial reforms

The Securities Act of 1933, also known as the 1933 Act and the "truth in securities" law, is a United States federal statute enacted during the Great Depression in response to the stock market crash of 1929. It was the first major federal legislation to regulate the offer and sale of securities, requiring issuers to register public offerings and disclose material information to investors.

Signed into law by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on 27 May 1933, the Act aimed to restore investor confidence by ensuring that buyers of securities received complete and accurate information, and by prohibiting deceit, misrepresentation and other fraud in the sale of securities.

Registration and prospectus

Unless an exemption applies, securities offered to the public must be registered with the federal regulator — initially the Federal Trade Commission, and from 1934 the newly created Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). Registration statements include a prospectus describing the issuer's business, finances and the terms of the offering. The Act imposes strict civil liability, notably under Sections 11 and 12, for material misstatements or omissions in registration statements and prospectuses.

Exemptions

The Act exempts certain securities, such as government bonds, and certain transactions, including private placements to sophisticated investors. Later measures, including Regulation D and the JOBS Act of 2012, expanded exemptions for smaller offerings and crowdfunding.

Relationship to the 1934 Act

The Securities Act governs primary offerings, while the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 regulates secondary trading, exchanges and brokers, and created the SEC. Together they form the foundation of United States securities regulation.
