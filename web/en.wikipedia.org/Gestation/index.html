Gestation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gestation is the period of development during the carrying of an embryo, and later fetus, inside viviparous animals. It is typical for mammals but also occurs for some non-mammals. Mammals during pregnancy can have one or more gestations at the same time, for example in a multiple birth. The time interval of a gestation is called the gestation period; in humans, this is commonly called pregnancy.

Mammals

In mammals, gestation begins when a fertilised egg (zygote) implants in the female uterus and ends once the fetus leaves the uterus during labour or an induced abortion. The developing offspring is called an embryo during the early stages of organ formation and a fetus thereafter. Gestation length varies enormously with body size and life history, from about 12–13 days in the Virginia opossum to roughly 22 months in African elephants — the longest of any land mammal. Small mammals tend to have short gestations and large litters of altricial young, while large mammals have long gestations and usually a single precocial offspring.

Humans

In human obstetrics, gestational age is measured from the first day of the last menstrual period, so a full-term pregnancy lasts about 40 weeks, although birth between 37 and 42 weeks is considered term. Development is divided into three trimesters. Only about 4–5 percent of babies are born on their exact calculated due date. Births before 37 weeks are preterm and carry increased risks; pregnancies beyond 42 weeks are post-term.

Non-mammals

Viviparity has evolved independently in many groups. Some sharks give birth to live young nourished through a yolk-sac placenta; certain snakes, lizards, and fish are live-bearing; and in seahorses, gestation takes place in a brood pouch on the male. In marsupials, most development occurs after an extremely brief gestation, with the tiny newborn completing its growth attached to a teat, often inside a pouch.
