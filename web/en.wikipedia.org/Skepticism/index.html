Skepticism (from the Greek skeptikos, "inquirer") is an attitude of doubt toward claims of knowledge. In philosophy, skepticism ranges from the ancient discipline of suspending judgement to modern arguments questioning whether certainty — or even knowledge itself — is possible.

Ancient skepticism

Pyrrhonism, founded by Pyrrho of Elis in the 4th century BC and preserved in the writings of Sextus Empiricus, aimed at ataraxia (tranquillity) through epoché, the suspension of judgement on all non-evident matters. The later Academy of Arcesilaus and Carneades developed a probabilist, "academic" skepticism directed against Stoic claims to certainty.

Modern philosophical skepticism

René Descartes used methodical doubt as a tool, entertaining the possibility of an evil demon deceiving him in order to find an indubitable foundation — "I think, therefore I am." David Hume pressed skeptical arguments about induction, causation and the external world, concluding that reason alone cannot establish them and that custom and habit guide belief. Skeptical problems — concerning the external world, other minds, the past and induction — remain central in epistemology.

Scientific skepticism

In contemporary usage, scientific skepticism denotes an approach that applies critical thinking and empirical testing to extraordinary claims, particularly those of pseudoscience, alternative medicine and the paranormal. Organisations such as the Committee for Skeptical Inquiry, and figures such as Carl Sagan and James Randi, popularised the maxim that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence.
