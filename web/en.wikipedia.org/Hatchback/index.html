A hatchback is a car body configuration with a rear door — the hatch — that swings upward to provide access to the cargo area. Hatchbacks typically feature a two-box design, folding rear seats to expand the load space, and are produced in three-door and five-door configurations (the hatch counting as a door). The body style became one of the most popular formats for small and family cars worldwide following its popularisation in the 1970s, typified by the Volkswagen Golf.

Characteristics

The defining features of a hatchback are a steep, near-vertical or sloping rear hatch hinged at roof level, a shared passenger/cargo volume (unlike a sedan's separate boot/trunk), and usually folding rear seats. The style is described as a two-box design: one box for the engine, one for passengers and cargo combined. When the rear window is more steeply raked and the opening resembles a sedan's bootlid, the car is often marketed as a liftback (or liftback coupé) instead.

History

Early hatchback-type cars included the 1938 Citroën Traction Avant Commerciale, which had a two-piece rear door, and the 1949 Kaiser-Frazer Vagabond. The modern concept matured in the 1960s with cars such as the Renault 4 (1961) and Renault 16 (1965), the latter often cited as the blueprint for the modern family hatchback. The 1970s oil crises and demand for compact, versatile cars drove widespread adoption: the Volkswagen Golf (1974), Honda Civic (1972) and Ford Fiesta (1976) established the hatchback as the default small-car format in Europe and much of the world, while remaining comparatively less dominant in North America, where sedans and later SUVs predominated.

Comparison with other body styles

Estate cars (station wagons) are longer, with a full-height cargo bay behind an extended roofline; sedans separate the cargo volume in a distinct third box. Crossovers and SUVs frequently adopt hatch rear doors but sit higher with distinct proportions. The hatchback's advantages include cargo flexibility and easy loading; drawbacks can include noise, security of visible cargo, and reduced structural stiffness versus a notchback.

Market

Hatchbacks historically dominated the European B- and C-segments and remain common in Asia and Latin America. Since the 2010s their share has declined as buyers shifted to crossover SUVs, prompting manufacturers to reposition or discontinue long-running hatchback nameplates in some markets.
