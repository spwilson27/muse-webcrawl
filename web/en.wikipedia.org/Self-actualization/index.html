Self-actualization

Self-actualization is the realization or fulfilment of one's talents and potentialities, regarded especially as a drive or need present in everyone. The concept is most closely associated with Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs, in which self-actualization sits at the top of the pyramid, pursued after physiological, safety, love and esteem needs are met.

Maslow's theory

Maslow described self-actualization as the desire "to become everything one is capable of becoming." He studied people he considered self-actualized, including Albert Einstein, Eleanor Roosevelt and Frederick Douglass, and listed characteristics such as acceptance of themselves and others, spontaneity, problem-centering, autonomy, continued freshness of appreciation, and peak experiences. In his later writing Maslow placed self-transcendence beyond self-actualization.

Other uses

The term was coined by the organismic theorist Kurt Goldstein, for whom self-actualization was the master motive of the organism. Carl Rogers developed a related concept in humanistic psychology, describing the "fully functioning person" who is open to experience and lives authentically. The idea has been influential in education, management theory and popular self-help, and has also been criticised for cultural bias toward individualism and for weak empirical support.
