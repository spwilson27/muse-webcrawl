The Diadochi (Latinisation of Greek Diádochoi, "Successors") were the rival generals, families and friends of Alexander the Great who fought for control of his empire after his death in Babylon in 323 BC. Their wars, lasting some forty years, partitioned Alexander's conquests into the Hellenistic kingdoms that dominated the eastern Mediterranean until the rise of Rome.

Division of the empire

The Partition of Babylon (323 BC) distributed satrapies among the generals while nominally preserving the empire for Alexander's infant son Alexander IV and his half-brother Philip III, under the regency of Perdiccas. Ptolemy took Egypt, Antipater held Macedon and Greece, Lysimachus received Thrace, Antigonus the One-Eyed governed much of Anatolia, and Seleucus became commander of the Companion cavalry before taking Babylonia.

Wars of the Diadochi

Perdiccas's attempt to preserve a unified empire collapsed with his murder during an invasion of Egypt in 320 BC. Successive wars followed, pitting Antigonus — who aimed to reunite the whole empire — against shifting coalitions. The murder of Alexander IV by Cassander (c. 309 BC) ended the Argead line; in 306–305 BC the surviving dynasts assumed royal titles. The Battle of Ipsus (301 BC), where Antigonus was killed by the combined forces of Lysimachus, Seleucus and Ptolemy, fixed the essential division. After the Battle of Corupedium (281 BC), where Lysimachus fell to Seleucus, and the subsequent Galatian invasions, three stable kingdoms emerged under the descendants of Ptolemy, Seleucus and Antigonus (the Epigoni, or "born after").

Legacy

The Hellenistic kingdoms founded by the Diadochi spread Greek language, cities and culture from Egypt to Central Asia, patronising the Library of Alexandria, Pergamum and the Seleucid foundations, while competing militarily until absorbed by Rome and Parthia.
