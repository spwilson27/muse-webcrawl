The Durand Line is the international land border between Afghanistan and Pakistan, running about 2,640 kilometres (1,640 mi) from the Iranian border to the Chinese border. It was established in 1893 by an agreement between Sir Mortimer Durand, foreign secretary of British India, and Emir Abdur Rahman Khan of Afghanistan.

History

The line was drawn as a frontier of spheres of influence after the Second Anglo-Afghan War, splitting the lands of the Pashtun and Baloch peoples between Afghanistan and British India. It was reaffirmed by later Afghan governments in the Treaty of Rawalpindi (1919) and subsequent agreements, though Afghanistan has disputed its permanence — in 1949 an Afghan loya jirga declared the Durand agreements void, and no Afghan government has formally recognised the line as an international border since.

Modern disputes

Pakistan, as successor state to British India in the region, regards the line as its settled international border. Tensions include cross-border militancy, trade closures, and Pakistan's construction of a border fence along most of the line since 2017, protested repeatedly by Kabul. The border region, largely Pashtun on both sides, remains one of the most sensitive issues in Afghan–Pakistani relations.
