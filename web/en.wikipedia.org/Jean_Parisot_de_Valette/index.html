Jean Parisot de Valette

Jean Parisot de Valette (4 February 1495 – 21 August 1568) was a French nobleman and the 49th Grand Master of the Order of Malta, from 1557 until his death. He is best remembered for commanding the defence of Malta during the Great Siege of 1565, when a few thousand Knights Hospitaller and Maltese defenders withstood a massive Ottoman invasion force. The victory made him a hero of Christian Europe. Malta's capital city, Valletta, founded by him in 1566, bears his name.

Early life and career

De Valette was born into a noble family of Quercy in southwestern France. He joined the Order of Saint John at about twenty, in the Langue of Provence, and was present at the Siege of Rhodes in 1522, when Suleiman the Magnificent expelled the Knights from their island base; he escaped with Grand Master Philippe Villiers de L'Isle-Adam and the survivors. After years of wandering, the Order received Malta and Tripoli from Emperor Charles V in 1530. De Valette rose through its ranks as a galley commander, fighting Ottoman and Barbary corsairs; in 1541 his galley was captured in an engagement and he spent a year as a galley slave before being freed in a prisoner exchange. He subsequently served as governor of Tripoli, Grand Prior of Saint-Gilles, and lieutenant to Grand Master Claude de la Sengle, whom he succeeded on 26 August 1557.

The Great Siege of Malta, 1565

Determined to eradicate the Knights — whose corsair warfare harassed Ottoman shipping — Suleiman dispatched an armada of some 180–200 vessels and 30,000–40,000 troops under the admiral Piali Pasha and the general Mustafa Pasha, arriving on 18 May 1565. Against them de Valette, aged seventy, disposed of roughly 500–700 knights, several thousand Maltese militia and Spanish and Italian soldiers — perhaps 6,000–9,000 men in all — concentrated in Birgu and Senglea across the Grand Harbour and in the small fort of St Elmo guarding its entrance.

St Elmo's heroic resistance cost the Ottomans weeks and thousands of men, including the corsair admiral Dragut, before falling on 23 June. Through July and August the Ottomans battered Birgu and Senglea with one of the heaviest bombardments of the century and launched repeated assaults by land and sea, all repelled; de Valette, famously refusing evacuation, directed the defence, repaired breaches under fire, and executed captured Ottoman agents to deter treachery. A small relief force from Sicily (the piccolo soccorso) slipped in during July, and the main relief army of Don García de Toledo landed some 8,000–10,000 men in September. The Ottomans, their army ravaged by casualties and disease, re-embarked on 11–13 September 1565. Ottoman losses are estimated at 25,000–35,000; the defenders lost perhaps 2,500 troops and several thousand Maltese civilians.

Founding of Valletta and death

Gifts and honours poured in from Europe; Pope Pius V offered de Valette a cardinal's hat, which he declined. Resolving that Malta must never again be so exposed, he laid the foundation stone on 28 March 1566 of a new fortified capital on Mount Sceberras, named Valletta in his honour, designed by the Italian engineer Francesco Laparelli. De Valette died of a fever, following a stroke suffered while hunting, on 21 August 1568. He was buried in the crypt of St John's Co-Cathedral in the city he founded; his epitaph praises him as the scourge of Asia and Africa and the shield of Europe.

Legacy

De Valette is remembered as one of the Order's greatest Grand Masters: austere, devout, and resolute, he embodied the Hospitallers' military-religious ideal. The Great Siege is considered a turning point that checked Ottoman expansion in the central Mediterranean, and his name survives in Valletta and in numerous streets and squares across Malta.
