Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party

The Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party (Arabic: حزب البعث العربي الاشتراكي, romanized: Ḥizb al-Baʿth al-ʿArabī al-Ishtirākī; Ba'ath meaning "renaissance" or "resurrection") is a political party founded in Syria by Michel Aflaq, Salah al-Din al-Bitar, and associates of Zaki al-Arsuzi. The party espoused Ba'athism, an ideology combining Arab nationalism, Arab socialism, and anti-imperialism, under the slogan "Unity, Freedom, Socialism".

Founded in Damascus in 1947, the party spread across the Arab world and ruled Iraq (1968–2003) and Syria (1963–2024). In 1966 the party split into rival Syrian- and Iraqi-dominated factions, each claiming to be the legitimate continuation of the original movement.

History

The party was formally established at its founding congress in Damascus in April 1947, merging Aflaq and Bitar's Arab Ba'ath Movement with al-Arsuzi's Arab Ba'ath. In 1952 it merged with Akram al-Hawrani's Arab Socialist Party to form the Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party. The party seized power in Syria in the coup of March 1963 and in Iraq briefly in 1963 and again in 1968. The 1966 schism produced two rival National Commands; the Iraqi branch was overthrown by the US-led invasion of 2003, and the Syrian branch's rule ended with the fall of Bashar al-Assad in December 2024.

Organisation

The party was organised hierarchically, modelled in part on communist parties, with cells, sections, branches, and regional commands for each country, overseen by a National Command claiming authority over the whole Arab nation. In power, both branches fused party and state, with the military playing a decisive role.
