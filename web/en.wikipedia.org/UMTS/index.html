The Universal Mobile Telecommunications System (UMTS) is a third-generation (3G) mobile cellular system for networks based on the GSM standard. Developed and maintained by the 3GPP (3rd Generation Partnership Project), UMTS was first introduced in 2001 and combines a W-CDMA radio access network with the GSM core network, offering higher data rates and simultaneous voice and data services.

Technology

UMTS uses wideband code-division multiple access (W-CDMA) with 5 MHz channels, supporting theoretical data rates of up to 2 Mbit/s in its original release. Successive enhancements — HSDPA and HSUPA, together known as HSPA, and later HSPA+ — raised peak downlink rates to 14.4 Mbit/s and beyond 42 Mbit/s, blurring the line with later generations. Networks consist of Node B base stations, radio network controllers (RNCs), and a core network handling circuit-switched voice and packet-switched data.

Deployment and decline

Commercial UMTS networks launched from 2001 onward, first in Japan and Europe, and for two decades formed the backbone of mobile broadband in much of the world. Handsets typically combined UMTS with GSM fallback. With the roll-out of LTE (4G) and 5G, operators began refarming UMTS spectrum; many networks in North America, Australia, and parts of Europe were shut down in the early 2020s, though UMTS remains in service in numerous countries.
