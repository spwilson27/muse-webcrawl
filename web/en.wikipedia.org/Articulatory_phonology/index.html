Articulatory phonology

Articulatory phonology is a linguistic theory developed by Catherine Browman and Louis Goldstein at Haskins Laboratories. It proposes that the basic units of speech are not abstract segments or features but gestures — the coordinated movements of the vocal tract articulators, such as the lips, tongue tip, tongue body, velum, and glottis.

Gestures and gestural scores

Each gesture is modelled as a dynamical system driving an articulator toward a constriction target in the vocal tract. Utterances are represented as gestural scores, showing gestures overlapping in time; this overlap explains phenomena such as coarticulation, assimilation, and casual-speech reduction without recourse to rewrite rules. The approach is closely connected to task dynamics, the computational framework used to model the gestures.

Relation to other theories

Articulatory phonology contrasts with traditional generative phonology by grounding phonological structure directly in physical action, unifying phonetics and phonology in a single framework. It has been applied to speech acquisition, disordered speech, and cross-linguistic patterns of timing and rhythm.
