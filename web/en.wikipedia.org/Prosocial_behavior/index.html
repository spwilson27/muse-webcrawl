Prosocial behavior

Prosocial behavior, or "voluntary behavior intended to benefit another", is a social behaviour that benefits other people or society as a whole, such as helping, sharing, donating, co-operating, and volunteering. The motivations behind prosocial behaviour may include empathy, concern for others' welfare, social norms such as reciprocity and social responsibility, and also more self-interested motives such as reputation or expected future help. It is contrasted with antisocial behaviour.

FieldSocial psychology, developmental psychology
ExamplesHelping, sharing, donating, volunteering, co-operating
Key motivesEmpathy, altruism, social norms, reciprocity

Research

Research on prosocial behaviour expanded greatly after the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in New York City and the subsequent studies by Bibb Latané and John Darley of the bystander effect, which showed that the presence of others can reduce the likelihood that any one individual helps in an emergency. Their decision model of helping identified stages — noticing the event, interpreting it as an emergency, assuming personal responsibility, knowing how to help, and deciding to act.

Development

Prosocial tendencies appear early: toddlers show instrumental helping from roughly 14–18 months, and sharing and comforting increase through childhood, influenced by parenting, modelling, and socialisation. Cross-cultural research finds prosociality in all human societies, though the forms it takes and the people it extends to vary.

Explanations

Evolutionary theories explain prosociality through kin selection, reciprocal altruism, and group selection. Psychologists emphasise the empathy–altruism hypothesis (C. Daniel Batson), which proposes that empathic concern can produce genuinely altruistic motivation, alongside social learning, moral identity, and personality traits such as agreeableness and honesty-humility.

Applications

Fostering prosocial behaviour is a goal in education, organisational life, and public policy, through volunteering programs, charitable giving incentives, and interventions that build empathy and social norms of helping.
