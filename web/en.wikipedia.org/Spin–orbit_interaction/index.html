In quantum physics, the spin–orbit interaction (also called spin–orbit effect, spin–orbit coupling, or spin–orbit splitting) is a relativistic interaction of a particle's spin with its motion inside a potential. A key example of this phenomenon is the spin–orbit interaction leading to shifts in an electron's atomic energy levels, due to electromagnetic interaction between the electron's magnetic dipole, its orbital motion, and the electrostatic field of the positively charged nucleus.

This phenomenon is detectable as a splitting of spectral lines, which can be thought of as a Zeeman effect product of two relativistic effects: the apparent magnetic field seen from the electron's perspective and the magnetic moment of the electron associated with its intrinsic spin.

In atoms

For an electron bound in an atom, the spin–orbit interaction contributes a term to the Hamiltonian proportional to the scalar product of the orbital angular momentum L and the spin S. The resulting energy shift depends on the total angular momentum J = L + S, splitting levels with the same orbital quantum numbers into closely spaced components — the origin of the fine structure of atomic spectra, such as the doublet structure of the sodium D lines.

Thomas precession

A naive calculation of the effect overestimates the splitting by a factor of two. The discrepancy is resolved by accounting for Thomas precession, a relativistic kinematic effect arising from the successive non-collinear Lorentz boosts of the electron as it orbits the nucleus, which contributes a factor of one half.

In solids

In solid-state physics, spin–orbit coupling plays a central role in spintronics, the spin Hall effect, Rashba and Dresselhaus effects in semiconductor heterostructures, and the physics of topological insulators, whose surface states are protected by strong spin–orbit interaction.
