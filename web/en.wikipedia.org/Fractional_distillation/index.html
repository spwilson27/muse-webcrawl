Fractional distillation

A fractionating column: repeated condensation and vaporisation separate a mixture by boiling point

Fractional distillation is the separation of a liquid mixture into its component parts, or fractions, based on differences in their boiling points. The mixture is heated so that vapour rises into a fractionating column, where repeated cycles of condensation and re-evaporation progressively enrich the vapour in the most volatile component, achieving a much better separation than a single simple distillation.

Principle

As vapour travels up the column it encounters a temperature gradient — hottest at the bottom, coolest at the top. Less volatile components condense and return downward, while more volatile components continue rising. Each condensation–vaporisation cycle is equivalent to a theoretical plate; columns packed with glass beads, metal rings, or structured packing provide a large surface area for many such cycles. The process is described by Raoult's law and Dalton's law, and composition changes are visualised on McCabe–Thiele diagrams.

Applications

Fractional distillation is fundamental to petroleum refining, where crude oil is separated in huge fractionating towers into gases, gasoline (petrol), naphtha, kerosene, diesel, lubricating oils, and bitumen. It is also used to separate liquefied air into nitrogen, oxygen, and argon, and in the production of distilled spirits and purification of chemicals and solvents. Mixtures forming azeotropes cannot be fully separated by ordinary fractional distillation.
