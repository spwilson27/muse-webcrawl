Parasitic disease

A parasitic disease, also known as parasitosis, is an infectious disease caused by parasites. Parasites are organisms which live in or on another organism (their host) and benefit by deriving nutrients at the host's expense. The organisms involved include single-celled protozoa, parasitic worms (helminths), and ectoparasites such as lice, fleas, and mites. Parasitic diseases affect billions of people worldwide, especially in tropical and subtropical regions, and are a major cause of morbidity among the world's poorest populations.

Major diseases

Among the most significant parasitic diseases are malaria, caused by Plasmodium protozoa transmitted by mosquitoes; schistosomiasis, caused by blood flukes; soil-transmitted helminthiases caused by roundworm, whipworm, and hookworm; lymphatic filariasis; onchocerciasis (river blindness); Chagas disease; leishmaniasis; and African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness). Many are classed as neglected tropical diseases. Transmission occurs through insect vectors, contaminated water or food, contact with soil, or freshwater snails that serve as intermediate hosts.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis traditionally relies on microscopic examination of blood, stool, or tissue samples to identify eggs, larvae, or the parasites themselves; antigen and antibody tests and molecular methods are increasingly used. Treatments include antiparasitic drugs such as albendazole and mebendazole for worms, praziquantel for flukes and tapeworms, and artemisinin-based combination therapies for malaria. Prevention emphasises sanitation, safe water, vector control (such as insecticide-treated bed nets), and mass drug administration campaigns, which have brought diseases like dracunculiasis (Guinea worm disease) close to eradication.
