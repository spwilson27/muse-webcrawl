Zuiderzee Works

The Zuiderzee Works (Dutch: Zuiderzeewerken) are a system of dams and dikes, land reclamation and drainage work in the Netherlands, among the largest hydraulic engineering projects undertaken by any country. They closed off the Zuiderzee, a shallow inlet of the North Sea, converting it into the freshwater IJsselmeer lake, and reclaimed vast polders — including the province of Flevoland.

Background

The Zuiderzee had long threatened the Dutch lowlands with storm floods, most disastrously in 1916. Engineer-politician Cornelis Lely had proposed enclosure and reclamation in 1891; the flood of 1916 and wartime food shortages finally spurred Parliament to pass the Zuiderzee Act of 1918.

Construction

Work began with the Amsteldiep dike (1924) and culminated in the Afsluitdijk, a 32-kilometre causeway-dam between North Holland and Friesland, closed on 28 May 1932. The enclosed Zuiderzee became the freshwater IJsselmeer. Enclosure dikes were then built around areas to be drained, and great pumping stations (gemaal) — steam, diesel and electric, such as the Leemans and Wortman stations — emptied the polders: Wieringermeer (1930), Noordoostpolder (1942), Eastern Flevoland (1956) and Southern Flevoland (1968), the last two forming the new province of Flevoland (established 1986) with the cities of Lelystad and Almere.

Effects and legacy

The works ended storm flooding from the Zuiderzee, created farmland and living space, supplied fresh water, and inspired the later Delta Works. Ecological concerns halted the planned Markerwaard polder; the Markermeer lake remains open water. The American Society of Civil Engineers listed the Zuiderzee Works among the Seven Wonders of the Modern World.
