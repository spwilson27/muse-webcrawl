Braided river

A braided river is a river characterised by a network of small channels separated by small, and often temporary, islands called braid bars. Braided streams occur in rivers with high slope and/or large sediment load. Braided channels are also typical of environments in which a reduction in stream competence on the downstream side of a delta leads to rapid deposition of blockages in mid-channel.

Braided streams tend to occur in rivers with high sediment loads or coarse grain sizes, and in rivers with steeper slopes than typical rivers with straight or meandering channel patterns. They are also associated with rivers with rapid and frequent variation in the amount of water they carry. Braided channels are found in a variety of environments all over the world, including gravelly mountain streams, sand-bed rivers, on alluvial fans, on alluvial plains, on river deltas, and across depositional plains.

Formation

Braided channels are formed when sediment, especially bed load, is deposited in mid-channel, creating braid bars around which the flow must divide. Braiding is distinct from anastomosing in that anastomosing channels have stable, vegetated banks and islands, whereas braid bars are unvegetated and ephemeral, shifting with each flood. High sediment supply overwhelms the stream's capacity to transport it, so excess material accumulates and obstructs the flow.

Mechanism

The sequence begins when a submerged bar forms by the deposition of coarse sediment. As the bar grows it eventually emerges above the water surface, dividing the channel into two or more subparallel channels that rejoin downstream. Continued deposition and erosion cause the pattern to shift continuously. Factors favouring braiding include abundant coarse sediment, weak or easily eroded banks, and steep valley slopes.

Examples

Notable braided rivers include:

  • The Brahmaputra River in India and Bangladesh, one of the largest braided rivers in the world
  • The Waimakariri, Rakaia, and Rangitata rivers in Canterbury, New Zealand
  • The Platte River in Nebraska, United States
  • The Yellow River in China
  • The Tagliamento in Italy, often described as the last remaining fully braided river in the Alps
  • The Kosi River in India

Ecology

The shifting mosaic of channels, bars, and floodplain habitats in braided rivers supports distinctive ecosystems. Gravel bars provide nesting habitat for birds such as the endangered wrybill of New Zealand, the only bird in the world with a beak bent sideways. The dynamic nature of these systems, however, makes their specialist species vulnerable to flow regulation, gravel extraction, and weed encroachment.
