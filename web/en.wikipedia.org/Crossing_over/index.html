Crossing over

Chromosomal crossover, or crossing over, is the exchange of genetic material during sexual reproduction between two homologous chromosomes' non-sister chromatids that results in recombinant chromosomes during meiosis. It is one of the final phases of genetic recombination, which occurs in the pachytene stage of prophase I of meiosis during a process called synapsis.

Process

Synapsis begins before the synaptonemal complex develops and is not completed until near the end of prophase I. Crossover usually occurs when matching regions on matching chromosomes break and then reconnect to the other chromosome; the visible points of exchange are called chiasmata (singular: chiasma). The resulting chromosomes carry new combinations of alleles, generating genetic diversity among offspring.

History

Crossing over was described in theory by Thomas Hunt Morgan, building on the observations of Frans Alfons Janssens, who proposed the phenomenon in 1909 based on microscopic chiasmata. Barbara McClintock and Harriet Creighton provided the first cytological demonstration of crossover in maize in 1931.

Consequences

Crossing over, together with independent assortment, is a principal source of genetic variation in sexually reproducing organisms. The frequency of crossover between two gene loci (the recombination frequency) was used to construct the first genetic linkage maps: one percent recombination equals one centimorgan. Unequal crossing over, in which segments are exchanged unevenly, can produce gene duplications and deletions, as in the mutations causing Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 1A and hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsies.
