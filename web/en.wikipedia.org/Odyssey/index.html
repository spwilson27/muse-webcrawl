Odyssey

The Odyssey (Ancient Greek: Ὀδύσσεια, romanised: Odýsseia) is one of two major ancient Greek epic poems attributed to Homer. It is one of the oldest extant works of literature still widely read by modern audiences. As with the Iliad, the poem is divided into 24 books. It follows the Greek hero Odysseus, king of Ithaca, and his journey home after the Trojan War.

Synopsis

After the ten-year war, Odysseus's voyage home to Ithaca lasts another ten years, during which he encounters the Lotus-Eaters, blinds the Cyclops Polyphemus — drawing the wrath of Poseidon — survives the Sirens, passes between Scylla and Charybdis, loses his crew after they slaughter the cattle of the sun god Helios, and spends seven years detained by the nymph Calypso. The poem opens in medias res, with Odysseus absent and his wife Penelope beset by suitors consuming his household, while his son Telemachus searches for news of him. Reaching Ithaca with the help of the Phaeacians, Odysseus, disguised as a beggar and aided by Athena, Telemachus and loyal servants, wins the contest of the bow and slaughters the suitors, finally revealing himself to Penelope.

Themes and structure

The poem is a nostos — a homecoming story — exploring identity, hospitality (xenia), loyalty, cunning (mētis) and the contrasting fortunes of returning heroes such as Agamemnon. Its non-linear narrative, beginning in the middle and using extended flashbacks in Odysseus's own voice at the Phaeacian court, was admired already in antiquity.

Transmission and influence

The Odyssey was composed in the oral-formulaic tradition in Homeric Greek, probably in the late 8th century BC. Together with the Iliad it stands at the head of the Western literary canon; it shaped Virgil's Aeneid and has inspired works from Dante and Tennyson to James Joyce's Ulysses (1922). Notable English translations include those by George Chapman, Alexander Pope, Emily Wilson (2017, the first published English translation by a woman) and many others.
