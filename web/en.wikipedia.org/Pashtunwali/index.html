Pashtunwali (Pashto: پښتونوالی) is the traditional lifestyle and customary code of conduct of the Pashtun people of Afghanistan and Pakistan. It is an unwritten ethical code that predates Islam in the region and continues to govern Pashtun social organization, particularly in rural and tribal areas. Scholars regard it as one of the oldest surviving customary legal systems in the world.

Main principles

The code is often summarized through three central obligations:

  • Melmastia (hospitality) — the obligation to show generous hospitality to all visitors and guests, without expectation of reward.
  • Nanawatai (asylum/forgiveness) — the duty to protect a person who seeks refuge, and to forgive a defeated or repentant enemy who formally submits.
  • Badal (revenge/justice) — the obligation to avenge an insult or injury, sometimes translated as "an eye for an eye," though it can also be discharged through compensation.

Other important values include Turah (bravery), Sabat (loyalty), Izzat and Nang (honor), Namus (honor of women and family), and respect for the decisions of the jirga, the council of elders.

Jirga

Disputes are traditionally resolved by a jirga, an assembly of tribal elders whose decisions are reached by consensus and are binding on the community. The jirga functions as Pashtun society's judicial and legislative institution, and its resolutions can involve fines, compensation, or the ritual exchange associated with reconciliation.

Relation to Islam and state law

Pashtunwali coexists, sometimes uneasily, with Islamic law (sharia) and the formal legal systems of Afghanistan and Pakistan. Where its customs conflict with Islamic teaching — for example in certain practices concerning women, such as swara (giving a girl in marriage to settle a feud) — religious scholars and reformers have criticized them, and such practices are illegal under state law.
