Lie theory

Lie theory is the area of mathematics concerned with Lie groups, Lie algebras, and their representations. Developed principally by the Norwegian mathematician Sophus Lie in the late 19th century, it provides the mathematical framework for studying continuous symmetry.

Origins

Lie sought to do for differential equations what Galois theory had done for algebraic equations: classify problems by their symmetry groups. He introduced continuous transformation groups, now called Lie groups, and their infinitesimal counterparts, Lie algebras. Early contributions also came from Wilhelm Killing and Élie Cartan, whose classification of semisimple Lie algebras remains fundamental.

Key concepts

A Lie group is a group that is also a smooth manifold, with smooth group operations; examples include the rotation group SO(3) and the general linear group. Its Lie algebra is the tangent space at the identity, equipped with the Lie bracket. The exponential map connects the algebra to the group. Representation theory studies how Lie groups and algebras act on vector spaces; the representation theory of compact groups was developed by Hermann Weyl and others.

Applications

Lie theory underpins much of modern physics: symmetry groups govern the Standard Model of particle physics, angular momentum in quantum mechanics, and conservation laws via Noether's theorem. It is also central to differential geometry, number theory (through automorphic forms and the Langlands program), and robotics and control theory.
