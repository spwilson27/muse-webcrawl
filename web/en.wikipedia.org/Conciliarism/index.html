Conciliarism

Conciliarism is a movement in the Catholic Church that held that the final authority in spiritual matters resided with a general (ecumenical) council, rather than with the pope alone. According to conciliarist teaching, a council representing the whole Church could judge, and even depose, a pope.

Origins

Conciliarist ideas drew on medieval canon law and the writings of thinkers such as Marsilius of Padua and William of Ockham, who questioned unlimited papal authority. The movement gained force during the Western Schism (1378–1417), when rival claimants to the papacy divided Latin Christendom and councils appeared to offer the only way to restore unity.

The great councils

The Council of Pisa (1409) attempted to resolve the schism but added a third claimant. The Council of Constance (1414–1418) succeeded in ending the schism and, in its decree Haec sancta (1415), declared that a general council derives its authority directly from Christ and that everyone, including the pope, must obey it in matters of faith and reform. The Council of Basel (1431–1449) later clashed with Pope Eugene IV and eventually weakened the movement by electing an antipope.

Decline and legacy

By the later fifteenth century, popes reasserted their supremacy, and Pius II's bull Execrabilis (1460) condemned appeals from a pope to a future council. Nevertheless, conciliarism influenced later theories of constitutionalism and limited government, and echoes of it appear in Gallicanism and in debates preceding the First Vatican Council's definition of papal infallibility (1870).
