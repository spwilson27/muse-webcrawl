Feather

Feathers display a wide range of colours and structures.

Feathers are epidermal growths that form a distinctive outer covering, or plumage, on both avian (bird) and some non-avian dinosaurs and other archosaurs. They are considered the most complex integumentary structures found in vertebrates and a premier example of a complex evolutionary novelty. Made of keratin, feathers grow out of follicles in the skin.

Structure

A typical contour feather consists of a central shaft (rachis) bearing a vane of barbs; the barbs carry smaller barbules with hooklets that zip the vane together into a coherent surface. The hollow base of the shaft, embedded in the follicle, is the calamus or quill. Feather types include stiff flight feathers (remiges on the wing and rectrices on the tail), contour feathers that streamline the body, soft insulating down feathers, semiplumes, filoplumes and bristles.

Functions

Feathers serve many functions: they form the aerodynamic surfaces that make powered flight possible in birds, provide insulation against heat loss (and, when fluffed, are among the most effective natural insulators), waterproof the body of aquatic birds, and are used in camouflage and visual display, including the elaborate courtship ornaments of birds of paradise and peacocks.

Evolution

Fossils from China and elsewhere show that many theropod dinosaurs bore feathers or feather-like filaments, indicating that feathers evolved before flight, likely first for insulation or display. Archaeopteryx and later fossil birds document the development of fully modern flight feathers.
