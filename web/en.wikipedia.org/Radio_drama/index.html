Radio drama

A radio drama performance: actors, sound effects and music combine to create a "theatre of the mind".

A radio drama (or radio play) is a dramatised, purely acoustic performance broadcast on radio or published as audio. With no visual component, radio drama depends on dialogue, music and sound effects to help the listener imagine the characters and story. It is credited with developing the "theatre of the mind".

By the 1930s and 1940s — the "Golden Age of Radio" — radio drama was a dominant form of entertainment in the United States and Britain, spanning detective serials, soap operas, thrillers, comedy and prestige adaptations of stage plays and novels. Orson Welles's Mercury Theatre on the Air achieved lasting fame with its 1938 adaptation of The War of the Worlds, presented as a simulated news broadcast.

History

Experiments with broadcasting plays began in the early 1920s, shortly after regular radio services started. In Britain, the BBC became a major patron of the form, commissioning original works by writers including Dylan Thomas, whose Under Milk Wood (1954) became a classic of the genre, and Samuel Beckett, who wrote All That Fall specifically for radio. Television displaced radio drama in the United States in the 1950s, but the form survived strongly in Britain and Germany — the German Hörspiel tradition — and on BBC Radio 4 and Radio 3.

Modern revival

The growth of podcasts and streaming audio in the 21st century has produced a revival of audio drama, with independent and commercial producers creating serialised fiction using radio drama techniques. Long-running programmes such as the BBC's The Archers, the world's longest-running radio soap, continue to draw large audiences.
