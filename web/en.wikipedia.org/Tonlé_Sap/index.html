The Tonlé Sap is a lake in the northwest of Cambodia and the largest freshwater lake in Southeast Asia. Combined with the Tonlé Sap River that connects it to the Mekong, it forms one of the world's most unusual hydrological systems: twice a year, the connecting river reverses its direction of flow.

The flood pulse

During the dry season the lake is small and shallow — about 2,700 km² and a metre deep — and drains southeast into the Mekong. When monsoon rains and Himalayan melt swell the Mekong in June, its waters push back up the Tonlé Sap River and flood the lake, which expands four- to six-fold to as much as 16,000 km², drowning forests and farmland. In October the flow reverses again. The reversal is celebrated in Cambodia by the Bon Om Touk water festival in Phnom Penh.

Ecology and people

The annual flood pulse makes the Tonlé Sap one of the most productive freshwater fisheries on Earth, supplying the majority of Cambodia's protein catch; fish such as the trey riel are fermented into prahok paste. Around the lake live communities in floating villages and houses on tall stilts, including Chong Khneas and Kampong Phluk. UNESCO designated the lake a biosphere reserve in 1997. Upstream Mekong dams, sand mining and climate change now threaten the timing and size of the flood pulse and the fishery it sustains.
