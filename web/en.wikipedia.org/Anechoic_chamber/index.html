Anechoic chamber

An anechoic chamber (from Greek: "no echo") is a room designed to stop reflections or echoes of either sound or electromagnetic waves. The interior surfaces are covered with wedge-shaped absorbers, traditionally made of fiberglass or foam, which dissipate incident energy instead of reflecting it. Chambers are also insulated against external noise, making them among the quietest places on Earth; the background noise level in the quietest chambers measures below −20 dBA, beneath the threshold of human hearing.

Acoustic chambers

Fully anechoic rooms absorb sound on all six surfaces, including a mesh floor suspended above absorptive wedges; hemi-anechoic rooms have a hard floor to simulate real-world ground conditions. They are used to test loudspeakers, microphones, hearing aids, and machinery noise, and for psychoacoustic research. Visitors often find stays in such chambers disorienting, because in the absence of echoes they hear their own heartbeat, breathing, and internal body sounds prominently.

Radio-frequency chambers

RF anechoic chambers are lined with radiation-absorbent material, usually carbon-loaded foam pyramids, and enclosed in a Faraday cage to exclude external signals. They are used for antenna measurements, radar cross-section testing, and electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) certification of electronic devices.

Notable chambers

Famous facilities include the anechoic chamber at Orfield Laboratories in Minneapolis, long cited as one of the quietest places on Earth, and Microsoft's Building 87 chamber, which recorded a background level of about −20.6 dBA in 2015. Composer John Cage's 1951 visit to an anechoic chamber at Harvard — where he heard two sounds he attributed to his nervous system and blood circulation — was a formative experience behind his silent piece 4′33″.
