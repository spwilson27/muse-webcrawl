Cosmic ray

Cosmic rays are high-energy particles or clusters of particles — primarily protons and atomic nuclei — that move through space at nearly the speed of light. They originate from the Sun, from outside the Solar System in the Milky Way, and from distant galaxies. Upon impact with Earth's atmosphere, they produce cascades of secondary particles called extensive air showers.

Composition and energy

About 89% of cosmic rays are protons, some 10% are helium nuclei, and about 1% are heavier nuclei, along with electrons and antimatter components such as positrons and antiprotons. Their energies span an enormous range, from about 107 eV to beyond 1020 eV for the rarest ultra-high-energy cosmic rays — far exceeding anything produced by terrestrial accelerators.

History

Victor Hess discovered cosmic rays in 1912 through balloon flights showing that ionising radiation increased with altitude, work for which he shared the 1936 Nobel Prize in Physics. The positron and the muon were both first identified in cosmic rays, and cosmic-ray research dominated particle physics before large accelerators were built.

Effects

Cosmic rays contribute to background radiation, produce cosmogenic isotopes such as carbon-14 used in radiocarbon dating, and pose a hazard to astronauts and high-altitude aircrew. They are also suspected of causing single-event upsets in electronics and may influence cloud formation.
