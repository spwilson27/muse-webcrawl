Diplopia

Diplopia is the simultaneous perception of two images of a single object that may be displaced horizontally or vertically in relation to each other. Also called double vision, it is a loss of visual focus under regular conditions, and is often voluntary. However, when occurring involuntarily, it results from impaired function of the extraocular muscles, where both eyes are aimed at the target but not converged nowhere correctly. Such issues occur when individual eye functions conflict with combined function.

Other namesDouble vision
SpecialtyOphthalmology, neurology
TypesMonocular, binocular (horizontal, vertical, torsional)
Examples of causesStrabismus, cranial nerve palsy, cataract, myasthenia gravis

Classification

Binocular diplopia is present only when both eyes are open and disappears when either eye is covered; it results from misalignment of the visual axes, as in strabismus or palsies of the third, fourth or sixth cranial nerves. It may be horizontal (images side by side), vertical (one above the other) or torsional. Monocular diplopia persists when the unaffected eye is closed and arises within the eye itself, most often from irregular refraction — causes include cataract, corneal irregularity or scarring, dislocated lens, severe astigmatism and dry eye.

Causes

Binocular diplopia can result from damage to the extraocular muscles or the nerves supplying them (cranial nerves III, IV and VI), disorders of the neuromuscular junction such as myasthenia gravis, orbital disease such as thyroid eye disease, brainstem or cerebral lesions, diabetes-related microvascular palsies, aneurysms, trauma and decompensating latent squints. Physiological diplopia — the normal doubling of objects outside the point of fixation — is usually suppressed unconsciously and is not pathological.

Diagnosis

Evaluation begins with distinguishing monocular from binocular diplopia by covering each eye in turn. Clinicians assess eye movements in the nine positions of gaze, perform cover and prism cover tests, and may use the Hess chart or Lancaster red-green test to identify the affected muscle. New-onset diplopia, especially with pain, pupil involvement or other neurological signs, warrants prompt investigation, including neuroimaging and blood tests, because it can signal aneurysm, stroke or tumour.

Treatment

Treatment addresses the underlying cause. Temporary measures include occluding one eye with a patch, prism lenses incorporated into spectacles, botulinum toxin injection into an extraocular muscle, and Fresnel press-on prisms. Strabismus surgery may realign the eyes once the condition is stable. Monocular diplopia from cataract or refractive causes is managed with lenses, pinhole correction or surgery as appropriate.
