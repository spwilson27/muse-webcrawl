Lysosome

A lysosome seen by electron microscopy

Discovered: 1955, Christian de Duve
Internal pH: ~4.5–5.0
Function: digestion of macromolecules and worn-out organelles

A lysosome is a membrane-bound organelle found in many animal cells. It is a spherical vesicle containing hydrolytic enzymes capable of breaking down virtually all kinds of biomolecules — proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, and lipids. The lysosome serves as the cell's digestive and waste-disposal system, degrading material taken in from outside the cell by endocytosis and recycling the cell's own worn-out components through autophagy.

Structure and enzymes

Lysosomes range from about 0.1 to 1.2 micrometres in diameter. A proton pump in their membrane acidifies the interior to pH 4.5–5.0, the optimum for the roughly 60 acid hydrolases they contain, including proteases (cathepsins), lipases, nucleases, and phosphatases. The membrane protects the rest of the cell: if a lysosome ruptures, its enzymes are largely inactive at the neutral pH of the cytosol.

Discovery

Lysosomes were identified biochemically by Christian de Duve in 1955, work that contributed to his share of the 1974 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Their name comes from the Greek lysis (loosening/dissolution) and soma (body).

Clinical significance

Inherited deficiencies of individual lysosomal enzymes cause lysosomal storage diseases such as Tay–Sachs disease, Gaucher disease, and Pompe disease, in which undegraded substrates accumulate in cells. Lysosomes also participate in cell signalling, membrane repair, pathogen destruction, and programmed cell death.
