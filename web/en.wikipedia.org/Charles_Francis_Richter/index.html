Charles F. Richter

Charles Francis Richter (/ˈrɪktər/; April 26, 1900 – September 30, 1985) was an American seismologist and physicist. He is most famous as the inventor of the Richter magnitude scale, which, until the development of the moment magnitude scale in 1979, quantified the size of earthquakes. Inspired by Kiyoo Wadati's 1928 paper on shallow and deep earthquakes, Richter first used the scale in 1935 after developing it in collaboration with Beno Gutenberg; both worked at the California Institute of Technology.

Early life and education

Richter was born in Overpeck, Ohio, and moved with his divorced mother to Los Angeles as a child. He attended Stanford University, earning his undergraduate degree in 1920, and began graduate work at the California Institute of Technology (Caltech) in 1923, receiving his Ph.D. in theoretical physics in 1928.

Career

Richter developed an interest in seismology while working at the Carnegie Institute; he then worked at the new Seismological Laboratory in Pasadena under the direction of Harry Wood. In 1927 Richter became a staff member at the lab. His partnership with Beno Gutenberg produced both the magnitude scale that bears Richter's name and the Gutenberg–Richter law describing the relationship between earthquake magnitude and frequency. Richter became a professor of seismology at Caltech in 1952 and published the influential textbooks Elementary Seismology (1958) and Seismicity of the Earth (with Gutenberg, 1954).

Personal life

Richter was an avowed naturist and promoted naturism with his wife. He was also an atheist and wrote poetry in his spare time. He died of congestive heart failure in Pasadena in 1985.
