Deregulation

Deregulation is the process of removing or reducing state regulations, typically in the economic sphere. It is the repeal of governmental regulation of the economy. It became common in advanced industrial economies in the 1970s and 1980s, argued for on the grounds that regulation inhibits free trade, creates oligopolies, is a form of rent-seeking, or produces perverse outcomes through regulatory capture.

The airline industry was a landmark case of US deregulation

History

In the United States, a wave of deregulation began under President Jimmy Carter — the Airline Deregulation Act of 1978, followed by trucking (Motor Carrier Act 1980) and rail (Staggers Act 1980) — and expanded under Ronald Reagan into telecommunications, energy, and finance. In the United Kingdom, the governments of Margaret Thatcher combined deregulation with privatization of state industries. Financial deregulation culminated in measures such as the repeal of the Glass–Steagall Act's separation of commercial and investment banking in 1999.

Arguments for and against

Supporters cite lower consumer prices, new entry and innovation — airline fares fell sharply after 1978 — and argue that market competition disciplines firms better than rules. Critics respond that some regulations protect safety, consumers, and financial stability, blaming deregulation for outcomes such as the savings and loan crisis, the California electricity crisis of 2000–01, and aspects of the 2008 financial crisis. Economists often distinguish deregulation from re-regulation: replacing old rules with markets requires new rules for competition, transmission access, or prudential oversight.

Related concepts

Deregulation is distinct from liberalization (opening markets) and privatization (transferring state assets to private owners), though the three were frequently pursued together in the late 20th century.
