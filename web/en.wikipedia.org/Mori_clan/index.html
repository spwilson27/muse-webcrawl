Mori clan

[Image: Mori clan mon (crest) and Matsuyama Castle]

The Mori clan (Japanese: 毛利氏, Mori-shi) was a Japanese samurai clan, descended from Oe no Hiromoto (1148-1225) and founded by his grandson Mori Suemitsu, who took his name from the Mori estate in Sagami Province. The clan rose to prominence in Aki Province in western Honshu, and during the Sengoku period under Mori Terumoto became one of the most powerful daimyo families in Japan, ruling ten provinces in the Chugoku region with an income of around one million koku.

Origins

The clan's distant ancestor, Oe no Hiromoto, was an influential courtier and administrator who served Minamoto no Yoritomo and helped found the Kamakura shogunate. The family's home base shifted west to Aki Province, where they served as local retainers (jito) under the Takeda and Ouchi clans before emerging as independent warlords in the sixteenth century.

Motonari and expansion

The clan's fortunes were transformed by Mori Motonari (1497-1571), famed for his cunning generalship and the victory at the Battle of Itsukushima (1555) against Sue Harukata. Motonari expanded Mori control over much of the Chugoku region, defeating the Ouchi of Suo and fighting the Amago in Izumo. He is remembered for the tale of the "three arrows," in which he taught his three sons - Mori Takamoto, Kikkawa Motoharu and Kobayakawa Takakage - that they must remain united, as three arrows together are far harder to break than one alone.

Sekigahara and Edo period

Motonari's grandson Mori Terumoto served as a regent for the Toyotomi and commanded a large force during the Korean invasions. In 1600 he was the nominal commander of the Western Army against Tokugawa Ieyasu at the Battle of Sekigahara; following defeat, the clan was reduced to the provinces of Suo and Nagato (present-day Yamaguchi Prefecture) and moved its seat to Hagi. During the Edo period the Mori ruled Nagato and Suo as the daimyo of Choshu Domain. Choshu became a leading force in the overthrow of the Tokugawa shogunate in the Meiji Restoration, and the Mori remained marquises in the Meiji peerage.
