Baze v. Rees

Baze v. Rees, 553 U.S. 35 (2008), was a decision of the Supreme Court of the United States concerning the constitutionality of lethal injection as a method of execution. The Court held that Kentucky's three-drug lethal injection protocol did not violate the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment, because the challengers had not shown that the protocol created a substantial risk of serious harm compared with known, feasible alternatives.

Background

Ralph Baze and Thomas Bowling, Kentucky death row inmates, challenged the state's lethal injection procedure. Kentucky used a sequence of three drugs: sodium thiopental to render the prisoner unconscious, pancuronium bromide to paralyse, and potassium chloride to stop the heart. The petitioners argued that if the anaesthetic were improperly administered, the prisoner could experience severe pain while paralysed and unable to signal distress. While the case was pending, executions by lethal injection were effectively halted nationwide.

Decision

The Court affirmed by a 7–2 vote, but without a majority rationale. Chief Justice John Roberts's plurality opinion, joined by Justices Kennedy and Alito, held that a method of execution is unconstitutional only if it poses a "substantial risk of serious harm" and there exists a known and feasible alternative that significantly reduces that risk. Several justices wrote concurrences with differing standards, making the controlling legal test a lasting subject of debate. Justices Ginsburg and Souter dissented.

Aftermath

Following the decision, executions resumed in Kentucky and other states. The reasoning was revisited in later lethal-injection cases, including Glossip v. Gross (2015) and Bucklew v. Precythe (2019), which built on the requirement that challengers identify a feasible alternative method.
