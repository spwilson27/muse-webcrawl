systemd

systemd is a suite of system and service management software for Linux, centred on an init system that runs as process ID 1 and brings up and supervises all other services. It was created in 2010 by Lennart Poettering and Kay Sievers at Red Hat and first shipped in Fedora 15 (2011); it has since become the default init system of nearly all major distributions, including Debian (from version 8), Ubuntu (from 15.04), RHEL 7, and Arch Linux.

Design

systemd organises work into units — services, sockets, devices, mounts, timers, and targets — described in declarative configuration files. It starts services in parallel by exploiting socket activation (inherited from ideas in Apple's launchd), tracks processes reliably with kernel cgroups, and provides the journal, a structured binary log managed by systemd-journald. Utilities include systemctl for controlling units, journalctl for logs, systemd-analyze for boot performance, and components such as systemd-resolved, systemd-networkd, and systemd-boot. Service files express dependencies explicitly, replacing fragile shell-script ordering in SysV init.

Controversy

systemd's broad scope and monolithic design have drawn sustained criticism from advocates of the Unix philosophy of small, single-purpose tools, and forks and alternatives such as OpenRC, runit, and Devuan (a Debian fork created to avoid systemd) exist. Supporters counter that its standardisation, sandboxing features, and declarative model solved real problems of the fragmented init landscape.
