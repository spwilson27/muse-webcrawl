Prebisch–Singer hypothesis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The hypothesis predicts a secular decline in the terms of trade of primary-commodity exporters.

The Prebisch–Singer hypothesis (also called the Prebisch–Singer thesis) holds that over the long run the price of primary commodities — food, raw materials, and minerals — declines relative to the price of manufactured goods, so that the terms of trade of commodity-exporting (typically developing) countries deteriorate over time. The hypothesis was proposed independently around 1950 by the Argentine economist Raúl Prebisch, in work for the UN Economic Commission for Latin America, and by the German-British economist Hans Singer.

Prebisch–Singer hypothesis

Proposed: c. 1949–1950

Proponents: Raúl Prebisch; Hans Singer

Claim: Secular decline in primary commodity terms of trade

Influenced: Structuralism, dependency theory, import-substitution industrialisation

Proposed mechanisms

Several mechanisms were offered. Demand for food and raw materials grows more slowly than income (Engel's law), whereas demand for manufactures rises faster. Workers in industrial countries, organised in strong unions, capture productivity gains as higher wages, while surplus labour in primary production keeps commodity prices down, so technical progress in industry raises incomes rather than lowering prices, while technical progress in agriculture lowers prices rather than raising incomes. Asymmetric market structures — oligopolistic manufacturing versus competitive commodity markets — reinforce the divergence.

Influence and evidence

The thesis became a foundation of Latin American structuralism and dependency theory, and justified import-substitution industrialisation policies across the developing world, as well as calls for a New International Economic Order in the 1970s. Empirical tests have been mixed: long-run studies of commodity price indices (notably by Grilli and Yang, and by UNCTAD) found statistically significant declines of roughly 0.5–1% per year for many non-fuel commodities over much of the 20th century, but results are sensitive to endpoints, structural breaks, and the treatment of quality improvements in manufactures; the commodity booms of the 2000s, driven by Chinese demand, temporarily reversed the trend for many exporters.
