Gaul

Image placeholder: Gaul on the eve of the Roman conquest

Gaul (Latin: Gallia) was a region of Western Europe first described by the Romans, encompassing present-day France, Belgium, Luxembourg, and parts of Switzerland, the Netherlands, Germany, and Northern Italy. According to Julius Caesar, who conquered the region in the Gallic Wars (58–50 BC), Gaul was divided into three parts: Gallia Celtica, Belgica, and Aquitania.

The Gauls

The inhabitants of Gaul were principally Celtic peoples speaking Gaulish, a Continental Celtic language, organised into numerous tribes and tribal confederations such as the Arverni, Aedui, Helvetii, and Belgae. Their society, part of the wider La Tène culture, was known to the Romans for its oppida (fortified towns), skilled metalwork, druid priesthood, and formidable warriors. Greek colonists had earlier founded Massalia (Marseille) on the Mediterranean coast, and Rome annexed the south as Gallia Narbonensis in the second century BC.

Roman conquest and rule

Caesar's conquest, recorded in his Commentarii de Bello Gallico, culminated in the defeat of the Arvernian leader Vercingetorix at the Siege of Alesia in 52 BC. Under Augustus, Gaul was reorganised into the provinces of Gallia Narbonensis, Aquitania, Lugdunensis, and Belgica. Roman Gaul prospered, with cities such as Lugdunum (Lyon) becoming major centres; the population adopted Latin, giving rise in time to the Gallo-Romance languages ancestral to French. From the third century AD the region suffered invasions and instability, and in the fifth century it was overrun by Germanic peoples; the Visigoths, Burgundians, and finally the Franks under Clovis established successor kingdoms, ending Roman Gaul.

Legacy

Gaul's legacy persists in French identity — the Gallic rooster is a national symbol — and in place names, archaeology, and popular culture, most famously the Asterix comics set in a Gaulish village that "holds out against the invaders."
