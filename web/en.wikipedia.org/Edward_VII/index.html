Edward VII (Albert Edward; 9 November 1841 – 6 May 1910) was King of the United Kingdom and the British Dominions, and Emperor of India, from 1901 until his death in 1910. The eldest son of Queen Victoria and Prince Albert, he was heir apparent for nearly sixty years — longer than any of his predecessors at the time — before ascending the throne at the age of 59.

Early life and Prince of Wales

Edward was educated at home under a strict regime devised by his parents and later attended Oxford and Cambridge without taking a degree. As Prince of Wales he undertook extensive ceremonial and diplomatic tours, including visits to Canada and the United States in 1860 and to India in 1875–76. Excluded by Victoria from most state business, he became a prominent figure in fashionable society; his social life, and several public scandals, contrasted with his mother's court.

Reign

Edward's coronation, scheduled for June 1902, was postponed when he underwent emergency surgery for appendicitis; he was crowned that August. As king he took an active interest in foreign affairs and military reform, supporting the modernisation of the Royal Navy and the British Army. His visits to European capitals and his role in encouraging the Entente Cordiale with France (1904) earned him the nickname "the Peacemaker". Relations between Britain, France and Russia were consolidated during his reign in the face of a rising Germany.

Death and legacy

Edward suffered a series of heart attacks and died at Buckingham Palace on 6 May 1910, during a constitutional crisis over the powers of the House of Lords. He was succeeded by his son George V. The Edwardian era, named for his reign, is remembered as a brief period of elegance and confidence between the Victorian age and the First World War.
