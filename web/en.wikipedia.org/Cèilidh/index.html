Cèilidh

A modern cèilidh dance in Scotland; a caller usually teaches each dance before it begins.

A cèilidh (pronounced "kay-lee"; Irish: céilí) is a traditional Gaelic social gathering from Scotland and Ireland. Historically the word — from Old Irish céilide, "a visit" — meant an evening of storytelling, poetry, songs and news in a neighbour's house. In modern usage it usually denotes an evening of social dancing to folk music, typically with dances taught by a caller, and it is a fixture of weddings, festivals and community halls throughout Scotland, Ireland and the Gaelic diaspora.

The traditional cèilidh

In the crofting communities of the Highlands and Islands and rural Ireland, the cèilidh house was a centre of oral culture: the seanchaidh (storyteller) recited tales and genealogies, singers performed waulking songs and laments, and fiddlers or pipers played. Gatherings structured courtship and communal memory long before print and broadcasting. Such house cèilidhs survive in Cape Breton, Nova Scotia, where Scottish Gaelic culture was transplanted in the nineteenth century and fiddle traditions remain especially vigorous.

The modern dance cèilidh

Today's cèilidh centres on set dancing: couples and groups perform dances such as the Gay Gordons, the Military Two-Step, the Canadian Barn Dance, the Dashing White Sergeant, Strip the Willow and the Eightsome Reel, to music from a small band of fiddle, accordion, piano and drums. The emphasis is social rather than virtuosic; callers walk novices through the figures. In Ireland, céilí dances — formalised by the Irish Dancing Commission in the early twentieth century from older group dances — are danced to traditional music in sets, and céilithe remain central to Gaelic League cultural life and to summer schools.

Cultural significance

The cèilidh is widely treated as an emblem of Gaelic sociability and has been consciously revived as part of Gaelic language and culture movements; events such as Scotland's Fèisean nan Gàidheal and the Royal National Mòd celebrate its musical repertoire. The word has entered general British usage for any lively folk-dance evening, sometimes detached from Gaelic content entirely.
