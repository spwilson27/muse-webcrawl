Royal Proclamation of 1763

The Royal Proclamation of 1763 was issued by King George III on October 7, 1763, following Great Britain's acquisition of French territory in North America at the end of the Seven Years' War. It reorganised Britain's new American empire and set out the framework for relations with Indigenous peoples, forbidding colonial settlement west of a line drawn along the Appalachian Mountains.

Provisions

The Proclamation created the new provinces of Quebec, East Florida and West Florida, and the island colony of Grenada. Lands west of the Appalachian divide were reserved as an "Indian Reserve" for Indigenous nations; colonists were forbidden to purchase land directly from Indigenous peoples — purchases could only be made by the Crown at public meetings — and settlers already beyond the line were ordered to withdraw. It also instructed colonial governors to encourage immigration to the new colonies to offset population pressure.

Legacy

The Proclamation Line angered American colonists and land speculators who coveted western lands, and is often counted among the grievances leading to the American Revolution. In Canada, by contrast, the Proclamation is regarded as a foundational constitutional document for Indigenous rights: it recognised Indigenous title and established the treaty-making process, and it is referred to in section 25 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. Its principles continue to be cited in Canadian Aboriginal law.
