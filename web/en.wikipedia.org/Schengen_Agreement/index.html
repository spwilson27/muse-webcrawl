The Schengen Agreement is a treaty between European states that led to the creation of the Schengen Area, within which internal border controls have largely been abolished and a common external border and visa policy apply. It was signed on 14 June 1985 aboard the river ship Princesse Marie-Astrid on the Moselle near the Luxembourg village of Schengen, close to the tripoint with France and Germany.

History

The original signatories were the three Benelux states, France and West Germany. A second treaty, the Schengen Convention of 1990, set out the practical measures: abolition of internal checks, harmonised external-border controls, the Schengen Information System (SIS) database, police cooperation and common visa rules. Implementation began in 1995 for seven countries. The Treaty of Amsterdam (1997) incorporated the Schengen rules into European Union law.

The Schengen Area

As of the mid-2020s the area comprises 29 countries: 25 EU member states (Ireland retains an opt-out; Cyprus is not yet in; Bulgaria and Romania joined fully on 1 January 2025) plus Iceland, Norway, Switzerland and Liechtenstein. More than 450 million people can travel between them without passport checks. Non-EU visitors generally receive a single short-stay entitlement of 90 days in any 180-day period across the whole area.

Temporary controls and criticism

Member states may temporarily reintroduce internal border checks in emergencies. Several did so during the 2015 migration crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic, and some controls have been repeatedly prolonged since, prompting debate about the system's resilience. The common external border is managed with support from Frontex, the EU border agency; the Entry/Exit System for third-country nationals began rollout in 2025.
