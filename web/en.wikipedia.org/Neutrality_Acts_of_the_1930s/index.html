Neutrality Acts of the 1930s

The Neutrality Acts reflected 1930s American isolationism.

The Neutrality Acts were a series of laws passed by the United States Congress in the 1930s in response to the growing conflict in Europe and Asia. Influenced by the findings of the Nye Committee, which suggested that arms manufacturers had profited from dragging the country into the First World War, and by widespread disillusionment with that war, the Acts sought to prevent the United States from being drawn into foreign wars by embargoing trade with belligerents.

The Acts

The Neutrality Act of 1935, passed in reaction to Italy's invasion of Ethiopia, imposed a general embargo on exporting arms and war materials to all belligerents and warned that Americans travelling on belligerent ships did so at their own risk. The 1936 Act renewed these provisions and prohibited loans or credits to belligerents. The 1937 Act extended the embargo to civil wars (aimed at Spain) and added a "cash and carry" provision allowing non-military goods to be sold to belligerents only if paid for in cash and transported on non-American ships. The 1939 Act, passed after the outbreak of war in Europe, repealed the arms embargo and put all trade on a cash-and-carry basis — a shift that in practice favoured Britain and France, who controlled the Atlantic sea lanes.

Repeal

President Franklin D. Roosevelt's administration progressively circumvented the Acts through measures such as the destroyers-for-bases deal (1940) and the Lend-Lease Act of March 1941. The remaining neutrality restrictions were effectively repealed in November 1941, weeks before the attack on Pearl Harbor brought the United States into the Second World War.
