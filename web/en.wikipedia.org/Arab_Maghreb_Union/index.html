The Arab Maghreb Union (AMU; French: Union du Maghreb arabe; Arabic: اتحاد المغرب العربي) is a regional trade and political agreement among five North African states — Algeria, Libya, Mauritania, Morocco, and Tunisia — intended to promote economic and eventually political integration across the Maghreb. It was established by the Treaty of Marrakech on 17 February 1989. Despite ambitious goals, the union has been largely dormant since the mid-1990s, paralysed chiefly by the dispute between Morocco and Algeria over Western Sahara.

History

The idea of Maghreb unity dates to the anti-colonial era — the Tangier conference of North African liberation movements in 1958 called for a united Maghreb after independence. After decades of stalled bilateral experiments, the five heads of state met at Zéralda, Algeria, in June 1988 and agreed to establish the union, signing the founding treaty at Marrakech the following February. The treaty envisaged free movement of goods, services, capital, and people, common economic policies, and cooperation in defence and foreign affairs.

Structure and paralysis

The AMU's highest organ is a Presidential Council of the five heads of state, which was to meet annually under a rotating chair, with a secretariat based in Rabat and specialised ministerial councils and a consultative (parliamentary) council. Summits effectively ceased after 1994 as relations between Algeria and Morocco deteriorated: the two countries' land border has been closed since 1994, and their rivalry over Western Sahara — where Algeria backs the Polisario Front against Morocco's claim — has blocked all substantive integration. Intra-Maghreb trade remains among the lowest of any regional bloc in the world, at only a few percent of members' total trade; economists estimate the "non-Maghreb" costs the region one to two percentage points of GDP growth annually. A Maghreb Development Bank, agreed in 1991, was finally inaugurated in Tunis in 2015, but broader revival efforts have repeatedly failed.
