Mount Kenya is an extinct volcano in Kenya and the second-highest peak in Africa, after Kilimanjaro. The highest peaks of the mountain are Batian (5,199 metres or 17,057 feet), Nelion (5,188 metres or 17,021 feet) and Point Lenana (4,985 metres or 16,355 feet). Mount Kenya is located in the former Central and Eastern provinces of Kenya; its peak is now the intersection of Meru, Embu, Kirinyaga, Nyeri and Tharaka Nithi counties, about 16.5 kilometres (10.3 mi) south of the equator, around 150 kilometres (90 mi) north-northeast of the capital Nairobi. Mount Kenya is the source of the name of the Republic of Kenya.

Mount Kenya is a volcano created approximately 3 million years after the opening of the East African Rift. Before glaciation, it was 7,000 m (23,000 ft) high. It was covered by an ice cap for thousands of years. This has resulted in very eroded slopes and numerous valleys radiating from the peak. There are currently 11 small glaciers, which are shrinking rapidly, and may disappear by 2050. The forested slopes are an important source of water for much of Kenya.

Geology and geography

Mount Kenya is a stratovolcano that was active in the Pliocene. Its crater was probably over 6,000 m (19,700 ft) high, and was higher than Kilimanjaro at the time. As it has been extinct for a long time, its peak has been heavily eroded. The summit area is formed of a ring of rugged peaks surrounding a caldera of approximately 3 km (1.9 mi) diameter. The highest peaks are Batian, Nelion and Lenana. Batian and Nelion are only 250 metres (820 ft) apart but separated by the Gate of the Mists, a gap of similar depth.

Ecology

The mountain slopes are clad in forest, bamboo, scrub and moorland, giving way above 3,500 m to rock, ice and snow. The vegetation varies with altitude and rainfall, and the mountain supports a rich variety of endemic species. Mount Kenya National Park, established in 1949, protects the region surrounding the mountain and was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1997. The mountain is home to elephants, buffalo, leopards and the rare bongo antelope, as well as distinctive giant groundsels and lobelias in the afro-alpine zone.

History

The mountain was first reported to Europeans by the German missionary Johann Ludwig Krapf in 1849. The first successful ascent of Batian was made in 1899 by Halford Mackinder, César Ollier and Josef Brocherel. Local peoples, including the Kikuyu, regard the mountain as sacred: in Kikuyu tradition the god Ngai resides on the summit, which they call Kirinyaga, meaning "mountain of brightness" — the origin of the country's name.

Climbing

Point Lenana can be reached by most fit walkers on trekking routes such as the Sirimon, Chogoria and Naro Moru routes, while Batian and Nelion require technical rock climbing. The climbing season runs during the dry seasons, roughly January to February and July to October.
