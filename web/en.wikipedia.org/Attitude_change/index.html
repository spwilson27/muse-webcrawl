Attitude change

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Central and peripheral routes to persuasion

Attitude change is a change in an individual's evaluation of a person, object, event, or idea, and is a central topic in social psychology. Attitudes can change through persuasion, through the resolution of cognitive dissonance, through social influence, and through direct experience.

Persuasion models

The elaboration likelihood model (Petty and Cacioppo) proposes two routes to persuasion: a central route, in which attitudes change through careful consideration of arguments, and a peripheral route, in which change depends on cues such as source attractiveness or credibility. Attitudes formed through the central route tend to be stronger and more durable.

Cognitive dissonance

Leon Festinger's theory of cognitive dissonance holds that inconsistency between attitudes, or between attitudes and behaviour, produces discomfort that people are motivated to reduce — often by changing an attitude. Classic demonstrations include the induced-compliance paradigm and effort justification.

Other mechanisms

Attitudes also shift through mere exposure, classical and operant conditioning, self-perception (Bem), balance pressures in social networks, and generational or life-course change. Resistance to persuasion has been studied under headings such as inoculation theory and reactance.
