Theodor Herzl (2 May 1860 – 3 July 1904) was an Austro-Hungarian Jewish journalist, lawyer, and writer who was the founder of Zionism as a modern political movement. Formed in the crucible of the fin-de-siècle Viennese anti-Semitism and the Dreyfus affair, his pamphlet Der Judenstaat ("The Jewish State", 1896) argued that Jews could escape persecution only by establishing a state of their own.

Life

Born in Pest (now Budapest) to a secular, German-speaking Jewish family, Herzl studied law at the University of Vienna and became a successful feuilleton editor for the Neue Freie Presse. As the paper's Paris correspondent he covered the trial and degradation of Alfred Dreyfus in 1894–95; the anti-Semitic fervor surrounding the affair convinced him that assimilation had failed.

Zionist leadership

In August 1897 Herzl convened the First Zionist Congress in Basel, Switzerland, which adopted the Basel Program calling for a publicly and legally assured home for the Jewish people in Palestine, and founded the World Zionist Organization, with Herzl as president. He pursued the goal diplomatically, meeting Sultan Abdul Hamid II, Kaiser Wilhelm II, and British officials; in 1903 he controversially entertained the British Uganda Scheme as a temporary refuge. His utopian novel Altneuland ("Old New Land", 1902) imagined a prosperous Jewish commonwealth; its motto, "If you will it, it is no dream", became a Zionist slogan.

Death and legacy

Herzl died of heart failure in 1904, aged 44, decades before the establishment of the State of Israel in 1948. In 1949 his remains were reinterred in Jerusalem on the hill renamed Mount Herzl in his honor. He is officially the only person named in Israel's Declaration of Independence, referred to as "the spiritual father of the Jewish State".
