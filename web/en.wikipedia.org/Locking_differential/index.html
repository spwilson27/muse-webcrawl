Locking differential

A locking differential mechanism

A locking differential is a mechanical component, commonly fitted to the driven axle of a vehicle, that can lock the two output shafts of a differential together so that both wheels rotate at the same speed regardless of differences in traction between them.

Principle

An ordinary (open) differential allows the wheels on an axle to rotate at different speeds, which is essential when cornering, but it directs torque preferentially to the wheel with the least resistance. If one wheel is on ice or lifted off the ground, it spins uselessly while the wheel with grip receives little torque. Locking the differential overcomes this by forcing both wheels to turn together, so torque can be transmitted through whichever wheel has traction.

Types

  • Manual (selectable) lockers — engaged by the driver via a switch, lever, pneumatic or electric actuator (e.g., ARB Air Locker, factory lockers on off-road vehicles). They are disengaged for normal road driving because locked wheels fight each other in turns, causing tyre wear and handling problems.
  • Automatic lockers — engage automatically when one wheel begins to spin faster than the other, and disengage when speeds equalise. Examples include the Detroit Locker. They can produce noticeable clicking and abrupt behaviour on paved roads.
  • Spool — a fixed coupling that permanently locks the axle; used mainly in drag racing and some off-road applications.

Applications

Locking differentials are standard or optional equipment on off-road vehicles such as the Jeep Wrangler Rubicon, Mercedes-Benz G-Class (which offers front, centre and rear locks) and Land Rover Defender, and are widely used in agriculture, construction machinery, and motorsport. They differ from limited-slip differentials, which only partially restrict the speed difference between the wheels, and from electronic traction control, which brakes a spinning wheel to mimic the effect.
