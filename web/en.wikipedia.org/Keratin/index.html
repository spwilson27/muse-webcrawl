Keratin

Keratin filaments form the protective outer structures of land vertebrates.

Keratin is one of a family of fibrous structural proteins, the key structural material making up scales, hair, nails, feathers, horns, claws, hooves and the outer layer of skin among vertebrates. Keratin also protects epithelial cells from damage or stress. It is insoluble in water and organic solvents, and is exceptionally durable: keratin structures such as hair and horn survive long after the rest of an animal's soft tissues have decayed.

Types

Keratins are divided into the α-keratins, found in the hair, wool, horns and skin of mammals, and the β-keratins, found in the scales, claws and beaks of reptiles and the feathers of birds. α-keratins are built from α-helical coiled coils; β-keratins from β-pleated sheets, which make feather and scale keratin harder and more rigid. Humans possess 54 functional keratin genes, expressed in specific patterns in different epithelia, and keratin filaments are a major component of the cytoskeleton of epithelial cells, where they belong to the intermediate filament family.

Structure and properties

The toughness of keratin comes from extensive cross-linking: cysteine residues form disulfide bonds between chains, and the more such bonds, the harder the material. Human hair is about 14% cysteine; the chemistry of breaking and re-forming these bonds underlies permanent waving and hair straightening. Keratin monomers assemble into bundles that form the intermediate filaments giving cells mechanical resilience.

Disease

Mutations in keratin genes cause a range of genetic skin and hair disorders, including epidermolysis bullosa simplex, pachyonychia congenita and various forms of ichthyosis, in which the fragility of keratinocytes makes the skin blister under minor friction. Keratin expression patterns are also used routinely in pathology to classify carcinomas, since tumours tend to retain the keratins of their tissue of origin.
