Ivan the Terrible

Tsar of all Russia
Reign: 16 January 1547 – 28 March 1584
Born: 25 August 1530, Kolomenskoye
Died: 28 March 1584, Moscow
Predecessor: Vasili III (as Grand Prince)
Successor: Feodor I

Ivan IV Vasilyevich (1530–1584), commonly known in English as Ivan the Terrible, was the Grand Prince of Moscow from 1533 and the first ruler of Russia to be crowned Tsar, from 1547 until his death. His long reign saw the conquest of the Tatar khanates of Kazan and Astrakhan, the beginning of Russia's expansion into Siberia, sweeping internal reforms — and, in its later decades, the terror of the Oprichnina. His Russian epithet, Grozny, is better translated "the Formidable" or "awe-inspiring" than "terrible".

Early reign and reforms

Ivan succeeded his father Vasili III at the age of three; his childhood, amid boyar factional struggles after his mother Elena Glinskaya's death, left him suspicious of the nobility. Crowned Tsar in 1547 and married to Anastasia Romanovna, he governed with the "Chosen Council" of advisers including Aleksei Adashev and the priest Sylvester. Their reforms produced a new law code (the Sudebnik of 1550), the Stoglav church council, local government changes, and a reformed standing army with the streltsy musketeers. The first Zemsky Sobor (assembly of the land) met in 1549.

Conquests

In 1552 Ivan conquered the Khanate of Kazan — commemorated by St Basil's Cathedral on Red Square — and in 1556 Astrakhan, giving Russia control of the Volga. His attempt to win a Baltic coast in the Livonian War (1558–1583) initially succeeded against the Livonian Order but ultimately failed against Poland-Lithuania and Sweden, exhausting the country. In 1581 the Cossack ataman Yermak began the conquest of Siberia.

Oprichnina

After Anastasia's death in 1560, Ivan's behaviour darkened. In 1565 he divided Russia into the oprichnina, his personal domain administered by a corps of oprichniki in black, and the zemshchina under the boyars. The oprichniki expropriated, exiled and murdered thousands of nobles, clergy and townspeople; the terror culminated in the sack of Novgorod in 1570. The oprichnina was abolished in 1572 after it failed to defend Moscow against a Crimean Tatar raid that burned the city in 1571. In November 1581, in a fit of rage, Ivan struck his eldest son and heir, Ivan Ivanovich, with his staff, killing him — an act immortalised in Ilya Repin's famous painting.

Death and legacy

Ivan died in March 1584, reportedly while setting up a game of chess, and was succeeded by his unworldly son Feodor I. His reign remains deeply divisive: he centralised the Russian state and expanded it into a multi-ethnic empire, but at the cost of terror, economic ruin in the north-west, and a succession crisis that fed into the Time of Troubles. Stalin-era culture rehabilitated him as a strong state-builder, and his image remains contested in Russia today.
