Homelessness in the United States

Homelessness in the United States refers to the issue of people without a fixed, regular, and adequate nighttime residence. The United States Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) conducts an annual Point-in-Time count on a single night in January; the 2024 count found more than 770,000 people experiencing homelessness, the highest figure recorded since the reporting began, with roughly two-thirds staying in sheltered locations and one-third unsheltered.

History

Homelessness became a conspicuous national issue in the 1980s, as deinstitutionalization of people with mental illness, cuts to federal housing programs, and economic change increased the number of people living on the streets. The McKinney–Vento Homeless Assistance Act of 1987 was the first major federal legislative response, funding emergency shelter, transitional housing, and supportive services. The 2009 HEARTH Act reauthorized and reshaped these programs around homelessness prevention and rapid re-housing.

Causes

Research commonly cites a shortage of affordable rental housing as the central structural driver, alongside poverty, unemployment, eviction, domestic violence, mental illness, and substance use disorders. Rent burdens have risen as housing costs outpace incomes in many metropolitan areas. Veterans, families with children, unaccompanied youth, and people leaving incarceration or foster care face elevated risk.

Responses

Responses include emergency shelters, transitional housing, permanent supportive housing, and Housing First programs that provide housing without preconditions. Local and state laws have also criminalized activities such as sleeping or camping in public; the Supreme Court's 2024 decision in City of Grants Pass v. Johnson held that enforcing such ordinances against homeless people does not violate the Eighth Amendment.
