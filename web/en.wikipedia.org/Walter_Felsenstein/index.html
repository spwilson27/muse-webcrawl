Walter Felsenstein (30 May 1901 – 8 October 1975) was an Austrian-born theatre and opera director, founder of the Komische Oper Berlin in 1947, which he led until his death. He is regarded as one of the pioneers of modern opera production, insisting that opera be staged as genuine theatre — realistic, text-centred, and acted with the rigour of spoken drama.

Career

Felsenstein studied engineering before turning to the theatre, acting in Lübeck and directing in theatres across Germany, including Basel, Freiburg, Cologne, and Frankfurt. During the Nazi period he worked at the Schiller Theatre in Berlin. After the war he took over the former Metropol-Theater in East Berlin and opened the Komische Oper in December 1947 with Johann Strauss's Die Fledermaus.

Method

Felsenstein's productions — of works ranging from Mozart's Die Zauberflöte and Verdi's Otello to Offenbach's Barbe-bleue and Britten's A Midsummer Night's Dream — were rehearsed for months with ensemble casts, always sung in German translation so audiences could follow every word. He demanded that singers act truthfully, that every gesture grow out of the music, and that sets and costumes serve the dramaturgy rather than spectacle. Several productions were filmed by DEFA. His approach, which he called "musical theatre" (Musiktheater), influenced generations of directors, and his company toured widely in both East and West despite the Cold War division of Berlin.
