Guild

A guild is an association of artisans or merchants who oversee the practice of their craft or trade in a particular area. Guilds flourished in medieval and early modern Europe, where they controlled training, standards, prices and entry into a trade in their town.

Structure and functions

Craft guilds regulated apprenticeship: a boy served an apprentice for several years, became a journeyman, and could rise to master only after producing a "masterpiece" judged by the guild. Guilds set quality standards, fixed working hours and wages, limited competition, and provided welfare for members, widows and orphans, as well as religious and social functions centred on a patron saint. Merchant guilds, such as those of the Hanseatic League, protected traders' interests abroad. In London, guilds survive as the livery companies.

Decline

From the eighteenth century, guilds were increasingly attacked as monopolies that hindered innovation and free trade. They were abolished in France during the Revolution (1791) and suppressed across much of Europe in the nineteenth century as industrial capitalism spread production beyond guild-controlled towns. The term is now also used for modern professional associations and, in gaming, for organised groups of players.
