Asymmetric warfare

Asymmetric warfare is a type of war between belligerents whose military power, resources or strategies differ significantly. Weaker combatants — including insurgents, guerrillas and resistance movements — use unconventional tactics, exploiting the vulnerabilities of a stronger opponent rather than confronting its strength directly. The term gained wide currency after the Cold War and especially following the September 11 attacks.

Definition and origins

The concept has ancient roots — from David and Goliath imagery to Fabius Maximus's delaying strategy against Hannibal — but the modern term was popularised by Andrew Mack's 1975 essay "Why Big Nations Lose Small Wars" and by United States strategic literature in the 1990s. Asymmetry may concern force, technology, organisation, values or time horizons.

Tactics

Typical methods include guerrilla warfare, ambushes, improvised explosive devices (IEDs), suicide attacks, hostage-taking, propaganda and information operations, cyber attacks, and the deliberate use of civilian environments to negate an opponent's firepower advantages. The weaker side aims to exhaust the stronger side's political will rather than to win set-piece battles.

Historical examples

Classic cases include the American Revolutionary War, the Peninsular War in Spain (the origin of the word "guerrilla"), the Vietnam War, the Soviet–Afghan War, and the Iraq and Afghanistan insurgencies of the 2000s–2020s, where technologically superior coalitions struggled against local insurgent networks.

Counter-strategies

Counterinsurgency (COIN) doctrine emphasises population security, intelligence, political reform and "hearts and minds" over purely military operations, as codified in the US Army/Marine Corps Counterinsurgency Field Manual (2006). Asymmetric conflicts raise difficult legal questions under international humanitarian law, which was designed largely for symmetric state warfare.
