Myocardial infarction

Myocardial infarction (MI), commonly a heart attack, occurs when blood flow to part of the heart muscle is blocked long enough to cause tissue death, most often when a coronary artery plaque ruptures and a clot forms. It is a leading cause of death worldwide.

Causes and types

Most MIs result from atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries. Classification by ECG distinguishes ST-elevation MI (STEMI) from non-ST-elevation MI (NSTEMI); the Universal Definition also types MI by mechanism, including plaque rupture (type 1) and supply–demand mismatch (type 2). Risk factors include smoking, hypertension, diabetes, high LDL cholesterol (including familial hypercholesterolemia), obesity and age.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Typical symptoms are central chest pressure radiating to the arm, neck or jaw, with sweating and breathlessness; presentations may be atypical or silent, especially in women, the elderly and diabetics. Diagnosis combines symptoms, ECG changes and a rise in cardiac troponin. Complications include arrhythmias, heart failure, cardiogenic shock and cardiac rupture.

Treatment and prevention

Emergency care aims to restore flow: aspirin and other antiplatelets, and primary percutaneous coronary intervention (stent angioplasty) or thrombolysis for STEMI. Long-term care uses statins, beta blockers, ACE inhibitors and antiplatelet therapy, plus smoking cessation and risk-factor control for prevention.
