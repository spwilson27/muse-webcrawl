Cancer immunotherapy (also called immuno-oncology) is the stimulation of the immune system to treat cancer, improving the immune system's natural ability to fight the disease. It is an application of the fundamental research of cancer immunology and includes several major categories, from checkpoint inhibitors to engineered cell therapies.

Unlike chemotherapy or radiation, which attack cancer cells directly, immunotherapies work by enabling or enhancing immune recognition of tumours. Responses can be durable — some patients with metastatic melanoma treated in early trials remained in remission a decade later — but only a minority of patients respond to any given therapy, and immune-related adverse events can be severe.

Checkpoint inhibitors

Tumours exploit immune checkpoints — brakes on T-cell activity — to evade destruction. Antibodies blocking CTLA-4 (ipilimumab, approved 2011) or the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, atezolizumab) release these brakes. The 2018 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to James Allison and Tasuku Honjo for this discovery. Checkpoint blockade is now standard treatment for melanoma, lung cancer, kidney cancer, Hodgkin lymphoma, and many other malignancies.

Cellular therapies

In CAR T-cell therapy, a patient's T cells are collected, genetically engineered to express a chimeric antigen receptor targeting a tumour antigen such as CD19, expanded, and reinfused. Tisagenlecleucel (Kymriah) and axicabtagene ciloleucel (Yescarta) were approved in 2017 for certain B-cell leukaemias and lymphomas, producing high remission rates in otherwise refractory disease. Related approaches include tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte therapy and TCR-engineered cells.

Other approaches

Monoclonal antibodies such as rituximab (anti-CD20) and trastuzumab (anti-HER2) target cancer cells directly and recruit immune effectors. Cancer vaccines aim to prime responses against tumour antigens; the dendritic-cell vaccine sipuleucel-T was approved for prostate cancer in 2010, and personalised neoantigen mRNA vaccines are in trials. Cytokines (interleukin-2, interferons) and oncolytic viruses such as talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC) also stimulate anti-tumour immunity.

Challenges

Major challenges include predicting which patients will respond (biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression and tumour mutational burden are imperfect), primary and acquired resistance, managing autoimmune-like toxicities, and extending cell therapies from blood cancers to solid tumours.
