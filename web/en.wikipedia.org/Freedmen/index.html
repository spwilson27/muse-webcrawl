A freedman or freedwoman is a formerly enslaved person who has been released from slavery, usually by legal means. In United States history the term most often refers to the roughly four million African Americans emancipated during and after the Civil War, culminating in the Thirteenth Amendment (1865).

Reconstruction era

Freedpeople reunited families separated by slavery, founded churches and schools, and sought land and fair labour contracts. Congress created the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands (the Freedmen's Bureau) in 1865 to provide food, medical care, legal aid and education; it helped establish thousands of schools and institutions such as Howard University. During Reconstruction, freedmen voted and held office across the South, including in Congress (e.g. Hiram Revels and Blanche K. Bruce in the Senate), before Jim Crow laws and disenfranchisement stripped away most political gains by 1900.

Other meanings

In ancient Rome, a libertus was a freed slave who owed duties to his former owner (patron) but whose children were full citizens; imperial freedmen ran much of the civil service. The term also applies to "Freedmen" of the Five Civilized Tribes — people of African descent held as slaves by Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek and Seminole nations and freed under the treaties of 1866, whose tribal citizenship remains contested.
