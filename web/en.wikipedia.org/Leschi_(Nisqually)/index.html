Leschi (Nisqually)

Born1808, near present-day Eatonville, Washington
Died19 February 1858, hanged at Fort Steilacoom
TribeNisqually
Known forLeadership in the Puget Sound War; wrongful-conviction exoneration (2004)

Leschi (1808 – February 19, 1858) was chief of the Nisqually Native American tribe in the Puget Sound region of present-day Washington State. A leader in the Puget Sound War of 1855–56, he was controversially convicted of murder for the wartime death of a militia officer and hanged in 1858 — a verdict widely challenged ever since.

Treaty and war

Leschi's mark appeared (by his account, under protest and possibly without his understanding) on the Treaty of Medicine Creek (1854), which confined the Nisqually to a small, largely unusable reservation away from their river and prairies. He argued the treaty was a fraud and refused to move. Fighting broke out in 1855; Leschi led Native forces in engagements including the Battle of White River, evading capture for months before surrendering or being betrayed into custody in 1856. After the reservation was enlarged, the war ended.

Trials and exoneration

Leschi was tried twice for the killing of militia colonel Abram B. Moses during a truce outing: the first jury hung — instructions that a killing in wartime cannot be murder were pivotal — while a second jury convicted him, and appeals failed. Army officers at Fort Steilacoom refused to carry out the sentence on army land, so he was hanged by territorial authorities near the fort's grounds in 1858. In 2004, a historical court of inquiry convened with Washington judges ruled that Leschi should not have been charged, as he had acted as a lawful combatant in a war; the state legislature formally recognised the injustice, and his name is honoured today by the Leschi neighbourhood and schools in Seattle and by the Nisqually Tribe.
