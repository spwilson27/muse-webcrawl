Sir Robert Giffen (1837–1910) was a Scottish economist and statistician, editor of financial publications and a leading authority on public finance. His name survives in microeconomics in the concept of the Giffen good.

[Image placeholder: Portrait of Sir Robert Giffen]

Life and career

Giffen was born in Strathaven, Lanarkshire, and trained as a solicitor in Glasgow before turning to journalism. Moving to London, he wrote for the Globe and became assistant editor of the Economist under Walter Bagehot, later editing the money article of The Times and founding the Investors' Review. From 1876 to 1897 he headed the statistical department of the Board of Trade, professionalising British official statistics. He was president of the Statistical Society (1882–84), was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society, and was knighted in 1895.

The Giffen good

Alfred Marshall attributed to Giffen the observation that a rise in the price of bread could make the poorest labouring families — who spent most of their income on it and could no longer afford dearer foods — buy more bread, not less. A good whose demand rises as its price rises, because the income effect outweighs the substitution effect, is called a Giffen good: an upward-sloping demand curve apparently violating the law of demand. Whether Giffen ever stated the claim in this form, and whether any real good clearly exhibits it, has been debated ever since; modern studies of rice in poor Chinese households provide the strongest candidate evidence.

Writings

His works include Essays in Finance (1880), The Progress of the Working Classes (1884) — an influential, optimistic statistical survey of rising real wages — The Case Against Bimetallism (1892), and Economic Inquiries and Studies (1904).
