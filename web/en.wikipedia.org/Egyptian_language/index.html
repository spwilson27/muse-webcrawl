Egyptian language

The Egyptian language is an Afro-Asiatic language spoken in ancient Egypt, with a documented written record spanning more than 4,000 years.

The Egyptian language, or Ancient Egyptian, is an Afro-Asiatic language spoken in ancient Egypt. It has the longest documented history of any language, with written records from about 3200 BC to the medieval period, when it was gradually replaced by Arabic after the Muslim conquest. Its latest stage, Coptic, survives as the liturgical language of the Coptic Orthodox Church.

Stages

Egyptologists divide the language into Old Egyptian (c. 2600–2000 BC), Middle Egyptian (c. 2000–1300 BC), Late Egyptian (c. 1300–700 BC), Demotic (c. 700 BC – AD 400) and Coptic. Middle Egyptian is considered the classical stage and remained in use for religious and monumental texts for millennia.

Writing systems

Egyptian was written in hieroglyphic, hieratic (a cursive priestly script) and later Demotic scripts, using a combination of phonetic signs, logograms and determinatives. Coptic adopted the Greek alphabet supplemented by Demotic-derived letters. The decipherment of hieroglyphs by Jean-François Champollion in 1822, using the Rosetta Stone, founded modern Egyptology.

Grammar

Classical Egyptian is a verb-initial language with suffix pronouns, a construct state for possession, and a system of aspect-based verb forms. Like other Afro-Asiatic languages, its roots are built mainly from consonants, with vowels not written in the hieroglyphic script.

Legacy

A number of English words derive from Egyptian through Greek and Coptic, and the language remains central to the study of one of the world's earliest civilizations.
