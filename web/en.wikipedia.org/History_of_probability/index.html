History of probability

The history of probability is the development of the mathematical theory of chance. Although games of chance are ancient, probability as a formal discipline began only in the 17th century, with the correspondence of Pascal and Fermat in 1654. It matured through the work of Huygens, Bernoulli, de Moivre, Bayes, and Laplace, and was placed on a rigorous axiomatic foundation by Kolmogorov in 1933. Today probability underlies statistics, physics, finance, and computer science.

From dice games to the normal distribution: probability grew out of gambling problems.

Before 1654

Gambling devices — astragali (knucklebones), dice, and cards — are thousands of years old, and ancient and medieval writers occasionally reasoned about chance: the Indian epic Mahabharata contains a gambling tale, and medieval commentators enumerated dice outcomes. Gerolamo Cardano's Liber de Ludo Aleae (written c. 1564, published 1663) computed odds for dice games and contains an early notion of equally likely outcomes, but it had little influence at the time. Galileo wrote a short treatise on dice at the request of a patron, explaining why three dice show 10 and 11 more often than 9 and 12.

The Pascal–Fermat correspondence

The conventional birth of probability theory is 1654, when the gambler Antoine Gombaud, the Chevalier de Méré, posed problems to Blaise Pascal — notably the "problem of points": how to divide the stakes of an interrupted game fairly. Pascal's exchange of letters with Pierre de Fermat produced the modern solution, based on enumerating equally likely future outcomes and the combinatorial triangle now called Pascal's triangle. Christiaan Huygens, hearing of the correspondence, published the first printed treatise on probability in 1657, introducing the concept of expected value.

The 18th century

Jacob Bernoulli's posthumous Ars Conjectandi (1713) proved the first law of large numbers: the observed frequency of an event stabilises near its probability. Abraham de Moivre's The Doctrine of Chances (1718, expanded 1738 and 1756) developed the normal approximation to the binomial distribution and generating functions. Thomas Bayes's essay, published posthumously in 1763, presented the theorem on inverse probability that now bears his name and underpins Bayesian statistics. Daniel Bernoulli applied probability to smallpox inoculation and introduced expected utility in resolving the St. Petersburg paradox.

Laplace and the classical theory

Pierre-Simon Laplace's Théorie analytique des probabilités (1812) crowned the classical period: it systematised generating functions, least squares, and the central limit theorem, and applied probability to astronomy, demography, and jurisprudence. Laplace's philosophical essay defined probability classically as the ratio of favourable to total equally possible cases — a definition later criticised as circular, prompting rival frequentist and subjective interpretations in the 19th and 20th centuries (Venn, von Mises, Ramsey, de Finetti, Savage).

The modern theory

The Russian school — Chebyshev, Markov (Markov chains), and Lyapunov — strengthened limit theorems, and Émile Borel and others connected probability with measure theory. In 1933 Andrey Kolmogorov's Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung axiomatised probability as a normalised measure on a sample space, the framework still standard today. Twentieth-century developments include stochastic processes (Wiener, Lévy, Itô), information theory, Monte Carlo methods, and the use of probability in quantum mechanics, genetics, and machine learning.
