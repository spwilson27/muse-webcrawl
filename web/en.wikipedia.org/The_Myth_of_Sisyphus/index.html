The Myth of Sisyphus (French: Le Mythe de Sisyphe) is a 1942 philosophical essay by Albert Camus. In it, Camus introduces his philosophy of the absurd, explored also in his novel The Stranger and the play Caligula, published as part of his "cycle of the absurd".

Summary

The essay opens with the famous line: "There is but one truly serious philosophical problem and that is suicide." Camus argues that the absurd arises from the confrontation between the human desire for meaning and the "unreasonable silence of the world". He rejects both physical suicide and "philosophical suicide" — the leap of faith he attributes to thinkers such as Kierkegaard, Jaspers, and Husserl — and instead advocates revolt: a lucid acceptance of the absurd without resignation. Chapters examine the "absurd man" through figures such as Don Juan, the actor, and the conqueror.

The myth

The final chapter retells the Greek myth of Sisyphus, condemned by the gods to roll a boulder up a mountain for eternity, watching it fall each time he reaches the summit. Camus finds Sisyphus heroic in his scorn of the gods and his conscious embrace of his fate, concluding with one of the most quoted lines in modern philosophy: "One must imagine Sisyphus happy."
