10 Downing Street

10 Downing Street in London is the official residence and office of the Prime Minister of the United Kingdom. The black door of Number 10 is one of the most famous in the world, and the address has become a metonym for the British government itself. It has been the home of British prime ministers since 1735, when King George II offered it to Sir Robert Walpole, the first prime minister, as a gift.

Behind its modest Georgian facade, Number 10 is a much larger complex than it appears: it is linked to 11 and 12 Downing Street and contains over 100 rooms, including the Cabinet Room, the State Dining Room, and apartments on the upper floors.

History

Downing Street was built in the 1680s by Sir George Downing on the site of a mansion called Hampden House. Number 10 was originally three properties: a large mansion overlooking St James's Park called "the house at the back", a smaller town house, and a cottage. In 1732 George II offered the properties to Robert Walpole, who accepted on condition they be held as an official residence for future First Lords of the Treasury. Architect William Kent joined the buildings into one.

Many 18th- and 19th-century prime ministers preferred their own grander London homes, but from the 20th century onward Number 10 became firmly established as the prime ministerial residence. The building was extensively reconstructed in 1957–1963 after being found close to collapse: the famous black bricks were in fact yellow London stock bricks painted black, and the facade was rebuilt in genuine black brick.

The door and security

The black front door, with its white "10" and lion-head knocker, cannot be opened from the outside — it has no keyhole — and is opened by a guard inside. Since an IRA mortar attack in 1991, security gates stand at the end of Downing Street and the street is closed to the general public.
