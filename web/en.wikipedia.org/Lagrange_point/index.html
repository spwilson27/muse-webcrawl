In celestial mechanics, a Lagrange point is a position in an orbital configuration of two large bodies where a small object affected only by gravity can maintain a stationary position relative to the two large bodies. At these points the gravitational forces of the two bodies and the centrifugal force balance one another. There are five such points, labelled L1 through L5, in the plane of the two large bodies' orbits.

The three collinear points L1, L2 and L3 were discovered by Leonhard Euler in 1765; Joseph-Louis Lagrange found the remaining two in 1772 while working on the three-body problem. L1, L2 and L3 are unstable equilibria — an object placed there drifts away without station-keeping — whereas L4 and L5, which form equilateral triangles with the two massive bodies, are stable and can collect dust and asteroids.

The five points

  • L1 lies between the two bodies. The Sun–Earth L1 hosts solar observatories such as SOHO and DSCOVR, giving an uninterrupted view of the Sun.
  • L2 lies beyond the smaller body. The Sun–Earth L2, about 1.5 million km from Earth, hosts the Gaia astrometry mission and the James Webb Space Telescope, which use halo orbits around it.
  • L3 lies behind the larger body, on the far side of its orbit; it is permanently hidden from the smaller body.
  • L4 and L5 lead and trail the smaller body by 60° in its orbit. Jupiter's L4 and L5 regions host the thousands of Trojan asteroids; Earth, Mars and Neptune also have known Trojans.

Applications

Because objects near L1 and L2 can be held in place with little fuel, these points are prized parking places for astronomy and space-weather monitoring. Halo and Lissajous orbits around the points provide the wide, stable views a fixed position cannot. Proposed uses of L4 and L5 include space colonies and way stations, popularised in science fiction; the Earth–Moon Lagrange points have also been proposed for collecting interplanetary dust (the debated Kordylewski clouds).
