Guns, Germs, and Steel: The Fates of Human Societies is a 1997 book by Jared Diamond, a professor of geography and physiology at UCLA. It argues that differences in power and technology between human societies originate primarily in environmental and geographic differences — not in biological differences between peoples — and it won the Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction in 1998.

Argument

Diamond frames the book around "Yali's question": why did wealth and power end up distributed as they are? His answer traces a chain of causation from geography to agriculture: Eurasia's east–west axis spread crops and livestock across similar latitudes and climates; the Fertile Crescent's wealth of domesticable plants and animals gave farmers food surpluses, which supported specialists, writing, centralised states, steel tools, ocean-going ships, and crowd diseases to which Eurasian populations evolved resistance. When Europeans reached the Americas, these "guns, germs, and steel" devastated indigenous societies such as the Inca.

Reception

The book was an international bestseller and was adapted into a 2005 National Geographic television documentary. While widely praised for its synthesis, it has been criticised by some historians and anthropologists for geographic determinism and oversimplification — charges Diamond addresses in later works such as Collapse (2005).
