Inverse (logic)

OriginalIf P, then Q
InverseIf not P, then not Q
Equivalent toThe converse of the contrapositive
Logically validNo (not equivalent to the original)

In logic, the inverse of a conditional statement is formed by negating both the hypothesis and the conclusion of the original statement. For the conditional "If P, then Q", the inverse is "If not P, then not Q".

Truth value

The inverse of a true statement is not necessarily true. For example, "If a figure is a square, then it is a rectangle" is true, but its inverse — "If a figure is not a square, then it is not a rectangle" — is false, since a figure may be a non-square rectangle. Asserting the inverse on the basis of the original is a formal fallacy known as denying the antecedent.

Relation to other transforms

The inverse is the contrapositive of the converse of the original statement, and is therefore logically equivalent to the converse. Just as the contrapositive is always equivalent to the original, the inverse and converse always share the same truth value as each other — but not necessarily the truth value of the original statement.

For "If P, then Q": the converse is "If Q, then P"; the inverse is "If not P, then not Q"; the contrapositive is "If not Q, then not P". Only the contrapositive is logically equivalent to the original conditional.
