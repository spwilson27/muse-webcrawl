Heinz London (7 November 1907 – 3 August 1970) was a German-born British physicist who worked on superconductivity and low-temperature physics. He was the younger brother of the theoretical physicist Fritz London.

Career

London studied physics in Bonn, Berlin, Munich and Breslau, completing his doctorate in 1934 — by which time the Nazi regime had made an academic career in Germany impossible for him as a Jew. He emigrated to England, working first at the Clarendon Laboratory in Oxford with Franz Simon's low-temperature group, then in Bristol during the war, and from 1946 at the Atomic Energy Research Establishment at Harwell, where he became deputy chief scientist.

Contributions

In 1935, with his brother Fritz, he published the London equations, the first successful phenomenological description of the electromagnetic behaviour of superconductors, which explained the Meissner effect and predicted the penetration depth now called the London penetration depth. In 1951 he proposed the dilution refrigerator, which uses the mixing of helium-3 and helium-4 to reach temperatures of a few millikelvin; the concept, first realised in the 1960s, remains the workhorse for continuous cooling below 0.3 K. He also invented the London moment gyroscope concept used in precision experiments.
