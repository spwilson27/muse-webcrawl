Silt

Silt is a granular sediment whose particles are intermediate in size between sand and clay. In the Udden–Wentworth scale, silt particles range from about 0.0039 to 0.0625 mm in diameter; in soil science, the USDA defines silt as particles 0.002–0.05 mm. Silt is readily transported by moving water and air, and is deposited in quiet water, on floodplains, and as wind-blown loess.

Formation

Silt is produced mainly by physical weathering — frost shattering, glacial grinding (rock flour) and abrasion in rivers — rather than by chemical decomposition, so silt grains are often dominated by hard, resistant quartz. Glacial flour gives meltwater its milky turquoise colour, and wind-deposited silt forms the deep, fertile loess deposits of China, central Europe and the American Midwest.

Properties and significance

Soils rich in silt (silt loams) hold moisture and nutrients well and are highly fertile; the annual Nile flood's silt sustained Egyptian agriculture for millennia, and the Yellow River carries enormous silt loads, giving it its name. However, silt also causes problems: silting fills reservoirs and harbours, promotes flooding when riverbeds aggrade (as on the lower Yellow River), and silty soils can be highly susceptible to liquefaction during earthquakes and to frost heave. In engineering, distinguishing silt from clay is important because silts are less plastic but can lose strength abruptly when saturated and shaken.
