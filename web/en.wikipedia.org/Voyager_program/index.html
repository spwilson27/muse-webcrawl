The Voyager program is an American scientific program that employs two interstellar probes, Voyager 1 and Voyager 2. They were launched in 1977 to take advantage of a favourable alignment of the two gas giants Jupiter and Saturn and the two ice giants Uranus and Neptune, to fly near them while collecting data for transmission back to Earth. After launch, the decision was made to send Voyager 2 near Uranus and Neptune as well.

Mission

Both spacecraft flew by Jupiter (1979) and Saturn (1980–81); Voyager 1 studied Titan closely while Voyager 2 continued to Uranus (1986) and Neptune (1989), remaining the only spacecraft to have visited the ice giants. The primary mission ended in 1989; the Voyager Interstellar Mission continues as the probes explore the outer heliosphere. Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause into interstellar space in August 2012 at about 121.6 AU from the Sun; Voyager 2 followed in November 2018.

As of the mid-2020s, Voyager 1 is the most distant human-made object from Earth, over 15 billion miles (about 162 AU) away, with radio signals taking more than 22 hours to arrive one-way. Both probes are powered by radioisotope thermoelectric generators whose output declines steadily; instruments are being shut down one by one to extend contact, which NASA expects may last into the early 2030s.

Golden Record

Each spacecraft carries a copy of the Voyager Golden Record, a 12-inch gold-plated copper phonograph record containing sounds and images selected to portray the diversity of life and culture on Earth — greetings in 55 languages, music from many cultures, natural and human-made sounds, and 115 images — intended as a message for any extraterrestrial intelligence that might find the probes. Carl Sagan chaired the committee that selected the contents.

Legacy

The program transformed knowledge of the outer planets: it discovered volcanic activity on Io, the first observed beyond Earth; found evidence of a subsurface ocean on Europa; characterised Saturn's rings, Titan's thick nitrogen atmosphere, Uranus's tilted magnetosphere, and Neptune's supersonic winds and Great Dark Spot. The famous "Pale Blue Dot" photograph of Earth, taken by Voyager 1 in 1990 from about 6 billion kilometres, was part of the Solar System "Family Portrait" series.
