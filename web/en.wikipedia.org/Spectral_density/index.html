Spectral density

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The power spectrum of a time series describes the distribution of power into frequency components composing that signal. According to Fourier analysis, any physical signal can be decomposed into a number of discrete frequencies, or a spectrum of frequencies over a continuous range. The statistical average of a certain signal or sort of signal (including noise) as analyzed in terms of its frequency content is called its spectrum. When the energy of the signal is concentrated around a finite time interval, especially if its total energy is finite, one may compute the energy spectral density. More commonly used is the power spectral density (PSD, or simply spectral density), which applies to signals existing over all time, or over a time period large enough in comparison with the duration of a measurement.

Definition

The power spectral density of a stationary random process is the Fourier transform of its autocorrelation function, a relationship given by the Wiener–Khinchin theorem. The total power of the signal equals the integral of the PSD over all frequencies, an instance of Parseval's theorem. The PSD of noise is often expressed in units of watts per hertz (W/Hz), or for voltage signals, V²/Hz.

Estimation

In practice, the PSD must be estimated from finite data. Methods include the periodogram, Welch's method of averaged overlapped segments, multitaper methods, and parametric (autoregressive) estimation. Estimation trades off frequency resolution against variance: longer or more averaged segments give smoother but less frequency-resolved estimates.

Applications

Spectral density analysis is used throughout engineering and science: characterizing noise in electronic circuits and communication systems, vibration analysis, audio processing, seismology, neuroscience (EEG spectra), and radio astronomy. Colors of noise — white, pink, and brown — are defined by the shape of their power spectra.
