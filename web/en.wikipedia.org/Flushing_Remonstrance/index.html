Flushing Remonstrance

The Flushing Remonstrance was a 1657 petition to Peter Stuyvesant, the director-general of the Dutch colony of New Netherland, in which residents of the town of Flushing (Vlissingen) protested a ban on Quaker worship. It is regarded as a notable early statement of religious freedom in colonial America and a precursor to the First Amendment's protections.

Background

Stuyvesant had issued ordinances against unapproved religious gatherings, aimed largely at Quakers, whose missionaries had begun arriving in the colony. In Flushing, some townspeople had sheltered Quakers and allowed meetings in their homes; one resident, Henry Townsend, was fined and banished for doing so.

The petition

On December 27, 1657, town clerk Edward Hart drafted the remonstrance, signed by some thirty residents. It argued that the town's patent from the Dutch West India Company guaranteed liberty of conscience, and that the signers could not in conscience persecute people of any faith, declaring they would not "lay violent hands" on others for their beliefs. The document cited the law of love, peace, and liberty in their hearts.

Aftermath

Stuyvesant rejected the petition and arrested several signers, but enforcement faltered after colonist John Bowne appealed his own banishment to the company in Amsterdam; the directors ordered Stuyvesant to allow private worship and connive at other faiths. The remonstrance's original manuscript is held by the New York State Archives.
