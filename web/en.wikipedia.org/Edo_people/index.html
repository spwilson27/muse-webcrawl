The Edo people, also known as the Bini, are an ethnic group native to Edo State in southern Nigeria. They speak the Edo language, an Edoid language, and are the descendants of the founders of the Benin Empire, one of the most prominent pre-colonial states of West Africa, centred on Benin City.

History

According to tradition, the Edo kingdom of Benin was ruled first by the Ogiso ("kings of the sky") dynasty and later by the line of Obas founded by Eweka I in around the 13th century. Under rulers such as Ewuare the Great (reigned c. 1440–1473), Oba Esigie and Oba Ozolua, Benin became a powerful empire controlling trade routes between the forest region and the coast, dealing with Portuguese merchants from the late 15th century.

The kingdom remained independent until 1897, when a British punitive expedition captured and sacked Benin City, exiled Oba Ovonramwen, and looted the famous Benin Bronzes — cast brass plaques and sculptures renowned as masterpieces of African art. The monarchy was restored in 1914, and the Oba of Benin remains an important traditional ruler and cultural figure.

Culture

Edo culture is noted for its bronze-casting guilds at Igun Street, its palace arts, elaborate coral-bead regalia, and festivals such as Igue, the Oba's annual blessing ceremony. Traditional religion centres on the worship of Osalobua (God), the deified ancestors and a pantheon of deities, though the majority of Edo people today are Christian. The Edo are also prominent in Nigeria's professions, academia and diaspora communities worldwide.
