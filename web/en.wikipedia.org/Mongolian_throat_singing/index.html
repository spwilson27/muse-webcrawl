Mongolian throat singing

Mongolian throat singing, also called overtone singing or khöömei (Mongolian: хөөмей), is a traditional form of singing practised among Mongolian, Tuvan and other peoples of Central Asia in which a single vocalist produces two or more distinct pitches simultaneously. By precisely shaping the vocal tract, the singer amplifies individual harmonics of a low fundamental drone, creating a flute-like melody above it. In 2010, Mongolian throat singing was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Technique and styles

The singer produces a sustained fundamental note and manipulates the tongue, lips, jaw and larynx to emphasise selected overtones. Several regional styles are recognised, including khöömei proper, the deep growling subharmonic style kargyraa, and the high, clear whistling sygyt. Performances traditionally imitate sounds of nature — wind, water, mountains and animals — reflecting the pastoral life of the steppe. Throat singing is often accompanied by instruments such as the morin khuur (horsehead fiddle), topshur (lute) and tovshuur.

Distribution and modern practice

The art is most strongly associated with western Mongolia (notably the Khovd and Altai regions) and with the neighbouring Russian republic of Tuva, where performers such as the groups Huun-Huur-Tu and Alash have brought it to world audiences. Mongolian ensembles like The Hu combine throat singing with rock instrumentation, achieving international popularity. Folklore links the origin of khöömei to herders imitating the sounds of wind across the steppe or of camels and their young.
