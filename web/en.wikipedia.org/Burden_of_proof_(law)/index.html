Burden of proof (law)

In a legal dispute, the burden of proof is the obligation on a party to produce evidence sufficient to establish the truth of facts in issue, and to persuade the fact-finder of those facts. It is conventionally divided into the burden of production (the duty to present enough evidence to raise an issue) and the burden of persuasion (the duty to convince the tribunal to the required standard).

Standards of proof

Legal systems recognise several standards. In criminal cases, the prosecution must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, the highest standard, reflecting the presumption of innocence. In most civil cases, the claimant must prove their case on the balance of probabilities (in the United States, by a preponderance of the evidence) — that a fact is more likely than not true. Intermediate standards such as clear and convincing evidence apply in certain civil matters, for example allegations of fraud or the termination of parental rights.

Allocation and shifting

The burden generally rests on the party asserting a fact — in criminal trials on the prosecution, which must prove every element of the offence. Defendants may bear evidential burdens for particular defences, such as insanity, and statutes sometimes impose a "reverse onus". In common law, the maxim semper necessitas probandi incumbit ei qui agit — the necessity of proof always lies with the person who lays charges — expresses the underlying principle.
