Social Darwinism

"Survival of the fittest" was applied to society by Spencer and others

Social Darwinism is the study and implementation of various theories and social policies which attempt to explain the laws of nature described by Charles Darwin — particularly the concept of natural selection — into politics, economics and society. The term, mostly used pejoratively by critics, became popular in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, when such ideas were invoked to justify laissez-faire capitalism, imperialism, racism and eugenics.

Origins

The phrase "survival of the fittest" was coined not by Darwin but by the philosopher Herbert Spencer, whose social theories actually preceded On the Origin of Species (1859) and drew as much on Lamarck. Thinkers such as William Graham Sumner in the United States argued against welfare and regulation on the grounds that they interfered with natural competition. Darwin himself applied evolutionary ideas to human society in The Descent of Man (1871) but held more nuanced views than many of his self-described followers.

Decline and legacy

After the Second World War, association with Nazi racial ideology discredited social Darwinism, and historians like Richard Hofstadter (in his 1944 book Social Darwinism in American Thought) cemented the term as a label for discredited attempts to ground social hierarchy in biology. Scholars continue to debate how widely such views were actually held and how directly they derived from Darwin.
