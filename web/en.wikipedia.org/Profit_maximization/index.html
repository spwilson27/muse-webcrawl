Profit maximization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Profit is maximised at the output where MR = MC

In economics, profit maximization is the short run or long run process by which a firm may determine the price, input and output levels that will lead to the highest possible total profit (or just profit in short). In neoclassical economics, the dominant approach to understanding firm behaviour, a firm is modelled as maximising profit subject to its production technology and the prices it faces.

The marginal cost–marginal revenue rule

Total profit equals total revenue minus total cost. Profit is maximised at the level of output at which the additional revenue from selling one more unit — marginal revenue (MR) — just equals the additional cost of producing it — marginal cost (MC). If MR exceeds MC, expanding output raises profit; if MC exceeds MR, contracting output raises profit. In perfect competition the firm is a price taker, so marginal revenue equals price, and the rule reduces to producing where price equals marginal cost. A monopolist or firm with market power instead sets output where MR = MC and charges the highest price the demand curve bears at that quantity.

Shutdown and long-run conditions

In the short run, a firm may continue producing despite losses if revenue covers variable costs; it shuts down if price falls below minimum average variable cost. In the long run in competitive markets, entry and exit drive economic profit to zero, so firms produce at minimum average total cost. Real firms may deviate from pure profit maximisation because of managerial objectives, incomplete information, satisficing behaviour, or social and regulatory constraints, which behavioural and managerial theories of the firm attempt to capture.
