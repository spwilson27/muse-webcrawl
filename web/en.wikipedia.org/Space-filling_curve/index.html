Space-filling curve

In mathematical analysis, a space-filling curve is a curve whose range reaches every point in a higher dimensional region, typically the unit square (or more generally an n-dimensional unit hypercube). Because Giuseppe Peano (1858–1932) was the first to discover one, space-filling curves in the two-dimensional plane are sometimes called Peano curves. The concept of a space-filling curve is counterintuitive because a curve is intuitively a one-dimensional object, yet space-filling curves manage to "cover" a two-dimensional area.

History

In 1890 Giuseppe Peano discovered a continuous curve, now called the Peano curve, that passes through every point of the unit square. His purpose was to construct a continuous mapping from the unit interval onto the unit square. Peano was motivated by an earlier result of Georg Cantor that two sets have the same cardinality if there is a bijection between them. Peano's example showed that a continuous curve could be surprisingly "large". The following year, David Hilbert published a simpler variation of the same idea, producing the Hilbert curve.

Properties

Space-filling curves are necessarily continuous but cannot be injective: a continuous bijection from the unit interval to the unit square does not exist, because such a map would be a homeomorphism, and removing a point from the interval disconnects it, whereas removing a point from the square does not disconnect it. Space-filling curves can, however, be "almost injective", failing injectivity only on a set of measure zero.

Examples

  • Peano curve — the original construction by Giuseppe Peano (1890).
  • Hilbert curve — a simpler recursive variant described by David Hilbert (1891).
  • Sierpiński curve — based on the triangular subdivision.
  • Moore curve — a loop version of the Hilbert curve.
  • Gosper curve, also called the flowsnake.
  • Morton order (Z-order curve) — maps multidimensional data to one dimension while preserving locality.

Construction

Most space-filling curves are constructed iteratively as a limit of a sequence of piecewise linear continuous curves, each of which passes ever closer to every point of the square. The n-th approximation divides the interval into subintervals and the square into subsquares, mapping each subinterval onto one subsquare. The limit curve is continuous by uniform convergence and its image is the whole square.

Applications

Space-filling curves are used in computer science for spatial indexing and locality-preserving ordering of multidimensional data, in image processing, in the design of traveling-salesman heuristics, and in halftoning. They are intimately connected with fractal geometry and with the theory of Hausdorff dimension: space-filling curves have Hausdorff dimension 2.
