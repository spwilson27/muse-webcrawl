SpeciesEastern white pine (Pinus strobus)

SymbolismUnity, peace, and shelter of the Great Law of Peace

The Tree of Peace (Tsyononkwara'toton in some translations) is the central symbol of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy) and of its constitution, the Great Law of Peace. According to Haudenosaunee oral tradition, the prophet known as the Great Peacemaker (Dekanawida), together with Hiawatha and Jigonhsasee, founded the Confederacy by uniting the Seneca, Cayuga, Onondaga, Oneida, and Mohawk nations — and later the Tuscarora — ending cycles of blood feuding.

Symbolism

The Peacemaker is said to have planted a white pine at Onondaga, beneath which the leaders of the nations buried their weapons of war. The tree's features carry the meaning of the union: its four great white roots spread to the four directions, guiding any people who wish to live in peace to the source; an eagle atop the tree watches for approaching danger; the long needles grow in clusters of five, representing the five original nations; and the tree's evergreen nature symbolises the enduring life of the peace. The weapons buried beneath it gave rise to the expression "to bury the hatchet."

Cultural significance

The Tree of Peace remains a living emblem for the Haudenosaunee and appears in their ceremonies, governance, and art. White pines have been planted as trees of peace in many places, including near the United States Capitol and at institutions honouring the Confederacy's influence on democratic ideas. Haudenosaunee tradition holds that leaders should act like the tree: providing shelter and protection, firmly rooted but flexible.
