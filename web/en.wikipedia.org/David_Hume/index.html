David Hume (born David Home; 7 May 1711 – 25 August 1776) was a Scottish philosopher, historian, economist, and essayist, known especially for his philosophical empiricism and scepticism. He is regarded as one of the most important figures in the history of Western philosophy, and a central figure of the Scottish Enlightenment alongside his friend Adam Smith.

Philosophy

In A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40), written in his twenties, Hume set out a science of human nature founded on experience. He divided perceptions into vivid impressions and fainter ideas, and argued that all ideas derive from impressions — the "copy principle". Examining causation, he concluded that we never perceive a necessary connection between events, only constant conjunction; our belief in cause and effect is a habit of mind. This yielded the problem of induction: no rational argument can justify the assumption that the future will resemble the past.

Hume's later Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748) recast these arguments, including his famous critique of testimony for miracles. In ethics, he argued that reason alone cannot move us to act — "reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions" — and identified the is–ought problem: moral conclusions cannot be derived from purely factual premises without explaining the step. His personal identity theory described the self as a "bundle" of perceptions without a unifying subject.

History and economics

Hume first became famous as a historian: his six-volume History of England (1754–61) was a bestseller and the standard history for a century. His Political Discourses (1752) contained influential essays on money, trade and interest, anticipating ideas of Adam Smith, whose close friend he was. Hume also developed the price–specie flow mechanism describing how trade imbalances self-correct under a gold standard.

Legacy

Immanuel Kant credited Hume with awakening him from his "dogmatic slumber", provoking the critical philosophy. Hume's influence runs through utilitarianism, logical positivism, and modern philosophy of science and religion. Though denied university chairs in his lifetime because of suspected atheism, he died celebrated in Edinburgh society.
