Moral responsibility

[Image: Lady Justice holding scales]
FieldEthics, philosophy of action
Key conceptsPraise, blame, free will, accountability
Major thinkersAristotle, P. F. Strawson, Harry Frankfurt

In philosophy, moral responsibility is the status of morally deserving praise, blame, reward, or punishment for an act or omission in accordance with one's moral obligations. Deciding what counts as "morally obligatory" is a principal concern of ethics. A person is typically said to be morally responsible when their actions are freely chosen and they understand, or could reasonably be expected to understand, the consequences.

Conditions

Philosophers commonly distinguish two conditions on responsibility. The control condition requires that the agent acted freely, rather than under compulsion or unavoidable ignorance of the circumstances. The epistemic condition requires that the agent knew, or should have known, what they were doing and its likely effects. Children, and adults with severe cognitive impairments, are usually held to be exempt or partially exempt because one or both conditions fail.

Free will and determinism

The problem of moral responsibility is closely tied to the debate over free will and determinism. Incompatibilists hold that if determinism is true, no one can be truly responsible; compatibilists reply that responsibility requires only that actions flow from the agent's own desires and character, not from an uncaused will. P. F. Strawson argued that responsibility is grounded in the "reactive attitudes" — resentment, gratitude, indignation — that structure human relationships, regardless of metaphysical questions.

Collective and legal responsibility

Responsibility may also attach to groups, organisations and corporations, raising questions about how blame can be distributed among members. Legal responsibility overlaps with the moral notion but is narrower, being codified in doctrines of liability, mens rea and negligence.

Moral luck

Thomas Nagel and Bernard Williams drew attention to "moral luck": cases where people are judged for outcomes beyond their control — two equally reckless drivers, only one of whom happens to hit a pedestrian, receive very different blame.
