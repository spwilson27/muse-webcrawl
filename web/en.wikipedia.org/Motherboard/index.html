Motherboard

A motherboard (also called a mainboard or system board) is the main printed circuit board in general-purpose computers and other expandable systems. It holds and allows communication between the central processing unit (CPU), memory (RAM), storage interfaces, expansion slots and peripheral connectors, and distributes electrical power to all of them. Nearly every component of a computer connects either directly to the motherboard or through it.

Components

Key features include the CPU socket, which determines which processors are supported; DIMM slots for memory; a chipset (now largely integrated into the CPU) managing data flow; PCIe expansion slots for graphics and other cards; SATA and M.2 connectors for storage; headers for USB, audio and fans; and the firmware chip storing the BIOS or UEFI that initialises the hardware at boot.

Form factors

Standardised dimensions — principally the ATX family developed by Intel from 1995 — define mounting-hole patterns and layout. Smaller variants such as microATX and Mini-ITX suit compact machines, while laptops and phones use highly integrated custom boards with soldered components.

History

Before the microprocessor, computers consisted of multiple boards in a card cage. The integration of CPU, memory and peripherals onto a single main board in late-1970s microcomputers, and its popularisation by the IBM PC (1981), established the motherboard as the standard architecture of personal computing.
