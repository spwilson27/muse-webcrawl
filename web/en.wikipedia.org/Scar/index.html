Scar

A scar (or cicatrix) is an area of fibrous tissue that replaces normal skin after an injury. Scars result from the biological process of wound repair throughout the body: when the dermis is damaged, the body lays down new collagen fibres in a dense, differently organised pattern to close the wound quickly. Scar tissue is composed of the same protein, collagen, as the tissue it replaces, but its basketweave architecture differs from normal skin, making it typically weaker, less elastic, lacking sweat glands and hair follicles, and more vulnerable to ultraviolet radiation.

Formation

Wound healing proceeds through haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling. During proliferation, fibroblasts deposit type III collagen and new blood vessels form; in remodelling, which can continue for a year or more, type I collagen predominates and tensile strength slowly recovers — but scarred skin reaches at most about 80% of the strength of unwounded skin. Scars often appear paler, pinker or shinier than surrounding skin because of their altered collagen alignment and reduced melanocyte function.

Types

  • Fine-line scars — the common result of minor wounds and surgery, initially red and raised, flattening and fading with time.
  • Atrophic scars — sunken scars left by acne or chickenpox, where tissue is lost beneath the skin.
  • Hypertrophic scars — raised, red scars that remain within the boundary of the original wound, often after burns.
  • Keloid scars — overgrowths of scar tissue that spread beyond the original wound, more common in darker skin types, and prone to recur after removal.
  • Contractures — scars, especially from burns, that tighten and can restrict movement across joints.

Scarring also occurs internally — in the heart after myocardial infarction, in the liver (cirrhosis) and lungs (fibrosis) — where it can seriously impair organ function.

Treatment

Most scars improve spontaneously over 12–18 months. Treatments include silicone gels and sheets, pressure therapy for burns, corticosteroid injections for keloids and hypertrophic scars, laser therapy, dermabrasion and surgical revision; none removes a scar completely. Deliberate scarification is also practised as body modification and in some cultures as ritual marking.
