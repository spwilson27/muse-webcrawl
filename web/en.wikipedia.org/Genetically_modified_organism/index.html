Genetically modified organism

A genetically modified organism (GMO) is any organism whose genetic material has been altered using genetic engineering techniques. GMOs are used to produce many medications and genetically modified foods, and are widely used in scientific research and the production of other goods. The term does not always imply direct insertion of DNA from another organism; it can also include modification by deletion or rearrangement of endogenous genes.

History

The first genetically modified organism was a bacterium created by Herbert Boyer and Stanley Cohen in 1973. Rudolf Jaenisch produced the first transgenic mouse in 1974. Commercialisation followed with genetically engineered human insulin in 1982 and the Flavr Savr tomato in 1994. Since the mid-1990s, cultivation of GM crops — chiefly soybean, maize, cotton, and canola engineered for herbicide tolerance and insect resistance — has expanded to hundreds of millions of hectares, concentrated in the Americas.

Applications

In medicine, engineered bacteria and cell lines produce insulin, growth hormone, clotting factors, and monoclonal antibodies, while genetically modified animals serve as models of human disease. In agriculture, traits include resistance to pests (via Bt toxins), tolerance of herbicides, virus resistance, and nutritional enhancement such as Golden Rice, engineered to produce vitamin A precursor. Industrial applications include enzymes for detergents and food processing.

Regulation and controversy

Regulatory approaches differ sharply: the United States regulates largely by product characteristics, while the European Union applies process-based rules, precautionary assessment, and labelling requirements. Scientific assessments by bodies such as the US National Academies and the World Health Organization have found approved GM foods to be no riskier than conventional counterparts, but public debate continues over corporate control of seed, environmental effects such as gene flow and herbicide use, and consumer choice.
