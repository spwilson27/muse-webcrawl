Positivism

Auguste Comte (1798–1857), the French philosopher who coined the term "positivism".

Positivism is a philosophical school that holds that all genuine factual knowledge can only be derived from the experience of natural phenomena and their properties and relations. Thus, information derived from sensory experience, as interpreted through reason and logic, forms the exclusive source of all certain knowledge. Positivism originated with the French philosopher Auguste Comte in the early 19th century.

Comte's system

Comte proposed a law of three stages: human thought passes through a theological stage, a metaphysical stage, and finally a positive (scientific) stage, in which explanations are based on observation, experiment, and comparison rather than on appeals to supernatural beings or abstract essences. He arranged the sciences in a hierarchy from mathematics through astronomy, physics, chemistry, and biology to sociology, a discipline he founded and placed at the summit.

Comte also developed a secular "Religion of Humanity" and coined the word "altruism". His motto "Order and Progress" (Ordem e Progresso) appears on the flag of Brazil, where positivism influenced the republican movement of 1889.

Later developments

In the 20th century, the Vienna Circle developed logical positivism, combining positivism with formal logic and the verification principle of meaning. Later philosophy of science, notably Karl Popper's falsificationism and Thomas Kuhn's theory of scientific revolutions, challenged positivist accounts. Positivist approaches also shaped legal positivism in jurisprudence and quantitative, empiricist traditions in the social sciences.
