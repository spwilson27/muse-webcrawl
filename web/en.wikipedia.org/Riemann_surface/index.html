Riemann surface

In mathematics, particularly in complex analysis, a Riemann surface is a one-dimensional complex manifold. Such surfaces are naturally the right setting for studying multivalued functions — such as the square root and the logarithm — because they convert them into ordinary single-valued holomorphic functions. Riemann surfaces can be described as deformed versions of the complex plane: locally each point looks like a patch of the complex plane, but the global topology can be quite different, as for spheres and tori.

History

Bernhard Riemann introduced the concept in his 1851 doctoral dissertation, in the context of extending analytic continuation beyond natural boundaries and treating multivalued functions geometrically by stacking sheets of the plane joined along branch cuts. The rigorous foundations were developed over the following decades by, among others, Felix Klein and Hermann Weyl, whose 1913 book Die Idee der Riemannschen Fläche gave the modern definition in terms of an atlas of charts with holomorphic transition functions.

Examples

  • The complex plane itself, and any open subset of it.
  • The Riemann sphere: the complex plane plus a point at infinity, the simplest compact Riemann surface (genus 0).
  • Complex tori, obtained as quotients of the plane by a lattice (genus 1), which are elliptic curves.
  • The surface built from w2 = z — two sheets joined at the branch point at the origin.

Classification

Every simply connected Riemann surface is conformally equivalent to one of three domains: the Riemann sphere, the complex plane, or the open unit disc — a consequence of the uniformization theorem, proved in the early twentieth century by Koebe and Poincaré. Compact Riemann surfaces are classified by their genus g: the number of "handles." Surfaces of genus one are elliptic curves; those of genus two or more are uniformized by the unit disc and have rich theory connected with algebraic curves, since every compact Riemann surface can be realized as an algebraic curve in projective space.

Functions on Riemann surfaces

Holomorphic and meromorphic functions on Riemann surfaces are governed by strong global constraints. On a compact surface every holomorphic function is constant (a form of Liouville's theorem), while meromorphic functions form a field of transcendence degree one over the complex numbers. The Riemann–Roch theorem relates the dimension of spaces of meromorphic functions with prescribed zeros and poles to the genus, and is a cornerstone of the theory.

Applications

Riemann surfaces appear throughout mathematics and physics: in algebraic geometry, number theory (modular curves), dynamical systems, and string theory, where the worldsheet of a string is a Riemann surface.
