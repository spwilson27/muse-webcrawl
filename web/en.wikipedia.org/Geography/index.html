Geography

Geography (from Ancient Greek geographia, literally "earth description") is the study of the lands, features, inhabitants, and phenomena of Earth. Geography is an all-encompassing discipline that seeks an understanding of Earth and its human and natural complexities — not merely where objects are, but also how they have changed and come to be. While geography is specific to Earth, many concepts can be applied more broadly to other celestial bodies in the field of planetary science.

Branches

Geography is typically divided into two main branches. Physical geography examines the natural environment, including climatology, geomorphology, hydrology, biogeography, and oceanography. Human geography studies people and their communities, cultures, economies, and interactions with the environment, encompassing subfields such as urban geography, economic geography, political geography, and population geography. A third area, technical geography, covers cartography, geographic information systems (GIS), and remote sensing.

History

The Greeks were the first known culture to actively explore geography as both a science and a philosophy, with major contributions by Eratosthenes, who calculated the circumference of the Earth, and Ptolemy, whose Geographia standardised map-making. The discipline expanded during the Islamic Golden Age and the European Age of Discovery, when systematic exploration filled in the world map. In the modern era, thinkers such as Alexander von Humboldt and Carl Ritter established geography as an academic discipline.

Key concepts

Core geographic concepts include place, space, scale, region, location, and landscape. Geographers use both quantitative methods, such as spatial statistics, and qualitative methods, such as fieldwork and interviews. The discipline bridges the natural sciences and the social sciences, making it a uniquely integrative field of study.
