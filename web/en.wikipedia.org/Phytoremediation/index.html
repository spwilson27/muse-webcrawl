Phytoremediation

Type
Bioremediation
Agents
Plants and associated microbes
Targets
Heavy metals, radionuclides, organic pollutants

Phytoremediation is the use of plants and their associated microorganisms to remove, degrade, or contain environmental contaminants in soil, water, and air. The term combines the Greek phyto (plant) with the Latin remedium (to remedy).

Mechanisms

Several processes are distinguished: phytoextraction, in which plants accumulate contaminants (especially heavy metals) in harvestable shoots; phytodegradation, the breakdown of organic pollutants by plant enzymes; phytostabilization, which immobilizes contaminants in the root zone; phytovolatilization, the release of transformed contaminants through leaves; and rhizofiltration, the absorption of pollutants from water by roots.

Hyperaccumulators

Certain species, called hyperaccumulators, tolerate and concentrate unusually high levels of metals. Thlaspi caerulescens (alpine pennycress) accumulates zinc and cadmium, while the fern Pteris vittata takes up arsenic. Sunflowers were famously planted after the Chernobyl disaster to extract radionuclides from ponds.

Advantages and limits

Phytoremediation is solar-driven, inexpensive, and publicly acceptable compared with excavation or chemical treatment, but it is slow, limited to the rooting depth, and generates contaminated biomass that must be disposed of or processed for metal recovery (phytomining).
