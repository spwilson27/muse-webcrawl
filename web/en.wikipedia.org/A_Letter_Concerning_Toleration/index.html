A Letter Concerning Toleration

A Letter Concerning Toleration
Author: John Locke
Original: Epistola de tolerantia (Latin, 1689)
English edition: 1689 (trans. William Popple)
Subject: Religious toleration

A Letter Concerning Toleration is a work of political philosophy by John Locke, first published anonymously in Latin in 1689 as Epistola de tolerantia, while Locke was in exile in the Netherlands. An English translation by William Popple appeared the same year. It is a foundational statement of the case for religious toleration and the separation of church and state.

Arguments

Locke argues that civil government concerns only "civil interests" — life, liberty, health, and property — while the care of souls belongs to individuals and voluntary churches. Because genuine belief cannot be produced by coercion, force is both illegitimate and useless in matters of religion: a magistrate can compel outward conformity but not faith. Churches, as voluntary societies, may set their own terms of membership but may not use civil penalties. Locke also argued toleration should extend across Christian denominations and to pagans, Muslims, and Jews.

Limits and reception

Locke notoriously excluded atheists, on the ground that oaths and promises — the bonds of society — could not bind them, and he was wary of groups whose allegiance he thought belonged to a foreign power, such as Catholics. The Letter was attacked by the Anglican clergyman Jonas Proast, prompting Locke to publish three further letters defending his position. The work influenced later Enlightenment thought and the framers of religious-liberty provisions in the United States.
