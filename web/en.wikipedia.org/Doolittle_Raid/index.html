The Doolittle Raid, also known as the Tokyo Raid, was an air raid on 18 April 1942 by the United States on the Japanese capital Tokyo and other places on Honshu island during World War II. It was the first American air operation to strike the Japanese home islands, and was carried out as retaliation after the attack on Pearl Harbor. Sixteen U.S. Army Air Forces B-25B Mitchell medium bombers, led by Lieutenant Colonel James Doolittle, were launched without fighter escort from the aircraft carrier USS Hornet deep in the Western Pacific.

The raid

The bombers were launched about 250 km farther out than planned after the task force was sighted by a Japanese picket boat. Each aircraft carried four 500-pound bombs and extra fuel in place of defensive armament, intending to bomb military targets in Tokyo, Yokohama, Yokosuka, Nagoya, Osaka, and Kobe, then continue to airfields in unoccupied China. All sixteen aircraft reached Japan and bombed their targets; anti-aircraft fire was light and no bomber was shot down over Japan.

Aftermath

Lacking the fuel to reach safe airfields, fifteen crews crash-landed, ditched, or bailed out over China; one aircraft landed at Vladivostok in the Soviet Union, where its crew was interned. Of the 80 airmen, three were killed in action, eight were captured by the Japanese (of whom three were executed and one died of disease), and the rest were sheltered by Chinese civilians and soldiers and smuggled to safety. Japanese reprisals in the Zhejiang-Jiangxi campaign killed an estimated 250,000 Chinese civilians.

Material damage was negligible, but the raid was a major boost to American morale and a profound psychological shock to Japan's leadership. It contributed to the decision to extend Japan's defensive perimeter, leading directly to the Battle of Midway, and forced Japan to retain fighter units at home for defence. Doolittle received the Medal of Honor and promotion to brigadier general.
