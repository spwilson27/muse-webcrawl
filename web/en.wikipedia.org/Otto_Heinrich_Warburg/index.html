Otto Heinrich Warburg


Born: 8 October 1883, Freiburg im Breisgau, German Empire
Died: 1 August 1970 (aged 86), Berlin, West Germany
Known for: Warburg effect; respiratory enzyme research
Awards: Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1931)

Otto Heinrich Warburg (8 October 1883 – 1 August 1970) was a German physiologist and physician who received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1931 for his discovery of the nature and mode of action of the respiratory enzyme (cytochrome c oxidase, his Atmungsferment). He is also remembered for the Warburg effect in cancer biology.

Research

Warburg, the son of physicist Emil Warburg, studied under Emil Fischer and worked at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Cell Physiology in Berlin-Dahlem, which he directed from 1931. He pioneered quantitative methods in biochemistry, including manometry for measuring gas exchange in tissue slices and photosynthesis in algae. In the 1920s he observed that tumour cells consume large amounts of glucose and produce lactate even in the presence of oxygen — aerobic glycolysis, now called the Warburg effect — a metabolic hallmark of cancer still central to research and exploited in PET imaging.

Later life

Despite his partly Jewish ancestry, Warburg survived the Nazi period in Germany, protected by his scientific prominence and, reportedly, personal intervention from high levels. He continued working in Berlin into his eighties, writing and directing his institute until his death in 1970.
