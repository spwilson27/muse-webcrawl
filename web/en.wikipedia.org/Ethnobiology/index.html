Ethnobiology

Ethnobiology is the scientific study of the relationships between peoples and their environments — in particular, how human cultures perceive, classify and use plants and animals. Combining anthropology, botany, zoology and ecology, it examines traditional ecological knowledge accumulated by indigenous and local communities over generations.

Subfields

Its best-known branches are ethnobotany, the study of plant uses in food, medicine, building and ritual, and ethnozoology, concerned with animals' cultural, economic and symbolic roles. Related fields include ethnomycology (fungi), ethnoecology and ethnomedicine. Ethnobiologists study folk taxonomies — the ways cultures name and group organisms — which often correspond closely to scientific classification.

History

The term "ethnobotany" was coined in 1896 by John William Harshberger, and ethnobiology emerged as a self-conscious discipline in the mid-twentieth century through work such as Harold Conklin's studies of Hanunóo plant classification in the Philippines. The Society of Ethnobiology was founded in 1977 and publishes the Journal of Ethnobiology.

Applications

Traditional knowledge documented by ethnobiologists has led to important drugs — quinine, aspirin and artemisinin among them — and guides conservation and the sustainable management of ecosystems. The field also engages questions of intellectual property and benefit-sharing, since indigenous knowledge has often been commercialised without compensation to its holders; the 1992 Convention on Biological Diversity and the Nagoya Protocol now frame research ethics in this area.
