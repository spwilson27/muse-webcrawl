A tandoor (Hindi: तंदूर) is a cylindrical clay or metal oven used for cooking and baking across South Asia, Central Asia, the Middle East, and the Caucasus. Charcoal or wood burned inside the oven heats its walls to very high temperatures, and food is cooked both by radiant heat and by direct contact with the hot clay.

Design and operation

The traditional tandoor is a pot-shaped clay vessel, often set into the ground or enclosed in masonry, with a narrow opening at the top. Temperatures inside can approach 480 °C (900 °F). Flatbreads such as naan and roti are slapped onto the inner wall, where they bake in a minute or two, while marinated meats are threaded onto long skewers and lowered into the oven vertically.

History and spread

Clay ovens of the tandoor type are ancient: remains resembling them have been found at Indus Valley sites such as Kalibangan, and related ovens include the Middle Eastern tannur and the Central Asian tandyr. The oven spread widely with Punjabi cuisine after the Partition of India in 1947, when restaurants in Delhi popularised tandoori cooking. Tandoori chicken — chicken marinated in yogurt and spices, famously served at the Moti Mahal restaurant — became one of the most internationally recognised Indian dishes.

Dishes

Besides tandoori chicken and naan, tandoors are used for chicken tikka, seekh kebabs, samosas, and in Central Asia for non (bread) and samsa pastries. The intense heat seals in moisture and gives food a characteristic charred, smoky flavour.
