Scramble for Africa

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Scramble for Africa was the invasion, annexation, division, and colonisation of most of Africa by seven Western European powers between 1881 and 1914, the height of the period known as New Imperialism. In 1870 Europeans controlled about 10 percent of Africa, chiefly coastal enclaves; by 1914 they controlled about 90 percent — only Ethiopia and Liberia remained fully independent.

Causes

Drivers included rivalry between European states, the search for raw materials and markets, new technologies (steamships, telegraphy, repeating rifles), and quinine, which made sustained penetration of the malarial interior survivable. Explorers and missionaries such as David Livingstone and Henry Morton Stanley, and Leopold II of Belgium's seizure of the Congo basin, intensified the competition.

Partition

To avoid war among themselves, the powers convened the Berlin Conference of 1884–85, whose General Act set rules of "effective occupation": a claim required actual administration. The resulting partition drew borders across ethnic and geographic realities. Flashpoints included the Fashoda Incident (1898) between Britain and France and the Moroccan crises between France and Germany. The conquest itself was violent — exemplified by the Congo Free State atrocities and the Herero and Nama genocide in German South West Africa (1904–08).

Legacy

Colonial boundaries largely survived decolonisation in the 1950s–60s, and the Scramble's political and economic consequences — artificial states, extractive institutions, and disrupted societies — continue to shape the continent.
