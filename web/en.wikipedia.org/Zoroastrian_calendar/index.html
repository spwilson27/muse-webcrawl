Zoroastrian calendar

The Zoroastrian calendar is a solar calendar used by followers of Zoroastrianism. In its traditional form it consists of 12 months of 30 days each, plus five additional days (the Gatha days) at the end of the year, totalling 365 days, with no regular leap-year correction in the versions used for much of its history — causing the calendar to drift against the seasons over centuries.

Months and days

Each month and each of the 30 days is dedicated to a particular divine being (a yazata). Month names in Middle Persian include Farvardin, Ordibehesht, Khordad, Tir, Amordad, Shahrivar, Mehr, Aban, Azar, Dey, Bahman and Esfand. The new year, Nowruz, falls at the start of Farvardin.

Variants

Three versions are in use among Zoroastrian communities: the Shahenshahi (imperial), Kadmi (Qadimi) and Fasli (seasonal) calendars, which differ by accumulated intercalation and, in the Fasli case, by a leap day every four years keeping Nowruz at the spring equinox. Disagreements over the calendar have historically divided communities in India (the Parsis) and Iran.

History

The calendar's roots lie in ancient Iranian timekeeping under the Achaemenid and Sasanian empires. Reforms under the Sasanians attempted to realign festivals with the seasons.
