Semasiology

Semasiology (from Greek σημασία, semasia, "signification", from σημαντικός semantikos, and -λογία, -logia) is a discipline in linguistics concerned with the question "what does the word X mean?". It is the study of meaning approached from the word or sign as the starting point: given a lexical form, semasiology examines the range of meanings it expresses.

Contrast with onomasiology

Semasiology is traditionally contrasted with onomasiology, which asks the reverse question: "what word expresses the concept Y?"—starting from a meaning or concept and seeking the words used to denote it. The two perspectives are complementary within the broader field of semantics. The distinction became standard in European linguistics in the early 20th century, notably in the work associated with the Wörter und Sachen movement.

History

The term was introduced in the 19th century, with Christian Karl Reisig's lectures on Latin philology often credited as an early source. Michel Bréal, whose Essai de sémantique (1897) founded semantics as a discipline, worked largely in the semasiological tradition, studying how word meanings change over time. Semasiology thus overlaps with the study of semantic change, including processes such as broadening, narrowing, amelioration, and pejoration.

Methods

Semasiological analysis examines the polysemy of a word—the network of its related senses—and how those senses are structured, for instance in terms of central and peripheral meanings (prototype structure). In lexicography, the semasiological approach organizes dictionary entries by headword, listing the senses of each word.
