Postcolonialism

Postcolonialism is the critical academic study of the cultural, political, and economic legacy of colonialism and imperialism, focusing on the impact of human control and exploitation of colonized people and their lands. Whereas the related field of postcolonial studies is often used synonymously, "postcolonialism" may also refer more broadly to the historical period after decolonization and to the body of literary and theoretical work that responds to colonial rule.

Definitions

The term "postcolonial" is contested. It can denote the period after a territory gains independence; the entire era since the beginning of colonization, because colonial power relations persist; or a critical stance toward colonial discourse regardless of period. Many scholars caution that the "post" should not be read as meaning colonialism's effects have ended, pointing to continuing economic dependency, cultural hierarchies, and settler colonialism in places such as the Americas, Australia, and Palestine.

Historical background

European colonial empires expanded from the fifteenth century onward, reaching their height in the early twentieth century, when Britain, France, Spain, Portugal, Belgium, Germany, and Italy controlled much of Africa, Asia, and the Pacific. The decades after 1945 saw rapid decolonization: India and Pakistan in 1947, Ghana in 1957, much of Africa by the early 1960s, and the Portuguese colonies in 1975. Thinkers such as Frantz Fanon, Albert Memmi, and Aimé Césaire articulated critiques of colonial domination during this period, laying the groundwork for the later academic field.

Themes

Postcolonialism addresses identity formation under colonial rule, the politics of language (including debates over writing in colonial languages versus indigenous languages, as in the work of Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o), land and resource extraction, racial classification, resistance and nationalism, and the recovery of indigenous knowledge systems. It also examines neocolonialism — the continuation of economic and cultural domination after formal independence.

Postcolonial literature

Writers including Chinua Achebe, Salman Rushdie, Wole Soyinka, Derek Walcott, Jamaica Kincaid, and Arundhati Roy reshaped English-language literature by narrating colonial and post-independence experience from local perspectives. Achebe's Things Fall Apart (1958) is often cited as a foundational response to colonial representations of Africa.

Debates

Debates within and about postcolonialism concern its academic jargon, its relationship to Marxism and poststructuralism, whether it adequately addresses settler colonialism and indigenous sovereignty, and the place of economic analysis. Scholars such as Aijaz Ahmad and Arif Dirlik criticized the field's institutional location in Western universities and its tendency toward culturalism.
