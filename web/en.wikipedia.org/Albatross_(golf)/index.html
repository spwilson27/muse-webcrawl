In golf, an albatross, also called a double eagle in the United States, is a score of three strokes under par on a single hole. It is achieved most commonly by holing out in two strokes on a par-five hole, or by scoring a hole in one on a par-four hole.

Rarity

An albatross is far rarer than a hole in one on a par-three: a long hole must be completed in just two shots, combining a long, accurate drive with a holed approach shot. Odds are commonly estimated in the range of a million to one for amateur golfers. In professional majors they are historic events — the most famous is Gene Sarazen's "shot heard 'round the world" in the final round of the 1935 Masters Tournament, when he holed a 4-wood from 235 yards on the par-five 15th at Augusta National, a shot that helped him force a playoff which he won.

Terminology

The term belongs to the bird-themed family of golf scores — birdie, eagle, albatross, and the near-mythical condor (four under par) — reflecting the increasing rarity of the birds after which they are named. "Double eagle" is the traditional American usage, while "albatross" is standard in Britain and most of the rest of the world.

Notable albatrosses

Only a handful have been recorded in the four men's majors, including Sarazen (1935 Masters), and Louis Oosthuizen's at the 2nd hole in the final round of the 2012 Masters — the first on that hole in tournament history. Albatrosses in women's majors and on the major tours are similarly rare and are usually commemorated by the club or course where they occur.
