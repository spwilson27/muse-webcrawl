Plant breeding

Plant breeding is the science of changing the traits of plants in order to produce desired characteristics. It has been used to improve the quality of nutrition in products for humans and animals, to increase yields, and to develop resistance to diseases, pests, drought, and other stresses. Plant breeding can be accomplished through many different techniques, ranging from simply selecting plants with desirable characteristics for propagation, to methods that make use of knowledge of genetics and chromosomes, to more complex molecular techniques.

History

Plant breeding began with the domestication of the first agricultural plants some 9,000 to 11,000 years ago, as Neolithic farmers selected the largest seeds and non-shattering heads of wild grasses, giving rise to wheat, barley, rice, and maize. For millennia breeding proceeded by empirical selection; the scientific basis arrived with Gregor Mendel's laws of inheritance in the 1860s, rediscovered in 1900.

The 20th century brought hybrid maize, the semi-dwarf wheat and rice varieties of the Green Revolution — for which Norman Borlaug received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970 — and mutation breeding using radiation and chemicals. Modern breeding also employs marker-assisted selection, genomic selection, tissue culture and doubled haploids, genetic engineering (transgenic crops), and gene editing with tools such as CRISPR.

Objectives and methods

Typical goals include higher yield, better quality (taste, nutrition, processing), resistance to pathogens such as wheat rusts, tolerance of drought, salinity and heat, and adaptation to mechanised agriculture. Classical methods include mass selection, pure-line selection, hybridisation followed by pedigree or backcross selection, and recurrent selection in outcrossing crops. Breeders' rights, governed internationally by the UPOV convention, allow developers of new varieties to control their commercial propagation for a limited period.

Issues

Debates around plant breeding include the genetic erosion caused by replacing diverse landraces with uniform modern varieties, dependence on a narrow set of crops and genes, seed industry concentration, and public controversy over genetically modified crops — countered by efforts in participatory breeding, gene banks, and conservation of crop wild relatives.
