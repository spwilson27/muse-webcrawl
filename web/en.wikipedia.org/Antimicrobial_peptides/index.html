Antimicrobial peptides

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), also called host defence peptides, are part of the innate immune response found among all classes of life. Fundamental differences exist between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells that are the targets of these peptides; they are potent, broad-spectrum antibiotics which have been demonstrated to kill Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, enveloped viruses, fungi, and even transformed cells.

Structure

AMPs are typically small, consisting of 12 to 50 amino acids, and are usually cationic and amphipathic, with a net positive charge and a substantial proportion of hydrophobic residues. Well-studied families include the defensins and cathelicidins of mammals, cecropins and magainins from insects and frogs, and bacteriocins produced by bacteria.

Mechanism of action

Most AMPs act by disrupting microbial membranes: electrostatic attraction to negatively charged bacterial surfaces is followed by pore formation or membrane disintegration, described by models such as the barrel-stave, toroidal pore, and carpet models. Some peptides instead cross the membrane and act on intracellular targets such as DNA, protein synthesis, or cell division.

Therapeutic potential

Because of rising antibiotic resistance, AMPs are being investigated as drugs. Examples in clinical use or development include polymyxins and daptomycin, though challenges include toxicity, stability, and production cost.
