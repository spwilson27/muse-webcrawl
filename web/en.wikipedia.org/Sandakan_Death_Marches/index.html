Sandakan Death Marches

DateJanuary – August 1945
LocationSandakan to Ranau, North Borneo
VictimsAustralian and British prisoners of war
DeathsApprox. 2,400 (only six survivors, all escapees)

The Sandakan Death Marches were a series of forced marches in British North Borneo during the Second World War in which the Imperial Japanese Army moved prisoners of war from the Sandakan POW camp to Ranau, about 260 kilometres inland, between January and August 1945. Of roughly 2,400 Australian and British prisoners held at Sandakan, only six men — all of them escapees — survived the war.

Background

From 1942, Allied prisoners were shipped to Sandakan to build a military airstrip. Conditions deteriorated sharply after 1943: rations were cut, officers were removed to Kuching, and prisoners were beaten and starved. As Allied forces advanced in 1945, the Japanese decided to move the remaining able-bodied prisoners westward across the mountains.

The marches

Three groups set out between January and June 1945 along jungle tracks through swamp and over the Crocker Range. Prisoners weakened by dysentery, beri-beri and malnutrition who fell behind were shot, bayoneted or left to die; of the first party of about 470 men, only a handful reached Ranau alive. Those too sick to march were killed at Sandakan, where the camp was destroyed. At Ranau the survivors continued to die of disease and starvation, and the last prisoners were massacred shortly before and after the Japanese surrender in August 1945.

Aftermath and commemoration

Six Australians escaped — among them Bombardier Richard Braithwaite and Warrant Officer Bill Sticpewich — and, sheltered by local people, lived to testify at war-crimes trials in Tokyo and Rabaul. The Sandakan Memorial Park now occupies the camp site, and an annual Anzac Day service and the commemorative Sandakan–Ranau track walk keep the marches in public memory in Australia and Sabah.
