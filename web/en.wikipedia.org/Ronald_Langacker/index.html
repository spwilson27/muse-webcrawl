Ronald Wayne Langacker (born December 27, 1942) is an American linguist and professor emeritus at the University of California, San Diego. He is best known as the founder of cognitive grammar, a comprehensive theory of language structure within the broader movement of cognitive linguistics.

Career

Langacker received his Ph.D. from the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign in 1966 and taught at UC San Diego from 1966 until his retirement in 2003. In the 1970s he was a prominent figure in the short-lived "space grammar" programme, which developed into cognitive grammar. His two-volume Foundations of Cognitive Grammar (1987, 1991) laid out the theory in detail, and he served as president of the International Cognitive Linguistics Association.

Cognitive grammar

Cognitive grammar rejects the idea of an autonomous, innate syntactic module. Instead, it holds that grammar is inherently meaningful and consists of conventionalized pairings of semantic and phonological structures called symbolic units. Lexicon, morphology, and syntax form a continuum of such units, learned and organized through general cognitive abilities such as categorization, schematization, and entrenchment.

Key notions in the theory include the distinction between trajector and landmark (the primary and secondary focal participants in a relation), profiling (the part of a conceptual base an expression designates), construal (the many ways a speaker can portray the same situation), and the analysis of grammar in terms of conceptual domains and image schemas. Langacker also developed an account of tense, modality, and clause structure known as the "epistemic model" and the analysis of subject and object in terms of the "action chain".
