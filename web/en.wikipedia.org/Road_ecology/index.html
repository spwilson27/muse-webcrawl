Road ecology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Road ecology is the study of the ecological effects of roads and highways on the environment and wildlife, and of measures to reduce those effects. The field grew rapidly from the 1990s, notably with the work of Richard T. T. Forman and colleagues, as road networks expanded to cover most terrestrial landscapes.

Effects

Roads affect ecosystems in several ways. Wildlife mortality from vehicle collisions kills hundreds of millions of animals annually and is a significant threat to some rare species, such as the Florida panther. Roads also create a barrier effect, fragmenting habitat and isolating populations, which can reduce genetic diversity — small populations on either side of a busy highway may diverge or decline. The road-effect zone extends well beyond the pavement: traffic noise masks birdsong and alters breeding behaviour, lights disturb nocturnal species, salt and heavy metals from runoff pollute roadside soils and streams, and road corridors allow invasive species to penetrate new areas. Some species, such as scavengers and certain raptors, benefit from roadside food resources.

Mitigation

The principal mitigation measures are wildlife crossings — vegetated overpasses ("ecoducts") and underpasses, often combined with fencing that funnels animals to them. Famous examples include the crossings in Banff National Park in Canada and the Netherlands' network of more than 600 ecoducts and fauna passages. Amphibian tunnels, fish-friendly culverts, lowered speed limits, and wildlife warning signs are also used. Planning tools aim to keep new roads out of critical habitat altogether, following the sequence of avoidance, minimisation, and compensation.
