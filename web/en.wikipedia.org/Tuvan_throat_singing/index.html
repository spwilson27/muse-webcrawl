Tuvan throat singing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Native nameKhӧӧmei (Хөөмей)
OriginTuva, Central Asia
Principal stylesKhӧӧmei, sygyt, kargyraa, ezenggileer, borbangnadyr
Notable ensemblesHuun-Huur-Tu, Yat-Kha, Alash

Tuvan throat singing, generally known as khӧӧmei (Tuvan: Хөөмей), is a form of overtone singing practiced by the people of Tuva, a republic in southern Siberia, as well as in Mongolia and other parts of Central Asia. In throat singing, a single vocalist produces two or more distinct pitches simultaneously by selectively amplifying harmonics of a fundamental drone.

Styles

  • Khӧӧmei — the general term and a middle style with a soft, mellow whistle-like overtone melody.
  • Sygyt — a style with a clear, bright, piercing whistle-like overtone, produced by pressing the tongue against the teeth.
  • Kargyraa — a very low, growling style that also engages the ventricular folds, producing a pitch an octave below the drone with rich upper harmonics.
  • Ezenggileer — a pulsing style imitating the rhythm of a horse's stirrups (ezengi).
  • Borbangnadyr — a warbling style whose name evokes rolling sounds, such as waves.

Cultural context

Throat singing traditionally imitates the sounds of nature — wind, water, birdsong, and animals — reflecting the animist worldview of Tuvan nomadic herders. It was historically performed solo or in informal settings, often accompanied by instruments such as the igil (bowed spike fiddle) or doshpuluur. After the Soviet period suppressed some traditions, throat singing was revived and popularized internationally by ensembles such as Huun-Huur-Tu (founded 1992) and through collaborations with Western musicians. The physicist Richard Feynman famously sought to visit Tuva partly out of fascination sparked by its stamps and culture, a story chronicled in Tuva or Bust!

Technique

The singer sustains a fundamental tone while shaping the vocal tract — lips, tongue, jaw, and soft palate — so that a single harmonic is resonantly amplified into a flute-like melody above the drone. Styles differ in fundamental pitch, resonance strategy, and the use of the false vocal folds, particularly in kargyraa.
