Cortical homunculus
FieldNeuroscience, neuroanatomy
Associated researcherWilder Penfield
RepresentsMotor and somatosensory maps of the body in the cerebral cortex

The sensory homunculus, with body parts sized by the cortical area devoted to them.

A cortical homunculus (Latin for "little man") is a distorted representation of the human body based on the neurological "map" of the areas of the cerebral cortex dedicated to processing motor functions or sensory input for different parts of the body. Body parts with fine motor control or fine discrimination of touch — such as the hands, lips, and tongue — are drawn grotesquely large, while the trunk and legs are small.

Origin

The maps derive from the work of Canadian neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield and his collaborators in the 1930s–1950s. While operating on conscious patients with epilepsy, Penfield electrically stimulated points on the cortex and recorded the movements or sensations produced, building maps of the primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus) and primary somatosensory cortex (postcentral gyrus). The resulting figures were drawn by medical illustrator Hortense Cantlie and have become iconic images in neuroscience.

Types

There are two main homunculi. The motor homunculus maps the body onto the primary motor cortex, which controls voluntary movement; the sensory (somatosensory) homunculus maps it onto the cortex receiving touch, proprioception, pain, and temperature signals. In both, the body is represented upside down along the gyrus — the feet near the top of the brain, the face near the bottom — and the amount of cortex devoted to each part reflects the precision of control or sensitivity required, not the part's physical size.

Later research

Modern imaging shows the maps are not fixed: they reorganise with learning, injury, and amputation, a phenomenon known as cortical plasticity. Recent studies have also refined Penfield's maps, identifying interlinked effector regions within the motor strip; nevertheless the homunculus remains a standard teaching model for the organisation of the cortex.
