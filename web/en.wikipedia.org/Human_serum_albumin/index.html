Human serum albumin (HSA) is the serum albumin found in human blood. It is the most abundant protein in human blood plasma, constituting about half of serum protein, and is produced in the liver at roughly 10–15 grams per day. Albumin transports hormones, fatty acids, bilirubin, and drugs, and maintains the oncotic pressure needed to keep fluid within the blood vessels.

Structure and function

Albumin is a globular, heart-shaped protein of three homologous domains, stabilized by 17 disulfide bridges, with a single free cysteine (Cys34) that acts as an antioxidant. It binds a wide variety of ligands at multiple sites — including the drug-binding Sudlow sites I and II — which makes it a major determinant of drug distribution and interactions. As the main contributor to plasma colloid osmotic pressure, falling albumin levels cause fluid to leak into tissues, producing edema.

Clinical significance

Low serum albumin (hypoalbuminemia) occurs in liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition, and inflammation, and is a strong predictor of poor outcomes. Rare inherited conditions include analbuminemia (near-absence of albumin) and familial dysalbuminemic hyperthyroxinemia. Glycation of albumin in diabetes produces glycated albumin, used as a marker of medium-term glycemic control.

Medical use

Purified human albumin solution is given intravenously as a plasma volume expander in certain settings — notably complications of cirrhosis such as spontaneous bacterial peritonitis and large-volume paracentesis, and in some cases of shock and burns — though its benefits over crystalloids in general critical care remain debated.
