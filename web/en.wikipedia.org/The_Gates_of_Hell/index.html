The Gates of Hell (French: La Porte de l'Enfer) is a monumental bronze sculptural group work by French artist Auguste Rodin that depicts a scene from the Inferno, the first section of Dante Alighieri's Divine Comedy. Standing 6 metres high, 4 metres wide, and nearly a metre deep, it contains some 180 figures. Rodin worked on it for 37 years — from the original 1880 commission until his death in 1917 — and it became the matrix from which many of his most famous independent sculptures were drawn.

History

The work was commissioned in 1880 as the entrance doors for a planned Museum of Decorative Arts in Paris. The museum was never built, but Rodin continued developing the doors at state expense, populating them with tortured figures from Dante and, increasingly, from Baudelaire and his own imagination. He never completed a definitive version; the first bronze cast was made only in the 1920s, after his death, from the plaster model preserved at the Musée Rodin, where a cast now stands in the garden.

Constituent figures

Many celebrated Rodin sculptures originated as figures for the Gates: The Thinker (originally Dante himself, brooding above the lintel), The Kiss (Paolo and Francesca), Ugolino and His Sons, The Three Shades surmounting the whole, Fugit Amor, and The Prodigal Son. As Rodin enlarged and exhibited these figures independently, the Gates became a kind of quarry or laboratory for his mature style — psychological, fragmentary, and expressive of torment and desire.
