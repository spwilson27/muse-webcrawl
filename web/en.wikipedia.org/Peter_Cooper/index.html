Peter Cooper

Peter Cooper (February 12, 1791 – April 4, 1883) was an American industrialist, inventor, philanthropist, and politician. He designed and built the first American steam locomotive, the Tom Thumb, founded the Cooper Union for the Advancement of Science and Art in New York City, and was the Greenback Party's candidate for President of the United States in 1876.

Business career

Born in New York City to a family of modest means and largely self-educated, Cooper worked successively as a coach-maker's apprentice, in the cloth-shearing business, and as a manufacturer of glue and isinglass, in which he made a fortune. He built the Canton Iron Works in Baltimore and in 1830 demonstrated the Tom Thumb locomotive for the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad. His rolling mills produced some of the first American iron railroad rails and structural beams, including those for the U.S. Capitol dome's era of construction; he also helped Cyrus Field lay the first transatlantic telegraph cable in 1858 and served as president of the New York, Newfoundland and London Telegraph Company.

Philanthropy

Cooper believed education should be "as free as water and air." In 1859 he founded the Cooper Union in Manhattan, offering free classes — later free tuition — in science, engineering and art to working people, men and women alike. Its Great Hall hosted a famous 1860 speech by Abraham Lincoln ("Right makes might").

Politics and legacy

An advocate of paper currency (greenbacks) and monetary reform, he was nominated for president in 1876 at the age of 85, the oldest person ever nominated by a major American political party ticket of the era. He also championed Native American rights and abolition of slavery. Peter Cooper is buried in Green-Wood Cemetery, Brooklyn.
