Myrmecophily (from Greek myrmex, "ant", and philia, "love") is the term for inter-species associations with ants. Organisms that live in association with ants are called myrmecophiles. The relationships range from mutualism and commensalism to parasitism, and myrmecophiles include beetles, butterflies, mites, spiders, plants and fungi.

Examples

Many caterpillars of the butterfly family Lycaenidae are attended by ants, which guard them from predators in exchange for honeydew-like secretions from a dorsal nectary organ. Some lycaenids go further: caterpillars of the large blue butterflies (Phengaris) produce sounds and chemical signals mimicking ant larvae, are carried into nests, and there feed on ant brood. Thousands of beetle species, notably staphylinids of the subfamilies Aleocharinae and Pselaphinae, live inside ant colonies, using chemical mimicry and tactile signals to integrate with their hosts. Plants called myrmecophytes, such as some acacias and Myrmecodia, provide nesting cavities and food bodies, and receive protection from herbivores in return.

Significance

Ants are dominant, social and long-lived colonial insects, so their nests offer food, shelter and enemy-free space to a vast number of associates. Myrmecophily is regarded as a major pathway in the evolution of symbiotic lifestyles and social parasitism.
