Milwaukee protocol

First used2004
First patientJeanna Giese
ConditionSymptomatic rabies
DeveloperRodney Willoughby Jr.

The Milwaukee protocol, sometimes called the Wisconsin protocol, is an experimental course of treatment for rabies infection in humans after symptoms have appeared — a stage of disease that is otherwise almost invariably fatal. The protocol places the patient into a chemically induced coma while administering antiviral drugs, on the theory that rabies kills through temporary dysfunction of the brain rather than widespread destruction of neurons, so protecting the brain may give the immune system time to produce antibodies.

It was first used in 2004 on Jeanna Giese, a 15-year-old from Wisconsin who had been bitten by a rabid bat and developed symptoms without prior vaccination. Treated by Dr. Rodney Willoughby Jr. at Children's Hospital of Wisconsin, she survived and became the first documented person to survive symptomatic rabies without having received the vaccine before illness onset.

Effectiveness debates

Despite the initial success, attempts to replicate the protocol have mostly failed, with only a small number of survivors among dozens of documented attempts, and some survivors may have been infected with less virulent bat rabies strains or had early immune responses. The protocol remains controversial, though updated versions continue to be refined by its proponents.
