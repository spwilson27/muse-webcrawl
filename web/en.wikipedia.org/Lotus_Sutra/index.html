The Lotus Sutra (Sanskrit: Saddharma Puṇḍarīka Sūtra, "Sutra on the White Lotus of the True Dharma") is one of the most influential and venerated texts of Mahayana Buddhism. Composed in stages between roughly the 1st century BCE and the 2nd century CE, it presents itself as a sermon delivered by the Buddha Shakyamuni on Vulture Peak late in his life. Its Chinese translation by Kumārajīva (406 CE) became the standard version in East Asia.

Teachings

The sutra's central teaching is the One Vehicle (ekayāna): the apparently different paths of the disciple, the solitary buddha, and the bodhisattva are all "skillful means" (upāya) by which the Buddha leads beings to the single goal of full Buddhahood, open to everyone — including women and the wicked, as illustrated by the dragon king's daughter and the prediction of Buddhahood for Devadatta. Famous parables include the burning house, the prodigal son, the conjured city, and the hidden jewel in the garment. Later chapters reveal that the Buddha's lifespan is immeasurably long and that he remains present in the world, and celebrate the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara (in the chapter often circulated separately as the Kannon Sutra).

Influence

The Lotus Sutra is the foundational scripture of the Tiantai (Tendai) school, which ranked it the Buddha's supreme teaching, and of the Nichiren schools, which centre devotion on chanting the title of the sutra (Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō). Its doctrines of universal Buddhahood and skillful means shaped East Asian Buddhism profoundly, and it remains among the most translated and studied of all Buddhist texts.
