Moore's law

Moore's law
Proposed: 1965, revised 1975
Author: Gordon Moore (co-founder of Intel)
Claim: transistor count on chips doubles roughly every two years

Moore's law is the observation that the number of transistors in an integrated circuit doubles approximately every two years, and the associated prediction that this trend would continue. It is named after Gordon Moore, who in a 1965 paper noted that the number of components per chip had doubled each year since 1959 and projected this to continue; in 1975 he revised the period to about two years. The "law" is an empirical observation and industry roadmap rather than a law of nature.

History and mechanism

Moore's observation rode on progress in photolithography and planar processing. For decades, Dennard scaling meant smaller transistors were also faster and more power-efficient per unit area, so doubling density delivered cheaper, faster, cooler chips. The industry organized itself around the trend through shared roadmaps (the International Technology Roadmap for Semiconductors), making Moore's law partly a self-fulfilling prophecy that coordinated investment and expectations.

Slowdown and workarounds

Dennard scaling broke down around 2005–2007 as leakage currents limited clock speeds (which plateaued near 4 GHz), prompting the shift to multicore processors. Since the 2010s, shrinking transistors has become slower and far more expensive—Rock's law notes the cost of fabrication plants doubles about every four years—and gains increasingly come from new architectures (GPUs, specialized accelerators), 3D stacking, chiplets, and improved packaging rather than classic transistor scaling. Node names such as "3 nm" no longer correspond to a literal transistor dimension.

Impact

Moore's law underwrote the personal computer, smartphone, and internet revolutions by making computation exponentially cheaper for half a century. Gordon Moore died in 2023; assessments differ on whether the law is "dead," but the broader trend of improving cost-performance in computing continues through heterogeneous integration and specialized hardware.
