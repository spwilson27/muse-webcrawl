Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome

Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome (WKS) is the combined presence of Wernicke's encephalopathy and Korsakoff syndrome, two conditions caused by thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency. The acute phase, Wernicke's encephalopathy, presents with confusion, eye-movement abnormalities and unsteady gait. If untreated, it often progresses to Korsakoff syndrome, a chronic amnesic state in which patients cannot form new memories and may confabulate — inventing plausible accounts to fill the gaps — while other cognitive abilities remain relatively intact.

Causes

The most common cause in developed countries is chronic alcohol misuse, which impairs thiamine intake, absorption and storage. Other causes include severe malnutrition, prolonged vomiting, eating disorders and the effects of chemotherapy or bariatric surgery. Damage centres on the mammillary bodies, the medial thalamus and periaqueductal grey matter.

Treatment and prognosis

Prevention and treatment rely on prompt, high-dose parenteral thiamine and abstinence from alcohol. The acute symptoms may resolve, but the amnesia of Korsakoff syndrome recovers fully in only about a quarter of patients, and many require long-term supported care. Sergei Korsakoff described the amnesic syndrome in 1887; the link between the two conditions was established in the mid-20th century.
