Coup d'état

French: coup d'État
Literally: "stroke of state"
First used: 17th-century France

A coup d'état is a sudden and decisive action in politics, especially the violent overthrow of an existing government by a small group. The phrase comes from French, literally "a stroke (blow) of state". Coups are typically executed by military officers or a palace faction and aim to seize state power quickly, often within hours, before opposition can organize.

Etymology

The expression was used in 17th- and 18th-century France to describe sudden, decisive measures taken by a ruler to secure power or remove threats, before it took on its modern sense of an illegal seizure of the state by insiders. Related terms include putsch (from Swiss German), golpe de estado in Spanish and Portuguese, and pronunciamiento, a form of military revolt historically associated with 19th-century Spain and Latin America.

Mechanisms

Successful coups generally require control of the armed forces' command structure, rapid seizure of communications, and the neutralization — through arrest, exile, or coercion — of the incumbent leadership. Coup plotters frequently announce a transitional council, promise elections, and blame corruption or disorder for their intervention.

Statistics

Political science datasets count hundreds of coup attempts since 1950, with roughly half succeeding for at least a week. Sub-Saharan Africa has recorded the largest number, though coups have occurred on every inhabited continent. Since the end of the Cold War, the frequency of coups declined for several decades, with a partial resurgence in the early 2020s sometimes described as a "coup belt" across West Africa.
