Ramsey's theorem

Ramsey's theorem, named after the British mathematician Frank P. Ramsey, is a foundational result in combinatorics stating that sufficiently large structures must contain orderly substructures. In graph-theoretic terms, for any positive integers m and n there is a smallest number R(m, n) — a Ramsey number — such that any complete graph on R(m, n) vertices whose edges are coloured red or blue contains either a red complete subgraph on m vertices or a blue complete subgraph on n vertices.

Example

R(3, 3) = 6: among any six people, there are always three who mutually know each other or three who mutually do not, illustrating that complete disorder is impossible.

Ramsey numbers

Exact Ramsey numbers are notoriously hard to compute; only a handful are known, and bounds on diagonal Ramsey numbers R(n, n) are a famous open area. Paul Erdős popularised the problem with the remark that if aliens demanded the value of R(5, 5) humanity might marshal its computers to find it, but for R(6, 6) it should instead prepare to fight.
