Icelandic (Icelandic: íslenska) is a North Germanic language spoken by about 330,000 people, the vast majority of whom live in Iceland, where it has been the country's official language since 2011. It is most closely related to Faroese and Norwegian. The language is more conservative than most other Germanic languages: while the other Germanic languages have reduced their inflectional systems, Icelandic retains a four-case synthetic grammar with strong and weak nouns, three genders, and rich verbal conjugation. Icelandic is able to adopt new words for technology without borrowing from other languages because of linguistic purism and official coinage of neologisms.

History

Old Norse was brought to Iceland by Norse settlers in the 9th and 10th centuries. From about 1100, written Icelandic diverged from Norwegian, and the medieval literature of Iceland — the sagas, Eddas and skaldic poetry — is the richest vernacular literature of medieval Europe. Little grammatical change occurred from the 12th to the 20th century, so Icelanders can read Old Norse sagas with relative ease. Danish rule from the late Middle Ages influenced vocabulary, but a 19th-century purist movement, associated with the journal Fjölnir, purged many Danish loanwords.

Phonology and grammar

Icelandic has a phonemic distinction between aspirated and unaspirated stops, preaspiration, and devoiced sonorants. Its alphabet adds the letters Ð/ð (eth), Þ/þ (thorn) and Æ/æ to the Latin set. Nouns decline in four cases (nominative, accusative, dative, genitive), two numbers and three genders; adjectives agree, and verbs conjugate for person, number, tense and mood. Word order is basically subject–verb–object, with verb-second order in main clauses.

Vocabulary and purism

Icelandic policy favours coining new terms from native roots: tölva ("computer", blending "number" and "prophetess"), sími ("telephone", reviving an old word for cord), and vefur ("web"). The Árni Magnússon Institute maintains terminology banks, and the government has funded language-technology programmes to ensure Icelandic survives in digital environments.
