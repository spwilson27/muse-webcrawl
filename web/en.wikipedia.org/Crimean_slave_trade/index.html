Crimean slave trade

The Crimean slave trade was the trade in captives carried on by the Crimean Khanate between the 15th and 18th centuries. Together with the raids that supplied it, the trade formed the economic backbone of the khanate. Captives taken in raids into East Slavic lands, the Caucasus, and Circassia were sold at Caffa (present-day Feodosia) and other Black Sea ports to buyers from the Ottoman Empire, the Middle East, and, in the earlier period, Mediterranean Europe. Estimates of the total number of people enslaved range from roughly two million to considerably more over the trade's three centuries.

Caffa (Kefe), the principal slave market of the Black Sea

Origins

Slave trading in the Black Sea region long predated the khanate: Genoese colonies such as Caffa had exported Slavic, Circassian, and Tatar slaves since the 13th century. After the Ottoman conquest of Caffa in 1475 and the subordination of the Crimean khans, the trade expanded under Ottoman protection, with the sultan receiving a customs share of every sale.

Operation of the trade

Captives taken in raids — the "harvest of the steppe" — were sorted in Crimea. The khan, Tatar nobles, and raiders took their shares; the rest were sold to merchants. Young women and boys fetched the highest prices; adult men were often sent to agricultural work, mines, or Ottoman galleys. Many captives were ransomed by their families or by charitable funds in Poland and Russia rather than sold abroad, and both states paid annual subsidies to Crimea partly intended to limit raiding.

Scale and impact

The trade depopulated large areas of the Ukrainian and south Russian steppe frontier, where farming communities lived under constant threat of capture. It shaped the military organisation of the borderlands, contributing to the rise of the Cossacks, and it supplied the Ottoman world with a substantial part of its enslaved population alongside the African slave trade. The trade also carried slaves from the Caucasus — Circassians prized for military and domestic service — into Ottoman households.

Decline and abolition

As Russia pushed its frontier fortifications southward and strengthened its armies, the raiding that fed the trade became increasingly difficult. The Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca (1774) and the Russian annexation of Crimea in 1783 ended the Crimean slave trade, although slave trading in the Caucasus and across the Black Sea persisted under other auspices into the 19th century.
