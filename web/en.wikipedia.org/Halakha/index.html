Halakha

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Halakha (Hebrew: הֲלָכָה), also transliterated as halacha, halocho, or halakha, is the collective body of Jewish religious laws derived from the Written and Oral Torah. It encompasses the 613 mitzvot (commandments) and later Talmudic and rabbinic law, as well as customs and traditions. The word derives from a Hebrew root meaning "to walk" or "to go" — the path on which one walks.

Sources and development

Halakha is based on biblical commandments as interpreted by the Oral Torah, recorded in the Mishnah (c. 200 CE) and elaborated in the Talmud (c. 300–500 CE). Medieval scholars produced systematic codes, of which the most influential are Maimonides' Mishneh Torah (12th century) and Joseph Karo's Shulchan Aruch (16th century), still the standard reference in Orthodox Judaism. Responsa literature — case-by-case rulings by rabbinic authorities — continues to apply halakha to new questions, from electricity on Shabbat to medical ethics.

Scope

Halakha governs ritual observance (prayer, festivals, dietary laws of kashrut), civil and criminal law, family life, and ethical conduct. Observance varies by movement: Orthodox Judaism regards halakha as binding divine law; Conservative Judaism regards it as binding but historically evolving; Reform Judaism treats it as guidance rather than obligation.
