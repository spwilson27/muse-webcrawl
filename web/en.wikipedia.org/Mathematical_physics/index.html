Mathematical physics

Mathematical physics is the development of mathematical methods for application to problems in physics. The Journal of Mathematical Physics defines the field as "the application of mathematics to problems in physics and the development of mathematical methods suitable for such applications and for the formulation of physical theories". An alternative definition uses the phrase to describe mathematics aimed at studying and solving problems inspired by physics within a mathematically rigorous framework, an usage sometimes associated with areas such as mathematical fluid dynamics or mathematical aspects of quantum field theory.

Scope

The field has expanded enormously since the 19th century. Its techniques include functional analysis, partial differential equations, differential geometry, probability theory, chaos theory, and harmonic analysis, applied to subjects such as classical mechanics, quantum mechanics, statistical mechanics, general relativity, and string theory. A hallmark of the discipline is the interplay between the two fields: physical problems suggest mathematical structures, while mathematical rigor feeds back into new physical insight.

History

The subject grew out of the work of Isaac Newton, who combined geometry with mechanics in the Principia. Joseph Fourier's study of heat conduction introduced Fourier series, rigorous methods for partial differential equations developed through the work of Lagrange, Hamilton, and Jacobi, and the 20th century saw the mathematization of quantum theory through the work of von Neumann, Weyl, Dirac, and others.

Rigorous versus theoretical physics

The term is used in a special sense for researchers who prove results mathematically, in contrast to theoretical physicists who may use mathematics more heuristically. Mathematical physicists may study topics such as the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the equations of a physical model, the spectral properties of quantum Hamiltonians, or precise results about phase transitions.
