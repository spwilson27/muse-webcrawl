Kathak

Kathak is one of the eight major forms of Indian classical dance. It traces its origins to the travelling bards of ancient northern India known as kathakas (storytellers), from the Sanskrit word kathā, "story". Kathak is distinguished by intricate footwork, rapid spins, rhythmic interplay with the tabla, and expressive facial gestures used in storytelling.

History

Kathak developed in the temples and courts of North India. During the Bhakti movement it absorbed devotional themes from the lives of Krishna and other deities, and under the Mughal emperors it moved into the court, acquiring Persian and Central Asian influences in costume, music and technique. In the nineteenth century it flourished in the courts of Lucknow, Jaipur and Benares, which gave rise to the three major gharanas (schools) named after those cities. During the British colonial period the dance fell into disrepute, but it was revived after Indian independence in 1947 as a major performing art.

Performance

A Kathak performance is accompanied by Hindustani music — typically tabla, pakhawaj, harmonium or sarangi, and vocals. Dancers wear strings of small bells (ghungroo) around their ankles, which accentuate the rhythmic footwork. A recital typically moves from slow invocation through pure dance pieces (nritta) to expressive narrative items (nritya), culminating in fast, virtuosic sequences of turns and rhythmic dialogue with the drummer. Noted exponents include Birju Maharaj of the Lucknow gharana.
