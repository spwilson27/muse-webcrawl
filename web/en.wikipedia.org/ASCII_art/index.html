ASCII art

ASCII art is a graphic design technique that uses printable characters from the ASCII standard to compose pictures. Because it requires only text, ASCII art could be produced and displayed on the earliest printers, terminals, and teletype machines, long before bitmapped graphics were common.

History

Typewriter art dates to the late 19th century, and computer ASCII art appeared in the 1960s on line printers, often in university computing centers. It flourished in the 1980s and 1990s on bulletin board systems (BBSs), where groups such as ACiD and iCE produced elaborate ANSI art for BBS welcome screens. ASCII art was also used in Usenet signatures, email, MUDs, and the NFO files accompanying warez releases.

Uses

Images are constructed character by character, exploiting the different visual densities of glyphs (for example, @ is "darker" than .). Automated converters can render digital images into ASCII by mapping pixel brightness to characters. Related forms include emoticons, the kaomoji of Japanese text culture, and "copypasta" text figures. ASCII art persists in programming culture, e.g., in source-code comments, cowsay, and hidden terminal Easter eggs.
