Petra (Arabic: ٱلْبَتْرَاء, Al-Batrāʾ; Ancient Greek: Πέτρα, "Rock") is an ancient city and archaeological site in southern Jordan, famous for its rock-cut architecture and water conduit system. It was the capital city of the Nabataean Kingdom and is one of the world's most famous archaeological sites, designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1985 and named one of the New 7 Wonders of the World in 2007.

History

The Nabataeans, an Arab people, made Petra their capital by the 4th century BC. Controlling the incense and spice trade routes between Arabia, Egypt, and the Mediterranean, the city flourished and may have housed 20,000–30,000 people at its peak in the 1st century AD. Its wealth was built on trade and on sophisticated hydraulic engineering — dams, cisterns, and ceramic pipelines — that made an artificial oasis in the desert.

In AD 106 the emperor Trajan annexed the Nabataean kingdom into the Roman province of Arabia Petraea. Petra continued to prosper under Roman rule but declined as sea-based trade routes rose and after an earthquake in 363 destroyed much of the city. By the early Islamic era it was largely abandoned and known only to local Bedouin.

Rediscovery

Petra was revealed to the Western world by the Swiss explorer Johann Ludwig Burckhardt in 1812, who entered the site disguised as a pilgrim. Systematic excavation and tourism followed in the twentieth century.

Architecture and sites

The city is entered through the Siq, a narrow gorge over a kilometre long flanked by cliffs up to 80 metres high. At its end stands Al-Khazneh ("the Treasury"), a 39-metre-high temple or royal tomb façade carved into the sandstone. Other major monuments include the Roman theatre, the Royal Tombs, the Street of Facades, the Colonnaded Street, and Ad Deir ("the Monastery"), the largest carved façade, reached by a stairway of some 850 steps.

Geology

The multicoloured sandstone of the region, banded in red, orange, and purple, gives Petra its popular epithet of a "rose-red city half as old as time."
