Local extinction

Local extinction, also called extirpation, is the disappearance of a species, population or taxon from a defined geographic area, even though it still survives elsewhere. It contrasts with global extinction, in which the species ceases to exist anywhere.

Causes

Local extinctions can result from habitat loss and fragmentation, overexploitation, introduced predators or competitors, pollution, disease and climate change, as well as natural events such as floods and fires. Small, isolated populations are especially vulnerable to demographic and environmental stochasticity.

Consequences

Extirpation can alter food webs and ecosystem processes in the affected area, and shrinks a species' range, sometimes as a precursor to global endangerment. Range contractions are tracked in regional Red List assessments.

Reintroduction

Because the species survives elsewhere, locally extinct populations can sometimes be restored through reintroduction or may recolonise naturally if habitat and connectivity recover — for example, the return of wolves and beavers to parts of their former ranges.
