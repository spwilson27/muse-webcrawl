United Nations peacekeeping

United Nations peacekeeping is the deployment of international forces, known as Blue Helmets, to help create the conditions for lasting peace in areas of conflict. Established in 1948 with the United Nations Truce Supervision Organization (UNTSO) in the Middle East, peacekeeping has grown into one of the UN's most visible activities: by the 2020s more than one million personnel had served in over 70 operations, with around 70,000–80,000 military and police personnel deployed in a dozen active missions at any time. Peacekeepers and their leaders won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1988.

Principles

Traditional peacekeeping rests on three principles articulated by Dag Hammarskjöld and Lester Pearson: consent of the host state, impartiality, and the non-use of force except in self-defence and defence of the mandate. Since the 1990s mandates have expanded into "multidimensional" missions — protecting civilians, supporting elections, disarmament, demobilisation and reintegration (DDR), security-sector reform, human rights monitoring and state-building — and in some cases, such as the MONUSCO Force Intervention Brigade in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, offensive operations against armed groups.

History

Early missions were unarmed observer missions. The first armed force, UNEF I (1956), was created by Lester Pearson during the Suez Crisis; Pearson later won the Nobel Peace Prize for the idea. The Congo crisis (1960–64) saw the UN's first large, controversial operation, ONUC. After Cold War–era stagnation, the 1990s brought an explosion of missions — Cambodia (UNTAC), El Salvador, Mozambique — but also catastrophic failures: UNAMIR's inability to stop the 1994 Rwandan genocide and the fall of Srebrenica in Bosnia in 1995, which led to the Brahimi Report (2000) and the doctrine of the "responsibility to protect." The 2000s saw very large deployments in Liberia, Sierra Leone, Haiti, Darfur (UNAMID, with the African Union), Lebanon (UNIFIL) and the DR Congo; later missions included South Sudan (UNMISS), Mali (MINUSMA, ended 2023) and the Central African Republic (MINUSCA).

Financing and challenges

Peacekeeping is financed by assessments under a special scale; the United States has been the largest contributor, historically assessed at over a quarter of the budget. Missions face chronic shortfalls of equipment (especially helicopters), slow deployment, unclear mandates, and scandals, including sexual exploitation and abuse by peacekeepers, which prompted a zero-tolerance policy from Secretary-General Kofi Annan onward. Studies generally find that peacekeeping significantly reduces the risk of renewed civil war where it is deployed robustly.
