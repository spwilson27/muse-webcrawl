Duality (optimization)

The duality principle in mathematical optimization asserts that optimization problems may be viewed from either of two perspectives, the primal problem and the dual problem. If the primal is a minimization problem, then the dual is a maximization problem, and any feasible solution to the dual gives a bound on the optimal value of the primal; conversely, if the primal is a maximization problem, the dual is a minimization problem and bounds the primal from the other side.

Linear programming duality

For a linear program in standard form — minimize cTx subject to Ax ≥ b, x ≥ 0 — the dual is: maximize bTy subject to ATy ≤ c, y ≥ 0. The weak duality theorem states that the objective value of any dual feasible solution is a lower bound on that of any primal feasible solution, so the difference between them — the duality gap — measures suboptimality. The celebrated strong duality theorem states that if either problem has an optimal solution, both do, and their objective values coincide: the gap is zero. Dual variables have an economic interpretation as shadow prices — the marginal value of relaxing a constraint — a reading pioneered by Leonid Kantorovich and Tjalling Koopmans in resource allocation.

Complementary slackness

At optimality, complementary slackness conditions hold: a primal constraint that is not tight has a zero dual variable, and a positive dual variable forces its primal constraint to hold with equality. These conditions, generalised as the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions, are central to nonlinear programming.

Lagrangian duality

For general (nonlinear and convex) problems, the dual is constructed through the Lagrangian: constraints are priced into the objective with multipliers, and the dual function is the infimum of the Lagrangian over the primal variables. For convex problems satisfying a constraint qualification such as Slater's condition, strong duality typically holds. Where it fails, a positive duality gap may remain — a phenomenon studied in integer and nonconvex programming. Duality underlies many algorithms, including the simplex method's dual variant, interior-point methods, and the decomposition techniques used in large-scale and machine-learning optimization (for example, the dual form of support vector machines).
