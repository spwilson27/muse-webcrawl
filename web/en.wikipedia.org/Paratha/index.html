Parathas are layered, pan-fried flatbreads eaten across South Asia.

A paratha (pronounced [pəɾɑːʈʰɑː]) is a layered flatbread native to the Indian subcontinent. The word is a blend of parat ("layer") and atta ("flour"), referring to the flaky layers formed by repeatedly folding and rolling dough brushed with ghee or oil. Parathas are usually pan-fried on a tava and served hot for breakfast or as an accompaniment to curries, dals and kebabs.

Preparation

A soft dough of whole wheat flour, water and salt is kneaded and rested. Portions are rolled out, spread with fat, and folded — in the common North Indian method the disc is folded into a triangle or coiled into a spiral — then rolled again and shallow-fried until golden and crisp, with more fat added during frying. The trapped fat separates the dough into distinct, flaky layers.

Variations

Stuffed parathas are among the most popular varieties: aloo paratha is filled with spiced mashed potato, gobi paratha with cauliflower, and others with paneer, radish (mooli), minced meat (keema) or mixed vegetables. The spiral-layered lachha paratha is left unstuffed. In South India the related parotta is made from refined flour (maida) and beaten by hand to produce a soft, ribbon-like layering; its diaspora descendants include Malaysian roti canai and Singaporean roti prata. The deep-fried puri-like versions and the Bengali triangular paratha are further regional forms.

Serving

Parathas are commonly served with butter, yoghurt, pickles, chutneys or raita, and in Punjab traditionally with a glass of lassi. They are a staple of roadside dhabas and of home cooking across India, Pakistan, Nepal, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka, and are widely available in South Asian restaurants worldwide.
