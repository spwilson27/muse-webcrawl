Project Daedalus

Project Daedalus was a study conducted between 1973 and 1978 by the British Interplanetary Society to design a plausible uncrewed interstellar probe. Its goal was to demonstrate that interstellar travel was feasible using technology extrapolated from that of the near future.

Design

The resulting design was a two-stage spacecraft of some 54,000 tonnes, powered by a fusion rocket using electron-beam inertial confinement fusion of deuterium/helium-3 pellets. Helium-3 would be mined from Jupiter's atmosphere by balloon factories. Daedalus would reach about 12% of the speed of light, arriving at Barnard's Star, 5.9 light-years away, after roughly 50 years of flight. It would not decelerate; sub-probes would be released to fly through the target system, and data relayed back by the second stage acting as a communications platform. Self-repairing "wardens" were included to maintain the ship over the decades-long mission.

Legacy

Daedalus remains a landmark in interstellar studies and inspired the later Project Icarus study (2009–2014) and influenced concepts such as Breakthrough Starshot.
