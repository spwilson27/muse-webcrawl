The Bronze Age is a historical period lasting from approximately 3300 to 1200 BCE, characterized by the use of bronze, the presence in some areas of proto-writing, and other early features of urban civilization. It is the second principal period of the three-age system proposed by Christian Jürgensen Thomsen for classifying ancient societies.

Overview

Bronze—an alloy of copper with tin or arsenic—is harder and more durable than stone or pure copper, enabling superior tools and weapons. Because tin is geographically scarce, Bronze Age societies developed long-distance trade networks linking, for example, Cornish and Central Asian tin sources to Mesopotamian and Egyptian workshops. The period saw the rise of cities, states, writing systems (cuneiform, hieroglyphs, Linear B), monumental architecture, and codified law.

Regional timelines

In the Near East the Bronze Age is conventionally divided into Early (3300–2100 BCE), Middle (2100–1550), and Late (1550–1200) phases, encompassing Sumer, Akkad, Babylon, Assyria, the Hittites, and New Kingdom Egypt. China's Bronze Age (Shang and Zhou dynasties) is famed for ritual bronze vessels; the Indus Valley produced fine bronze casting such as the "Dancing Girl" of Mohenjo-daro. Europe's Bronze Age (c. 3200–600 BCE) includes the Minoan, Mycenaean, Únětice, and Urnfield cultures.

Collapse

Around 1200 BCE, the Late Bronze Age collapse destroyed or disrupted most Eastern Mediterranean palace economies—attributed to some combination of invasion (the "Sea Peoples"), drought, earthquake, systems collapse, and the spread of ironworking, which ushered in the Iron Age. Monumental finds such as mineral-rich regions remind us how resource control underpinned these metal economies.
