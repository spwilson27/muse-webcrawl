Dioecy

Holly (Ilex) is dioecious: male and female flowers grow on separate plants.

Dioecy (from Ancient Greek di- "two" and oikos "house") is a characteristic of a species in which individual organisms are either male or female — that is, the male and female reproductive organs occur on separate individuals. A species with this condition is called dioecious. The term is used mainly in botany for plants, but also applies to some invertebrate animals.

In plants

About 6% of flowering plant species are dioecious. In a dioecious plant species, each individual produces only staminate (male) flowers or only pistillate (female) flowers, so pollination requires a male plant near a female plant for fruit and seed production. Familiar examples include holly, kiwifruit, asparagus, ginkgo, willow, poplar, spinach, hops, and cannabis. Dioecy contrasts with monoecy, in which a single plant bears both male and female flowers, and with hermaphroditism, in which individual flowers contain both sexes.

In animals

Separate sexes are the norm in most animals, so the term dioecy is less often applied to them, but it is used for species — such as some corals, sponges, and worms — where colonies or individuals might otherwise be confused with hermaphroditic forms. In zoology the equivalent concept is often described as gonochorism.

Evolution

Dioecy is thought to have evolved repeatedly from hermaphroditic ancestors, often via gynodioecy (populations with females and hermaphrodites) as an intermediate state. Proposed advantages include outcrossing enforced by the separation of sexes, which avoids inbreeding depression, and specialization of each sex for its reproductive role. Disadvantages include the "cost of males" — only half the population can bear seeds — and dependence on pollinators or proximity for fertilization.
