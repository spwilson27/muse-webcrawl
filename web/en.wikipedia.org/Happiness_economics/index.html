Happiness economics

FieldEconomics, psychology
Key conceptsSubjective well-being, life satisfaction, Easterlin paradox

Happiness economics is the study of happiness, well-being, and quality of life using the tools of economics together with survey measures of subjective well-being. It questions the traditional reliance on income and GDP as measures of welfare.

Easterlin paradox

In 1974 Richard Easterlin observed that, although richer people within a country tend to report higher happiness, average reported happiness did not rise over time in the United States despite economic growth, and cross-country income differences did not map simply onto happiness. This "Easterlin paradox" launched the modern field, though later research by Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers has challenged parts of it.

Findings

Research consistently links unemployment, ill health, bereavement, and poor relationships to lower well-being, while income shows diminishing returns. Adaptation and social comparison mean that relative income often matters as much as absolute income.

Policy

Governments including those of Bhutan (Gross National Happiness), the United Kingdom, and New Zealand have experimented with well-being indicators and "well-being budgets". The annual World Happiness Report ranks countries by self-reported life evaluations.
