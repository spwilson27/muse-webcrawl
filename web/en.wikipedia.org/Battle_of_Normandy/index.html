The Battle of Normandy, codenamed Operation Overlord, was the Allied invasion and liberation of German-occupied Western Europe in the summer of 1944. It began on 6 June 1944 — D-Day — with the largest seaborne invasion in history, when some 156,000 American, British, Canadian and other Allied troops landed on five Normandy beaches (Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno and Sword) supported by airborne drops, overwhelming naval gunfire and air supremacy. Over the following three months the Allies broke out of the beachhead, destroyed much of the German army in France at the Falaise Pocket, and liberated Paris on 25 August 1944.

The landings

Years of planning, deception (Operation Fortitude, which convinced Germany the invasion would come at Calais) and logistics preceded D-Day. Nearly 7,000 vessels crossed the Channel; airborne divisions dropped inland to seize bridges and cause confusion. Resistance varied: at Omaha Beach American forces suffered heavy casualties, while other beaches were secured more quickly. By nightfall the Allies held a foothold, though none of the first-day objectives inland — including Caen — had been fully taken.

The campaign

Fighting in Normandy's hedgerow bocage country was slow and costly. The British and Canadians ground down German panzer divisions around Caen; the Americans took Cherbourg and then, in Operation Cobra (late July), broke through in the west. A German counterattack at Mortain failed, and Allied armies encircled the German Seventh Army in the Falaise Pocket in mid-August. Meanwhile Operation Dragoon landed in southern France. By the end of August the Seine had been crossed, Paris liberated, and the battle was over.

Significance

The battle established the Western Front that, together with the Soviet advance in the east, crushed Nazi Germany within a year. Casualties were heavy on all sides — roughly 200,000+ Allied and comparable German losses, plus an estimated 15,000–20,000 French civilian deaths, many from Allied bombing of Norman towns. Normandy's beaches, cemeteries and museums remain major sites of remembrance.
