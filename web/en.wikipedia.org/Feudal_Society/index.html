Feudal Society

Feudal Society (French: La Société féodale) is a book by the French historian Marc Bloch, published in two volumes in 1939 and 1940. It is a landmark of the Annales school of historiography, offering a sweeping comparative analysis of European society between roughly 900 and 1300 — its kinship structures, ties of dependence, modes of thought, and the institutions historians call feudalism.

Content

Bloch began from a puzzle: how the collapse of public authority after the invasions of the 9th and 10th centuries produced a society bound together by personal dependence — vassalage, the fief, and the manor. The first volume, The Growth of Ties of Dependence, surveys the invasions, the environment and ways of life, mental attitudes, and the bonds of kinship and homage. The second, Social Classes and Political Organisation, examines the nobility, chivalry and knighthood, the church, the classes of society, and the re-emergence of states.

Approach

Bloch treated feudalism not as a legal checklist but as a whole social environment, comparing regions from England to Japan and drawing on law, literature, linguistics, and archaeology. He famously sketched its essential features: a subject peasantry; widespread use of the service tenement (the fief) instead of salary; the supremacy of a class of specialised warriors; ties of obedience and protection binding man to man; and, amid this, the survival of other forms of association, family and state.

Influence

Feudal Society remains one of the most read works of medieval history and a model of comparative, problem-oriented history. Later scholars — notably Susan Reynolds in Fiefs and Vassals (1994) — challenged the coherence of "feudalism" itself, but Bloch's social portrait, including his caveats about regional variation, still frames the debate. Bloch, a co-founder of the Annales journal, joined the French Resistance and was executed by the Gestapo in June 1944.
