Chuckwagon

A chuckwagon (also spelled chuck wagon) is a type of field kitchen covered wagon historically used for the storage and transportation of food and cooking equipment on cattle drives and in lumber camps in the United States and Canada. Such wagons formed part of a wagon train of settlers or fed traveling workers such as cowboys or lumberjacks.

In modern times, chuckwagons are used at certain outdoor events, such as chuckwagon races and cooking competitions, and are featured in competitive events like the Calgary Stampede's chuckwagon races.

History

The chuckwagon is credited to Texas rancher Charles Goodnight, who in 1866 modified a sturdy army surplus wagon — an old Studebaker — for use on the cattle trails. Goodnight added a slanted "chuck box" at the rear of the wagon with compartments for foodstuffs, a hinged lid that folded down into a work table, and storage for bedrolls, tools, and water barrels. The term "chuck" was frontier slang for food.

The wagon's driver, usually called "Cookie," was typically the second most important man on a cattle drive after the trail boss. Cookie prepared meals, maintained the wagon, and often served as barber, banker, and medic for the crew.

Design

A traditional chuckwagon is a four-wheeled wagon drawn by horses or mules, fitted with a canvas bonnet stretched over wooden bows. The chuck box at the rear held coffee, beans, dried fruit, salt pork, flour, and spices in individual cubbyholes. Beneath the wagon hung a cowhide sling, the "possum belly," used to carry firewood and dried cow chips for fuel.

Chuckwagon cooking

Chuckwagon cooking became a competitive event in the 20th century. Cooks prepare traditional trail meals — typically steak, beans, potatoes, sourdough biscuits, and a cobbler or fried pie — cooked in Dutch ovens over an open fire, judged on authenticity and taste.
