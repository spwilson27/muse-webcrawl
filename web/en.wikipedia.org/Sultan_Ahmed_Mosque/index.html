Sultan Ahmed Mosque

The Sultan Ahmed Mosque (Turkish: Sultan Ahmet Camii), popularly known as the Blue Mosque for the blue İznik tiles that adorn its interior, is an Ottoman imperial mosque located in Istanbul, Turkey. Built between 1609 and 1616 during the reign of Sultan Ahmed I, it remains a functioning mosque and one of the most famous landmarks of the city.

History

Ahmed I commissioned the mosque on the site of the former Great Palace of Constantinople, facing the Hagia Sophia across Sultanahmet Square. Its architect, Sedefkâr Mehmed Ağa, a pupil of the great Mimar Sinan, synthesised Byzantine and classical Ottoman elements. The choice of six minarets caused controversy, since the mosque of the Kaaba in Mecca also had six; the sultan resolved the matter by funding a seventh minaret for Mecca.

Architecture

The mosque is arranged around a large courtyard of nearly the same size as the prayer hall. The main dome, about 23.5 metres in diameter, is flanked by four semi-domes and supported by four massive piers. More than 20,000 handmade İznik tiles in blue, green and turquoise, with tulip, carnation and cypress designs, line the interior galleries, giving the mosque its popular name. Over 200 stained-glass windows admit light, and the floors are covered with carpets donated by the faithful.

The complex

The mosque formed the centre of a külliye (complex) including a madrasa, a hospital, a primary school, a market, baths, a public kitchen and the tomb of Ahmed I. Today it stands within the UNESCO World Heritage listing of the Historic Areas of Istanbul, directly across from the Hagia Sophia.

Visits

The mosque remains open for worship five times a day and receives large numbers of visitors outside prayer times. Pope Benedict XVI visited in 2006, the second papal visit to a mosque in history.
