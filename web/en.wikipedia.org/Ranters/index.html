Ranters

The Ranters were one of a number of dissenting groups that arose during the English Commonwealth (1649–1660), a period of religious and political upheaval following the English Civil War. Contemporary pamphleteers accused them of antinomianism — the belief that Christians freed by grace are not bound by moral law — together with pantheism, swearing, drunkenness and sexual licence.

Beliefs

Figures associated with the Ranters, such as Abiezer Coppe, Laurence Clarkson and Jacob Bauthumley, expressed ideas that orthodox contemporaries found shocking: that God is present in all creatures and things, that sin exists only in the imagination, and that heaven and hell are states experienced in this life. Clarkson's A Single Eye (1650) argued that all actions are indifferent to the truly illuminated believer.

Suppression and historiography

Parliament passed the Blasphemy Act of 1650 aimed at Ranter doctrines, and leading figures were arrested; Coppe recanted and Clarkson later became a Muggletonian. Historians debate how far the Ranters were an organised movement at all. The historian J. C. Davis argued that "the Ranters" were largely a construction of hostile pamphleteers and a nervous press, while others, following Christopher Hill, see a genuine radical milieu whose ideas anticipated later religious liberalism.
