The Battle of Verdun was fought from 21 February to 18 December 1916 on the Western Front in north-eastern France during the First World War. It was the longest battle of the war and one of the most costly in history. The German Chief of Staff, Erich von Falkenhayn, chose the fortified salient of Verdun — a place of immense symbolic importance to France — intending, in his famous phrase, to "bleed France white" through attrition. Instead the battle bled both armies nearly equally and became the defining symbol of the war's futility and of French endurance.

The battle

The German attack opened with an unprecedented artillery bombardment by some 1,200 guns. Fort Douaumont, the keystone of the Verdun defences, fell almost undefended on 25 February, causing a crisis. General Philippe Pétain took command of the defence, organising the rotation of troops (the noria system) and a supply lifeline along the "Voie Sacrée" road from Bar-le-Duc, along which trucks passed day and night. Fort Vaux was besieged and captured in June after heroic resistance; German advances were finally halted in July, partly because the Allied offensive on the Somme drew off German forces. In the autumn, French counterattacks under Robert Nivelle and Charles Mangin recaptured Douaumont (October) and Vaux (November) using creeping barrages.

Legacy

Around 300 days of fighting over a few square miles left roughly 700,000 or more casualties and a lunar landscape of craters that remains scarred today; several villages destroyed in the battle were never rebuilt. "On ne passe pas" — "They shall not pass" — became the battle's emblematic slogan. Verdun's ossuary at Douaumont holds the remains of some 130,000 unidentified soldiers, French and German. In 1984 Chancellor Helmut Kohl and President François Mitterrand famously clasped hands there, symbolising Franco-German reconciliation.
