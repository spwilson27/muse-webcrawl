Coral bleaching

Coral bleaching is the loss of colour in stony corals when stressed corals expel the photosynthetic dinoflagellate algae (zooxanthellae, of the family Symbiodiniaceae) that live symbiotically within their tissues, or when the algae lose their pigments. Deprived of the algae that supply most of their food and their colour, the corals turn white, exposing their calcium carbonate skeletons. Bleached corals are not yet dead, but prolonged bleaching starves them and can cause mass mortality.

Causes

The principal trigger is unusually warm sea water: sustained temperatures even 1–2 °C above the summer maximum can initiate bleaching. Other stressors include intense solar radiation, unusually cold water, exposure at extreme low tides, freshwater dilution, sedimentation and pollution. Warming-driven bleaching results from damage to the algae's photosynthetic machinery, which produces reactive oxygen species that harm host and symbiont alike.

Mass bleaching events

Global mass-bleaching events driven by El Niño and background ocean warming were recorded in 1998, 2010 and 2014–2017, the last affecting reefs worldwide for three consecutive years. Australia's Great Barrier Reef suffered severe back-to-back bleaching in 2016 and 2017 and further events in 2020, 2022 and 2024, with the loss of large fractions of shallow-water coral cover in the worst-affected regions.

Recovery and adaptation

If stress is brief, corals can regain symbionts and recover over weeks to months; survivors may repopulate with more heat-tolerant symbiont types. Repeated bleaching, however, outpaces recovery, and reefs shift toward algal-dominated states. Research responses include identifying heat-resilient coral genotypes, assisted breeding and larval reseeding, reducing local stressors such as overfishing and nutrient runoff, and — as the fundamental remedy — limiting global warming, since projections show most warm-water reefs bleaching annually by mid-century under high-emission scenarios.
