Atmospheric chemistry


Major chemical processes of the atmosphere

Atmospheric chemistry is a branch of atmospheric science and environmental chemistry that studies the chemical composition of the Earth's atmosphere, the reactions that transform it, and the ways those processes affect air quality, climate, and ecosystems. Although the atmosphere is dominated by nitrogen and oxygen, trace gases present at parts per million or billion — such as ozone, methane, nitrogen oxides and hydroxyl radicals — drive most of its chemistry.

Key processes

Sunlight powers photochemistry: ultraviolet radiation splits molecules into reactive fragments called radicals. The hydroxyl radical (OH) is often called the "detergent of the atmosphere" because it oxidises most pollutants and greenhouse gases, determining their lifetimes. In the stratosphere, ozone absorbs ultraviolet radiation, and its destruction by chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) produced the Antarctic ozone hole — a problem addressed by the Montreal Protocol of 1987. In the troposphere, reactions between nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds in sunlight generate ground-level ozone and photochemical smog, harmful to health and crops.

Methods and applications

Atmospheric chemists combine laboratory kinetics studies, field measurements from aircraft, balloons and satellites, and computer models that couple chemistry with meteorology. The field underpins air-quality regulation, assessments of climate change produced by bodies such as the IPCC, and understanding of acid rain, aerosols (fine particles), and the oxidation capacity of the atmosphere. Acid rain, driven by sulphur and nitrogen emissions forming sulphuric and nitric acids, damaged lakes and forests in the late 20th century before emissions controls reduced it in many regions.
