Time-sharing

In computing, time-sharing is the concurrent use of a computer by multiple users, in which the operating system rapidly switches the processor among many interactive jobs, giving each user the illusion of having a machine to themselves. The concept emerged in the late 1950s and became the dominant mode of computing in the 1960s and 1970s, delivered through terminals connected to a central mainframe or minicomputer.

History

Because early computers were extremely expensive, time-sharing was devised to let many people use one machine efficiently: while one user thought or typed, the processor served others. Christopher Strachey filed a patent for "time sharing" in 1959, and John McCarthy championed the idea at MIT. The first successful demonstration was MIT's Compatible Time-Sharing System (CTSS) in 1961. It was followed by systems such as Dartmouth's DTSS (1964, which spread BASIC), MIT's Multics project, and DEC's TOPS-10 and RSTS/E for the PDP series. Fernando Corbató's group at MIT won the Turing Award recognition for this pioneering work.

How it works

The scheduler divides processor time into short slices (quanta), often tens of milliseconds, and rotates among ready processes. Interactive response feels immediate because human typing and reading are slow relative to the machine. Memory protection and preemptive scheduling keep one user's program from interfering with another's, and accounting systems charged users for CPU seconds, memory and connect time — the original "utility computing" business model of service bureaus such as Tymshare, CompuServe and GEIS.

Decline and legacy

The arrival of cheap personal computers in the late 1970s and 1980s ended the classic time-sharing era, as users gained dedicated machines. However, its ideas pervade modern computing: multitasking operating systems, virtual machines, remote login, and cloud computing — where many tenants share large server farms — are direct descendants of time-sharing, as is the very notion of interactive computing itself.
