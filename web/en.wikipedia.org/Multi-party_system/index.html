Multi-party system


Type: Party system
Contrasted with: Two-party system, one-party system
Common under: Proportional representation

A multi-party system is a political system in which more than two political parties have a realistic prospect of winning seats and participating in government, typically forming coalition governments. It contrasts with two-party systems, where two major parties dominate, and one-party systems, where only one party may govern.

Characteristics

Multi-party systems are strongly associated with proportional representation electoral systems, as described by Duverger's law, which links plurality voting to two-party outcomes and proportional voting to multiple parties. Because no single party often wins an outright majority, governments are usually coalitions, and smaller parties can wield influence as coalition partners or kingmakers.

Advantages and disadvantages

Supporters argue multi-party systems represent voters' diverse views more faithfully and encourage consensus. Critics point to governmental instability, lengthy coalition negotiations, and diluted accountability, since voters cannot easily assign credit or blame. Political scientist Giovanni Sartori distinguished moderate pluralism from polarized pluralism, where numerous anti-system parties strain governance.

Examples

Countries commonly described as multi-party include Germany, the Netherlands, Israel, India, and Brazil. The effective number of parties is a statistical measure used to compare systems.
