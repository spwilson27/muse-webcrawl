Porfirio Díaz

Born15 September 1830, Oaxaca, Mexico
Died2 July 1915 (aged 84), Paris, France
Presidency1876–1880; 1884–1911

José de la Cruz Porfirio Díaz Mori (15 September 1830 – 2 July 1915) was a Mexican general and politician who served seven terms as President of Mexico, dominating the country for 35 years in the period known as the Porfiriato. A hero of the Reform War and the Second French Intervention — he won fame at the Battle of Puebla in 1862 — Díaz seized power in 1876 under the Plan de Tuxtepec, ostensibly in the name of no re-election, and then held the presidency almost continuously until the Mexican Revolution forced him into exile in 1911.

The Porfiriato

Díaz's rule rested on the slogan "order and progress" and the policy of pan o palo — "bread or the club" — for opponents. Guided by positivist advisers known as the científicos, his governments encouraged foreign investment, built some 19,000 kilometres of railway, expanded mining and export agriculture, and stabilised Mexico's finances. The costs fell heavily on the rural poor: communal village lands were surveyed and sold, the Yaqui and Maya peoples were violently suppressed, and real wages stagnated while a small elite grew rich.

Fall and exile

In a 1908 interview Díaz declared Mexico ready for democracy and announced he would retire, prompting Francisco I. Madero to challenge him in the 1910 election. Díaz jailed Madero and declared himself re-elected, triggering the Mexican Revolution. In May 1911, after federal defeats, Díaz resigned and sailed for Europe, remarking that Madero had "unleashed a tiger." He died in Paris in 1915 and is buried at Montparnasse Cemetery. His legacy remains contested in Mexico: moderniser to some, dictator whose inequalities made revolution inevitable to others.
