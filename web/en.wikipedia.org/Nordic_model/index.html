The Nordic model comprises the economic and social policies as well as typical cultural practices common to the Nordic countries (Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway and Sweden). It combines a free-market capitalist economy and open trade with a comprehensive welfare state, extensive public services, strong labour unions and collective bargaining, and high levels of taxation funding universal benefits.

Overview

Characteristic features include publicly funded universal healthcare and education (including free higher education), generous parental leave and child benefits, public pensions, low levels of income inequality by international comparison, and labour markets combining flexibility for employers with security for workers — in Denmark described as "flexicurity". Wage setting is largely handled by social partners rather than statutory minimum wages in most of the countries.

History

The model developed after the Second World War, building on earlier compromises between labour and capital such as Sweden's Saltsjöbaden Agreement (1938) and Denmark's September Compromise (1899). Post-war governments, frequently led by social democratic parties, expanded universal welfare while maintaining export-oriented private industry. From the 1980s the countries undertook market-oriented reforms — deregulation, pension reform and central bank independence — while retaining high social spending.

Criticism and challenges

Critics point to high tax burdens, ageing populations that strain pension and care systems, housing shortages in capitals, and difficulties integrating immigrants into labour markets. Supporters note the countries' persistent high rankings in productivity, innovation, social mobility and life satisfaction.
