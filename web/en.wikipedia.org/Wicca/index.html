Wicca is a modern Pagan religion that emerged in England in the first half of the 20th century and was brought to public attention in the 1950s by Gerald Gardner, who claimed it preserved an ancient witchcraft tradition. Scholars generally regard it as a new religious movement drawing on Western esotericism, ceremonial magic, folklore and romanticised pre-Christian religion.

Beliefs and practice

Most Wiccans are duotheistic, honouring a Goddess and a God (often the Horned God), and celebrate an annual cycle of eight Sabbats known as the Wheel of the Year, including Samhain, Yule, Imbolc and Beltane, alongside lunar Esbats. Rituals are often conducted in a cast circle, using tools such as the athame (ritual knife), wand, chalice and pentacle. Many Wiccans practise magic and observe the moral counsel of the Wiccan Rede and the threefold law of return.

Traditions

Wicca is decentralised and diverse. "British Traditional Wicca" (Gardnerian, Alexandrian and related lines) requires initiation into a coven and keeps its rites oathbound, while eclectic and solitary practitioners — the majority today — adapt the religion freely. Raymond Buckland introduced Gardnerian Wicca to the United States in the 1960s, and the feminist Dianic tradition arose in the 1970s.

Demographics

Estimates of adherents worldwide range from hundreds of thousands to over a million, concentrated in the United States, the United Kingdom and other English-speaking countries. Wicca has gained legal recognition as a religion in several jurisdictions, including for military chaplaincy purposes in the U.S.
