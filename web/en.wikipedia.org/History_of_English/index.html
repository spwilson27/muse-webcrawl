History of English

The history of English traces the development of the English language from its Germanic origins in the fifth century to the present day. English is conventionally divided into three main stages: Old English (c. 450–1100), Middle English (c. 1100–1500), and Modern English (from c. 1500), the last subdivided into Early Modern and Late Modern English.

Origins

English descends from the West Germanic dialects spoken by the Angles, Saxons and Jutes who settled in Britain from the mid-fifth century. Its closest relatives are Frisian and Low German. The language they brought absorbed some influence from Brittonic Celtic (mostly in place names) and from Latin, both through contact with Roman Britain and later through the Church.

Old English

Old English was a fully inflected language with grammatical gender, a complex case system and a largely Germanic vocabulary. Norse invasions from the late eighth century brought extensive Scandinavian vocabulary and helped simplify inflections in the north. The best-known literary work is the epic poem Beowulf; King Alfred the Great (r. 871–899) promoted learning and translation into English.

Middle English

The Norman Conquest of 1066 brought French as the language of the court and administration for some three centuries, profoundly reshaping English vocabulary — thousands of French words entered the language. Inflectional endings continued to erode, and word order became more fixed. By the fourteenth century English had regained official use; Geoffrey Chaucer's Canterbury Tales exemplifies the London dialect that underlies the standard language.

Early Modern English

The Great Vowel Shift (c. 1400–1700) transformed the long vowels of English, creating much of the mismatch between modern spelling and pronunciation. The printing press, introduced to England by William Caxton in 1476, helped standardise spelling. The Renaissance brought a flood of Latin and Greek borrowings, while the works of Shakespeare and the King James Bible (1611) fixed much idiom. Grammarians and dictionary-makers, culminating in Samuel Johnson's Dictionary (1755), codified usage.

Late Modern and global English

The Industrial Revolution and the British Empire spread English worldwide and generated vast new vocabularies for science and technology. English became the dominant global language in the twentieth century, and distinct national and regional varieties — American, Indian, Nigerian, Australian and many others — continue to develop. Today English has roughly 1.5 billion learners and speakers, with non-native speakers far outnumbering native ones.
