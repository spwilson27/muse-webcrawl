Solon

Solon (Greek: Σόλων; c. 630 – c. 560 BC) was an archaic Greek statesman, lawmaker, political philosopher and poet. He is credited with having laid the foundations for Athenian democracy and is counted among the Seven Sages of Greece. His reforms to the constitution and economy of Athens, enacted in 594 BC, averted a descent into civil war and reshaped Athenian society.

Background

In the early 6th century BC, Athens was in crisis. Political power was monopolised by aristocratic families, the Eupatridae, while many small farmers had fallen into debt bondage — some had been sold into slavery for debt. Appointed archon with special powers in 594 BC, Solon was given authority to enact reforms both sides could accept.

Reforms

Solon's most famous measure was the seisachtheia, the "shaking off of burdens": he cancelled existing debts, freed those enslaved for debt, and forbade loans secured on the person. Politically, he divided citizens into four classes based on agricultural wealth — pentacosiomedimnoi, hippeis, zeugitai and thetes — and opened offices to the upper classes by property rather than birth alone. He established (or re-established) the Council of Four Hundred and gave the popular assembly a role in electing officials and hearing appeals; the Heliaia, a popular court, allowed citizens to challenge magistrates' decisions. He also reformed weights, measures and coinage and encouraged immigration of skilled craftsmen to Athens.

Poetry and travels

Solon justified his reforms in elegiac verse, fragments of which survive; he presented himself as a mediator who had given neither side an unjust victory. According to Herodotus, after his reforms he left Athens for ten years of travel, visiting Egypt and Lydia, where he famously warned King Croesus that no man should be counted happy until he is dead.

Legacy

Later Athenian democrats traced their constitution back to Solon, and his name became a byword for the wise lawgiver. In English usage, "solon" came to denote a legislator or member of a legislature.
