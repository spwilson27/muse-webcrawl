Star polygon

Regular star polygons in Schläfli notation.

In geometry, a star polygon is a type of non-convex polygon. A regular star polygon is formed by connecting every q-th vertex of a regular p-sided polygon to the next, continuing until returning to the start; it is denoted by the Schläfli symbol {p/q}, where p and q are coprime. The most familiar example is the pentagram, {5/2}.

Examples

Regular star polygons include the pentagram {5/2}, two heptagrams {7/2} and {7/3}, the octagram {8/3}, and further forms for nonagons and decagons. When p and q are not coprime, the figure is a compound of multiple polygons: the hexagram {6/2} is a compound of two triangles, familiar as the Star of David.

History

Star polygons have been used decoratively since antiquity—the pentagram was a symbol of the Pythagoreans—and were systematically studied by Johannes Kepler, who in Harmonices Mundi (1619) extended the concept of polyhedra to star polyhedra such as the small stellated dodecahedron. Thomas Bradwardine and Charles de Bovelles also contributed early treatments.

Applications

Star shapes appear in flags, emblems, architecture and art, and geometrically in the ground plans of star forts. Isotoxal star polygons and stellations extend the idea into tilings and polyhedra.
