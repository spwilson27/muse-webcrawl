Halo (optical phenomenon)

A halo is an optical phenomenon produced by light (typically from the Sun or Moon) interacting with ice crystals suspended in the atmosphere. Halos can take many forms, ranging from coloured or white rings to arcs and spots in the sky. They are most often seen in cold weather when diamond dust is present, or when cirrostratus clouds cover the sky.

The 22° halo

The most common halo is the 22° halo, a ring of angular radius about 22° around the Sun or Moon. It forms when light passes through randomly oriented hexagonal ice crystals; refraction through the 60° prism angle of the crystals, with a minimum deviation of about 22°, concentrates light at that angle. The inner edge is sharp and slightly reddish, the outer edge diffuse and bluish. Halos around the Moon are usually perceived as white.

Other forms

Related phenomena include the 46° halo (from refraction through 90° prism faces); sun dogs (parhelia), bright spots at the same altitude as the Sun on either side; circumzenithal and circumhorizontal arcs; tangent arcs; the parhelic circle; and light pillars. Particular crystal shapes and orientations produce particular displays, making complex halo displays a rich subject for atmospheric optics.

Weather lore

Because cirrostratus often precedes a warm front, a halo around the Sun or Moon is a traditional sign of approaching rain — "ring around the moon, rain soon".
