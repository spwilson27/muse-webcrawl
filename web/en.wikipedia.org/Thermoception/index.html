Thermoception

Thermoception, or thermoreception, is the sensation and perception of temperature, or more accurately, temperature differences inferred from heat flux. It deals with a series of events and processes required for an organism to receive a temperature stimulus, convert it to a molecular signal, and recognise and characterise the signal in order to trigger an appropriate response. Thermoception is one of the somatosensory modalities.

In mammals, temperature receptors are located mainly in the skin, the cornea of the eye, and the urinary bladder. Separate warm and cold receptors exist: cold receptors signal via Aδ and C fibres and fire most strongly around 25–30 °C, while warm receptors fire as temperature rises toward about 45 °C, above which pain receptors (nociceptors) take over and the sensation becomes one of heat pain.

Mechanism

At the molecular level, temperature is transduced by transient receptor potential (TRP) channels. TRPV1 channels open at temperatures above about 43 °C and are also activated by capsaicin, the compound that makes chilli peppers feel hot; TRPV3 and TRPV4 respond to warmth; TRPM8 opens at cool temperatures below about 26 °C and is activated by menthol, producing the sensation of coolness; TRPA1 has been implicated in noxious cold. Signals travel via the spinothalamic tract to the thalamus and somatosensory cortex, while information about core body temperature reaches the hypothalamus, which coordinates autonomic responses such as sweating, shivering and changes in blood flow.

In other animals

Some animals possess specialised infrared sensing organs. Pit vipers, pythons and boas have pit organs that detect infrared radiation from warm prey; vampire bats have heat sensors in their noses that help them locate blood vessels. Certain insects, such as some butterflies and blood-feeding bugs, also detect thermal cues from hosts.
