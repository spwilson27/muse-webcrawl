Sugar glider

The sugar glider (Petaurus breviceps) is a small, omnivorous, arboreal, and nocturnal gliding possum. The common name refers to its predilection for sugary foods such as sap and nectar and its ability to glide through the air, much like a flying squirrel. They have very similar habits and appearance to the flying squirrel, despite not being closely related — an example of convergent evolution.

Description

The sugar glider has a squirrel-like body with a long, partially prehensile tail. Males are slightly larger than females, with a head-and-body length of about 24 to 30 cm (9 to 12 in) and a weight of 100 to 160 grams. A membrane called the patagium extends from the fifth finger to the first toe, allowing the animal to glide up to 45 metres (150 ft) between trees. The fur is thick, soft, and usually blue-grey, with a black stripe running along the back.

Distribution and habitat

Sugar gliders are native throughout the northern and eastern parts of mainland Australia, and are present in the surrounding islands of Tasmania, Papua New Guinea, and Indonesia. They inhabit woodlands and forests, nesting in leaf-lined hollows in trees in social groups of up to seven adults and their young.

Diet and behaviour

Sugar gliders are omnivorous, feeding on the sweet sap of species of eucalyptus and acacia, as well as nectar, pollen, and insects. In winter, when food is scarce, they may enter daily torpor to conserve energy. They are highly social and communicate through vocalisations, chemical odours, and scent marking.

As pets

Sugar gliders are popular exotic pets in many countries, including the United States, though their keeping is restricted or prohibited in some jurisdictions, including their native Australia. Because they are social animals, keeping a single sugar glider can cause stress and behavioural problems.

Conservation

The sugar glider is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN, owing to its wide distribution and large population. However, habitat loss from land clearing and bushfires poses local threats.
