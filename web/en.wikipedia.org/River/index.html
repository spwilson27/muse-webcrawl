River

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A river is a natural flowing watercourse, usually freshwater, flowing towards an ocean, sea, lake, or another river. In some cases a river flows into the ground or dries up entirely. A river begins at a source (or more often several sources), follows a path called a course, and ends at a mouth. The water in a river is confined to a channel, made up of a stream bed between banks. Rivers are an important part of the water cycle and have shaped landscapes and human civilization throughout history.

Flow and classification

The study of rivers and inland waters generally is called surface hydrology; the study of the flow patterns and landforms created by rivers is fluvial geomorphology. Rivers are classified by discharge, by whether they flow all year (perennial) or only seasonally (intermittent or ephemeral), and by their pattern: straight, meandering, braided, or anastomosing. The Strahler stream order ranks tributaries hierarchically from small headwater streams to the main stem.

Formation and landscape

Rivers erode their valleys, transport sediment, and deposit it in floodplains, deltas, and alluvial fans. Characteristic landforms include meanders, oxbow lakes, natural levees, waterfalls, and V-shaped valleys in upland reaches. Over geological time rivers carve canyons such as the Grand Canyon.

Human use

The earliest civilizations arose along rivers — the Nile, Tigris and Euphrates, Indus, and Yellow River — which supplied irrigation water and fertile alluvium. Rivers remain vital for drinking water, agriculture, transport, fisheries, hydropower, and industry, and their management raises issues of flooding, damming, pollution, and transboundary water rights.
