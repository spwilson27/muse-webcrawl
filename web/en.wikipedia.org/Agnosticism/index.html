Agnosticism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Agnosticism is the view that the existence of God, of the divine, or of the supernatural is unknown or unknowable. It can also mean doubting or being noncommittal on a topic more broadly. The term was coined by the English biologist Thomas Henry Huxley in 1869, who formed it from the Greek a- (without) and gnosis (knowledge), contrasting it with the Gnostics' claims to esoteric knowledge.

Definitions

Agnosticism is commonly distinguished from atheism (lack of belief in gods) and theism (belief in gods): an agnostic may be an "agnostic atheist" who does not believe but does not claim to know, or an "agnostic theist" who believes while acknowledging uncertainty. Philosophers also distinguish strong (hard) agnosticism — the view that the existence of gods is inherently unknowable — from weak (soft) agnosticism, which holds only that it is currently unknown.

History

Ideas resembling agnosticism appear in ancient thought: Protagoras expressed uncertainty about the gods, and the Nasadiya Sukta of the Rigveda questions whether even the gods know the origin of the universe. In modern philosophy, David Hume's scepticism and Immanuel Kant's limits on metaphysical knowledge influenced later agnostic thought. Huxley used the term to describe his stance in debates with the Metaphysical Society, insisting on following reason as far as it would take him and not pretending conclusions were certain when they were not demonstrable.

Demographics

Surveys in many countries find a substantial share of respondents identifying as agnostic, "not sure", or religiously unaffiliated. Agnosticism is also a common position among scientists, though views vary widely by field and country.
