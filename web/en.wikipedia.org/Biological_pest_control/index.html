Biological pest control

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Biological control or biocontrol is a method of controlling pests such as insects, mites, weeds and plant diseases using other organisms. It relies on predation, parasitism, herbivory or other natural mechanisms, and is an important component of integrated pest management (IPM) programmes. Control agents include predators such as ladybird beetles, parasitoid wasps, nematodes, and microorganisms including bacteria such as Bacillus thuringiensis, viruses and fungi.

Types

Three strategies are usually distinguished. In classical biological control (importation), a pest's natural enemy is introduced from its region of origin to control an invasive pest, as when the vedalia beetle was imported from Australia to California in the 1880s and saved the citrus industry from cottony cushion scale. Augmentation involves mass-rearing and releasing natural enemies to supplement existing populations, common in greenhouse production. Conservation modifies the environment — for example by planting refuges or reducing broad-spectrum insecticides — to favour enemies already present.

Advantages and risks

Successful biocontrol can be self-sustaining, inexpensive and free of chemical residues, and pests rarely evolve resistance to living enemies. However, introduced agents can themselves become problematic: the cane toad, released in Australia to control beetle pests, became a damaging invader, and some parasitoids attack non-target native species. Modern programmes therefore require host-specificity testing and regulatory approval before release.

Applications

Biological control is used in agriculture, horticulture, forestry and the control of invasive weeds in natural ecosystems. Examples include the use of Trichogramma wasps against moth eggs, entomopathogenic nematodes against soil pests, and mycoherbicides against weeds.
