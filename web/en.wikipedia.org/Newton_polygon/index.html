In mathematics, the Newton polygon is a tool for understanding the behaviour of polynomials over local fields, or more generally over any field equipped with a valuation. Given a polynomial with coefficients in such a field, one plots the points (i, v(ai)) formed by each exponent i and the valuation of its coefficient, and takes the lower convex hull of these points; the resulting polygonal line is the Newton polygon.

The fundamental theorem of the method states that the slopes of the polygon's segments give the valuations of the polynomial's roots in an algebraic closure, and the horizontal length of each segment gives the number of roots with that valuation. Segments of length one correspond to roots already lying in the field.

Applications

Newton polygons are central in p-adic analysis and algebraic number theory: they determine how primes ramify in extensions generated by polynomial roots, and they underlie the Newton–Puiseux theorem on series expansions of algebraic functions. A classical application is an irreducibility criterion related to Dumas's criterion, which generalises Eisenstein's criterion. In genus computations for curves over local fields and in the theory of Newton strata in arithmetic geometry, variants of the construction remain fundamental.

Newton described the analogous parallelogram construction in correspondence of 1676 and in the Enumeratio; the modern formulation was developed by later authors including Victor Puiseux.
