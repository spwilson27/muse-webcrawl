AMBER Alert

An AMBER Alert (also stylized as Amber Alert) is a child abduction emergency alert system used in the United States and adopted in various forms by other countries. The name stands for America's Missing: Broadcast Emergency Response and is also a legacy of Amber Hagerman, a nine-year-old girl who was abducted and murdered in Arlington, Texas, in 1996.

History

Following Amber Hagerman's murder, Dallas–Fort Worth broadcasters teamed with local police in 1996–1997 to develop an early warning system for abducted children. The model spread nationwide; a federal AMBER Alert program was established under the PROTECT Act of 2003, and the system was later integrated with the Wireless Emergency Alerts system so that messages are pushed to mobile phones.

Activation criteria

The U.S. Department of Justice recommends that alerts be issued only when law enforcement confirms an abduction, the child is at risk of serious injury or death, there is enough descriptive information about the victim and abductor, and the child is 17 years old or younger. The restrictions are intended to avoid desensitizing the public with overuse.

Effectiveness and criticism

Officials credit AMBER Alerts with contributing to the recovery of hundreds of children. Some researchers and criminologists have questioned how often the alerts are decisive, and have criticized "alert fatigue" when messages are sent over wide areas at night.
