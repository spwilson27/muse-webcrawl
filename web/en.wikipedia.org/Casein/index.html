Casein (from Latin caseus, "cheese") is a family of related phosphoproteins (αs1, αs2, β, and κ) that are commonly found in mammalian milk, comprising about 80 per cent of the proteins in cow's milk and between 20 and 60 per cent of the proteins in human milk. Cheese consists mainly of casein with milk fat; casein is also a major component of curd and has many industrial uses beyond food.

Structure

Caseins are hydrophobic, loosely structured (often described as intrinsically disordered) proteins with phosphorylated serine clusters that bind calcium phosphate. In milk they aggregate into colloidal particles called casein micelles, roughly 100–300 nm in diameter, stabilized at their surface by κ-casein. This micellar form keeps the calcium-rich protein suspended and digestible; digestion of casein is slow, releasing amino acids gradually, a property marketed in sports nutrition as "anti-catabolic".

Cheesemaking and coagulation

Casein precipitates out of milk at pH 4.6 (acid casein). In cheesemaking, the enzyme chymosin (in rennet) cleaves κ-casein, destabilizing the micelles so they aggregate into a firm curd. Variants of β-casein — A1 and A2 — differing by a single amino acid, have received attention because digestion of A1 β-casein can release the opioid peptide β-casomorphin-7; claims of health differences between A1 and A2 milk remain contested.

Uses

Beyond cheese, casein and its salts (caseinates) are used as food emulsifiers, in protein supplements, infant formula and coffee whiteners. Historically casein was a major industrial material: it formed the basis of one of the earliest plastics (galalith, "milk stone"), casein glues, casein paint (used since ancient Egyptian times and by 20th-century artists), fibre (Lanital) and coatings for paper. Casein allergies are among the most common food allergies in infants, though many children outgrow them.
