An iwan (Persian: ایوان, eyvān; Arabic: إيوان, īwān) is a rectangular hall or space, usually vaulted, walled on three sides, with one end entirely open. The formal gateway to the iwan is called a pishtaq, a Persian term for a portal protruding from the facade of a building, usually decorated with calligraphy bands, glazed tilework, and geometric designs. Iwans are among the most characteristic features of Persian and Islamic architecture.

History

The iwan is generally thought to have originated in the architecture of the Parthian Empire (3rd century BCE–3rd century CE) and was fully developed under the Sasanian Empire. The great barrel-vaulted iwan at Ctesiphon, known as the Taq Kasra, remains one of the largest single-span vaults of unreinforced brickwork in the world and is the most famous surviving Sasanian example.

With the spread of Islam, the iwan was incorporated into mosque, madrasa, palace, and caravanserai design across Iran, Central Asia, Mesopotamia, and eventually Mughal India. The classic four-iwan plan, in which four iwans face onto a central courtyard, became the standard layout for mosques and madrasas in Persia from the Seljuk period onward, exemplified by the Jameh Mosque of Isfahan.

Design and function

An iwan is typically a vaulted space that opens onto a courtyard and serves as a shaded, semi-public transitional zone between the courtyard and the rooms behind it. In mosques, the largest iwan usually faces Mecca and leads to the prayer hall or a domed sanctuary. In palaces, the iwan functioned as an audience hall where the ruler could appear before his court while remaining partly secluded.

Iwans are frequently framed by a tall rectangular portal, the pishtaq, which rises above the roofline and is often flanked by minarets or turrets in later examples. Surfaces were richly decorated with glazed tiles, carved stucco, and muqarnas vaulting.

Notable examples

  • Taq Kasra at Ctesiphon, Iraq — the great Sasanian iwan.
  • Shah Mosque and Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque at Naqsh-e Jahan Square, Isfahan.
  • The Taj Mahal at Agra, whose mausoleum facade is organised around a central iwan.
  • The Registan at Samarkand, whose madrasa facades are dominated by monumental iwans.
