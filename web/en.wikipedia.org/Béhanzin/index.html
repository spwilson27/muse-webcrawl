Béhanzin (1845 – 10 December 1906), born Gbèhinazin and also known as Gbèhanzin, was the eleventh (counting Adandozan) king of Dahomey, in present-day Benin, reigning from 1889 until his surrender to the French in 1894. He is remembered as a symbol of resistance to European colonization for his leadership of the Fon kingdom during the Franco-Dahomean Wars, commanding an army that included the famous women soldiers known to Europeans as the Dahomey Amazons.

Reign and war with France

Béhanzin succeeded his father Glele in 1889 and inherited a dispute with France over the port of Cotonou and French claims to a protectorate. After failed negotiations and attacks on French positions, the Second Franco-Dahomean War (1892–1894) saw General Alfred-Amédée Dodds lead an expedition into the interior. Dahomean forces fought fiercely but were outgunned; Béhanzin burned his capital, Abomey, rather than let it fall, and finally surrendered in January 1894.

Exile and legacy

The French deposed Béhanzin and exiled him first to Martinique and then to Blida, Algeria, where he died in 1906. His remains were returned to Dahomey in 1928. Béhanzin remains a national hero in Benin; royal treasures looted from Abomey during the war, long held in French museums, became a prominent case in restitution debates, and France returned 26 works to Benin in 2021.
