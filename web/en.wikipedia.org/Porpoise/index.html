Porpoises are small toothed whales of the family Phocoenidae, closely related to dolphins but differing in their blunt, rounded heads without a prominent beak, their spade-shaped (rather than conical) teeth, and a triangular dorsal fin in most species. There are eight living species, including the widespread harbour porpoise and the critically endangered vaquita of the Gulf of California.

Biology

Porpoises inhabit coastal waters of the Northern Hemisphere and parts of South America and Asia; the finless porpoises also ascend rivers such as the Yangtze. They feed on small schooling fish and squid, using high-frequency echolocation clicks. Porpoises are fast, shy animals that rarely bow-ride or leap clear of the water, and they mature quickly with relatively short lifespans of roughly 8–20 years.

Conservation

Bycatch in gillnets is the principal threat to most porpoise populations. The vaquita, reduced to around a dozen individuals, is the world's most endangered cetacean, and the baiji — a related river dolphin lost in the same era — illustrates the pressure on riverine species. Harbour porpoises are protected in many jurisdictions but remain vulnerable to noise, pollution and fishing gear.
