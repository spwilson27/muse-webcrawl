Parsley

Parsley, or garden parsley (Petroselinum crispum), is a species of flowering plant in the family Apiaceae, native to the central Mediterranean region. It is widely cultivated as an herb, spice, and vegetable, and is one of the most commonly used culinary herbs in the world. The two main cultivated forms are curly-leaf parsley, often used as a garnish, and flat-leaf (Italian) parsley, generally preferred for cooking because of its stronger flavour. A third form, Hamburg parsley, is grown for its thick, edible root.

Cultivation

Parsley is a biennial plant: it forms a rosette of leaves in the first year and flowers and sets seed in the second. Its seeds are notoriously slow and erratic to germinate, sometimes taking several weeks — a slowness that generated folklore associating the plant with the underworld; Greek myth linked parsley with the hero Archemorus, and the Greeks crowned victors of the Isthmian Games with parsley wreaths. It grows best in moist, well-drained soil in sun or partial shade.

Culinary use

Parsley is a staple of European, Middle Eastern, and American cooking. It is the main ingredient of tabbouleh, a component of bouquet garni, fines herbes, gremolata, green sauce, and chimichurri (in some recipes), and it flavours soups, stews, sauces, and salads. The leaves are rich in vitamins A, C, and K and in folate and iron. Because the plant is so often used as a mere garnish, "parsley" has also become a metaphor for superficial decoration.

Caution

Parsley resembles several poisonous relatives, including fool's parsley and poison hemlock, which historically made careful identification important. In very large quantities, parsley preparations — especially the apiol-rich seed oil — can be toxic.
