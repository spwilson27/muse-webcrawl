Saint George and the Dragon

SubjectSaint George slaying a dragon
SourceGolden Legend by Jacobus de Voragine (c. 1260s)
SettingSilene, Libya (in the legend)
Feast day23 April
PatronageEngland, Georgia, Catalonia, many cities and soldiers

Saint George and the Dragon is a famous legend in which Saint George, a Roman soldier and Christian martyr, slays a dragon to rescue a princess, leading the grateful inhabitants of a city to convert to Christianity. The tale became one of the most popular saint's legends of the Middle Ages and is retold in Jacobus de Voragine's Golden Legend (c. 1260s), from which it spread throughout Europe.

The legend

According to the Golden Legend, a fierce dragon made its nest at the spring that supplied water to the city of Silene in Libya. The townspeople were obliged to offer the dragon sheep, and when sheep ran short, human victims chosen by lot. Eventually the lot fell on the king's own daughter, who was sent out dressed as a bride to be devoured.

Saint George, a tribune in the Roman army who was passing by, heard the princess's story, crossed himself, and charged the dragon on horseback, wounding it with his lance. He then had the princess fasten her girdle around the dragon's neck and led the now-docile beast back into the city. There George promised to kill the dragon if the people would convert to Christianity and be baptised; the king and fifteen thousand subjects agreed. George slew the dragon, and on the spot where it died the king built a church to the Virgin Mary and Saint George, from whose altar a spring flowed that cured disease.

Origins and development

Saint George himself was a real historical figure — a soldier martyred at Nicomedia in 303 during the Diocletianic Persecution — but the dragon episode is a later, legendary addition, first attested in the eleventh and twelfth centuries, initially in Georgia and Cappadocia. Scholars see echoes of much older dragon-slayer myths: Perseus rescuing Andromeda, and ancient Near Eastern combat myths in which a storm god defeats a chaos serpent. The legend gained enormous currency during the Crusades, when George appeared to crusaders as a warrior saint and vision of him was reported at the Siege of Antioch in 1098.

In art and heraldry

The image of the mounted saint spearing the dragon became one of the most recognisable in Christian art, depicted by painters including Paolo Uccello, Raphael, Tintoretto and Gustave Moreau, and carved on churches across Europe. The red cross of Saint George on a white field is the flag of England and of Georgia (the five-cross flag), and George is the patron saint of both countries, as well as of Catalonia, where his day (Sant Jordi) is celebrated with gifts of books and roses. Moscow's coat of arms likewise shows Saint George slaying the dragon.
