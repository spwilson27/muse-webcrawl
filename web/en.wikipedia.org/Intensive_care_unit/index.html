Intensive care unit

An intensive care unit (ICU), also known as an intensive therapy unit or critical care unit (CCU), is a special department of a hospital or health care facility that provides intensive care medicine. ICUs cater to patients with severe or life-threatening illnesses and injuries, which require constant care, close supervision from life-support equipment, and medication in order to ensure normal bodily functions.

Staff and equipment

ICUs are staffed by specially trained physicians (intensivists), nurses, respiratory therapists, and other specialists, with high staff-to-patient ratios. Typical equipment includes mechanical ventilators, cardiac monitors, dialysis machines, infusion pumps, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Specialized units include neonatal, pediatric, cardiac, surgical, and neurological ICUs.

History

Modern intensive care is often traced to the 1952 Copenhagen polio epidemic, when Bjørn Ibsen organized manual ventilation of patients by teams of medical students, dramatically reducing mortality. The first dedicated ICUs opened in the late 1950s, and intensive care medicine is now a recognized specialty or subspecialty in most health systems.
