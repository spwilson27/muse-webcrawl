Schema theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The schema theorem (also called Holland's schema theorem or the fundamental theorem of genetic algorithms) is a result in the theory of genetic algorithms, formulated by John Holland in the 1970s. It describes how short, low-order, above-average schemata—patterns within chromosomes—receive exponentially increasing numbers of trials in successive generations.

Definitions

For binary strings of length l, a schema is a template over the alphabet {0, 1, *}, where * is a wildcard. The order of a schema, o(H), is the number of fixed positions; the defining length, δ(H), is the distance between the first and last fixed positions. A population of strings implicitly samples many schemata at once—a property Holland called implicit parallelism; a population of n strings processes on the order of n³ schemata.

Statement

The theorem gives a lower bound on the expected number of representatives m(H, t+1) of a schema H in the next generation, in terms of its current number m(H, t), its average fitness f(H) relative to the population average f̄, and the probabilities that crossover (rate pc) and mutation (rate pm) disrupt it: roughly, m(H, t+1) ≥ m(H, t) · [f(H)/f̄] · [1 − pc·δ(H)/(l−1) − o(H)·pm]. Thus schemata that are short, low-order, and fitter than average tend to proliferate. The associated building-block hypothesis holds that genetic algorithms work by combining such short, fit schemata into progressively better solutions.

Criticism and status

The schema theorem has been criticized because it is an inequality about a single generation and ignores the constructive effects of crossover and the creation of new schemata; it therefore does not, by itself, prove that genetic algorithms converge to good solutions. Alternative theoretical frameworks—including Markov chain models, exact dynamical models, and the no free lunch theorems—have since supplemented schema-based analysis, but the theorem remains historically central to the field.
