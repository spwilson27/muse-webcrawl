Sanxingdui (Chinese: 三星堆, "Three Star Mound") is an archaeological site and Bronze Age culture near Guanghan in Sichuan, China. Associated with the ancient kingdom of Shu, it flourished roughly between 1700 and 1050 BC, contemporary with the Shang dynasty of the Yellow River valley yet strikingly distinct in style. Its discovery transformed understanding of early Chinese civilisation, showing that it developed from multiple regional centres rather than a single cradle.

Discoveries

Jade artefacts were first found at the site in 1929, but the breakthrough came in 1986, when two sacrificial pits yielded hundreds of objects that had been deliberately burned and buried: bronze statues and masks with exaggerated, angular features and protruding eyes, gold foil masks and staffs, jade blades, elephant tusks, and a 2.6-metre bronze "spirit tree". A standing bronze figure 2.6 metres tall is among the largest bronzes of its era anywhere in the world. Excavations from 2019 onward uncovered six further pits with more than ten thousand additional artefacts, including a bronze box containing jade and gold masks.

Interpretation

Sanxingdui's iconography — dominated by eyes, birds, and sun motifs — suggests a theocratic society whose religion differed from that of the Shang, and its art has no clear precedent or successor in the Chinese record. The culture left no deciphered written records. Whether it corresponds to the semi-legendary Shu kings of later texts such as Cancong remains debated. Its masterpieces are displayed at the Sanxingdui Museum in Guanghan and in major museums worldwide.
