Self-harm

Self-harm is intentional conduct that is understood as physical injury to oneself. A common form is cutting, but self-harm also covers a wide range of behaviours including burning, scratching, and hitting body parts. It is listed in the DSM-5 as a proposed disorder, nonsuicidal self-injury. Self-harm is associated with a variety of mental disorders such as depression, eating disorders, and borderline personality disorder, and those who self-harm have a higher risk of suicide.

Terminology

Terms such as self-injury, deliberate self-harm, and parasuicide have been used in the literature. Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is distinguished from suicide attempts by the absence of intent to die, though the behaviours can co-occur and self-harm is a risk factor for later suicide attempts.

Causes and functions

Self-harm is most commonly described as a coping mechanism for emotional pain, intense anger, or feelings of numbness and dissociation. Proposed functions include regulating affect, self-punishment, influencing or communicating with others, and generating a feeling of control. Risk factors include childhood abuse or neglect, mental illness, bullying, and social isolation.

Epidemiology

Self-harm is most common in adolescence and young adulthood, with onset typically between the ages of 12 and 14. Rates vary considerably by population and method of measurement; community surveys of adolescents in many countries report lifetime rates in the range of roughly 10–20 percent.

Treatment

Effective treatments may include dialectical behavior therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy, and treatment of underlying conditions such as depression. Harm reduction approaches and safety planning are also used in clinical practice.
