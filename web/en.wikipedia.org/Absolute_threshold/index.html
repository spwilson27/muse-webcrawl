Absolute threshold

In neuroscience and psychophysics, the absolute threshold is the smallest level of a stimulus that an organism can detect. It was originally defined by Gustav Fechner and the early psychophysicists as the intensity below which a stimulus is never perceived and above which it is always perceived; because detection is in practice probabilistic, the modern operational definition is the intensity detected on some fixed proportion of trials, conventionally 50%.

Examples

Classic textbook illustrations of the sensitivity of the senses include hearing the tick of a clock at about six metres under quiet conditions, seeing a candle flame at tens of kilometres on a clear dark night, and tasting a teaspoon of sugar dissolved in several litres of water. The threshold of hearing varies strongly with frequency and is lowest for sounds around 2,000–5,000 Hz; its standard reference is expressed as 0 dB sound pressure level at 1,000 Hz.

Measurement

Standard methods include the method of limits, the method of constant stimuli, and adaptive staircase procedures. Signal detection theory has largely superseded the idea of a fixed threshold, separating an observer's sensitivity (d′) from their response criterion, and treating "threshold" as a convenient summary rather than a hard sensory boundary.
