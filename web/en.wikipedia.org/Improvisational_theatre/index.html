Improvisational theatre

Improvisational theatre, often called improv, is a form of theatre, often comedy, in which most or all of what is performed is unplanned or unscripted, created spontaneously by the performers. In its purest form, the dialogue, action, story, and characters are created collaboratively and in the moment. Modern improvisational theatre developed in the mid-20th century through the work of Viola Spolin, Keith Johnstone, and groups such as The Second City in Chicago.

History

Improvised performance has roots in the commedia dell'arte of 16th-century Italy, whose troupes performed unscripted scenarios with stock characters. In the 1930s and 1940s, Viola Spolin developed theatre games in Chicago as a way of training actors in spontaneity; her son Paul Sills went on to co-found The Compass Players and The Second City. In Britain, Keith Johnstone developed Theatresports at the Royal Court Theatre and later in Calgary, emphasising status, narrative, and the acceptance of offers. Del Close later codified long-form improvisation, including the Harold format.

Forms

Short-form improv consists of brief games and scenes, often driven by audience suggestions, as popularised by the television show Whose Line Is It Anyway? Long-form improv builds extended scenes, monologues, or full-length plays from a single suggestion. Popular long forms include the Harold, the Armando, and the La Ronde.

Principles

Core principles include "Yes, and...", the practice of accepting a partner's offer and building on it; making one's partner look good; active listening; and commitment to character and scene. Applied improvisation adapts these techniques to education, therapy, and corporate training.
