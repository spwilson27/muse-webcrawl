Luigi Galvani

Luigi Galvani
Born: 9 September 1737, Bologna, Papal States
Died: 4 December 1798, Bologna
Known for: animal electricity; galvanism

Luigi Galvani (9 September 1737 – 4 December 1798) was an Italian physician, physicist and philosopher who pioneered the study of bioelectricity. He is famous for his discovery that the muscles of dead frogs' legs twitch when touched by dissimilar metals, which he interpreted as "animal electricity" intrinsic to living tissue.

Animal electricity

From the 1780s Galvani, working with his wife Lucia Galeazzi Galvani at the University of Bologna, experimented on frog legs with electrical machines, lightning and metal contacts. In his De viribus electricitatis in motu musculari commentarius (1791) he argued that animal tissue contained an innate electrical fluid. Alessandro Volta disputed this, contending the electricity came from the contact of two different metals — the bimetallic effect — a controversy that led Volta to invent the voltaic pile (the first battery) in 1800. In fact both men were partly right: nerves and muscles do conduct electrical signals, while metal junctions do generate current.

Legacy

Galvani's name survives in "galvanism", the galvanometer, galvanised metal and galvanic cells. His experiments inspired Mary Shelley's Frankenstein and the whole science of electrophysiology. Late in life his refusal to swear loyalty to the Cisalpine Republic cost him his university post; he died in poverty in 1798.
