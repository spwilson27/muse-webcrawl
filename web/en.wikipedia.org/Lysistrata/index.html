Lysistrata

A modern staging of Lysistrata

Author: Aristophanes

First performed: 411 BC, Athens

Genre: Old Comedy

Lysistrata (Greek: Λυσιστράτη, "she who disbands armies") is an ancient Greek comedy by Aristophanes, originally performed in Athens in 411 BC. It is a comic account of one woman's extraordinary mission to end the Peloponnesian War between Greek city-states: Lysistrata persuades the women of Greece to withhold sexual privileges from their husbands and lovers until the men negotiate peace, and to seize the Acropolis — and with it the war treasury — in the meantime.

Plot

Frustrated by years of war, Lysistrata summons women from Athens, Sparta, and other cities and swears them to abstain from sex, an oath sealed over a bowl of wine. The older women occupy the Acropolis while the younger women tease their returning husbands with elaborate excuses. Attempts by a magistrate and his archers to retake the citadel are repelled. Finally, with both sides desperate, Spartan and Athenian envoys negotiate peace, Lysistrata mediates by personifying Reconciliation, and the play closes with feasting and song.

Themes and legacy

Written twenty years into the Peloponnesian War and after the disastrous Sicilian Expedition, the play is notable for its sexual and political frankness and for making its case against war through farce rather than tragedy. Its premise made it a touchstone for later pacifist and feminist movements; it inspired the 2003 "Lysistrata Project" of worldwide readings against the Iraq War and Spike Lee's 2015 film Chi-Raq. Because of its obscenity the play was long excluded from school curricula and, in some jurisdictions, suppressed under anti-obscenity laws.
