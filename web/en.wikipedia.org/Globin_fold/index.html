Globin fold

The globin fold is a common three-dimensional fold in proteins. This fold is found in the globin-like protein superfamily, whose best-known members are haemoglobin and myoglobin, as well as in certain phytochromes and cyanobacterial phycocyanins. The globin fold is considered a "structural fossil", in the sense that proteins carrying it are thought to descend from a common ancestor that pre-dates the divergence of the major kingdoms of life.

Structure

The globin fold typically consists of eight alpha helices, conventionally labelled A through H, arranged in a three-over-three sandwich that wraps around a central pocket. In the classical globins this pocket binds a haem (heme) prosthetic group, whose iron atom coordinates an oxygen molecule, enabling oxygen transport and storage in blood and muscle. The helices are connected by short loops, and the fold is stabilised by hydrophobic packing in its interior.

Despite sharing the same overall architecture, globin-fold proteins can have very low sequence identity with one another, sometimes below 20 percent, which makes the fold a classic example of structure being more strongly conserved through evolution than amino-acid sequence.

Members

Proteins adopting the globin fold include:

  • Haemoglobin, the oxygen carrier in vertebrate red blood cells
  • Myoglobin, the oxygen store in muscle tissue
  • Neuroglobin and cytoglobin, found in vertebrate nervous and other tissues
  • Leghaemoglobins, which supply oxygen to nitrogen-fixing bacteria in legume root nodules
  • Flavohaemoglobins and truncated haemoglobins of bacteria and fungi

Evolution

Because globin-fold proteins occur in bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes, the fold is believed to be extremely ancient, possibly arising before the last universal common ancestor diversified. Variations on the fold include proteins in which some of the canonical helices are absent, such as the truncated haemoglobins, and multi-domain proteins in which a globin domain is fused to enzymatic domains.
