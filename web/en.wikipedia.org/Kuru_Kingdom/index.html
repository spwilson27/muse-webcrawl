The Kuru kingdom was a Vedic Indo-Aryan tribal union in northern India, encompassing parts of the modern-day states of Haryana, Delhi, and western Uttar Pradesh in India. It emerged in the Middle Vedic period (c. 1200–900 BCE) and is generally considered the first recorded state-level society in South Asia. The Kuru realm is the setting of much of the early Vedic literature and is closely associated with the epic tradition of the Mahabharata, whose central conflict, the Kurukshetra War, is fought between two branches of the Kuru royal family, the Kauravas and the Pandavas.

History

The Kuru clan appears to have formed from an alliance of Bharata and Puru tribes in the region between the Sarasvati and Drishadvati rivers, an area later celebrated as Kurukshetra, "the field of the Kurus." Under kings such as Parikshit and Janamejaya, the kingdom consolidated control over the upper Ganges–Yamuna doab. Its capitals included Āsandīvat and later Hastinapura.

The Kuru state is credited with organising the Vedic hymns into collections and developing the śrauta ritual tradition. Iron technology, associated with the Painted Grey Ware culture, spread during this period. By about 500 BCE the kingdom had declined, eclipsed by the rising mahājanapadas such as Kosala and Magadha, though tradition holds that a Kuru lineage continued as an oligarchic gaṇasaṅgha into the early centuries BCE.

Society and culture

Kuru society is described in the Brahmana texts as structured around the emerging varna system, with the king (rājan) at its head, advised by priests and assemblies. The period saw the composition of the Brahmanas, Aranyakas, and the earliest Upanishads, which transformed the ritual religion of the early Veda.
