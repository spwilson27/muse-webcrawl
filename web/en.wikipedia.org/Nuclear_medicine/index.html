Nuclear medicine

Nuclear medicine, or nucleology, is a medical specialty involving the application of radioactive substances in the diagnosis and treatment of disease. In nuclear medicine procedures, radionuclides are combined with other elements to form chemical compounds, or else combined with existing pharmaceutical compounds, to form radiopharmaceuticals. These radiopharmaceuticals, once administered to the patient, can localize to specific organs or cellular receptors, allowing diseases to be imaged or treated based on cellular function and physiology rather than anatomy alone.

Diagnostic procedures

Common techniques include single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), which typically uses technetium-99m, and positron emission tomography (PET), which commonly uses fluorine-18 labelled fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG). Nuclear medicine scans are used to assess the heart, bones, thyroid, kidneys, brain, and many cancers.

Therapy

Radionuclide therapy uses radioactive substances to treat disease, notably iodine-131 for hyperthyroidism and thyroid cancer, lutetium-177 therapies for neuroendocrine tumours and prostate cancer, and radium-223 for bone metastases.

Safety

Nuclear medicine involves exposure to ionizing radiation; procedures are justified and optimized so that doses are kept as low as reasonably achievable, and the diagnostic benefit is weighed against the small risk from radiation.
