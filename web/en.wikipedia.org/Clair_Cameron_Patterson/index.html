Clair Cameron Patterson (2 June 1922 – 5 December 1995) was an American geochemist. Born in Mitchellville, Iowa, he developed the uranium–lead dating method, from which he calculated the age of the Earth as 4.55 billion years — a figure far more accurate than any previously accepted. His later research revealed widespread lead contamination of the environment from industrial sources, and he became a prominent campaigner for the removal of lead from gasoline.

Early life and education

Patterson grew up in Iowa and attended Grinnell College, graduating in 1943. During the Second World War he worked on the Manhattan Project, first at the University of Chicago and later at Oak Ridge, Tennessee, where he became expert in mass spectrometry. After the war he returned to the University of Chicago for his doctorate, working under Harrison Brown on methods for measuring minute quantities of lead isotopes in rocks.

Dating the Earth

Working at the California Institute of Technology from 1952, Patterson applied lead-isotope measurements to iron meteorites, which he reasoned represented the primordial composition of the solar system. In 1956 he published the definitive result: the Earth and the meteorites are the same age, 4.55 billion years, plus or minus about 70 million years. The figure has been refined only slightly since.

Lead contamination research

Patterson's painstaking efforts to eliminate contamination from his laboratory samples led him to realize that lead was pervasive in the modern environment. By comparing lead levels in ancient ice cores and in prehistoric human remains with modern samples, he showed that human body burdens of lead had risen dramatically with industrialization, particularly from tetraethyllead additives in gasoline. His 1965 paper "Contaminated and Natural Lead Environments of Man" challenged the lead industry, and his decades of advocacy contributed to regulations phasing out leaded gasoline in the United States.

Legacy

Patterson received the Tyler Prize for Environmental Achievement in 1995. The asteroid 2511 Patterson and Patterson Peak in Antarctica are named in his honour.
