Immunosuppression

Immunosuppression
Definition: Reduction of the activation or efficacy of the immune system
Methods: Drugs, radiation, surgery, disease
Major risk: Opportunistic infection

Immunosuppression is a reduction of the activation or efficacy of the immune system. Some portions of the immune system itself have immunosuppressive effects on other parts, and immunosuppression may also occur as an adverse reaction to treatment of other conditions.

Deliberately induced

In medicine, immunosuppression is intentionally induced in organ transplantation to prevent rejection of the graft, and in autoimmune diseases to dampen harmful immune activity. Drugs used include corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors such as ciclosporin and tacrolimus, antimetabolites such as mycophenolate, and biological agents that target T cells or cytokines. The first successful organ transplants became practical with the development of these agents in the mid-20th century.

Non-deliberate causes

Immunosuppression can also arise from diseases such as HIV/AIDS, cancers of the immune system, malnutrition, ageing, and certain infections, as well as from cancer chemotherapy and radiation therapy.

Consequences

A suppressed immune system leaves the body vulnerable to opportunistic infections and to certain cancers. Patients on long-term immunosuppression are monitored with blood tests and screened for infection and malignancy. See also Immunodeficiency and Organ transplantation.
