Papal supremacy

Papal supremacy is the doctrine of the Catholic Church that the Pope, as successor of Saint Peter and Vicar of Christ, holds full, supreme, and universal jurisdiction over the whole Church — a power "supreme, full, immediate, and universal" in teaching, sanctifying, and governing. It is distinguished from papal primacy, the more general claim to a primacy of honour and authority among bishops, which many other Christians accept in some form.

Biblical and historical foundations

Catholics ground the doctrine in Matthew 16:18–19, where Christ gives Peter the "keys of the kingdom", and in John 21, where the risen Christ charges Peter to feed his sheep. The claim developed through the early centuries as the bishops of Rome were appealed to in disputes, and was asserted strongly by popes such as Leo I (440–461). In the Middle Ages it was articulated in documents like Gregory VII's Dictatus Papae (1075) and Boniface VIII's bull Unam Sanctam (1302).

Definition

The First Vatican Council's constitution Pastor Aeternus (1870) defined papal supremacy together with papal infallibility in matters of faith and morals taught ex cathedra. The Second Vatican Council's Lumen Gentium (1964) reaffirmed the doctrine while also teaching the collegiality of the bishops with and under the Pope.

Ecumenical issues

Papal supremacy is a principal point of division between the Catholic Church and both Eastern Orthodoxy — where it contributed to the East–West Schism of 1054 — and Protestant churches arising from the Reformation. Orthodox Christians generally accept a primacy of honour for Rome within a conciliar structure but reject universal immediate jurisdiction.
