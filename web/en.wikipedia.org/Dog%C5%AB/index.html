Dogū

Dogū (Japanese: 土偶, "earthen figures") are small humanoid and animal figurines made during the later part of the Jōmon period (roughly 14,000–400 BC) of prehistoric Japan. They are among the most distinctive artifacts of Jōmon culture and are found mainly in eastern Japan.

Description

Dogū are made of fired clay and range from a few centimetres to about 40 cm tall. Many depict female figures with exaggerated features — large eyes, wide hips and small waists — sometimes with elaborate patterns representing clothing, tattoos or jewelry. Famous types include the "goggle-eyed" shakōki-dogū of the Tōhoku region, heart-shaped-faced dogū, and horned-owl dogū. Most excavated examples are deliberately broken, with limbs or heads missing.

Interpretation

The purpose of dogū is unknown. Proposed functions include fertility symbols, effigies used in healing rituals (transferring illness or injury to the figure, which was then broken), toys, or representations of spirits or deities. Their frequent breakage and deposition in refuse pits, burials and ritual sites suggests a role in rites of disposal or renewal. Dogū are often contrasted with the later, simpler haniwa of the Kofun period.
