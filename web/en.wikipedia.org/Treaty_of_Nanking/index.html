Treaty of Nanking

Signed29 August 1842
LocationAboard HMS Cornwallis, Nanjing, China
Effective26 June 1843 (exchange of ratifications)
PartiesUnited Kingdom; Qing dynasty (China)
LanguagesEnglish; Chinese

The Treaty of Nanking (Chinese: 南京條約; pinyin: Nánjīng tiáoyāo) was a peace treaty which ended the First Opium War (1839–1842) between Great Britain and the Qing dynasty of China. It was signed on 29 August 1842 aboard the British warship HMS Cornwallis in Nanjing (then romanised as Nanking). It was the first of what the Chinese later termed the "unequal treaties".

Background

The First Opium War arose from disputes over the British trade in opium and Chinese efforts to suppress it. After a series of British victories culminating in the capture of Zhenjiang and the threat to Nanjing, the Qing court sued for peace. The British plenipotentiary was Sir Henry Pottinger, and the Qing negotiators included Qiying and Yilibu.

Terms

The main provisions of the treaty were:

  • Hong Kong Island was ceded to Britain in perpetuity.
  • Five ports were opened to British trade and residence: Canton (Guangzhou), Amoy (Xiamen), Foochow (Fuzhou), Ningpo (Ningbo), and Shanghai.
  • China was to pay an indemnity of 21 million silver dollars.
  • The Cohong merchant monopoly in Canton was abolished, allowing British merchants to trade with whomever they pleased.
  • Fixed, moderate tariffs were to be established for British goods.
  • British and Chinese officials were to correspond on a footing of equality.

Aftermath

The treaty was followed in 1843 by the Treaty of the Bogue, which granted Britain extraterritoriality and most-favoured-nation status. Similar treaties were soon extracted by the United States (Treaty of Wanghia, 1844) and France (Treaty of Whampoa, 1844). Hong Kong Island remained a British colony until the handover of Hong Kong in 1997. In Chinese historical memory, the treaty marks the beginning of the "century of humiliation".
