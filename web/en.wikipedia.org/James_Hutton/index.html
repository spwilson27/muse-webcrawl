James Hutton (3 June 1726 – 26 March 1797) was a Scottish geologist, agriculturalist, chemical manufacturer, naturalist, and physician. Often called the founder of modern geology, he originated the theory of uniformitarianism — the idea that the Earth was shaped by the same slow processes still observable today.

Theory of the Earth

Hutton presented his ideas to the Royal Society of Edinburgh in 1785 and expanded them in Theory of the Earth (1795). Observing unconformities such as the one at Siccar Point — where vertical greywacke strata are capped by gently tilted red sandstone — he concluded that rock layers required immense spans of time to form, erode, and be uplifted again. He famously wrote that the geological record shows "no vestige of a beginning, — no prospect of an end."

Influence

Hutton's prose was famously difficult, and his ideas reached a wide audience through John Playfair's Illustrations of the Huttonian Theory of the Earth (1802). Charles Lyell later built on uniformitarianism, which in turn influenced Charles Darwin's thinking about gradual change over deep time. Hutton also argued that granite was intruded molten rock (plutonism), against the then-dominant view that all rocks precipitated from a primordial ocean.
