Boogie-woogie

Boogie-woogie is a music genre of African-American origin that became popular during the late 1920s and 1930s, developed in African-American communities in the Southern United States. It is characterised by a strong, repetitive left-hand bass figure—typically a walking pattern of eight notes to the bar ("eight-to-the-bar")—over a twelve-bar blues progression, with improvised right-hand melodic phrases. The genre is closely associated with solo piano, though it was also played by big bands and small combos, and it became a foundation of rhythm and blues and rock and roll.

Boogie-woogie piano is driven by a relentless ostinato bass in the left hand.

Origins

Boogie-woogie grew out of the barrelhouse piano styles played in lumber and turpentine camps and rent parties across Texas, Louisiana, and the Mississippi Delta, where pianists imitated the rhythm of steam locomotives. The first recording to use the term was Clarence "Pine Top" Smith's "Pine Top's Boogie Woogie" (1928); Meade Lux Lewis's "Honky Tonk Train Blues" (1927) became a foundational record. The style travelled north with the Great Migration to Chicago and Kansas City.

The boogie craze

Producer John Hammond's "From Spirituals to Swing" concerts at Carnegie Hall in 1938 and 1939 showcased boogie-woogie pianists Albert Ammons, Meade Lux Lewis, and Pete Johnson to national acclaim. The trio's appearances and residencies at Café Society sparked a nationwide craze: big bands—including those of Tommy Dorsey ("Boogie Woogie," 1938), Count Basie, and Benny Goodman—recorded boogie numbers, and the Andrews Sisters scored with "Boogie Woogie Bugle Boy" (1941). The name also attached to a popular swing dance.

Musical characteristics

A boogie-woogie performance typically maintains a shuffled or straight-eighth ostinato bass pattern (such as the "eight-to-the-bar" broken octaves or the walking "Chicago" pattern) beneath right-hand riffs, trills, and cross-rhythms. Pieces are usually built on the 12-bar blues form in a fast tempo, emphasising rhythmic drive over harmonic complexity.

Legacy

Boogie-woogie's driving bass lines passed directly into jump blues, rhythm and blues, and rock and roll pianists such as Fats Domino, Little Richard, and Jerry Lee Lewis, and guitarists adapted the patterns into rock riffs. Revivals and specialist festivals continue in Europe and the US, and competitive boogie-woogie dancing remains a recognised dance sport category.
