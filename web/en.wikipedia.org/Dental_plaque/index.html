Dental plaque

Dental plaque
TypeBacterial biofilm
LocationTooth surfaces, esp. at the gum line
Associated diseasesDental caries, gingivitis, periodontitis
PreventionBrushing, flossing, professional cleaning

Dental plaque is a biofilm of microorganisms (mostly bacteria, with fungi such as Candida in some cases) that grows on surfaces within the mouth. It is a sticky, colourless or pale yellow film that forms on teeth between brushings, especially along the gum line and in protected niches. If not removed regularly, plaque mineralises into calculus (tartar) and drives the two commonest oral diseases: dental caries (tooth decay) and periodontal disease.

Formation

Within minutes of cleaning, salivary glycoproteins form an acquired pellicle on enamel. Pioneer bacteria — chiefly streptococci such as Streptococcus sanguinis and S. mutans — adhere to the pellicle and multiply into microcolonies embedded in a self-produced matrix of polysaccharides. The biofilm matures over days: its microbial community grows more diverse and more anaerobic, particularly in the gingival sulcus.

Health effects

Plaque bacteria ferment dietary sugars to acids that demineralise enamel and dentine, causing caries. Plaque accumulating at and below the gum line provokes gingivitis (reversible gum inflammation); in susceptible individuals this can progress to periodontitis, with destruction of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone and eventual tooth loss. Plaque control is therefore the foundation of preventive dentistry.

Removal and control

Mechanical disruption — toothbrushing twice daily, interdental cleaning with floss or brushes — is the main control method; antimicrobial mouthwashes (e.g., chlorhexidine) and fluoride toothpaste are adjuncts. Mineralised plaque (calculus) cannot be removed by brushing and requires professional scaling.
