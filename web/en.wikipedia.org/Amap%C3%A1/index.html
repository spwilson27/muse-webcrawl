Amapá (Portuguese pronunciation: [amaˈpa]) is one of the 26 states of Brazil. It is located in the far north of the country, bordering French Guiana to the north, the Atlantic Ocean to the east, the state of Pará to the south and west, and Suriname to the northwest. Its capital and largest city is Macapá. The state has 0.4% of the Brazilian population and is responsible for only 0.22% of the Brazilian GDP.

The vast majority of the state is covered by the Amazon rainforest, and Amapá is one of the best-preserved states in Brazil in terms of its native vegetation; roughly 70% of its territory is protected in conservation units and indigenous lands. The equator crosses the state, passing through Macapá, where the Marco Zero (Zero Mark) monument and the Zerão stadium mark the line.

History

The region was contested between Portugal and France for centuries; the Treaty of Utrecht (1713) set the Oyapock River as the boundary, though disputes continued into the late 19th century. Amapá became a federal territory in 1943, when it was separated from Pará, and was elevated to statehood with the promulgation of the Brazilian Constitution of 1988, with its first elected governor taking office in 1991.

Geography

Amapá's coastline on the Atlantic is strongly influenced by the mouth of the Amazon River, whose fresh water and sediment reach far into the ocean. The state contains part of the Tumucumaque Mountains National Park, one of the largest tropical forest national parks in the world. Major rivers include the Oyapock, which forms the border with French Guiana, and the Jari.

Economy

The economy is based on mining (gold, manganese, iron), forestry, fishing, and the public sector. The port of Santana handles mineral exports. Brazil nuts, açaí, and timber are among the principal extractive products.
