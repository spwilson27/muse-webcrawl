Eminent domain in the United States

Eminent domain in the United States refers to the power of federal, state and local governments to take private property for public use upon payment of just compensation, as provided by the Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment.

Governments may exercise the power directly or delegate it to private corporations such as railroads and utilities. Property taken is usually valued at fair market value, and owners may challenge both the necessity of the taking and the amount of compensation in court, in a process called condemnation.

Landmark cases include Berman v. Parker (1954), upholding urban renewal in Washington, D.C.; Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff (1984), upholding land redistribution; and Kelo v. City of New London (2005), which held that economic development qualifies as a public use. The Kelo decision led more than forty states to enact reforms tightening their eminent domain laws.
