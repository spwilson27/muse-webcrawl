Joystick

A joystick, sometimes called a flight stick, is an input device consisting of a stick that pivots on a base and reports its angle or direction to the device it is controlling. Joysticks are used as the principal flight control in many aircraft, as controllers for video games, and to operate machinery such as cranes, wheelchairs, and industrial robots.

History

The control column or "stick" appeared in early aircraft in the first decade of the 20th century; the word "joystick" is often attributed to pioneer aviators such as Robert Loraine. Electrical joysticks were developed for applications including the German Fritz X and Henschel Hs 293 guided weapons of the Second World War, and later for powered wheelchairs and NASA's Lunar Roving Vehicle. In the 1970s and 1980s the joystick became the standard controller for arcade games and home computers: the Atari 2600's CX10/CX40 stick, with a single button, became an iconic design, and manufacturers such as Competition Pro and Kempston supplied rugged microswitch-based sticks for systems like the Commodore 64 and ZX Spectrum.

Types

Digital joysticks use switches to report direction in discrete steps (typically eight directions). Analog joysticks use potentiometers or Hall effect sensors to report continuous position, enabling proportional control, as in flight-simulator sticks. Modern game controllers combine small thumb-operated analog sticks with buttons and triggers. Specialist variants include force-feedback sticks, HOTAS (hands on throttle and stick) sets for flight simulation, and isometric pointing sticks.

Applications

Beyond gaming and aviation, joysticks control CCTV cameras, forklifts and cranes, surgical robots, unmanned aerial vehicles, and mobility aids. Assistive joysticks allow people with limited movement to operate computers and wheelchairs.
