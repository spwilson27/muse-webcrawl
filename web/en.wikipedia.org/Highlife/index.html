Highlife is a music genre that originated in present-day Ghana in the early 20th century, during its history as a colony of the British Empire (the Gold Coast). It uses the melodic and main rhythmic structures of traditional Akan music, but is played with Western instruments such as brass, guitars, and later keyboards. Highlife music follows the traditional Ga, Akan, and Ewe dance rhythms, incorporating melodies from church music, military brass bands, and sea shanties brought by sailors.

The name "highlife" is generally traced to the 1920s, when the music was associated with the dance halls and clubs attended by the local elite—the "high life"—in contrast to the palm-wine music played in bars by and for working people. The genre became the dominant popular music of Ghana and spread widely through West Africa, particularly Nigeria, where it developed its own major stars and styles.

History

Origins

Highlife emerged from several streams: the brass bands and regimental bands of the colonial era, the guitar-based palm-wine music (named for the beverage consumed while listening) played with local percussion and the seperewa harp-lute traditions, and "adaha" and "osibisaaba" styles fusing indigenous rhythms with imported instruments. Early pioneers included Jacob Sam and the Kumasi Trio, who recorded in London in 1928, among the first commercial recordings of West African music.

Golden age

After the Second World War, dance-band highlife flourished. E. T. Mensah and his Tempos band, formed in the late 1940s, became the genre's most famous ambassadors, touring internationally and popularising a big-band style with trumpets, saxophones, and guitar sung in multiple Ghanaian languages and pidgin. Mensah earned the title "King of Highlife". Ghana's independence in 1957 under Kwame Nkrumah gave the music official encouragement, and bands multiplied.

In Nigeria, highlife took deep root in the east and west; stars such as Bobby Benson, Victor Olaiya, Rex Lawson, and later Osita Osadebe built huge followings, blending highlife with local languages and rhythms. During the 1960s and 1970s, guitar-band highlife—exemplified in Ghana by Nana Acheampong's predecessors, the African Brothers Band, and E.K. Nyame's earlier guitar bands—became dominant, while Fela Kuti fused highlife with jazz and funk in Lagos to create Afrobeat.

Decline and offshoots

Economic hardship, military rule, and curfews in Ghana in the late 1970s and 1980s damaged the live music scene; many musicians emigrated, especially to Germany, where they developed "burger-highlife", an electronic, synthesiser-driven variant. In the 1990s hiplife—highlife fused with hip hop, pioneered by Reggie Rockstone—became Ghana's dominant youth genre. Classic highlife nevertheless remains central to Ghanaian cultural identity, celebrated at funerals, festivals, and state occasions.

Characteristics

Highlife typically features prominent guitars playing interlocking melodic patterns, horn sections, and call-and-response vocals, over dance rhythms in 4/4 or 12/8 derived from traditional bell patterns. Lyrics are often proverbial, moralising, or celebratory, sung in Akan (Twi/Fante), Ga, Ewe, Igbo, Yoruba, or English depending on the band and audience.
