Stir frying

Stir frying (Chinese: 炒; pinyin: chǎo) is a Chinese cooking technique in which ingredients are fried in a small amount of very hot oil while being stirred or tossed in a wok. The technique originated in China and spread throughout East and Southeast Asia and, with the Chinese diaspora, the world. Stir frying is prized for its speed, its economical use of fuel, and the distinctive seared flavour — wok hei, "breath of the wok" — it imparts.

Technique

Ingredients are cut into small, uniform pieces so they cook quickly and evenly. The wok is heated until very hot, oil with a high smoke point is added, then aromatics such as garlic, ginger and scallions, followed by protein, then vegetables ordered by cooking time, and finally sauce. In the related technique of bao, the food is tossed with a rapid flip of the wok so it briefly leaps through the hottest air above the pan. Meat is often marinated and "velveted" — coated in cornstarch and briefly blanched — to keep it tender.

History

Stir frying developed in China by the Han dynasty era and became widespread during the Song dynasty, partly because the round-bottomed wok and the technique suited scarce fuel: small pieces cook in minutes over a fast, hot fire. The term entered English through Buwei Yang Chao's 1945 cookbook How to Cook and Eat in Chinese, which coined "stir-fry" as a translation of chǎo.

Dishes

Famous stir-fried dishes include Kung Pao chicken, chop suey, chow mein and lo mein noodles, fried rice, beef with broccoli, mapo tofu, and Thai pad see ew and pad thai (the latter adapted into Thai cuisine). Stir frying is also a base technique for countless home dishes built from whatever ingredients are at hand.
