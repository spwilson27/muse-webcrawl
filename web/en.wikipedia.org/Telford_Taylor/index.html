Telford Taylor (24 February 1908 – 23 May 1998) was an American lawyer and brigadier general best known as chief counsel for the prosecution in the Nuremberg Military Tribunals, the twelve subsequent trials of Nazi war criminals held after the International Military Tribunal.

Career

Educated at Williams College and Harvard Law School, Taylor worked for New Deal agencies and the Senate before joining the Army in 1942, serving in military intelligence with the Ultra signals programme. At Nuremberg he was a key deputy to chief prosecutor Robert H. Jackson at the first trial, then succeeded Jackson as chief prosecutor for the twelve subsequent trials (1946–1949), involving doctors, judges, industrialists, SS officers, and military commanders.

Later life and writings

Taylor became a professor of law at Columbia University, a prominent critic of McCarthyism and of the Vietnam War, and an advocate for international criminal law. His books include The Anatomy of the Nuremberg Trials (1992), a widely praised memoir and history, as well as Nuremberg and Vietnam: An American Tragedy (1970). He argued consistently that the Nuremberg principles should bind all nations, including the United States.
