Natufian culture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Natufian culture is an archaeological culture of the Late Epipaleolithic period in the Levant, dating to roughly 15,000 to 11,500 years before present. The Natufians are notable as among the earliest known sedentary or semi-sedentary hunter-gatherer communities, living in the region shortly before the emergence of agriculture in the Neolithic.

Discovery and chronology

The culture was named by British archaeologist Dorothy Garrod after Wadi en-Natuf near Shuqba Cave in the Judaean hills, where she excavated in the late 1920s and 1930s. Sites are distributed across the modern territories of Israel, Palestine, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria and Cyprus. The Natufian is commonly divided into an Early phase (c. 15,000–13,000 BP) and a Late phase (c. 13,000–11,500 BP), spanning the Bølling–Allerød warming and the Younger Dryas cold interval.

Settlements and subsistence

Natufian base camps such as Eynan (Ain Mallaha), Hayonim, and Hilazon Tachtit contain stone-built round houses, storage pits, and communal cemeteries. Subsistence rested on intensive harvesting of wild cereals (emmer wheat and barley) with flint sickles, grinding with stone mortars and pestles, and hunting of gazelle, deer, wild boar and smaller game. Ground stone tools, bone implements, and early domestic dogs are characteristic finds.

Burials and art

Natufian burials, sometimes decorated with shell, bone, and stone ornaments, include some of the earliest evidence of flower burials and of human–dog co-burial. Portable art consists of carved bone and stone figurines, including depictions of animals and human figures.

Legacy

Because Natufian communities already harvested wild cereals intensively and lived in permanent villages, the culture is often regarded as a direct precursor to the first farming communities of the Pre-Pottery Neolithic. Debate continues over whether the Younger Dryas climatic downturn pushed Natufian groups toward cultivation.
