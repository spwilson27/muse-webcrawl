The Heraion of Samos was a large sanctuary to the goddess Hera, situated on the island of Samos in the eastern Aegean, about 6 km west of the ancient city of Samos (modern Pythagoreio). It was one of the most important sanctuaries of the Greek world and the site of one of the largest temples ever built in Greece. Together with the Pythagoreion, it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1992.

History

Cult activity at the sanctuary began in the Bronze Age and was established by the Geometric period: the first temple (hekatompedos, "hundred-footer") dates to the 8th century BC. Around 570–560 BC the architects Rhoecus and Theodorus built a colossal dipteral temple in the Ionic order, destroyed by earthquake or fire a few decades later. Its replacement, begun under the tyrant Polycrates around 530 BC, measured roughly 55 by 105 metres with a double colonnade of more than 150 columns; it was never fully completed and was gradually pillaged in Roman and Byzantine times. Today only one column stands, at about half its original height of nearly 20 metres.

The sanctuary

A paved Sacred Way linked the sanctuary to the city, lined with votive statues including the famous Geneleos Group, whose originals are in the Samos archaeological museum. Excavations, conducted mainly by the German Archaeological Institute from 1910 onward, have uncovered the great altar, stoas, a Roman-era complex, and a wealth of votive offerings — bronze griffin heads, ivory carvings, and imports from Egypt, Syria, Cyprus and Persia — testifying to the sanctuary's wide connections.

Mythology

According to Samian tradition, Hera was born on the island beneath a lygos (chaste tree) by the river Imbrasus, and the sanctuary claimed to preserve the ancient wooden cult image of the goddess. Herodotus counted the temple among the greatest engineering achievements of the Greeks.
