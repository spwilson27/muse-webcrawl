Logic

Logic studies valid patterns of inference

Logic is the study of correct reasoning. It includes both formal and informal logic. Formal logic is the science of deductively valid inferences or logical truths. It studies how conclusions follow from premises independent of the specific content of the arguments. Informal logic, by contrast, examines reasoning and argumentation in natural language, including the identification of fallacies.

History

Logic was developed independently in several cultures. Aristotle's Organon founded syllogistic logic, which dominated Western thought for two millennia. In India, the Nyāya school developed theories of inference, and in China the Mohists studied argumentation. Medieval scholastic logicians in Europe and the Islamic world elaborated on the Aristotelian tradition. Modern symbolic logic began in the 19th century with George Boole's algebra of logic and Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879), which introduced quantifiers and function-argument analysis. The 20th century saw the development of mathematical logic, with landmark results such as Gödel's incompleteness theorems.

Branches

Propositional logic studies inferences based on logical connectives such as "and", "or" and "not". Predicate logic adds quantifiers ("for all", "there exists") and relations between objects. Modal logic extends classical logic with operators for necessity and possibility. Other major areas include proof theory, model theory, set theory, computability theory, and philosophical logic. Non-classical logics, such as intuitionistic, many-valued, fuzzy and paraconsistent logics, reject one or more classical principles.

Applications

Logic underpins mathematics, computer science (type theory, formal verification, logic programming), linguistics, law and philosophy. An argument is deductively valid if its conclusion cannot be false when its premises are true; a fallacy is an error in reasoning that appears persuasive.
