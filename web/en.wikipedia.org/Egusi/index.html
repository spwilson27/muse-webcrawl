Egusi

Egusi (also spelled egwusi) refers to the protein-rich seeds of certain cucurbitaceous plants (such as Citrullus lanatus, Cucumeropsis mannii, and Lagenaria siceraria), which, after being dried and ground, are used as a major ingredient in West African cuisine. The term is also used for the thickened soup or stew prepared with the ground seeds — one of the most popular dishes of Nigeria and neighbouring countries, especially among the Igbo, Yoruba, and Ibibio peoples.

The seeds

Egusi seeds are fat- and protein-rich endosperms of gourds, melons, and squashes cultivated widely in West and Central Africa. The seeds are extracted from the mature fruit, fermented or washed, dried, and then hulled and ground into a pale powder. The oil expressed from the seeds is also used in cooking. Egusi seeds are nutritionally valuable, containing roughly 50 percent oil and 30 percent protein by weight, along with significant amounts of essential amino acids and minerals.

Egusi soup

Egusi soup is prepared by combining the ground seeds with palm oil, leafy vegetables (such as bitter leaf, ugu/fluted pumpkin leaves, or spinach), chillies, onions, crayfish, stock, and meat or fish. Two principal methods exist: the frying method, in which the egusi paste is fried in palm oil before liquids are added, producing a loose, crumbly texture; and the boiling (set) method, in which balls of egusi paste are poached, producing distinct lumps. Regional and personal variants include the addition of periwinkle, snails, stockfish, or fermented locust beans (iru/ogiri).

Cultural significance

The dish is a staple at celebrations, weddings, and funerals across southern Nigeria, Ghana, Cameroon, and the wider West African diaspora, where it is typically served with "swallow" accompaniments such as pounded yam, fufu, eba, or amala. In 2023, a quantity of egusi seeds was sent to the International Space Station as part of an experiment on the behaviour of African heritage seeds in space, a first for a West African crop.
