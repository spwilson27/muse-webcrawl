Tabor Light

Icon of the Transfiguration on Mount Tabor

The Tabor Light (Greek: Φῶς τοῦ Θαβώρ) is the light revealed on Mount Tabor at the Transfiguration of Jesus, identified in Eastern Orthodox theology with the uncreated light (Greek: Ἄκτιστον Φῶς) seen by the saints in contemplative prayer. Saint Gregory Palamas taught that the light of Tabor is not a created phenomenon but the very glory and energy of God, perceived by the apostles Peter, James and John, and likewise accessible to purified Christians.

The hesychast controversy

In the 14th century, the Calabrian monk Barlaam argued that any claim to experience God directly was impossible and that the light of the Transfiguration was created. Palamas responded that God is imparticipable in his essence but participable in his uncreated energies, of which the Tabor Light is the pre-eminent manifestation. Councils held in Constantinople in 1341, 1347 and 1351 vindicated the Palamite position.

In Orthodox theology

The Tabor Light is closely connected with the doctrines of the essence–energies distinction and of theosis: by grace, the saints participate in the same uncreated light that radiated from Christ, without becoming God by nature. Icons of the Transfiguration traditionally depict Christ within a mandorla of white and gold rays, representing this divine radiance.
