Gregory Palamas

Gregory Palamas (Greek: Γρηγόριος Παλαμᾶς; c. 1296 – 1357 or 1359) was a Byzantine Greek theologian and Eastern Orthodox cleric of the late Byzantine period. A monk of Mount Athos and later Archbishop of Thessalonica, he is famous for his defence of hesychasm — the monastic practice of contemplative prayer — and for articulating the essence–energies distinction, which became dogma in the Eastern Orthodox Church. He is venerated as a saint, with his feast day on 14 November and on the Second Sunday of Great Lent.

Life

Palamas was born in Constantinople into a senatorial family from Asia Minor. After his father's death, he was raised under the patronage of Emperor Andronikos II and educated in philosophy and theology. Around age twenty he renounced a court career for monastic life on Mount Athos, where he practised the Jesus Prayer under guidance and also spent time in the sketes around Beroea. Threatened by Turkish raids, he relocated to Thessalonica and was ordained a priest in 1326, returning later to Athos and becoming abbot of the Esphigmenou monastery.

Hesychast controversy

In the 1330s the Calabrian philosopher-monk Barlaam attacked the hesychasts' claims to experience uncreated divine light, deriding them as omphalopsychoi ("navel-gazers"). Palamas responded with the Triads in Defence of the Holy Hesychasts, arguing that the light of the Transfiguration is uncreated, that God is participable in his energies though unknowable in his essence, and that bodily participation in prayer is legitimate. A series of councils followed a turbulent course through the Byzantine civil war of 1341–1347: Palamas was at times condemned and imprisoned under his opponents' ascendancy, but councils in 1347 and decisively in 1351 vindicated his teaching, and his chief opponent Gregory Akindynos was anathematised.

Later years

In 1347 Palamas was appointed Archbishop of Thessalonica, though civil unrest delayed his entry into the city. In 1354, travelling to Constantinople, he was captured by Ottoman Turks and held for about a year in Anatolia, where he engaged in theological dialogues with Muslim scholars before being ransomed. He died at Thessalonica in 1357 (some sources give 1359). In 1368 Patriarch Philotheos glorified him as a saint.

Theology and legacy

Palamas's synthesis — grounded in Scripture and the Church Fathers — remains normative in Orthodox theology, shaping its doctrines of grace, deification (theosis), and sacramental life. His Homilies and ascetic writings are still widely read. His theology was little known in the West until the 20th century, when the work of John Meyendorff and others made "Palamism" a central topic in Orthodox studies and ecumenical dialogue.
