Essence–energies distinction

Diagram of the essence–energies distinction

In Eastern Orthodox theology, the essence–energies distinction is the teaching that the divine essence (ousia) of God is unknowable and imparticipable, while his uncreated energies (energeiai) — such as life, wisdom and grace — truly proceed from God and can be participated in by creatures. The distinction was systematically formulated by Saint Gregory Palamas in the 14th century and confirmed by the Palamite councils of Constantinople (1341–1351).

Background

Antecedents appear in the Cappadocian Fathers: Saint Basil of Caesarea wrote that "we know our God from His energies, but we do not undertake to approach His essence", and Saint Gregory of Nyssa and Saint Maximus the Confessor developed similar distinctions between what God is in himself and how he acts.

Significance

The distinction safeguards both divine transcendence and real communion with God: in theosis, the deified Christian participates in God's energies, supremely the uncreated light of Mount Tabor, without ceasing to be a creature or comprehending the divine essence. Critics in the West have at times argued that the distinction compromises divine simplicity; Orthodox theologians reply that the energies are not created effects nor parts of God, but God himself in his self-communication.
