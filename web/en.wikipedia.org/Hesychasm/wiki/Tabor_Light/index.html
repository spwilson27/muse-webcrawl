Tabor Light

In Eastern Orthodox theology, the Tabor Light (also Light of Tabor, Uncreated Light) is the light revealed on Mount Tabor at the Transfiguration of Jesus, identified with the light seen by Paul at his conversion and with the light experienced by saints in contemplative prayer. According to the doctrine articulated by Gregory Palamas, this light is not a created physical phenomenon but the uncreated energy of God himself — the glory and radiance of the divine nature, in which human beings can truly participate without comprehending the divine essence.

Biblical basis

The Gospels describe Christ's face shining "like the sun" and his garments becoming white as light on the mountain (Matthew 17). Orthodox hymnography and patristic commentary — for example in Maximus the Confessor and John of Damascus — interpreted this radiance as the eternal glory of Christ's divinity, briefly unveiled to the disciples, and as a foretaste of the eschatological glory in which the righteous "will shine like the sun" (Matthew 13:43).

Hesychast controversy

The nature of the light became the centre of the 14th-century hesychast controversy. The hesychast monks of Mount Athos maintained that, through the Jesus Prayer, the purified could perceive the same uncreated light. Barlaam of Calabria objected that any light seen with bodily eyes must be created and that claiming to see God's uncreated glory was heretical. Gregory Palamas defended the monks: God is invisible and incomprehensible in his essence, but his uncreated energies — of which the Taboric light is the paradigm — are genuinely God and genuinely communicable. Councils at Constantinople in 1341, 1347, and 1351 endorsed Palamas's teaching, which remains dogma in the Orthodox Church.

Significance

The doctrine undergirds the Orthodox understanding of deification (theosis): salvation is not merely a legal status but a real participation in the uncreated life and light of God. The Transfiguration (6 August) is one of the Twelve Great Feasts, and St Gregory Palamas is commemorated on the Second Sunday of Great Lent as the champion of the doctrine. The theme recurs in Orthodox spirituality, from the Philokalia to Seraphim of Sarov's conversation with Motovilov, in which the saint is described as radiant with the light of the Holy Spirit.
