Hesychasm
Tradition: Eastern Orthodox Christianity
Key figures: Gregory Palamas, Gregory of Sinai, John Climacus
Core practice: The Jesus Prayer
Center: Mount Athos

Hesychasm is a contemplative tradition of prayer in Eastern Orthodox Christianity, centred on stillness (hesychia, Greek for "quietness" or "rest") and the repetitive invocation of the Jesus Prayer: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner." Practised especially by monks, hesychasm aims at union with God through purification of the mind and heart, and — in its developed theological form — holds that the faithful may experience the uncreated light of God, the same light witnessed by the disciples at the Transfiguration of Christ on Mount Tabor.

History

The roots of hesychasm lie in the early monastic movements of Egypt, Palestine, and Sinai. Writers such as Evagrius Ponticus, John Climacus (author of The Ladder of Divine Ascent), and later Symeon the New Theologian described methods of interior prayer joining the mind to the heart. In the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries the practice was systematised by monks of Mount Athos, including Gregory of Sinai, who spread its techniques through the Greek and Slavic worlds.

The Hesychast Controversy

In the 1330s the Calabrian monk and philosopher Barlaam criticised hesychast methods and the claim that practitioners could perceive divine light. Gregory Palamas, an Athonite monk and later Archbishop of Thessalonica, defended the tradition by distinguishing between God's essence, which remains wholly unknowable, and God's energies, which are truly divine yet participable. A series of councils at Constantinople (1341, 1347, and 1351) vindicated Palamas's teaching, which became authoritative doctrine in the Orthodox Church.

Practice

Hesychast prayer typically involves controlled breathing, a bowed posture, and the rhythmic repetition of the Jesus Prayer coordinated with the heartbeat, under the guidance of a spiritual elder (geron or starets). The goal is not ecstasy but watchfulness (nepsis) and the "prayer of the heart," in which invocation becomes continual. Texts of the tradition were gathered in the eighteenth-century anthology known as the Philokalia, which fuelled a revival of hesychasm in Greece, Russia, and beyond.
