Theosis (Eastern Christian theology)

Theosis (Greek: θέωσις), also called deification or divinisation, is a transformative process whose aim is likeness to or union with God, as taught by the Eastern Orthodox Church and Eastern Catholic Churches. It is the central doctrine of Orthodox soteriology: salvation is understood as the progressive participation of human persons, by grace, in the uncreated energies of God, becoming "partakers of the divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4) while remaining creatures.

Doctrine

The classic patristic formula is Athanasius of Alexandria's: "He was made man, that we men might be made gods" (On the Incarnation). The Fathers taught that the Son of God, by assuming human nature, opened it to transformation; Christians in turn are deified through baptism, the Eucharist, prayer, and ascetic struggle, in synergy (cooperation) with divine grace. Deification is by grace and not by nature: the deified remain human and never become God in essence, but they participate in God through his energies — the essence–energies distinction systematised by Gregory Palamas. The process is often described in three stages drawn from monastic literature: purification (katharsis), illumination (theoria), and deification (theosis) in the strict sense.

History

The theme runs from Irenaeus and Clement of Alexandria through the Cappadocians, Maximus the Confessor, and Symeon the New Theologian to Palamas and the hesychast tradition. The holy people of God in the Old Testament, the Transfiguration, and the lives of the saints — whose relics and icons the Church venerates — are presented as anticipations and fruits of deification. The doctrine waned in some later Orthodox manuals influenced by Western scholastic categories but was recovered in the 20th-century neo-patristic revival associated with Georges Florovsky, Vladimir Lossky, and John Meyendorff.

Comparison with Western traditions

Western Christianity contains analogous teachings — Roman Catholic doctrines of divinisation in Aquinas and the Catechism ("partakers of the divine nature"), Wesleyan Christian perfection, and C. S. Lewis's popular formulation that God became man so that men might become "little Christs" — but theosis has a distinctively central, experiential, and liturgical place in Orthodoxy, closely bound to hesychasm, the Jesus Prayer, and the sacraments. Ecumenical dialogues have often found the doctrine a point of convergence rather than division.
