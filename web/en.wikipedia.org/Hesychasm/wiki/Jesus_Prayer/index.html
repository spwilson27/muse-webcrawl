Jesus Prayer

The Jesus Prayer, also known as the Prayer of the Heart, is a short formulaic prayer esteemed and advocated in Eastern Christianity and Catholicism:

"Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner."

The prayer is repeated continually as a means of practising Saint Paul's injunction to "pray without ceasing" (1 Thessalonians 5:17). It is most common in the Eastern Orthodox tradition, where it forms the core of the hesychast practice of contemplative prayer, but it is also used in Eastern Catholicism, Roman Catholicism, and increasingly among Anglicans and other Western Christians.

Origins and history

The prayer's roots lie in the desert monasticism of Egypt, Palestine, and Sinai in the 4th–6th centuries: short cries to God such as "O God, make speed to save me" and the tax collector's cry "God, have mercy on me, a sinner" (Luke 18:13) were repeated by the Desert Fathers as a defence against distracting and tempting thoughts. The tradition was systematised by writers such as John Climacus, Hesychius of Sinai, and Philotheus of Sinai, and in the 14th century its practitioners — the hesychasts, defended by Gregory Palamas — linked the prayer with the vision of uncreated divine light. The classic collection of texts on the prayer, the Philokalia, and the 19th-century Russian work The Way of a Pilgrim spread the practice widely among the laity.

Practice

Traditional instruction, ideally given by a spiritual father or mother, progresses through stages: vocal prayer, mental prayer, and "prayer of the heart," in which the prayer becomes spontaneous and self-acting. The practitioner typically sits with head bowed, coordinating the words with the breath — "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God" on the inhalation, "have mercy on me, a sinner" on the exhalation — and uses a prayer rope (Greek komboskini, Russian chotki) of 33, 50, 100, or 300 knots to count repetitions. The aim is unceasing interior prayer and inner stillness (hesychia), not ecstasy or altered states; teachers warn against pursuing experiences or practising the breathing technique without guidance.

Theological basis

The prayer combines the Christological confession of Jesus as Lord and Son of God with a plea for mercy grounded in the parable of the Pharisee and the publican, expressing humility and repentance. Veneration of the Holy Name — the conviction that the name of Jesus invoked in faith bears his presence and power — underlies the practice, which was the centre of the early-20th-century "Name-Worshipping" (Imiaslavie) controversy in Russia.

Forms of the prayer

Shorter and longer variants exist, from "Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me" to "Lord Jesus Christ, Son and Word of the living God, through the prayers of the Theotokos and all the saints, have mercy on me." The essential kernel is the invocation of Jesus' name with the plea for mercy.
