Hesychasm

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hesychasm (Greek: ἡσυχασμός, romanized: hēsychasmós, from ἡσυχία, hēsychía, "stillness, rest, quiet, silence") is a tradition of prayer in the Eastern Church and in particular the Eastern Orthodox Church, practised by the hesychast (Greek: ἡσυχαστής), a person devoted to it. Based on Jesus's injunction in the Gospel of Matthew to go into one's closet to pray, hesychasm, in retreat to solitude, developed as a distinct — and much-debated — tradition of prayer by the 14th century.

The hesychast defends the position that, with man in a state of stillness and freed from the senses, illumination by the "uncreated light" of the Holy Spirit may be attained, as seen by the apostles on Mount Tabor at the Transfiguration of Christ — an experience of the uncreated energies of God. Hesychasm was defended theologically by St Gregory Palamas and condemned by his opponents, notably Barlaam of Calabria. A series of councils in Constantinople (1341–1351) upheld Palamas's teaching, which became part of the dogmatic tradition of the Eastern Orthodox Church.

Etymology and background

The term derives from the Greek hesychia, stillness or quiet, a concept prominent in the writings of the Desert Fathers and in the Philokalia, the anthology of spiritual texts compiled in the 18th century. Monastic stillness was pursued from the earliest centuries of Egyptian and Byzantine monasticism — by Evagrius Ponticus, John Climacus (author of The Ladder of Divine Ascent), and Symeon the New Theologian — as the setting in which unceasing prayer and the vision of divine light became possible.

Practice

The hesychast retreats into solitude, ideally under the guidance of a spiritual father (a staretz or geron), and practises the Jesus Prayer — "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner" — repeated continuously, often coordinated with breathing and posture, with the aim of uniting the mind (nous) with the heart. The practice belongs to a broader ascetic discipline of watchfulness (nepsis), fasting, and psalmody. Its goal is theosis, the deification of the human person by grace. Practitioners claimed that, purified by this discipline, they could perceive the uncreated light of Tabor with their bodily eyes.

The Hesychast controversy

In the 1330s the practice was attacked by Barlaam of Seminara, a Calabrian Greek scholar who had come to Constantinople and who ridiculed the psychosomatic prayer techniques of the monks and denied that created beings could see the uncreated light of God. Gregory Palamas (1296–1359), an Athonite monk, defended the hesychasts in his Triads in Defence of the Holy Hesychasts. Councils at Constantinople in 1341 condemned Barlaam; later councils (1347 and 1351) vindicated Palamas against further opponents including Gregory Akindynos and Nikephoros Gregoras. Palamas, by then Archbishop of Thessalonica, was canonized in 1368, and his Sunday (the Second Sunday of Great Lent) is celebrated in the Orthodox Church.

Essence and energies

Palamas's theological resolution rests on the distinction between God's essence — utterly transcendent and unknowable — and God's energies or activities, which are uncreated yet truly God, and in which human beings can genuinely participate. Critics in the Latin West, and some modern historians, have debated whether this distinction implies a division in God; Orthodox theologians maintain that essence and energies are distinct but inseparable. The distinction underlies Orthodox teaching on grace, the sacraments, and deification.

Later history

Hesychasm remained central to Athonite monasticism and spread through the Slavic world. The 18th-century publication of the Philokalia by Nicodemus of the Holy Mountain and Macarius of Corinth (1782), and its Slavonic and Russian translations, fuelled a hesychast revival; the anonymous Russian classic The Way of a Pilgrim popularized the Jesus Prayer in the 19th century. In the 20th century the practice influenced theologians of the Orthodox diaspora and drew wider Christian interest in contemplative prayer.
