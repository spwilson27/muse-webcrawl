Evidentiality

In linguistics, evidentiality is the grammatical marking of the source of information for a statement: whether the speaker witnessed an event directly, inferred it from evidence, or heard it reported by others. In many languages evidential marking is obligatory — a speaker cannot utter a neutral sentence about a past event without indicating how they know it.

Types of evidentials

Typologists commonly distinguish direct (visual or sensory) evidence, inferred evidence (from physical results or reasoning), and reported (hearsay) evidence. Some languages make finer distinctions: Quechua, for example, uses the suffix -mi for direct personal knowledge, -si for hearsay, and -chá for conjecture. Tariana, an Arawakan language of Amazonia, has an unusually rich system distinguishing visual, non-visual sensory, inferred, assumed, and reported evidence, combined with tense.

Distribution

Grammatical evidentials are found in many Indigenous languages of the Americas, in Tibetan and other Himalayan languages, in Turkish and the Balkan languages (where the renarrative mood plays this role), and in Japanese and Korean (with more limited systems). Standard English lacks obligatory grammatical evidentiality, expressing source of knowledge lexically instead ("I saw that...", "apparently...", "they say...").

Relation to epistemology

Evidentiality is of interest to philosophy because it shows how grammars can force speakers to track the justification of their beliefs — a theme that also arises in theological debates such as the evidential problem of evil.
