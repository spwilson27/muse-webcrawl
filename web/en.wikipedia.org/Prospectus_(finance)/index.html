Prospectus (finance)

A prospectus discloses the details of a securities offering.

In finance, a prospectus is a disclosure document used by a company or fund that is issuing securities to describe the security, the issuer's business and finances, and the terms and risks of the offering to potential investors. Under the U.S. Securities Act of 1933, a prospectus forming part of a registration statement must be filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission before shares can be sold to the public in an initial public offering.

Contents

A prospectus typically includes audited financial statements, a description of the business and management, use of proceeds, capitalization, dilution, underwriting arrangements, and an extensive risk factors section. Material misstatements or omissions can give rise to strict liability for the issuer and underwriters, making due diligence on prospectus contents a central part of securities practice.

Types

A preliminary prospectus (the "red herring") is circulated during the waiting period before the registration becomes effective; the final prospectus accompanies or precedes confirmations of sale. Mutual funds and exchange-traded funds issue their own prospectuses, including simplified summary prospectuses, describing objectives, fees, and risks.
