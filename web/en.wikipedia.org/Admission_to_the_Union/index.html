Admission to the Union

Admission to the Union is provided by the Admissions Clause of the United States Constitution, which authorises the Congress to admit new states into the Union. Since the establishment of the United States in 1776, the number of states has expanded from the original thirteen to fifty, most admitted from territories acquired or organised by the federal government.

Constitutional basis

Article IV, Section 3 provides that new states may be admitted by Congress, but no new state may be formed within the jurisdiction of an existing state, or by the junction of states or parts of states, without the consent of the state legislatures concerned as well as of Congress. Under the equal footing doctrine, articulated in cases such as Coyle v. Smith (1911), each new state enters the Union with the same sovereignty and status as the original states.

Process

The usual process involves a territory adopting a constitution and petitioning Congress, which passes an enabling act or admission act, sometimes followed by a presidential proclamation — as when Hawaii and Alaska were admitted in 1959, the most recent admissions. A handful of states, including Vermont, Texas, and California, were admitted without first existing as organised US territories in the usual form.
