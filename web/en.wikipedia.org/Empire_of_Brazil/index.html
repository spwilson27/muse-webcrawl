The Empire of Brazil was a 19th-century state that broadly comprised the territory of modern Brazil and, until 1828, Uruguay. It was established in 1822 when the Portuguese prince regent Dom Pedro proclaimed Brazilian independence, and it endured until 1889, when a military coup proclaimed a republic. The empire was a constitutional monarchy under two emperors, Pedro I and his son Pedro II, whose 49-year reign brought unusual political stability to the region.

The Empire of Brazil in the nineteenth century

Independence and Pedro I

The Portuguese court had fled to Rio de Janeiro in 1808 during the Napoleonic Wars, elevating Brazil's status. When King João VI returned to Lisbon in 1821 and the Portuguese parliament tried to reduce Brazil to colonial status again, his son Pedro refused to leave — the "Fico" ("I am staying") — and on 7 September 1822 proclaimed independence on the banks of the Ipiranga River. He was crowned Emperor Pedro I in December 1822. A constitution followed in 1824, granting the emperor a unique "Moderating Power". Pedro's reign was troubled by the loss of the Cisplatine Province (Uruguay), financial problems, and conflicts with the legislature; he abdicated in 1831 in favour of his five-year-old son.

Regency and Pedro II

A turbulent regency (1831–1840) saw regional revolts across the country. Pedro II was declared of age and crowned in 1841, and his long reign brought consolidation: rebellions were suppressed, the economy grew on coffee exports, railways and telegraphs spread, and Brazil, allied with Argentina and Uruguay, defeated Paraguay in the Paraguayan War (1864–1870). Brazil was the last Western country to abolish slavery: the trade was ended in 1850, followed by the Law of the Free Womb (1871), the Sexagenarian Law (1885), and finally the Golden Law of 1888, signed by Princess Isabel, which abolished slavery entirely.

Fall of the monarchy

Abolition alienated slave-owning planters, while the military and urban republicans grew disaffected and the emperor aged without a male heir. On 15 November 1889 Marshal Deodoro da Fonseca proclaimed the republic in a nearly bloodless coup; Pedro II accepted exile and died in Paris in 1891.

Legacy

The empire preserved Brazil's territorial unity while Spanish America fragmented into many republics, and it left durable institutions and symbols — including Brazil's green-and-yellow colours, derived from the imperial flag.
