Thalassaemia

Thalassaemia (also spelled thalassemia) is a group of inherited blood disorders characterised by reduced or absent production of one of the globin chains of haemoglobin. The two main types are alpha thalassaemia, caused by defects in the alpha-globin genes on chromosome 16, and beta thalassaemia, caused by mutations in the beta-globin gene (HBB) on chromosome 11. The resulting imbalance of globin chains causes anaemia, ineffective red-cell production, and haemolysis. The name derives from the Greek thalassa ("sea"), because the condition was first characterised in people of Mediterranean origin.

Signs and symptoms

Severity ranges from silent carrier states with no symptoms, through mild microcytic anaemia (thalassaemia trait), to transfusion-dependent thalassaemia major (historically called Cooley's anaemia), in which severe anaemia presents in infancy with failure to thrive, pallor, jaundice, skeletal deformities from expanded bone marrow, and enlargement of the liver and spleen. A moderately severe form, haemoglobin H disease, arises in alpha thalassaemia.

Distribution and genetics

Thalassaemias are among the most common monogenic disorders worldwide. They are most prevalent in the Mediterranean, the Middle East, South and Southeast Asia, and sub-Saharan Africa — a distribution thought to reflect the protection that carrier states confer against severe malaria. Inheritance is autosomal recessive for beta thalassaemia.

Treatment

Severe forms are managed with regular red-cell transfusions and iron chelation therapy to control transfusional iron overload. Allogeneic stem cell transplantation can be curative. Gene therapy approaches, including lentiviral beta-globin addition (betibeglogene autotemcel) and CRISPR-based editing (exagamglogene autotemcel), have received regulatory approvals in recent years. Carrier screening and prenatal diagnosis programmes have sharply reduced births of affected children in several countries.
