Controlled Substances Act

The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) is the statute establishing federal U.S. drug policy under which the manufacture, importation, possession, use and distribution of certain substances is regulated. It was passed by the 91st United States Congress as Title II of the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970 and signed into law by President Richard Nixon. The CSA is codified at 21 U.S.C. § 801 et seq.

Schedules

The Act created five schedules (classifications), numbered I through V, with decreasing restrictions and penalties as the schedule number increases. Placement depends on a substance's accepted medical use, potential for abuse, and safety. Schedule I substances — such as heroin, LSD and (federally) cannabis — are deemed to have high abuse potential and no currently accepted medical use. Schedule II includes drugs such as morphine, oxycodone, fentanyl and amphetamine, which have accepted medical uses but high abuse potential. Schedules III, IV and V include progressively less restricted medications such as ketamine, benzodiazepines and certain cough preparations.

Administration

The Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA), together with the Food and Drug Administration and the Department of Health and Human Services, administers the scheduling process; the Attorney General may add, remove or transfer substances between schedules. The CSA also implements United States obligations under the Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs and other international treaties. Substances may be controlled on an emergency basis, and the Act provides for registration of manufacturers, prescribers and dispensers of controlled substances.
