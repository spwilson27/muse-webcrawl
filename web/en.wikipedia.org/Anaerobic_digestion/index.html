Anaerobic digestion

Anaerobic digestion is a sequence of processes by which microorganisms break down biodegradable material in the absence of oxygen. The process is used for industrial or domestic purposes to manage waste or to produce fuels. The digestion produces biogas, consisting mainly of methane and carbon dioxide, and a nutrient-rich residue called digestate that can be used as fertiliser.

Stages

  1. Hydrolysis — complex organic molecules are broken into simple sugars, amino acids, and fatty acids.
  2. Acidogenesis — acidogenic bacteria convert these products into volatile fatty acids, ammonia (see ammonia), carbon dioxide, and hydrogen sulfide.
  3. Acetogenesis — acetogens produce acetic acid, the main substrate for methane formation.
  4. Methanogenesis — methanogenic archaea convert acetate, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide into methane.

Applications

Anaerobic digestion is used to treat sewage sludge, food waste, and animal manures; to stabilise waste in landfills; and increasingly to produce renewable natural gas from purpose-grown crops and residues. Capturing methane that would otherwise escape to the atmosphere is a significant climate benefit, since methane is a potent greenhouse gas.

History

Biogas was used to heat bath water in Assyria as early as the 10th century BC, and scientific study of digestion began in the 19th century. Farm-scale digesters spread widely in China and India in the 20th century, and large industrial plants are now common in Europe and North America.
