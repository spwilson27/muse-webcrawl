Loiasis

Loiasis is a filarial disease caused by the parasitic nematode Loa loa, which is transmitted to humans by deer flies (genus Chrysops) in the rainforest belt of West and Central Africa. It is often called the "African eye worm" because the adult worm may visibly migrate across the conjunctiva of the eye.

Signs and symptoms

Many infections are asymptomatic, particularly in visitors, though residents of endemic areas may carry microfilariae in the blood without symptoms. Characteristic features include Calabar swellings — transient, itchy, non-pitting angioedema, usually on the limbs, which arises from allergic reactions to the migrating worms — and the striking subconjunctival migration of an adult worm across the eyeball, which, while alarming, rarely damages vision. Other manifestations include itching, muscle and joint pains, fatigue, and, with chronic infection, complications affecting the heart, kidneys and nervous system.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on a history of travel to or residence in an endemic area, characteristic symptoms, eosinophilia, and detection of microfilariae in daytime blood samples or identification of an adult worm in the eye or skin. The standard treatment is diethylcarbamazine (DEC), which kills both microfilariae and adult worms; however, in patients with very high microfilarial loads, rapid killing of parasites can provoke severe encephalopathy, so caution and sometimes pretreatment to lower the parasite burden (for example with albendazole or apheresis) are required.

Loiasis complicates mass treatment for other filarial diseases: ivermectin campaigns against onchocerciasis have caused serious adverse events in people heavily co-infected with Loa loa, prompting screening strategies in co-endemic areas.
