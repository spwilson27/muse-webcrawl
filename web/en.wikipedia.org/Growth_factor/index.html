Growth factor

TypeSignalling protein
FunctionStimulates cell growth, proliferation, differentiation
First describedNerve growth factor (Rita Levi-Montalcini, 1950s)
ExamplesNGF, EGF, PDGF, FGF, VEGF, IGF, TGF-β

A growth factor is a naturally occurring protein (occasionally a steroid hormone) capable of stimulating cell growth, proliferation, healing and differentiation. Growth factors act as signalling molecules between cells: they bind to specific receptors on the surface of target cells and trigger intracellular pathways that govern the cell cycle, survival and development.

History

The first growth factor, nerve growth factor (NGF), was discovered by Rita Levi-Montalcini and Viktor Hamburger in the 1950s through its dramatic effect on growing nerve fibres; Levi-Montalcini and Stanley Cohen, who discovered epidermal growth factor (EGF) and developed the assays, shared the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Major classes

Well-known growth factors include platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), released in wound clotting; fibroblast growth factors (FGF), central to embryonic patterning and angiogenesis; vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), the key driver of new blood-vessel formation; insulin-like growth factors (IGF-1 and IGF-2), mediators of growth hormone action; transforming growth factors (TGF-α and TGF-β), which can stimulate or inhibit proliferation depending on context; and colony-stimulating factors, which drive blood-cell production.

Signalling

Most growth-factor receptors are receptor tyrosine kinases (as for EGF, PDGF, FGF and insulin-family signals) or, for the TGF-β superfamily, serine/threonine kinases. Activation launches cascades such as the Ras–MAPK and PI3K–Akt pathways, altering gene expression. Growth factors may act on the secreting cell itself (autocrine), on neighbours (paracrine), or at a distance via the blood (endocrine).

Medical significance

Because growth factors control cell division, their malfunction is central to cancer: many oncogenes encode growth factors, their receptors or downstream signalling proteins, and targeted therapies (such as anti-VEGF and HER2-directed drugs) block these signals. Clinically, recombinant growth factors are used to stimulate blood-cell recovery after chemotherapy (G-CSF, erythropoietin) and investigated for wound healing and regenerative medicine.
