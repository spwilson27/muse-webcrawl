Independent regulatory agency

An independent regulatory agency (or independent regulatory authority, regulator or commission) is a public authority responsible for exercising autonomous authority over a sector of economic or social activity, separated to some degree from direct control by the elected government. Such bodies are typically charged with setting and enforcing rules, licensing participants, and protecting consumers or market integrity, while being accountable to the legislature and subject to judicial review.

Rationale

Independence is justified on several grounds: technical expertise, consistency of policy across electoral cycles, credible commitment (for example in monetary policy and utility regulation), and insulation of day-to-day decisions from political pressure. Critics point to a democratic deficit, the risk of regulatory capture by the regulated industry, and weak accountability.

Examples

Examples include central banks such as the Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank, securities regulators, competition authorities, energy regulators such as Ofgem in Great Britain, and telecommunications regulators. Their degree of statutory independence, appointment procedures and funding (often industry levies rather than general taxation) vary widely between countries.
