Conceptual art
Years active1960s–present
Key figuresMarcel Duchamp (precursor), Sol LeWitt, Joseph Kosuth, Lawrence Weiner, Art & Language
Core ideaThe concept or idea behind the work takes precedence over traditional aesthetic and material concerns

Conceptual art, also referred to as conceptualism, is art in which the concept or idea involved in the work takes precedence over traditional aesthetic, technical, and material concerns. Many works of conceptual art, sometimes called installations, may be constructed by anyone simply by following a set of written instructions — a method central to Sol LeWitt's definition in his 1967 essay "Paragraphs on Conceptual Art".

Origins

The French artist Marcel Duchamp is usually seen as the movement's chief precursor: his "readymades", beginning with Bicycle Wheel (1913) and Fountain (1917), a urinal submitted to an exhibition, challenged the definition of art itself. The term "conceptual art" was coined by Henry Flynt in 1961 and popularised by LeWitt and the critic Lucy Lippard, whose book Six Years: The Dematerialization of the Art Object (1973) chronicled the movement.

Characteristics

Conceptual artists rejected the commercialism and object-centred focus of the art market, questioning the role of the artist, the gallery and the unique art object. Language became a primary medium: Joseph Kosuth's One and Three Chairs (1965) presents a chair, a photograph of a chair and a dictionary definition of "chair". Lawrence Weiner worked almost exclusively with text statements; the British collective Art & Language pursued an explicitly theoretical, text-based practice.

Other strands include performance art, land art and institutional critique, in which artists such as Hans Haacke exposed the political and financial networks of museums.

Legacy and criticism

Conceptual art profoundly influenced subsequent practice: instruction-based works, documentation as artwork, and art as idea are now commonplace, and artists such as the Young British Artists and Ai Weiwei drew on its strategies. Critics have variously attacked it as anti-visual, elitist or the institutionalisation of Duchamp's joke; the Stuckist movement, founded in 1999, explicitly opposed it in favour of painting.
