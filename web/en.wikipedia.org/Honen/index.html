Hōnen

Hōnen (法然, May 13, 1133 – January 29, 1212), born Seishimaru, was a Japanese Buddhist monk and the founder of the Jōdo-shū (Pure Land) school of Buddhism in Japan. He taught that liberation could be attained simply by reciting the name of Amida Buddha — the practice known as nembutsu — and thereby opened the Buddhist path to salvation to common people, including women, fishermen and others excluded from the monastic disciplines of his age.

Early life

Hōnen was born in 1133 in the town of Kume in Mimasaka Province (present-day Okayama Prefecture). His father, Uruma no Tokikuni, was a provincial official who was assassinated when Hōnen was nine years old. On his deathbed, his father urged him not to seek revenge but to seek enlightenment. The boy entered a monastery, and at the age of fourteen he was sent to study at Enryaku-ji on Mount Hiei, the great centre of Tendai Buddhism.

Search for a teaching for all

Distinguished as a scholar-monk, Hōnen nevertheless became convinced that the elaborate practices of the established schools placed salvation beyond the reach of ordinary people living in the age of mappō, the latter days of the Dharma. After years of study, in 1175 he read the commentary of the Chinese master Shandao, which taught exclusive reliance on the nembutsu, and experienced a conversion to the Pure Land teaching. He left Mount Hiei and settled at Yoshimizu in the eastern hills of Kyoto, teaching all who came to him.

Teaching and the Senchakushū

In 1198 Hōnen composed his principal work, the Senchaku Hongan Nembutsushū ("A Collection of Passages on the Nembutsu Chosen in the Original Vow"), arguing that amid the many Buddhist practices, Amida Buddha's Original Vow had selected the recitation of his name, "Namu Amida Butsu", as the practice by which anyone could be born in the Pure Land. His following grew rapidly and included courtiers, warriors and commoners, drawing hostility from the established temples.

Persecution, exile and death

In 1204 the monks of Mount Hiei petitioned against the exclusive nembutsu teaching, and in 1207 the court of the retired Emperor Go-Toba suppressed the movement: several of Hōnen's disciples were executed and Hōnen himself, then seventy-five, was exiled to Tosa Province on Shikoku. He was pardoned in 1211 and returned to Kyoto, where he died the following year, in 1212. Among his leading disciples was Shinran, founder of the Jōdo Shinshū school.

Legacy

Hōnen is revered as the patriarch of Japanese Pure Land Buddhism. His main temple is Chion-in in Kyoto, and his influence on later Kamakura Buddhism — through Shinran and through the general turn toward simple, faith-centred practice — was profound.
