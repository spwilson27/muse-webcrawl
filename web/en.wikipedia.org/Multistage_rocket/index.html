Multistage rocket


Purpose: Achieve high delta-v for orbital and deep-space flight
Key concept: Discard empty stages to shed mass
Equation: Tsiolkovsky rocket equation

A multistage rocket (also step rocket) is a launch vehicle that uses two or more rocket stages, each containing its own engines and propellant. When one stage's fuel is exhausted, it is jettisoned so that the remaining stages do not waste energy accelerating empty structure.

Why staging is needed

According to the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, the velocity change a rocket can achieve depends on the ratio of its initial to final mass. A single stage carrying enough propellant to reach orbit would need an impractically light structure; staging multiplies the effective mass ratios of the stages, making orbital velocity attainable. Most orbital launchers use two to four stages.

Configurations

In serial (tandem) staging, stages stack vertically and fire one after another, as in the Saturn V. In parallel staging, strap-on boosters fire alongside a core stage and drop away, as with the Space Shuttle's solid rocket boosters. Upper stages optimize their engines for vacuum with larger nozzles. Trade-offs include added complexity, cost, and failure modes at separation events.

History

Multi-stage fireworks and rockets were described in medieval China and by early modern experimenters; Konstantin Tsiolkovsky and Robert Goddard analysed staging theoretically, and Goddard patented a multistage design in 1914. The first multistage rocket to reach space was the American Bumper in 1949, a V-2 with a WAC Corporal second stage.
