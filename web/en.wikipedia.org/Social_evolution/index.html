Social evolution

Social evolution
FieldAnthropology; sociology; evolutionary studies
Key figuresHerbert Spencer, Lewis Henry Morgan, Leslie White, Elman Service
RelatedCultural evolution; sociocultural evolution

Social evolution is the idea that societies and cultures change over time in patterned ways, and the study of how and why they do so. Nineteenth-century thinkers such as Herbert Spencer and Lewis Henry Morgan proposed unilinear sequences — for example, Morgan's stages of "savagery", "barbarism", and "civilization" — through which all societies were thought to pass.

Critique and reformulation

In the early twentieth century, Franz Boas and his students attacked unilinear evolutionism for ethnocentrism and weak evidence, favouring historical particularism. Mid-century anthropologists such as Leslie White and Julian Steward revived evolutionary thinking in multilinear form, tying social change to energy capture and ecological adaptation. Elman Service's bands–tribes–chiefdoms–states typology became influential in archaeology.

Modern approaches

Contemporary research on cultural evolution applies formal models of transmission, selection, and drift to cultural traits (dual inheritance theory), while political science and cliodynamics study state formation and cycles of integration and disintegration. Biological "social evolution" is also used for the evolution of social behaviour, such as altruism and eusociality, in animals.

Criticism

The concept remains contested: critics argue that evolutionary stage schemes can smuggle in progressivist value judgements, while proponents insist that long-run regularities — urbanisation, increasing scale and complexity — are real phenomena requiring explanation.
