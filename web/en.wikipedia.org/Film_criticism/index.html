Film criticism is the analysis and evaluation of films and the film medium. It ranges from journalistic reviews that guide audiences on new releases to academic film theory that examines cinema's aesthetics, ideology, and history. Criticism differs from film scholarship chiefly in emphasis, though the boundaries overlap.

History

Writing on film began with the medium itself, but serious criticism developed with journals such as France's Cahiers du cinéma (founded 1951), where writers including André Bazin and the future directors of the French New Wave developed the auteur theory — the idea that the director is a film's principal author — which Andrew Sarris imported to American criticism. Pauline Kael championed a personal, combative style in The New Yorker, while Roger Ebert became the most widely read critic in the United States through his newspaper reviews and television programmes.

Academic criticism

From the 1970s, film studies in universities drew on semiotics, psychoanalysis, Marxism, and feminism — Laura Mulvey's 1975 essay "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" and its concept of the "male gaze" being especially influential. Later schools include cognitivism, Deleuzian film philosophy, and star and genre studies.

The digital era

The internet dispersed criticism beyond newspapers: aggregator sites such as Rotten Tomatoes summarise professional reviews, blogs and video essays reached mass audiences, and professional critics' influence on box office diminished relative to opening-weekend marketing and social media. Debates continue over the role of criticism — consumer guidance, artistic interpretation, or cultural history.
