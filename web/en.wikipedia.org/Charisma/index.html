Charisma

Charisma is a personal quality of magnetic charm, presence, and influence that enables an individual to inspire devotion, enthusiasm, and loyalty in others. The word derives from the Greek χάρισμα (charisma), "gift of grace," from χάρις (charis), "grace" or "favor." In early Christianity, particularly in the epistles of Paul, charismata were spiritual gifts bestowed by the Holy Spirit, such as prophecy, healing, and speaking in tongues.

Weber's charismatic authority

The sociologist Max Weber borrowed the term for his influential theory of authority. Alongside traditional and legal-rational authority, Weber identified charismatic authority, which rests on devotion to the exceptional sanctity, heroism, or exemplary character of an individual leader and the order that leader reveals or creates. Charismatic authority is inherently unstable in Weber's account: it depends on repeated proof of the leader's powers, arises in times of crisis, and tends to become "routinized" into traditional or bureaucratic forms after the leader's death.

Modern usage and research

In everyday usage, charisma refers to a compelling interpersonal appeal — a mixture of confidence, expressiveness, warmth, and rhetorical skill. Psychologists have studied charisma as a composite of perceived power and warmth, and some researchers (notably John Antonakis and colleagues) argue that charismatic leadership tactics can be taught and measured. Political uses of the term range from transformative leaders such as prophets and revolutionaries to populist figures whose authority rests on personal appeal rather than institutions or programs.
