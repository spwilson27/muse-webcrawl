Purposive approach

The purposive approach (sometimes called purposive interpretation or purposivism) is an approach to statutory and constitutional interpretation under which courts construe legislation in the light of the purpose the legislature intended it to serve, rather than by the literal meaning of its words alone. It is one of the principal approaches to interpretation in common law jurisdictions, alongside textualism and the literal and golden rules, and it is the dominant approach in the interpretation of European Union law and of many modern codified legal systems.

Development

The purposive approach grew out of the older "mischief rule" from Heydon's Case (1584), which directed courts to consider the defect a statute was passed to remedy. In the twentieth century, courts in the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and elsewhere moved toward an explicitly purposive method. Landmark statements include Lord Griffiths's speech in Pepper v Hart [1993], which relaxed the rule against consulting parliamentary debates, and the Canadian Supreme Court's adoption of E. A. Driedger's "modern principle": the words of an Act are to be read in their entire context and in their grammatical and ordinary sense harmoniously with the scheme of the Act, its object, and the intention of Parliament.

Application

A court using the purposive approach identifies the purpose of the provision — from its text, structure, legislative history, and social context — and chooses, among the meanings the words can bear, the one that best advances that purpose. In EU law, the Court of Justice interprets measures teleologically, by reference to their aims and place in the treaties, which may produce uniform interpretations diverging from any single member state's reading. Constitutional courts also use purposive interpretation when applying broadly worded rights guarantees to new circumstances.

Criticism

Critics, including textualists such as the late United States Supreme Court justice Antonin Scalia, argue that purposivism licenses judges to substitute their own view of a law's purpose for the compromise actually enacted, undermining legislative supremacy and predictability. Defenders respond that some recourse to purpose is unavoidable, since statutory words are often ambiguous, and that anchoring interpretation in the enacted scheme constrains judges more honestly than an asserted literalism that conceals choices.
