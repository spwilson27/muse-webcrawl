Geostrategy

Geostrategy, a subfield of geopolitics, is the formulation of national strategy in which geographical factors — location, terrain, resources, climate and access to seas and trade routes — are central considerations. Where geopolitics analyses the relationship between geography and power in general, geostrategy applies that analysis to the planning of a state's foreign policy, military posture and economic security.

History of the concept

The strategic significance of geography was recognised from antiquity, but the modern discipline grew from late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century thinkers. Alfred Thayer Mahan emphasised sea power and chokepoints; Halford Mackinder's 1904 "Heartland" thesis held that control of the Eurasian interior was the key to world power; and Nicholas Spykman countered with the "Rimland" of coastal Eurasia. Karl Haushofer and German Geopolitik gave the field a darker association with expansionist ideology, but the analytical study of geography and strategy revived during the Cold War, notably in the American policy of containment.

Core concerns

Geostrategic analysis typically examines: the state's position relative to oceans, mountain ranges and neighbours; strategic depth and vulnerability to invasion; control of maritime chokepoints such as straits and canals; access to energy and mineral resources; and the geography of alliances and buffer zones. The concept is also applied below the state level, to regions, and in business, where firms analyse geography in market strategy.

Contemporary applications

Modern geostrategy addresses the Arctic as sea ice recedes, competition over South China Sea shipping lanes, the security of undersea cables and pipelines, space and cyber domains, and the geographic concentration of critical supply chains such as semiconductors and rare-earth processing. Critics caution that geostrategic reasoning can slide into geographic determinism, understating the roles of technology, economics and choice.
