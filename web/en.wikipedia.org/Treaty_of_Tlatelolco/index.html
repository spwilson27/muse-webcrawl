Treaty of Tlatelolco

Signed14 February 1967
LocationTlatelolco, Mexico City, Mexico
Effective22 April 1968

The Treaty of Tlatelolco, formally the Treaty for the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons in Latin America and the Caribbean, established Latin America and the Caribbean as a nuclear-weapon-free zone. It was signed in the Tlatelolco district of Mexico City on 14 February 1967 and entered into force in 1968.

Provisions

Parties undertake not to test, use, manufacture, acquire, or possess nuclear weapons, and not to permit their deployment or storage on their territories. The treaty created a regional verification body, the Agency for the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons in Latin America and the Caribbean (OPANAL), headquartered in Mexico City, and relies also on International Atomic Energy Agency safeguards. Additional Protocols bind states with territories in the zone, and the nuclear-weapon states, not to act contrary to the zone's status and not to use or threaten to use nuclear weapons against parties.

Significance

Negotiated in the aftermath of the Cuban Missile Crisis, the treaty was the first nuclear-weapon-free zone established in a densely populated region and became a model for later zones in the South Pacific, Southeast Asia, and Africa.
