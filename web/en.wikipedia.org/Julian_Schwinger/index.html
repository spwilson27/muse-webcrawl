Julian Seymour Schwinger (1918 – 1994) was an American theoretical physicist, one of the founders of quantum electrodynamics (QED). He shared the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physics with Richard Feynman and Shin'ichirō Tomonaga "for their fundamental work in quantum electrodynamics, with deep-ploughing consequences for the physics of elementary particles".

Life and career

A child prodigy from New York City, Schwinger published his first physics paper at 16 and earned his doctorate from Columbia University at 21 under Isidor Rabi. He worked with J. Robert Oppenheimer at Berkeley, then at Purdue and the MIT Radiation Laboratory during the Second World War, where radar work led him to techniques in electromagnetic theory he later applied to QED. In 1945 he joined Harvard, becoming one of its youngest full professors, and in 1972 moved to UCLA.

Work

Schwinger's covariant formulation of QED and his treatment of renormalization enabled the first precise calculations of effects such as the anomalous magnetic moment of the electron and the Lamb shift. He made major contributions to quantum field theory, the theory of magnetic monopoles, the Schwinger model, source theory, and the quantum theory of measurement; his students included several future Nobel laureates, among them Sheldon Glashow and Walter Gilbert. He also wrote notable biographical and historical work on Einstein's legacy.
