House of Commons of the United Kingdom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The House of Commons is the lower house of the Parliament of the United Kingdom. Like the upper house, the House of Lords, it meets in the Palace of Westminster in London. The Commons is the democratically elected body of Parliament: its members, known as Members of Parliament (MPs), are elected by first-past-the-post in single-member constituencies.

Type: Lower house of Parliament
Seats: 650
Presiding officer: Speaker
Meeting place: Palace of Westminster, London
Elections: First-past-the-post

Role

The Commons has the principal legislative power in the United Kingdom. The government of the day is formed from the party or coalition able to command its confidence, and the prime minister is by convention a member of the Commons. Under the Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949, the Commons can enact most legislation without the consent of the Lords, whose power is limited to delay; money bills must originate in the Commons and can be delayed by the Lords for only one month.

History

The modern Commons descends from the House of Commons of England, joined in 1707 by Scottish members to form the Commons of Great Britain and in 1801 by Irish members to create the Commons of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland. The Reform Acts of the 19th century gradually widened the franchise and redistributed seats; women gained the vote on equal terms in 1928.

Procedure

The chamber’s green benches seat members on two sides divided by a central aisle, a layout derived from St Stephen’s Chapel. Business includes oral questions, debates on government and opposition motions, and the stages of public bills. The Speaker presides, maintaining order and selecting members to speak.
