The Song of Roland

The Song of Roland (French: La Chanson de Roland) is an 11th-century epic poem based on the Battle of Roncevaux Pass in 778, during the reign of Charlemagne. It is the oldest surviving major work of French literature and the most famous of the chansons de geste.

Synopsis

Charlemagne's army campaigns in Spain against the Muslim king Marsile of Saragossa. The traitor Ganelon, Roland's stepfather, betrays the Frankish rearguard to the Saracens. Roland, commanding the rearguard with his companion Oliver and Archbishop Turpin, is ambushed at Roncevaux. Refusing for too long to blow his olifant horn to summon help, Roland dies heroically; Charlemagne returns, defeats Marsile's forces and the emir Baligant, and has Ganelon executed for treason.

Historical basis

The historical ambush of 778 was carried out by Basque forces, not Saracens, and the historical Hruodland (Roland), prefect of the Breton March, was among the fallen. The poem transforms this defeat into a martyrdom within a Christian–Muslim holy war, reflecting the crusading era in which it was composed.

Manuscript and legacy

The oldest manuscript, held at the Bodleian Library, Oxford, contains about 4,000 lines in assonanced laisses. The poem shaped the French national epic tradition and the medieval Matter of France.
