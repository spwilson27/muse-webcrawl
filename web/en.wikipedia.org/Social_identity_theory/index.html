Social identity theory

Social identity theory is a theory in social psychology developed by Henri Tajfel and John Turner in the 1970s, explaining intergroup behavior in terms of people's identification with groups. It proposes that part of an individual's self-concept derives from membership in social groups, together with the value and emotional significance attached to that membership, and that people are motivated to maintain a positive social identity — typically by favorably comparing their in-group with relevant out-groups.

Minimal group paradigm

The theory grew out of Tajfel's minimal group experiments (1970–71), in which participants assigned to groups on trivial or even random criteria — such as a preference for paintings by Klee or Kandinsky — nonetheless allocated more rewards to anonymous in-group members than to out-group members. The mere act of categorization, the studies suggested, was sufficient to produce in-group favoritism, without prior conflict, personal interaction, or self-interest.

Processes

The theory identifies three cognitive processes: social categorization (sorting people, including oneself, into groups), social identification (adopting the group's identity and norms), and social comparison (evaluating the in-group against out-groups to achieve positive distinctiveness). When social identity is unsatisfactory, members may pursue individual mobility, social creativity (redefining the dimensions of comparison), or social competition with the out-group, depending on beliefs about the permeability, legitimacy, and stability of group boundaries.

Extensions and influence

Turner's self-categorization theory extended the approach by analyzing when people categorize themselves at personal versus group levels. The broader "social identity approach" has been applied to organizational behavior, leadership, health, crowd behavior, and political mobilization, and intersects with research on prejudice, stereotyping, and realistic conflict theory.
