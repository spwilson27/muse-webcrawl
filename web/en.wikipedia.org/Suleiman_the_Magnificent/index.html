Suleiman, tenth and longest-reigning sultan of the Ottoman Empire.

Suleiman I (1494–1566), commonly known in the West as Suleiman the Magnificent and in Turkish as Kanunî Sultan Süleyman, "Suleiman the Lawgiver," was the tenth and longest-reigning sultan of the Ottoman Empire, ruling from 1520 until his death in 1566. Under his administration the empire reached the peak of its territorial extent and power, ruling over at least 25 million people.

Reign

Succeeding his father Selim I, Suleiman personally led thirteen major campaigns. He conquered Belgrade (1521) and Rhodes (1522), crushed Hungary at the Battle of Mohács (1526), and besieged Vienna in 1529, marking the empire's furthest advance into Central Europe. In the east he took Baghdad and much of Mesopotamia from Safavid Persia; in the Mediterranean his admiral Hayreddin Barbarossa made the Ottoman fleet dominant, taking Algiers, Tripoli and the coast of Yemen and the Horn of Africa. The Siege of Malta in 1565, however, ended in Ottoman defeat.

The Lawgiver

Suleiman oversaw a comprehensive codification of imperial law (the kanun) supplementing the sharia, reforming taxation, land tenure and the judiciary — work that earned him his Turkish epithet. His reign was also a golden age of Ottoman art and architecture under the chief architect Mimar Sinan, whose Süleymaniye Mosque crowns Istanbul, and of poetry — the sultan himself wrote verse under the pen-name Muhibbi.

Family and succession

Breaking with tradition, Suleiman legally married Hürrem Sultan (Roxelana), a former concubine of Ruthenian origin, whose influence at court was immense. Palace intrigues led to the execution of his grand vizier Ibrahim Pasha (1536) and of his eldest capable son, Şehzade Mustafa (1553). Suleiman died of natural causes during the siege of Szigetvár in Hungary in September 1566; his death was concealed for weeks to preserve army morale. He was succeeded by his son Selim II.
