Klein–Gordon equation

The Klein–Gordon equation (Klein–Fock–Gordon equation or sometimes Klein–Gordon–Fock equation) is a relativistic wave equation, related to the Schrödinger equation. It is second-order in space and time and manifestly Lorentz-covariant. It is a quantized version of the relativistic energy–momentum relation and describes spin-0 particles such as the Higgs boson and (approximately) pions.

Statement

The equation follows from the relativistic dispersion relation E2 = (pc)2 + (mc2)2 by substituting the quantum operators for energy and momentum. In its common form:

(1/c2) ∂2ψ/∂t2 − ∇2ψ + (mc/ħ)2ψ = 0

or, using the d'Alembert operator □ and the reduced Compton wavelength, (□ + m2)ψ = 0 in natural units. For m = 0 it reduces to the ordinary wave equation.

History

Erwin Schrödinger first arrived at the equation while seeking a relativistic version of his wave equation, but set it aside because it did not reproduce the fine structure of hydrogen. It was published in 1926 independently by Oskar Klein and Walter Gordon, and also by Vladimir Fock. Early difficulties included negative-energy solutions and a probability density that could be negative, which led physicists to prefer Dirac's first-order equation for electrons.

Interpretation in quantum field theory

In modern quantum field theory the Klein–Gordon equation is reinterpreted: ψ is not a single-particle wavefunction but a field operator (or classical field) whose quanta are spin-0 particles. The negative-energy solutions correspond to antiparticles, and the conserved current is read as a charge density rather than a probability density. The real Klein–Gordon field describes neutral scalars; the complex field describes charged scalars with distinct antiparticles.

Applications

The equation governs the Higgs field in the Standard Model (with additional self-interaction terms), models pions in nuclear physics, and appears in condensed matter and cosmology, for example in models of scalar-field dark matter and inflation.
