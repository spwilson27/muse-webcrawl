The Creation of Adam (Italian: Creazione di Adamo) is a fresco painting by Italian artist Michelangelo, forming part of the Sistine Chapel's ceiling, painted c. 1508–1512. It illustrates the biblical creation narrative in which God gives life to Adam, the first man.

Composition

Adam reclines languidly on the left, his right arm extended and his hand drooping, while God — an older, bearded figure borne aloft by angels within a billowing mantle — stretches out his right hand toward Adam's. The fingers of the two figures nearly, but famously do not, touch: the small gap between them is the painting's charged focal point. The panel is the fourth in the series of Genesis scenes running along the centre of the ceiling and the second of the scenes completed in the second phase of work.

Interpretations

Art historians have debated the shapes around God: the pink mantle and surrounding figures have been read as an anatomically accurate cross-section of the human brain (proposed by physician Frank Meshberger in 1990), as a uterus, and as a representation of the soul or divine intellect. Adam's pose mirrors God's, echoing Genesis 1:27 — man created in God's image.

Legacy

The image of the two near-touching hands is among the most reproduced religious paintings of all time and an icon of Renaissance humanism, endlessly parodied and referenced in popular culture — from film posters to the opening imagery of television series.

Restoration

The fresco was cleaned during the great Sistine Chapel restoration of 1980–1994, revealing Michelangelo's vivid original colours beneath centuries of grime and earlier repainting.
