Humber

Humber Estuary
Location: Northern England
Source: Confluence of Trent and Ouse near Faxfleet
Mouth: North Sea (between Spurn Head and Donna Nook)
Length: 62 km (38.5 mi) tidal estuary
Crossing: Humber Bridge (opened 1981)

The Humber is a large tidal estuary on the east coast of Northern England, formed at Trent Falls, Faxfleet, by the confluence of the rivers Ouse and Trent, and flowing east and south-east for about 62 km (38.5 miles) to the North Sea between Spurn Head in the East Riding of Yorkshire and Donna Nook in Lincolnshire.

Geography and economy

The Humber drains a catchment of some 24,000 square kilometres — about a fifth of England — and forms the historic boundary between Yorkshire to the north and Lincolnshire to the south; the name gave the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of Northumbria ("land north of the Humber") its identity. Its ports — Hull, Grimsby, Immingham and Goole — have made it one of Britain's busiest trading estuaries, handling large shares of the country's coal, oil, chemical and ro-ro ferry traffic, with major refineries and chemical works along its south bank. Immingham is among the UK's largest ports by tonnage.

Crossings and environment

Until 1981 the estuary could be crossed only by ferry or by long road detours; the Humber Bridge, opened that year and once the world's longest single-span suspension bridge (main span 1,410 m), now carries the A15 between Hessle and Barton-upon-Humber. The estuary's mudflats, saltmarshes and sandbanks — including the Spurn peninsula — are internationally important for wading birds and designated under the Ramsar Convention and EU/UK nature protection regimes.
