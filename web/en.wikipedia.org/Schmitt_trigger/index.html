Schmitt trigger

In electronics, a Schmitt trigger is a comparator circuit with hysteresis implemented by applying positive feedback to the non-inverting input of a comparator or differential amplifier. Its output switches between two states at different input thresholds — an upper threshold for a rising signal and a lower one for a falling signal — which makes it immune to small amounts of noise on the input.

History

The circuit was invented by the American scientist Otto H. Schmitt in 1934, while he was a graduate student, and described in his 1938 paper "A Thermionic Trigger." It was originally conceived in the study of neural impulse propagation in squid nerves.

Operation and implementations

Schmitt triggers can be built from an operational amplifier with positive feedback, from two transistors, or from dedicated integrated circuits such as the 74HC14 hex inverting Schmitt trigger. The two threshold voltages divide the input range into three regions and give the device a form of memory: the output depends on the input's history.

Applications

Typical uses include converting slow or noisy analogue signals into clean digital waveforms, debouncing mechanical switches, signal conditioning, and, with an added resistor–capacitor network, relaxation oscillators that generate square waves.
