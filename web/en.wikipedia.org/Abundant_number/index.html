Abundant number

In number theory, an abundant number or excessive number is a positive integer for which the sum of its proper divisors is greater than the number itself. Equivalently, the sum of all its divisors (including itself), denoted σ(n), satisfies σ(n) > 2n. The smallest abundant number is 12, whose proper divisors 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 sum to 16. Numbers that are not abundant are either perfect (sum equal to the number) or deficient (sum less than the number).

Examples

The first abundant numbers are 12, 18, 20, 24, 30, 36, 40, 42, 48, 54, 56, 60, 66, 70, 72, 78, 80, 84, 88, 90, 96, 100, 102, 104, 108, 112, 114, 120 and so on. Every multiple of a perfect number or of an abundant number is abundant, which means multiples of 6 are all abundant or perfect.

Properties

The smallest odd abundant number is 945 = 3³·5·7. There are infinitely many abundant numbers, both even and odd. The set of abundant numbers has a well-defined natural density of about 0.2476, as computed by Behrend and refined by Deléglise in 1998. Every integer greater than 20,161 can be written as the sum of two abundant numbers; this result was established in the study of "expressible" numbers, and 20,161 is known to be the largest integer that cannot.

Abundancy index

The ratio σ(n)/n is called the abundancy index of n. Numbers with abundancy index above 2 are abundant; an index of exactly 2 marks perfect numbers. Abundancy indices are multiplicative, and for abundant numbers the index can be arbitrarily large. A weird number is an abundant number that is not the sum of any subset of its proper divisors; the smallest is 70.
