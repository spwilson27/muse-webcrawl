Helena (mother of Constantine I)

Helena, also known as Saint Helena or Helena of Constantinople (c. 246/248 – c. 330), was an Augusta of the Roman Empire and mother of Emperor Constantine the Great. Born in the lower classes traditionally in Drepanum, Bithynia, she became the consort of the future emperor Constantius Chlorus, who set her aside to marry into the imperial family; after Constantine's accession in 306 she returned to court, received the title of Augusta, and wielded considerable influence.

Christianity and pilgrimage

Helena converted to Christianity — later tradition often credits her influence on Constantine's own conversion, though the direction of influence is debated. In her late seventies she undertook a celebrated pilgrimage to Palestine and the eastern provinces (c. 326–328), founding churches at the sites of Christ's Nativity in Bethlehem and of the Ascension on the Mount of Olives, and distributing alms to the poor and to churches.

The True Cross tradition

By the later fourth century, Christian writers such as Ambrose, Rufinus, and Socrates Scholasticus credited Helena with discovering the True Cross and the other relics of the Passion during her pilgrimage, together with the nails used in the Crucifixion. The account became one of the most famous legends of the medieval Church; fragments of the Cross were distributed across the empire.

Legacy

Helena died around 330 in Constantine's presence and was buried in her mausoleum in Rome, now a ruin attached to the church of Santi Marcellino e Pietro. She is venerated as a saint in the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Anglican, and Lutheran churches, with her feast day on 18 August (West) or 21 May (East, shared with Constantine). Many churches, the Atlantic island of Saint Helena, and numerous towns bear her name.
