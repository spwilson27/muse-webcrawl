Graphite

Graphite is a crystalline form of the element carbon. It consists of layers of carbon atoms arranged in hexagonal rings; within each layer the atoms are strongly bonded, but the layers are held together only by weak van der Waals forces, so they slide over one another easily — making graphite soft, slippery, and an excellent dry lubricant. Graphite is the most stable form of carbon under standard conditions and, unlike diamond, conducts electricity. The name comes from the Greek graphein, "to write/draw", from its use in pencils.

Structure and properties

Each carbon atom in a graphite layer bonds to three neighbours in a planar hexagonal lattice (a single layer is graphene). The fourth valence electron is delocalised across the layer, giving graphite its electrical conductivity along the planes — unusual for a non-metal. Graphite sublimes rather than melts at atmospheric pressure (around 3,600 °C) and is chemically inert under many conditions, making it valuable for high-temperature crucibles and furnace linings.

Occurrence and production

Natural graphite occurs in metamorphic rocks such as marble, schist, and gneiss, formed from buried organic matter. The famous Borrowdale deposit in Cumbria, England — discovered in the 16th century — supplied exceptionally pure graphite that was sawn into sticks for marking and, encased in wood, became the first pencils. Synthetic graphite is made by heating petroleum coke or coal-tar pitch to near 3,000 °C (the Acheson process).

Uses

Graphite is the "lead" in pencils (mixed with clay for hardness grades), the anode material in virtually all lithium-ion batteries — a fast-growing source of demand — a lubricant, a moderator in some nuclear reactors, the electrodes of electric-arc furnaces, and a component of refractories, brake linings, and carbon-fibre composites. Demand from electric vehicles has made graphite a strategic material; China dominates both mining and processing.
