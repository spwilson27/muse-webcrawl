Ethylene glycol poisoning

Ethylene glycol poisoning is poisoning caused by drinking ethylene glycol, most commonly found in antifreeze. Early symptoms resemble alcohol intoxication, followed by nausea, vomiting, and later kidney failure, metabolic acidosis and death if untreated. Long-term outcomes may include permanent kidney damage.

Mechanism

Ethylene glycol itself is moderately toxic, but its metabolites are far more dangerous. Alcohol dehydrogenase converts it to glycolaldehyde and then glycolic acid, causing severe high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis; further metabolism to oxalic acid produces calcium oxalate crystals that deposit in the kidneys and other tissues.

Stages and diagnosis

Classically three stages are described: a neurological stage (0.5–12 hours), a cardiopulmonary stage (12–24 hours) and a renal stage (24–72 hours). Diagnosis rests on history, blood gas and osmolal gap measurements, calcium oxalate crystals in urine, and, where available, serum ethylene glycol levels; it is often presumptive.

Treatment and prevention

Treatment aims to block metabolism with fomepizole (preferred) or ethanol, correct acidosis with bicarbonate, and remove toxin and metabolites by haemodialysis in severe cases. Prevention includes safer propylene-glycol antifreeze, child-resistant containers and bittering agents. Thousands of exposures are reported annually in the United States, with dozens of deaths.
