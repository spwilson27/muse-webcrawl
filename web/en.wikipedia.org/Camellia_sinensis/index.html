Camellia sinensis

Camellia sinensis is a species of evergreen shrub in the family Theaceae whose leaves and leaf buds are used to produce tea. All true teas — white, green, yellow, oolong, black and fermented teas such as pu-erh — come from this single species; the differences among them arise from cultivar, growing conditions and, above all, the degree of oxidation and processing after harvest. Herbal "teas" or tisanes, by contrast, are infusions of other plants.

Description and varieties

Left unpruned, the tea plant can grow into a small tree up to about 9 metres tall, but in cultivation it is kept as a waist-high bush to make plucking easier. It has glossy, serrated elliptical leaves and fragrant white flowers with yellow stamens. Two principal varieties are recognised: C. sinensis var. sinensis, the small-leaved, hardy China plant, and var. assamica, the large-leaved Assam plant suited to warm lowland climates; a third, the Cambodian type, is sometimes distinguished.

Cultivation and chemistry

Tea is grown across tropical and subtropical Asia and in parts of Africa and South America, ideally with abundant rainfall and acidic soils, at elevations up to about 2,000 metres; high-grown teas are prized for flavour. The leaves contain caffeine (historically called "theine"), the amino acid L-theanine, and polyphenols called catechins, which oxidise during black-tea manufacture to form theaflavins and thearubigins. Typically the top two leaves and bud are plucked by hand or machine at intervals through the growing season.
