Kerogen

Kerogen is solid organic matter in sedimentary rocks. Consisting of a mixture of organic chemical compounds, it is the most abundant source of organic compounds on Earth, exceeding the total organic content of living matter many times over. Kerogen is insoluble in normal organic solvents because of the high molecular weight of its component compounds. It is the precursor from which petroleum and natural gas are generated when buried and heated.

Formation

Kerogen forms from the decomposed remains of algae, plankton, and terrestrial plant material deposited in sediments. During burial, biological polymers such as proteins and carbohydrates are broken down and recondensed into the disordered macromolecular structure of kerogen. Under increasing temperature with depth — the process of catagenesis — kerogen cracks to produce liquid and gaseous hydrocarbons, the basis of conventional and unconventional fossil fuel deposits.

Types

Geochemists classify kerogen into types according to its source material and hydrogen content: Type I (lacustrine algal, oil-prone), Type II (marine planktonic, oil- and gas-prone, characteristic of most marine source rocks and oil shales), Type III (terrestrial woody material, gas-prone), and Type IV (inert, oxidised organic matter with little hydrocarbon potential). The van Krevelen diagram plots kerogen types by their hydrogen/carbon and oxygen/carbon ratios.

Oil shale

Immature kerogen locked in oil shale must be heated artificially (retorted) to yield shale oil; it should not be confused with shale oil produced from mature source rocks by hydraulic fracturing.
