Germanic mythology

Yggdrasil, the world tree of Norse cosmology, the best-preserved branch of Germanic mythology.

Germanic mythology consists of the body of myths native to the Germanic peoples, including Norse mythology, Anglo-Saxon mythology, and Continental Germanic mythology. It was transmitted orally for centuries before being recorded, most fully in the Icelandic Eddas and sagas of the thirteenth century, and features a pantheon headed by Odin (Woden), along with gods such as Thor, Baldr, and Freyja, and a cosmos centred on the world tree Yggdrasil.

Sources

The principal sources are the Old Norse Poetic Edda and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, supplemented by skaldic poetry, sagas, runic inscriptions, and place names. For the continental and English traditions the record is thinner: Tacitus's Germania, the Merseburg charms, the Old English poem Beowulf, and fragments preserved by medieval chroniclers provide the main evidence.

Cosmology and themes

Germanic myth describes the creation of the world from the body of the primordial giant Ymir, the division of beings among gods, elves, dwarves, giants, and humans, and the foretold destruction and rebirth of the world at Ragnarök. Common themes include the power of fate (wyrd), the value of courage in a doomed struggle, and the deeds of heroes such as Sigurd the dragon-slayer.

Legacy

Germanic mythology survives in weekday names (Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday), in place names across northern Europe, and in a vast modern influence on fantasy literature, beginning with J. R. R. Tolkien's legendarium and extending to contemporary film, games, and comics.
