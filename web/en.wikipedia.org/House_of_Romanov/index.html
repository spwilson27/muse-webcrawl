House of Romanov

The House of Romanov was the reigning dynasty of Russia from 1613 until the abdication of Nicholas II in 1917. It was the second dynasty to rule Russia, following the Rurikids, and under its rule Russia grew from a tsardom into one of the largest empires in history.

The Romanovs ruled Russia for just over three centuries.

Rise

After the Time of Troubles, an assembly (Zemsky Sobor) elected the sixteen-year-old Michael Romanov tsar in 1613. His descendants consolidated the state; Peter the Great (r. 1682–1725) westernised the army and administration, founded St Petersburg and took the title of Emperor in 1721.

The empire

Under Catherine the Great Russia expanded into Ukraine, Crimea and Poland. The 19th-century Romanovs — Alexander I, who defeated Napoleon, Nicholas I, the reforming Alexander II, who emancipated the serfs in 1861, and Alexander III — ruled an empire spanning Eastern Europe, Siberia and Central Asia. Succession after 1797 followed Paul I's law of primogeniture, though from 1762 the throne was in practice held by the related House of Holstein-Gottorp-Romanov.

Fall

Defeat in the Russo-Japanese War, the Revolution of 1905 and the strains of the First World War eroded the monarchy. In March 1917 Nicholas II abdicated in the February Revolution; in July 1918 he, his wife Alexandra and their five children were executed by the Bolsheviks at Yekaterinburg. Surviving Romanovs lived in exile, and rival claimants to the headship of the house persist among their descendants.
