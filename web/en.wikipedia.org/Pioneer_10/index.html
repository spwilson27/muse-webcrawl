Pioneer 10 (originally designated Pioneer F) is an American space probe, launched in 1972 and weighing 258 kilograms (569 pounds), that completed the first mission to the planet Jupiter and became the first of five artificial objects to achieve the escape velocity needed to leave the Solar System. This space exploration project was conducted by the NASA Ames Research Center in California. The space probe was manufactured by TRW Inc.

Pioneer 10 achieved a velocity high enough to leave the Solar System. Because of its exceptional velocity in comparison to other craft and its direction away from the Sun relative to the galactic center, Pioneer 10 is actually being outrun by the faster-moving Voyager 1 spacecraft.

Mission background

The Pioneer program was conceived in the late 1950s as a series of lunar and interplanetary probes. By the late 1960s, NASA approved a mission to explore the outer Solar System, taking advantage of a rare planetary alignment—sometimes called the "Grand Tour" geometry—that occurs once every 176 years. Pioneer 10 and its twin, Pioneer 11, were approved in February 1969.

Launch and trajectory

Pioneer 10 was launched on March 3, 1972, from Cape Canaveral, Florida, aboard an Atlas-Centaur rocket. It was the fastest man-made object to leave the Earth, reaching a speed of more than 51,000 km/h (32,000 mph) relative to Earth. It crossed the orbit of Mars in May 1972 and entered the asteroid belt in July 1972, emerging safely in February 1973—the first spacecraft to traverse the belt.

Jupiter encounter

The spacecraft began photographing Jupiter in November 1973. Its closest approach came on December 4, 1973, at a distance of about 132,252 km (82,178 mi) from the cloud tops. During the encounter it obtained detailed images of the planet and its moons, measured the intense radiation belts, mapped Jupiter's magnetic field, and confirmed that Jupiter is predominantly a liquid planet. The intense radiation damaged some instrument commands but the spacecraft survived.

Deep space journey

After Jupiter, Pioneer 10 continued on an escape trajectory from the Solar System, heading generally toward the star Aldebaran in the constellation Taurus. It crossed the orbit of Saturn in 1976 and the orbit of Neptune (then the outermost planet, owing to Pluto's eccentric orbit) on June 13, 1983, becoming the first human-made object to pass beyond the orbits of all known planets.

The mission was formally ended on March 31, 1997, when funding ceased, though the spacecraft continued to be tracked in a low-power mode. The last successful telemetry reception was on April 27, 2002. NASA's Deep Space Network attempted a final contact on March 3–5, 2006, without success.

The Pioneer plaque

At the suggestion of Eric Burgess and with the encouragement of Carl Sagan, Pioneer 10 carries the Pioneer plaque, a gold-anodized aluminium plate designed by Sagan and Frank Drake and drawn by Linda Salzman Sagan. The plaque depicts a man and a woman, a map of the Solar System, and the position of the Sun relative to the galactic center and 14 pulsars, intended as a message to any extraterrestrial intelligence that might intercept the spacecraft.

Instruments

Pioneer 10 carried eleven scientific instruments, including a helium vector magnetometer, plasma analyzer, charged particle instrument, cosmic ray telescope, Geiger tube telescope, trapped radiation detector, meteoroid detector, asteroid/meteoroid detector, ultraviolet photometer, imaging photopolarimeter, and an infrared radiometer. Power was supplied by four radioisotope thermoelectric generators fueled by plutonium-238.

Legacy

Pioneer 10 demonstrated that a spacecraft could survive passage through the asteroid belt and the harsh radiation environment of Jupiter, paving the way for the Voyager, Galileo, and later outer-planet missions.
