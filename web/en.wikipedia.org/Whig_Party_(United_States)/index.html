Whig Party (United States)

Whig Party
Founded: 1833
Dissolved: 1856
Leaders: Henry Clay, Daniel Webster
Presidents: William Henry Harrison, John Tyler, Zachary Taylor, Millard Fillmore
Ideology: American System; modernisation; congressional supremacy

The Whig Party was a major political party in the United States active from 1833 to 1856. It formed in opposition to the policies of President Andrew Jackson and the Democratic Party, taking its name from the English Whigs who had resisted royal power — Jackson's enemies called him "King Andrew". The party formed one half of the Second Party System, rivalling the Democrats for two decades.

Programme

The Whigs supported Henry Clay's American System: a national bank, protective tariffs, and federal funding for internal improvements such as roads and canals. They favoured a strong Congress over the presidency, moral and cultural modernisation, and drew support from entrepreneurs, bankers, evangelical Protestants and urban professionals in the North, and from planters and merchants in the South.

Elections and presidents

The Whigs won the presidency twice, in 1840 with William Henry Harrison and in 1848 with Zachary Taylor — both generals who died in office, succeeded by John Tyler and Millard Fillmore respectively. Harrison's 1840 "Tippecanoe and Tyler Too" campaign was a landmark of modern mass electioneering. Tyler was expelled from the party after vetoing its bank bills. Other leading Whigs included Daniel Webster, William Henry Seward and a young Abraham Lincoln, who served one term in Congress as a Whig.

Collapse

The party tore itself apart over the expansion of slavery. The Compromise of 1850 and especially the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854 split Northern and Southern Whigs irreparably. Northern Whigs flowed into the new Republican Party, others briefly into the anti-immigrant Know Nothing movement, and the Whig Party had effectively ceased to exist by 1856.
