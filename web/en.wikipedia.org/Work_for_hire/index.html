Work for hire

A work made for hire (or work for hire) is a work created by an employee within the scope of employment, or certain specially commissioned works, for which the employer or commissioning party — rather than the actual creator — is legally considered the author and copyright owner. The doctrine is defined in United States copyright law in 17 U.S.C. § 101, with parallels in the "corporate authorship" rules of other countries.

Legal basis (U.S.)Copyright Act, 17 U.S.C. §§ 101, 201(b)
Copyright term (U.S.)95 years from publication or 120 years from creation, whichever is shorter
Applies toEmployee works; nine categories of commissioned works with a signed written agreement

United States law

Under the Copyright Act of 1976, a work for hire arises in two situations: (1) a work prepared by an employee within the scope of employment, or (2) a specially ordered or commissioned work in one of nine enumerated categories — such as contributions to collective works, translations, instructional texts, and parts of motion pictures — where the parties expressly agree in a signed writing that it is a work for hire. In Community for Creative Non-Violence v. Reid (1989), the Supreme Court held that "employee" carries its common-law agency meaning, distinguishing employees from independent contractors.

Consequences

Because the employer is the statutory author, the individual creator has no copyright to assign, and the author's termination and renewal rights generally do not apply. The term of copyright also differs from that for individual authors. Moral rights under the Visual Artists Rights Act may still be waived or retained depending on the work. Contracts frequently combine work-for-hire language with a backup assignment of rights in case a court finds the doctrine inapplicable.
