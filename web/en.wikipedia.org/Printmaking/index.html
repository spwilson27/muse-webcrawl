Printmaking

Printmaking is a process of creating artworks by printing, normally on paper, but also on fabric, wood, metal and other surfaces. It is a means of producing multiples of artwork (an edition), each print (or impression) being considered an original work of art, not a copy. Printmaking is often used as a general term for graphic art processes.

Techniques

Relief printing (woodcut, linocut, wood engraving) inks the raised surface left after cutting away the design. Intaglio (engraving, etching, drypoint, mezzotint, aquatint) holds ink in lines or areas incised into a metal plate. Planographic methods include lithography, based on the repulsion of oil and water on a flat stone or plate, and monotype, which yields a single impression. Stencil methods include screen printing (serigraphy), in which ink is forced through a mesh.

History

Printed images appeared in China with woodblock printing; in Europe, woodcuts circulated from the early 15th century, and engraving was developed by goldsmiths. Albrecht Dürer raised the woodcut and engraving to major art forms; Rembrandt's etchings are landmarks of intaglio. Japanese ukiyo-e woodblock prints influenced European art in the 19th century. In the 20th century, screen printing was popularised by Pop artists such as Andy Warhol, and contemporary printmakers combine traditional and digital processes.

The print as original

Prints are typically signed and numbered by the artist (e.g. 12/50), identifying the impression's place in the edition. Because the matrix can be reprinted, conventions about editions, proofs, and posthumous printings are central to the print market and to connoisseurship.
