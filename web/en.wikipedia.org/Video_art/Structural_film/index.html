Structural film

Structural film was an experimental film movement prominent in the United States in the 1960s and early 1970s, and closely related to the broader field of video art and avant-garde cinema. The term was coined by the critic and theorist P. Adams Sitney in 1969 to describe films whose overall shape — rather than narrative or imagery — is predetermined and supplies the work's principal content.

Characteristics

Sitney identified four characteristic devices, of which a given film usually employed one or more:

  • Fixed camera position — a stationary frame throughout.
  • Flicker effect — rapid alternation of frames, often black and clear.
  • Loop printing — repetition of the same footage.
  • Rephotography off a screen — refilming projected images.

Filmmakers and works

Key figures include Michael Snow (Wavelength, 1967, a 45-minute slow zoom across a room; Back and Forth, 1969), Hollis Frampton (Zorns Lemma, 1970), Tony Conrad (The Flicker, 1966), Paul Sharits, Ernie Gehr, Joyce Wieland and George Landow (Owen Land). In Britain a related "structural/materialist film" movement, associated with Peter Gidal and the London Film-Makers' Co-operative, pursued a more explicitly political, anti-illusionist practice.

Relationship to video art

Structural film emerged alongside early video art: both stressed the material properties of their medium, duration and process over representation, and artists such as Snow and Frampton worked across film, video and other media. The movement influenced later gallery-based moving-image practice and minimalism in cinema.
