Romanticism

Romanticism (also known as the Romantic era) was an artistic, literary, musical, and intellectual movement that originated in Europe towards the end of the 18th century and in most areas was at its peak in the approximate period from 1800 to 1850. Romanticism was characterised by an emphasis on emotion and individualism as well as the glorification of the past and nature, preferring the medieval to the classical.

Definition and origins

Partly a reaction against the Industrial Revolution and the rationalism of the Enlightenment, Romanticism elevated folk art and ancient custom, spontaneous emotion over reason, and the sublime in untamed nature. In literature it is associated with the British poets William Wordsworth, Samuel Taylor Coleridge, Lord Byron, Percy Bysshe Shelley and John Keats; in Germany with Goethe, Schiller and the Jena circle; and in France with Victor Hugo and the painters Théodore Géricault and Eugène Delacroix.

Visual arts

Romantic painting stressed dramatic landscapes, turbulent seas, ruins and the overwhelming power of nature, as in the work of Caspar David Friedrich and J. M. W. Turner. In the United States the Hudson River School applied Romantic ideals to the American wilderness.

Music

Romantic music expanded the expressive range of the orchestra and the piano; composers such as Beethoven, Schubert, Chopin, Liszt, Berlioz and Wagner treated music as a vehicle for profound personal and national feeling.

Legacy

Romanticism fuelled liberal and nationalist movements across Europe and left a lasting imprint on literature, art and music, even as Realism and later modernism reacted against it.
