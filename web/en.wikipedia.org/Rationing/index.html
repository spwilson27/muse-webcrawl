Rationing

Rationing is the controlled distribution of scarce resources, goods, or services, or an artificial restriction of demand. Rationing controls the size of the ration, the portion of resources allocated to a person or group. It is most often imposed by governments during wartime, famine, or other emergencies, when the supply of essential goods such as food, fuel, and raw materials cannot meet demand through the free market.

Methods

Rationing may be administered through coupons or stamps that entitle the holder to purchase a fixed quantity of a controlled good, through points systems that let consumers allocate a monthly allowance among different goods, or through direct allocation of supplies to institutions. Rationing is usually accompanied by price controls intended to prevent hoarding, profiteering, and black markets, though illegal markets in rationed goods commonly arise.

History

Modern mass rationing is associated above all with the two world wars. During the First World War, Britain introduced food rationing in 1918; Germany and other belligerents rationed extensively as blockades bit. In the Second World War, Britain began rationing petrol in 1939 and food in January 1940, under the Ministry of Food; some items, such as clothing, remained rationed into the postwar years, and meat rationing did not end until 1954. The United States rationed gasoline, rubber, sugar, meat, and other goods under the Office of Price Administration, issuing ration books and tokens to households.

Rationing has also been used in peacetime: in centrally planned economies, in Cuba's long-running libreta system, during the 1970s energy crises, and during droughts for water supplies.

Economic analysis

Economists note that rationing guarantees a minimum share of scarce goods to everyone, which market allocation by price alone does not, but it also suppresses price signals that would encourage increased production and efficient use, and it creates administrative costs and incentives for evasion.
