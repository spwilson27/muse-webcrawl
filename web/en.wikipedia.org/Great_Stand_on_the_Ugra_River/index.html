Great Stand on the Ugra River

Date: 8 October – 11 November 1480

Location: Ugra River, near Kaluga, Russia

Combatants: Grand Duchy of Moscow vs. Great Horde

Result: Muscovite victory; end of Tatar tribute

The Great Stand on the Ugra River (Russian: Великое стояние на Угре) was a standoff in 1480 between the forces of Akhmat Khan of the Great Horde and Grand Prince Ivan III of Moscow, which is traditionally regarded as the event that ended the Mongol-Tatar domination of Russia — the so-called "Tatar Yoke".

Background

By the late 15th century the Grand Duchy of Moscow had grown powerful under Ivan III, who consolidated the Russian lands and in 1476 ceased paying the customary tribute to the Horde. Khan Akhmat marched to enforce submission in 1480, allied with Casimir IV of Poland-Lithuania, and advanced to the Ugra River, a tributary of the Oka forming the border between Muscovy and Lithuania.

The stand

From October 1480 the two armies faced each other across the river. Attempts by the Tatars to cross were repelled by Russian forces using firearms and artillery. Ivan negotiated while also moving forces against Akhmat's Lithuanian ally's lands. With winter approaching, supplies failing, his Lithuanian allies not arriving, and the Crimean Khanate — allied with Moscow — threatening his rear, Akhmat withdrew on 11 November 1480, ending the campaign without a general battle. He was killed by Siberian and Nogai rivals the following year.

Significance

After 1480 Moscow never resumed the tribute, and Russian historiography treats the stand on the Ugra as the formal end of more than two centuries of Mongol-Tatar overlordship dating from the invasion of 1237–1240. Modern historians note that Horde power was already fragmented and that the event's importance is partly symbolic — the Great Horde survived until 1502 — but the date remains a landmark in Russian national history.
