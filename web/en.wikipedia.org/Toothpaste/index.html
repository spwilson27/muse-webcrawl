Toothpaste


Toothpaste on a toothbrush

Toothpaste is a paste or gel dentifrice used with a toothbrush to clean and maintain the aesthetics and health of teeth. It promotes oral hygiene by helping remove dental plaque and food debris, suppressing halitosis, and delivering active ingredients — most notably fluoride — that help prevent tooth decay and gum disease (gingivitis).

Composition

Typical toothpaste contains abrasives (such as hydrated silica or calcium carbonate) to remove stains and plaque, humectants (sorbitol, glycerol) to prevent drying, water, detergents such as sodium lauryl sulfate to create foam, thickeners, flavourings, and fluoride compounds such as sodium fluoride or sodium monofluorophosphate, usually at 1,000–1,500 ppm in adult toothpastes. Specialised varieties may include desensitising agents like potassium nitrate, antimicrobials, or whitening agents.

History

Tooth powders were used in ancient Egypt, Greece and China, made from ingredients such as crushed bones, oyster shells and charcoal. Modern toothpaste in a tube dates to the 1890s, when Colgate began selling paste in collapsible tubes. Fluoride toothpastes were introduced commercially in the 1950s (Crest, 1955) and became a major factor in the decline of dental caries in developed countries.

Toothpaste is not intended to be swallowed; children's varieties are often flavoured to encourage brushing but should be used in pea-sized amounts because excessive fluoride ingestion can cause dental fluorosis in developing teeth.
