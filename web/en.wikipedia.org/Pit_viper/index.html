Pit viper

The pit vipers, or pit adders, are a subfamily of vipers, Crotalinae, found in Eurasia and the Americas. They are distinguished by the presence of a heat-sensing pit organ located between the eye and the nostril on both sides of the head. Currently, 23 genera and 151 species are recognized. These are also the only viperids found in the Americas.

Description

These snakes range in size from the diminutive hump-nosed viper, which grows to 30–45 cm (12–18 in), to the bushmaster, which can reach 3.65 m (12.0 ft) in length, making it the longest venomous snake in the Americas. Like all viperids, pit vipers have long, hinged fangs at the front of the upper jaw that fold back when not in use, allowing them to deliver venom deep into prey.

Pit organ

The pit organ is a highly developed infrared sensing system. It consists of a deep pit with a membrane over it; the membrane senses thermal radiation much as the retina of the eye does, allowing the snake to build a thermal image of warm-blooded prey. Experiments have shown that even when deprived of sight and smell, pit vipers can strike accurately at moving warm targets less than 0.2 °C warmer than the background.

Distribution and habitat

Pit vipers inhabit a wide range of habitats, from deserts and rainforests to open steppe and mountain slopes, and are found across Asia and the Americas. Well-known groups include the rattlesnakes of North America, the lanceheads of Central and South America, the habu of Okinawa, and the bushmaster.

Venom

Pit viper venom is a complex mixture of enzymes and toxins that typically causes local tissue damage, disruption of blood clotting, and sometimes systemic effects. Many species are medically significant, and bites require prompt treatment.
