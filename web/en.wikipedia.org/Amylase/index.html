Amylase

An amylase is an enzyme that catalyses the hydrolysis of starch into sugars. Amylase is present in the saliva of humans and some other mammals, where it begins the chemical process of digestion. Foods that contain large amounts of starch but little sugar, such as rice and potatoes, may acquire a slightly sweet taste as they are chewed because amylase degrades some of their starch into sugar.

Amylase
EC number: 3.2.1.1 (α-amylase)
Function: hydrolyses starch to sugars
Found in: saliva, pancreatic juice, plants, microorganisms

Classification

Alpha-amylases break the internal α-1,4-glycosidic bonds of starch randomly, producing dextrins, maltose, and maltotriose. Beta-amylases, found in plants and microorganisms, cleave maltose units from the non-reducing ends of chains and are important in brewing and sweetener production. Gamma-amylase cleaves at α-1,6 linkages as well.

Uses and clinical significance

In the pancreas, amylase helps digest dietary starch in the small intestine. Blood amylase levels are measured clinically, as elevated serum amylase can indicate acute pancreatitis. Industrially, amylases are used in brewing, baking, textile desizing, and in the production of high-fructose corn syrup and biofuels. Amylase was the first enzyme discovered, isolated from malt extract by Anselme Payen in 1833 (as "diastase").
