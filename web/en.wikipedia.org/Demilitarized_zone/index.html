Demilitarized zone

A demilitarized zone (DMZ or DZ) is an area in which treaties or agreements between states, military powers or contending groups forbid military installations, activities, or personnel. A demilitarized zone often lies along an established frontier or boundary between two or more military powers or alliances. A demilitarized zone may sometimes form a de facto international border, such as the Korean Demilitarized Zone.

Notable zones

The Korean Demilitarized Zone, established by the 1953 armistice agreement, is a strip of land about 250 km long and 4 km wide running across the Korean Peninsula, separating North and South Korea; paradoxically it is one of the most heavily militarized borders in the world along its edges, while the zone itself, largely untouched for decades, has become an involuntary wildlife sanctuary. Other notable examples include the demilitarized zone that divided North and South Vietnam during the Vietnam War, the buffer zone in Cyprus patrolled by the United Nations since 1974, and the demilitarized Sinai arrangements between Egypt and Israel.

Purpose and status

Demilitarized zones are created to reduce the risk of accidental clashes and escalation, to separate opposing forces after a ceasefire, or to neutralize disputed territory. Some demilitarized zones have remained in place for decades and in effect become permanent borders, while others lapse when peace treaties resolve the underlying conflict.
