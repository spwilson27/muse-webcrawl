Matter

In classical physics and general chemistry, matter is any substance that has mass and takes up space by having volume. All everyday objects that can be touched are ultimately composed of atoms, which are made up of interacting subatomic particles. In everyday as well as scientific usage, matter generally includes atoms and anything made up of them, and any particles (or combination of particles) that act as if they have both rest mass and volume.

Matter does not include massless particles such as photons, or other energy phenomena or waves such as light or heat. Matter exists in various states (also known as phases). These include classical everyday phases such as the solid, liquid, and gas — for example water exists as ice, liquid water, and gaseous steam — but other states are possible, including plasma, Bose–Einstein condensates, fermionic condensates, and quark–gluon plasma.

Definitions

In physics, there is no single universal definition of matter; it is often defined in terms of what it is composed of (particles with rest mass), by contrast with antimatter, or operationally through its gravitational and inertial properties described by general relativity and particle physics. The Standard Model describes ordinary matter as composed of quarks and leptons, held together by fundamental forces.

States of matter

The classical states of matter are distinguished by the strength of intermolecular forces and the arrangement of particles. Adding or removing energy — typically through heating or cooling — causes phase transitions. Beyond the classical states, high-energy and extreme-density conditions produce exotic states studied in laboratories and inferred in astrophysical bodies such as neutron stars.

Matter in the universe

Ordinary (baryonic) matter makes up only about 5% of the energy density of the observable universe; the remainder is attributed to dark matter (about 27%) and dark energy (about 68%). Most of the mass of ordinary matter comes from protons and neutrons, whose mass derives largely from the binding energy of quarks via quantum chromodynamics.
