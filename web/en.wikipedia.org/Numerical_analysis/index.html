Numerical analysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Babylonian tablet with an approximation of √2

Numerical analysis is the study of algorithms that use numerical approximation (as opposed to symbolic manipulations) for the problems of mathematical analysis. It is concerned with methods for solving continuous problems — involving real or complex variables, differential equations, and large systems of equations — where exact analytic solutions are unavailable or impractical.

Areas of study

Major areas include solving equations and systems of equations (linear and nonlinear), interpolation, extrapolation and regression, numerical integration and differentiation, optimisation, and the numerical solution of ordinary and partial differential equations. Closely related is numerical linear algebra, which deals with matrix computations such as factorisations and eigenvalue problems.

Error and stability

Because computations are performed with finite precision, numerical analysis studies how errors — truncation error from approximating infinite processes, and roundoff error from floating-point arithmetic — propagate. Key concepts include conditioning of a problem, stability of an algorithm, and rates of convergence.

History

The field has ancient roots: Babylonian mathematicians computed square roots by iteration nearly 4,000 years ago. Modern numerical analysis grew rapidly with the advent of electronic computers in the 1940s, with pioneers such as John von Neumann and Herman Goldstine.
