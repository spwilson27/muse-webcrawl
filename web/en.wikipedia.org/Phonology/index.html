Phonology

Phonology is the branch of linguistics that studies how languages systematically organize their sounds. Although the term is closely related to phonetics, which studies the physical properties of speech sounds, phonology concerns the abstract, mental representation of sounds — how they function to distinguish meaning within a particular language. The fundamental unit of phonological analysis is the phoneme.

Basic concepts

A phoneme is an abstract unit of sound that distinguishes one word from another (e.g., English /p/ vs. /b/ in pat vs. bat, demonstrated by minimal pairs). Contextual variants of a phoneme are its allophones: for example, the aspirated [pʰ] in pin and the unaspirated [p] in spin are allophones of /p/ in English. Phonology also studies syllable structure, stress patterns, intonation, and the permissible sequences of sounds (phonotactics) in a language.

History

The discipline emerged from the work of Jan Baudouin de Courtenay and the Kazan school in the late 19th century, was central to the structuralism of the Prague School, and was transformed by generative phonology following Chomsky and Halle's The Sound Pattern of English (1968). Later frameworks include autosegmental phonology, metrical phonology, and optimality theory.
