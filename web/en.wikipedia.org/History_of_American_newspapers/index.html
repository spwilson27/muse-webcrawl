History of American newspapers

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of American newspapers begins in the colonial era, when the first newspaper in British North America, Publick Occurrences Both Forreign and Domestick, appeared in Boston in 1690 and was promptly suppressed. From a handful of colonial weeklies, the American press grew into the penny press of the 1830s, the mass-circulation dailies of the industrial era, the "yellow journalism" of the 1890s, and the great metropolitan papers of the twentieth century, before entering a period of steep decline in the digital age as advertising and readership moved online.

Colonial era

Colonial newspapers were typically four-page weeklies printed by postmasters and printers, dependent on official favour and filled with foreign news reprinted from London papers. Notable titles included the Boston News-Letter (1704), the first continuously published newspaper in the colonies, and Benjamin Franklin's Pennsylvania Gazette. The 1735 trial of John Peter Zenger in New York, acquitted of seditious libel after his lawyer argued that truth should be a defence, became a celebrated precedent for press freedom. Newspapers were central to the political mobilisation of the American Revolution, and the First Amendment (1791) constitutionally protected freedom of the press.

The partisan press and the penny press

In the early republic, newspapers were openly partisan organs subsidised by political parties through printing contracts and post office patronage. Editors such as John Fenno, Philip Freneau, and later William Lloyd Garrison (whose abolitionist Liberator began in 1831) made the press an arena of fierce political conflict. A revolution came in 1833 with Benjamin Day's New York Sun, sold for one cent and supported by advertising rather than subscriptions and party money; James Gordon Bennett's New York Herald (1835) and Horace Greeley's New-York Tribune (1841) followed. The penny press created the modern news business: professional reporters, human-interest stories, crime news, and aggressive news gathering, aided by the telegraph (and the founding of the Associated Press cooperative in 1846 to share its costs).

Civil War to yellow journalism

The Civil War vastly expanded war reporting and the appetite for news. After the war, cheap paper, steam presses, and urbanisation produced mass-circulation dailies. Joseph Pulitzer's New York World combined sensational crusading with mass appeal; William Randolph Hearst bought the rival New York Journal in 1895, and the two papers' circulation war—marked by lurid headlines, stunts, and jingoistic coverage of the Cuban crisis—gave "yellow journalism" its name and was popularly (if exaggeratedly) blamed for pushing the United States into the Spanish–American War of 1898. At the same time, Adolph Ochs's purchase of The New York Times in 1896 promoted a sober alternative model of comprehensive, factual reporting under the slogan "All the News That's Fit to Print".

The twentieth century

The 20th century brought professionalisation—journalism schools, codes of ethics, and the norm of objectivity—and consolidation into chains (Hearst, Scripps, Knight Ridder, Gannett). Muckraking magazines and, later, broadcast competition reshaped newspapers' role. Metropolitan dailies reached their peak influence covering the Second World War, the civil rights movement, Vietnam, and Watergate, when reporting by the Washington Post helped bring down President Richard Nixon. From the 1970s, evening papers died, morning papers consolidated, and most cities became one-newspaper towns, though surviving papers remained highly profitable into the 1990s.

Decline in the digital era

The internet destroyed the newspaper business model: classified advertising migrated to sites such as Craigslist and later to Google and Facebook, display advertising followed audiences online, and free digital news undermined subscriptions. From the mid-2000s, circulation and newsroom employment fell sharply; thousands of local papers closed—the United States has lost roughly a third of its newspapers since 2005—creating "news deserts", while hedge-fund ownership and successive rounds of layoffs hollowed out many survivors. National brands such as The New York Times and The Wall Street Journal rebuilt substantial digital subscription businesses, but the crisis of local news remains a major public concern.
