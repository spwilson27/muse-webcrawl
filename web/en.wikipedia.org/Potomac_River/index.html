The Potomac River is a major river in the Mid-Atlantic region of the United States, flowing about 405 miles (652 km) from the Allegheny Mountains of West Virginia to the Chesapeake Bay. It forms much of the boundary between Maryland and West Virginia and Virginia — uniquely, the river itself lies almost entirely within Maryland's borders by colonial charter — and it flows past Washington, D.C., the national capital, whose metropolitan area of more than six million people depends on it for drinking water. Historically nicknamed "the Nation's River," the Potomac drains a watershed of nearly 15,000 square miles across four states and the District of Columbia.

Course

The river has two headwater branches. The North Branch rises at the Fairfax Stone in West Virginia and flows northeast past Cumberland, Maryland; the South Branch winds through the Shenandoah Valley region. They join near Green Spring, West Virginia. At Harpers Ferry the Potomac is joined by its largest tributary, the Shenandoah River, and cuts through the Blue Ridge in a famous water gap. Below, at Great Falls and Little Falls, the river drops over the Fall Line — the head of navigation — before becoming a wide tidal estuary at Washington, reaching 11 miles wide at its mouth.

History

Algonquian-speaking peoples, including the Piscataway, lived along the river for millennia; the name derives from "Patawomeck," recorded by Captain John Smith, who explored the river in 1608. George Washington's home, Mount Vernon, overlooks the river, and Washington championed its navigation, founding the Potowmack Company whose canals preceded the Chesapeake and Ohio Canal (1828–1850), which parallels the Maryland bank for 184.5 miles. The river was a strategic frontier throughout the Civil War — the Army of the Potomac took its name from it — and saw fighting at Antietam Creek, Ball's Bluff, and Harpers Ferry.

Environment

By the 1960s the Potomac was notorious for pollution; President Lyndon Johnson called it "a national disgrace" in 1965, and its Washington reaches suffered algal blooms and fish kills. Decades of wastewater treatment upgrades and watershed management have greatly improved water quality: bald eagles, American shad, and rockfish have returned, and the river's health grade from the Potomac Conservancy has risen to "B" in recent assessments, though stormwater runoff, algal blooms, and intersex fish remain concerns. The river also supplies about 78% of the Washington region's drinking water and is home to the endangered shortnose sturgeon and the freshwater mussel fauna of the upper basin.
