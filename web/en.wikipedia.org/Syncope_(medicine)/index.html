Syncope (medicine)

Syncope, commonly known as fainting or passing out, is a loss of consciousness and muscle strength characterised by a fast onset, short duration, and spontaneous recovery. It is caused by a transient decrease in blood flow to the brain, usually from low blood pressure. Before an episode, a person may feel lightheaded, nauseated, sweaty, or have visual disturbances; recovery is generally rapid once the person is lying flat and cerebral perfusion is restored.

Causes

Syncope is classified into three main categories. Reflex (neurally mediated) syncope, including the common vasovagal faint, results from a reflex bradycardia and vasodilation triggered by pain, emotion, prolonged standing, or the sight of blood. Orthostatic syncope follows a fall in blood pressure on standing, due to dehydration, blood loss, autonomic failure, or medications. Cardiac syncope arises from arrhythmias or structural heart disease—such as aortic stenosis or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy—and carries the most serious prognosis. Up to a third of cases remain unexplained after initial evaluation.

Diagnosis and management

Evaluation centres on history, physical examination, blood pressure measurements lying and standing, and an electrocardiogram; tilt-table testing, cardiac monitoring, and echocardiography are used selectively. Treatment depends on the cause: reassurance, hydration, counter-pressure manoeuvres, and avoidance of triggers for vasovagal syncope; medication review and volume repletion for orthostatic causes; and pacemakers, ablation, or surgery for cardiac causes. Syncope accounts for roughly 1–3% of emergency department visits.

Epidemiology

Fainting is common: up to 40% of people report at least one episode in their lifetime. It shows peaks in adolescence and in older age, and in the elderly it is often multifactorial and associated with falls and fractures. Distinguishing syncope from seizures, hypoglycaemia, and psychogenic pseudosyncope is a key diagnostic task.
