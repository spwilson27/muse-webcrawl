Clerestory

Clerestory
Pronunciation: /ˈklɪərstɔːri/
Element: Upper level of windows above an adjoining roof
Found in: Basilicas, Gothic cathedrals, Romanesque churches
Purpose: Light and ventilation of tall interiors

In architecture, a clerestory (also clearstory) is the upper part of the nave of a basilican church or other large building, containing a level of windows above the roofs of the side aisles and below the main roof, which admits light to the central space. The term is also applied more generally to any raised strip of windows set above an adjacent roofline.

History

Clerestories appeared in ancient Egyptian temple halls, such as the hypostyle hall at Karnak, where a raised central roof allowed light through stone grilles. The device became fundamental in Roman basilicas and in early Christian churches such as Old St Peter's in Rome. In Romanesque and especially Gothic architecture, the clerestory formed the third level of the nave elevation, above the arcade and triforium; advances in vaulting and the flying buttress allowed ever larger clerestory windows filled with stained glass, as at Chartres and Amiens cathedrals.

Later uses

Beyond churches, clerestory windows appear in railway carriages and double-decker buses (raised central roofs), in industrial "sawtooth" factory roofs, and in modern houses, where they bring daylight deep into a plan while preserving privacy. In Frank Lloyd Wright's Prairie houses and in contemporary passive design, clerestories are used for daylighting and stack ventilation.
