Absolute risk reduction

In epidemiology, the absolute risk reduction (ARR), also called risk difference, is the absolute difference in outcomes between a control group and a treatment group. It is the inverse of the number needed to treat. In contrast, the relative risk reduction is the difference in event rates between two groups, expressed as a proportion of the event rate in the untreated group.

For example, consider a hypothetical drug which reduces the risk of colon cancer from 2 cases per 5000 people to 1 case per 5000 people. The relative risk reduction is 0.5 (or 50%), while the absolute risk reduction is 0.0002 (or 0.02%). This example illustrates why absolute risk reduction is sometimes considered the more informative measure for clinical and public-health decisions: a large relative reduction may apply to a very small absolute risk.

Calculation

The absolute risk reduction is calculated as:

ARR = (risk in control group) − (risk in treatment group)

If the treatment increases the risk of an adverse outcome, the same subtraction yields a negative value, which is then usually reported as an absolute risk increase (ARI). The number needed to treat (NNT) is computed as 1 ÷ ARR, and the number needed to harm (NNH) as 1 ÷ ARI.

Interpretation

Unlike relative measures, the ARR depends on the baseline risk of the population studied, so it may not generalise directly to populations with different baseline risks. Reporting both absolute and relative measures is recommended when presenting the results of clinical trials.
