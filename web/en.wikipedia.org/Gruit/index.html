Gruit

Gruit (alternately grut or gruyt) is a herb mixture used for bittering and flavouring beer, popular before the extensive use of hops as the standard bittering agent. Gruit was the typical flavouring of beer in much of Europe from the early medieval period until the 16th century, when hopped beer largely displaced it.

Ingredients

The composition of gruit varied by region and was often a closely guarded local monopoly (the "gruitrecht", or gruit right, was a valuable tax and licensing privilege held by cities and rulers, who sold the mixture to brewers). Its core herbs commonly included sweet gale (bog myrtle), mugwort, and yarrow, supplemented by many others such as ground ivy, horehound, heather, juniper, ginger, caraway, aniseed, nutmeg, and cinnamon. Some mixtures contained herbs with mild narcotic or stimulating effects, contributing to the reputed intoxicating character of gruit ales.

Decline

Hops gradually replaced gruit between the 11th and 16th centuries, spreading from northern Germany and the Low Countries. Hops offered superior preservative qualities, a cleaner bitterness, and — importantly — freedom from the gruit tax and monopoly, which also made hopped beer a symbol of Protestant resistance to the (often Catholic) gruit monopolies. Purity laws such as Bavaria's Reinheitsgebot of 1516 codified hops as the permitted seasoning. Gruit beer survived longest in parts of the Low Countries, Scotland (heather ale), and Scandinavia.

Revival

Craft brewers have revived gruit ales since the late 20th century, and an International Gruit Day (1 February) has been celebrated since 2013 to promote the style. Modern gruits use a wide range of herbs, spices, and botanicals in place of hops.
