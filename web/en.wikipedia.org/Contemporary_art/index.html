Contemporary art is the art of today, produced by artists who are living in the present day. For many art historians and institutions, the contemporary period begins after the Second World War or around 1970, succeeding modern art. Contemporary art is notable for its global scope, its diversity of media and methods — from painting and sculpture to installation, video, performance, sound, and digital art — and the absence of a single dominant movement or style.

Characteristics

Contemporary artists frequently engage directly with social and political questions — identity, globalization, ecology, technology, and memory — and often blur the boundaries between art, activism, and everyday life. Conceptual strategies, in which the idea is paramount, coexist with revivals of craft and figurative painting. Much contemporary work is site-specific or participatory, created for biennials, public spaces, and digital platforms as much as for the gallery wall.

Institutions and market

The contemporary art world is organized around museums of contemporary art, international exhibitions such as the Venice Biennale and documenta, art fairs such as Art Basel and Frieze, and a commercial gallery system concentrated in cities including New York, London, Berlin, Hong Kong, and Seoul. Auction prices for living artists and recently deceased modern masters have reached hundreds of millions of dollars, prompting debate about art as an asset class.

Debates

Critics dispute when the contemporary begins, whether "contemporary" names a style or merely a time period, and how far the market shapes artistic production. The rise of non-Western art histories has challenged Euro-American narratives, emphasizing multiple modernities and regional scenes.
