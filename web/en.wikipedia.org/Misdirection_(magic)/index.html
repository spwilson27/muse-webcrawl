Misdirection (magic)

Misdirection is a form of deception in magic and sleight of hand in which the performer directs the audience's attention to one thing so as to distract it from another—typically the secret action (the "method") that produces the effect. It is considered one of the foundational principles of conjuring: as the classic maxim puts it, "the hand is quicker than the eye" is a myth; a skilled performer instead manages where and when the audience looks.

Principles

Magicians distinguish between passive misdirection, which relies on natural tendencies of attention—people follow the performer's gaze, gestures, larger movements, novelty, and social cues—and active misdirection, which deliberately creates a focus of interest: a question, a joke, a dramatic moment, or an apparent moment of relaxation ("off-beat") during which the secret move is executed. Timing is central: the secret action is typically performed at a moment when the audience believes nothing important is happening.

Theorists such as Nevil Maskelyne, Jean-Eugène Robert-Houdin, and in modern times Tommy Wonder, Dai Vernon, and Juan Tamariz have analysed misdirection as applied psychology. Cognitive scientists have adopted stage magic as a tool for studying attention, change blindness, and inattentional blindness, collaborating with magicians to design experiments in which misdirection conceals visible events.

Related concepts

Misdirection is closely related to, but distinct from, sleight of hand: sleights are the physical techniques, while misdirection is the attentional framework that hides them. Outside entertainment, analogous techniques are discussed in pickpocketing, confidence tricks, military deception, and covert operations.
