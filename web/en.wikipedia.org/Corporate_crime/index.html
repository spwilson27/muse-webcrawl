Corporate crime

In criminology, corporate crime refers to crimes committed either by a corporation (a business entity having a separate legal personality), or by individuals acting on behalf of a corporation or other business entity, in furtherance of the organisation's goals. It is distinct from occupational crime, which benefits the individual perpetrator, and from white-collar crime more broadly, a term coined by Edwin Sutherland, who pioneered the study of offences by respectable, high-status offenders.

Types

Corporate offences include fraud and false accounting, bribery of officials, antitrust and competition violations, environmental offences such as illegal dumping, health and safety violations causing injury or death (sometimes prosecuted as corporate manslaughter), the sale of unsafe products, and tax evasion. Notable cases include the Enron accounting scandal (2001), the Volkswagen emissions scandal (2015), and the Bernard Madoff investment fraud.

Enforcement difficulties

Prosecuting corporate crime is complicated by the diffusion of responsibility within organisations — the "problem of many hands" — and, in jurisdictions following the identification doctrine, by the need to attribute criminal intent to a "directing mind" of the company. Reforms such as the United Kingdom's failure-to-prevent model (for bribery and fraud) and deferred prosecution agreements in the United States seek to make enforcement more effective. Critics argue that penalties are often treated as a cost of doing business and that sanctions rarely reach senior decision-makers, while others caution that aggressive prosecution can harm innocent shareholders and employees.
