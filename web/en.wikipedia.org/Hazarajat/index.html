The mountainous Hazarajat, homeland of the Hazara people.

Hazarajat (Persian/Dari: هزاره‌جات), also written Hazaristan, is the mountainous homeland region of the Hazara people in the central highlands of Afghanistan. It encompasses principally Bamyan, Daykundi, and parts of Ghazni, Ghor, Maidan Wardak, Uruzgan, Parwan, and Samangan provinces. The term -jāt is a Persian suffix meaning "land of"; Hazarajat thus means "land of the Hazara."

Geography

The region is rugged mountain country on the western Hindu Kush, at elevations of roughly 2,000 to 4,000 metres, with harsh winters. It contains the sources of several major rivers — the Helmand, Hari Rud, Murghab, Balkh, and Kabul tributaries. Notable sites include the Band-e Amir lakes, Afghanistan's first national park, and the Bamyan Valley, famous for the 6th-century Buddhas of Bamyan destroyed by the Taliban in 2001.

History

The Hazara, a predominantly Twelver Shia people speaking Hazaragi, a dialect of Dari with Turkic and Mongolic loanwords, are traditionally believed to descend in part from Mongol armies of the 13th century. The Hazarajat remained largely autonomous until the campaigns of Amir Abdur Rahman Khan in the 1890s, which conquered the region with great brutality, massacres, enslavement, and land confiscations that drove many Hazara into exile in Iran and British India (notably Quetta). Hazaras suffered systemic discrimination through the 20th century, persecution and massacres under the Taliban in the 1990s (including the 1998 Mazar-i-Sharif killings), and attacks by Islamic State–Khorasan after 2015. Following the Taliban's return to power in 2021, concerns over Hazara security and rights have continued.
