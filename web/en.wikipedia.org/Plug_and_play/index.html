Plug and play

In computing, a plug and play (PnP) device or computer bus is one with a specification that facilitates the recognition of a hardware component in a system without the need for physical device configuration or user intervention in resolving resource conflicts. The term "plug and play" has since been expanded to any system in which a device works immediately when connected.

History

Plug and play was promoted by Microsoft, Intel, and Compaq in the early 1990s and became widely known with the release of Windows 95 in 1995, which included built-in support for automatically detecting and configuring ISA expansion cards. Before PnP, users often had to set jumpers and manually assign IRQ lines, DMA channels, and I/O addresses, a process that was error-prone and led to the sarcastic nickname "plug and pray".

Modern buses

The PCI bus was designed with automatic configuration in mind, and USB devices are inherently plug and play: when a USB peripheral is attached, the host enumerates it, loads the appropriate device driver, and makes it available without a reboot. Hot swapping extends the concept to devices that can be added or removed while the system is running.

UPnP

Universal Plug and Play (UPnP) applies similar ideas to networking, allowing devices to discover each other and establish services automatically on a local network.
