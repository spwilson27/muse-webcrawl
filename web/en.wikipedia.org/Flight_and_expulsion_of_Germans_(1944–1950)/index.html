Flight and expulsion of Germans (1944–1950)

The flight and expulsion of Germans during and after World War II (1944–1950) was the forced migration of ethnic German populations from Central and Eastern Europe to Germany and Austria. It was the largest population transfer in European history, involving between 12 and 14.6 million people.

Flight before the Red Army

Beginning in late 1944, millions of Germans fled westward from East Prussia, Silesia, Pomerania and other eastern territories ahead of the advancing Red Army, fearing reprisals after Nazi atrocities in the Soviet Union. Evacuations such as Operation Hannibal by sea from Baltic ports were overtaken by disaster, including the sinking of the Wilhelm Gustloff in January 1945, one of the deadliest maritime disasters in history.

Expulsions

After the war, the Potsdam Agreement (August 1945) sanctioned the "orderly and humane" transfer of German populations from Poland, Czechoslovakia and Hungary. In practice, both the chaotic "wild expulsions" of 1945 and the later organized transfers were accompanied by violence, internment camps, forced labour, expropriation and severe hardship. Germans were expelled from territories that became part of Poland (east of the Oder–Neisse line) and the Soviet Union (northern East Prussia), from the Sudetenland in Czechoslovakia under the Beneš decrees, and from Yugoslavia, Romania and Hungary.

Human cost and memory

Estimates of deaths among the expellees vary widely, from about 500,000 to over 2 million in older West German estimates. The expellees (Vertriebene) formed influential organizations in postwar West Germany, and the expulsions remain a subject of historical and political debate in Germany, Poland and the Czech Republic.
