Pressure-fed engine

A pressure-fed engine is a type of liquid-propellant rocket engine in which propellants are forced from their tanks into the combustion chamber by a high-pressure gas, rather than by turbopumps. The pressurant—usually helium or nitrogen stored in separate high-pressure vessels—regulates tank pressure so that fuel and oxidizer flow through the feed lines at the required rate.

Advantages and disadvantages

The pressure-fed cycle is the simplest rocket engine cycle: it has few moving parts, needs no turbomachinery, and is highly reliable and easy to restart, making it attractive for spacecraft propulsion, reaction control systems, and small launch vehicles. Examples include the Apollo Lunar Module descent and ascent engines and the AJ10 engine used on the Apollo Service Module and Delta II upper stage.

The main drawback is performance. Because the tanks must withstand the full feed pressure, they are heavy, and practical chamber pressures are low (typically under about 2 MPa), limiting thrust and efficiency. For large launch vehicle boosters, pump-fed cycles such as the gas-generator or staged combustion cycles are preferred despite their complexity.
