Clear-air turbulence

Clear-air turbulence
Abbreviation: CAT
Phenomenon: Erratic air motion in cloudless skies
Typical altitude: 23,000–39,000 ft (cruising levels)
Cause: Wind shear near jet streams and tropopause

Clear-air turbulence (CAT) is the turbulent movement of air masses in the absence of any visual clues, such as clouds, that occurs mainly at high altitude. It is a significant hazard to aviation because aircraft can encounter it unexpectedly: unlike turbulence associated with thunderstorms, it is invisible to pilots and largely undetectable by conventional weather radar, which senses precipitation rather than air motion.

Causes

CAT arises mainly from strong vertical and horizontal wind shear, particularly near the jet streams at the tropopause (roughly 7–12 km altitude), where fast-moving air borders slower air. Kelvin–Helmholtz instabilities along these shear layers can generate breaking waves and eddies. Mountain waves propagating upward from terrain and the vicinity of upper-level fronts and troughs are other sources. Because the phenomenon is patchy and transient, forecasts are given in terms of probability over broad regions along flight routes.

Effects and safety

Most encounters cause only light to moderate buffeting, but severe CAT has injured passengers and cabin crew — injuries during turbulence are the most common cause of harm in non-fatal airline accidents — and rare extreme events have caused structural damage. Research indicates that clear-air turbulence has intensified over busy routes such as the North Atlantic in recent decades, a trend attributed to climate change strengthening temperature gradients and wind shear at cruising altitudes. Mitigation measures include route planning around forecast shear zones, pilot reports (PIREPs), experimental lidar detection, and the routine use of seat belts.
