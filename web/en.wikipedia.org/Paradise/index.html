Paradise

The word "paradise" originally denoted a walled Persian royal park or garden.

In religion and philosophy, paradise is a place of exceptional happiness and delight. The term is used in several religions to describe the abode of the righteous dead, and in the Abrahamic traditions it is closely associated with the Garden of Eden and with Heaven. The English word derives, via Greek paradeisos, from the Old Iranian pairidaēza, "walled enclosure" — the name for the great hunting parks of the Achaemenid Persian kings.

Etymology and origins

Xenophon and other Greek writers borrowed paradeisos to describe Persian royal parks stocked with trees and game. When the Hebrew Bible was translated into Greek in the Septuagint, the word was used for the Garden of Eden, giving it its religious sense. In the New Testament, "paradise" appears three times, including Jesus's promise to the penitent thief: "today you will be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43).

In Judaism and Christianity

In Second Temple Judaism, paradise (Hebrew Gan Eden) came to denote the dwelling of the righteous in the afterlife. Christian theology variously identifies paradise with the Garden of Eden restored, with the intermediate state of the blessed dead, or with Heaven itself. Medieval imagination mapped paradise as a physical place — often a walled garden at the top of the mountain of Purgatory in Dante's Divine Comedy. In the Latter Day Saint tradition, paradise is a part of the spirit world where righteous spirits await resurrection.

In Islam

The Qur'an's usual word for paradise is Jannah ("garden"), whose highest level is al-Firdaws — a cognate of "paradise". Jannah is depicted as a lush garden with rivers of water, milk, honey and wine, promised to the faithful. Islamic tradition describes eight gates of paradise and rewards for martyrs and the righteous.

Other traditions

Analogous concepts include the Elysian Fields of Greek myth, Valhalla in Norse tradition, the Buddhist Pure Lands, and the Zoroastrian Garodman. By extension, "paradise" is used secularly for any place of supreme bliss, from tropical islands to John Milton's epic Paradise Lost (1667), the classic English treatment of the Fall.
