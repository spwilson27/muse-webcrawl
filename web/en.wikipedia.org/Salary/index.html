Salary

A salary is a form of periodic payment from an employer to an employee, which may be specified in an employment contract. It is contrasted with piece wages, where each job, hour or other unit is paid separately, rather than on a periodic basis. From the point of view of running a business, salary can also be thought of as the cost of acquiring and retaining human resources for running operations, and is then termed personnel expense or salary expense.

History

The word "salary" derives from the Latin salarium, connected with sal (salt), possibly referring to a soldier's allowance for buying salt, salt being a valuable commodity in antiquity. Through the medieval and early modern periods, salaried offices became common in church, state, and commerce. In modern economies the salary is the standard compensation for professional, managerial, and administrative ("white-collar") work, while hourly wages remain common in many manual and service occupations.

Characteristics

A salaried employee typically receives the same gross pay each pay period — weekly, fortnightly, or most commonly monthly — regardless of the exact number of hours worked, and in many jurisdictions is exempt from overtime pay rules that protect hourly workers (for example, "exempt" employees under the US Fair Labor Standards Act). Salaried compensation is usually expressed as an annual figure and is often accompanied by benefits such as paid leave, health insurance, and pension contributions, which together form the total compensation package.

Negotiation and determination

Salaries are set by labour-market supply and demand, collective bargaining, statutory minimum wages, internal pay structures, and individual negotiation. Many employers use salary bands for each grade of job. Executive salaries attract particular scrutiny because of their scale relative to median pay. See also Minimum wage and Employment.
