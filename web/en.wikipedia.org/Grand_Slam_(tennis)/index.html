Grand Slam (tennis)

In tennis, a Grand Slam refers to winning all four major championships in a single calendar year: the Australian Open, the French Open, Wimbledon, and the US Open. The term also denotes each of the four tournaments individually ("a Grand Slam tournament" or "major"). Winning all four across a career, not necessarily in one year, is a "Career Grand Slam".

Tournaments: Australian Open (hard), French Open (clay), Wimbledon (grass), US Open (hard)

First singles Grand Slam: Don Budge (1938)

Most singles titles: Novak Djokovic (24, men); Margaret Court (24, women)

The four majors

The Australian Open (Melbourne, January, hard court), the French Open or Roland Garros (Paris, May–June, clay), Wimbledon (London, June–July, grass — the oldest tournament, founded 1877), and the US Open (New York, August–September, hard court) together form the sport's premier events, offering the most ranking points and prize money in tennis. Each draws 128 singles players in the main draw and is played over two weeks.

Calendar Grand Slam winners

Only five singles players have achieved the calendar-year Grand Slam: Don Budge (1938), Maureen Connolly (1953), Rod Laver (1962 and 1969 — the only man to do it twice, and the only one in the Open Era), Margaret Court (1970), and Steffi Graf (1988, whose feat combined with Olympic gold is called the Golden Slam). In doubles and wheelchair tennis there have been further instances, including Dylan Alcott's Golden Slam in 2021.

Other uses of the term

"Grand Slam" is also used for winning the four majors consecutively across two seasons (a "Non-calendar year Grand Slam", achieved by Novak Djokovic in 2015–16 as the "Djoker Slam") and for career achievements such as the Career Golden Slam — all four majors plus Olympic gold — completed by Graf, Andre Agassi, Rafael Nadal, Serena Williams, and Djokovic.
