Public prosecution

Public prosecution is the conduct of criminal proceedings by an official acting on behalf of the state or the public, as opposed to a private prosecution brought by an individual victim. In most modern legal systems the public prosecutor — variously titled district attorney, Crown prosecutor, state attorney, or procureur — decides whether to bring charges, conducts the case at trial, and is expected to act as a "minister of justice" rather than simply to seek convictions.

Organization in different systems

In the United States, prosecution is highly decentralized: federal cases are handled by United States Attorneys within the Department of Justice, while most crime is prosecuted by locally elected district attorneys at county level. In England and Wales, the Crown Prosecution Service, created by the Prosecution of Offences Act 1985 and headed by the Director of Public Prosecutions, took over most prosecutions from the police in 1986. In civil-law systems, prosecutors typically form a hierarchical corps within or alongside the judiciary — in France, the parquet under the Minister of Justice; in Germany, the Staatsanwaltschaft — and in inquisitorial traditions they may supervise the judicial investigation itself. China's people's procuratorates combine prosecution with a broader legal-supervision role.

Discretion and accountability

Prosecutors commonly apply a two-stage test: whether the evidence affords a realistic prospect of conviction, and whether prosecution is in the public interest. The breadth of prosecutorial discretion — including charge selection and plea bargaining, which resolves the great majority of cases in the United States — makes the office a major site of debate about consistency, racial and socioeconomic disparity, and democratic accountability.
