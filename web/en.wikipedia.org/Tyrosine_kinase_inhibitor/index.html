Tyrosine kinase inhibitor

Drug classTargeted cancer therapy
MechanismBlocks tyrosine kinase enzymes
First approvedImatinib (2001)
ExamplesImatinib, erlotinib, sunitinib, osimertinib

A tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) is a pharmaceutical drug that inhibits tyrosine kinases — enzymes that transfer phosphate groups from ATP to tyrosine residues on proteins, acting as "on/off" switches in cell signalling. Many tyrosine kinases are mutated or overexpressed in cancer, so blocking them can halt tumour growth; TKIs are a cornerstone of targeted cancer therapy.

Mechanism

Most TKIs are small molecules that compete with ATP for the kinase binding pocket, preventing phosphorylation of downstream substrates in pathways driving cell division and survival. Antibodies against receptor tyrosine kinases, such as trastuzumab (anti-HER2), act on similar targets from outside the cell.

Clinical milestones

Imatinib, approved in 2001 for chronic myeloid leukaemia, transformed a fatal disease driven by the BCR-ABL fusion kinase into a manageable chronic condition and launched the era of precision oncology. Later TKIs target EGFR in lung cancer (gefitinib, erlotinib, osimertinib), VEGF receptors in kidney and other cancers (sunitinib, pazopanib), ALK (crizotinib, alectinib) and BTK in B-cell malignancies (ibrutinib).

Resistance and side effects

Tumours commonly evolve resistance through secondary mutations in the kinase domain — such as the T790M "gatekeeper" mutation in EGFR — driving development of successive inhibitor generations. Side effects vary by target and include rash, diarrhoea, hypertension and, for some agents, cardiac toxicity.
