Peninsular Ranges

The Peninsular Ranges are a group of mountain ranges in Southern California and the Baja California Peninsula in Mexico. They form part of the Pacific Coast Ranges of North America, extending roughly 1,500 km (930 mi) from the Los Angeles Basin and the Channel Islands south through Baja California to near La Paz.

Geography

In the United States the ranges include the Santa Ana Mountains, San Jacinto Mountains, Santa Rosa Mountains, Palomar Mountain, Laguna Mountains, and the San Ysidro Mountains. The highest point in the U.S. portion is San Jacinto Peak (3,302 m). In Mexico the ranges continue as the Sierra de Juárez and Sierra de San Pedro Mártir, whose Picacho del Diablo (3,096 m) is the highest point on the Baja California Peninsula, followed by the Sierra de la Laguna in the south.

Geology

The ranges are largely composed of Mesozoic granitic rocks of the Peninsular Ranges Batholith, formed as magma intruded during subduction along the western margin of North America. The ranges are bounded by major fault systems, including the San Andreas and San Jacinto faults to the east and the Elsinore fault zone.

Ecology

The ranges carry chaparral and oak woodland at lower elevations, coniferous forest at higher elevations, and grade into desert on their eastern slopes. Much of the region lies within the California chaparral and woodlands and California montane chaparral ecoregions, noted for high endemism; the Sierra de San Pedro Mártir and Sierra de la Laguna are important refuges, the latter a UNESCO biosphere reserve.
