English Dissenters or English Separatists were Protestant Christians in England who separated from the Church of England during the sixteenth, seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Often known collectively as Nonconformists, they included Presbyterians, Independents (later Congregationalists), Baptists and Quakers. Dissenters rejected the authority of the established church in matters of doctrine, worship or church government.

Origins

Dissent grew out of Puritan dissatisfaction with the Elizabethan religious settlement, which many regarded as insufficiently reformed. Under the Stuarts, separatist congregations faced persecution and some emigrated, notably to New England. The English Civil War and the Commonwealth temporarily gave the godly parties power, but the Restoration of Charles II in 1660 re-established Anglican supremacy.

The penal laws

The Act of Uniformity 1662 required clergy to assent to the Book of Common Prayer; around 2,000 ministers who refused were ejected in the "Great Ejection". The Clarendon Code — including the Conventicle Act and Five Mile Act — restricted dissenting worship and preaching, while the Test Acts barred Dissenters from public office and the universities. Persecution varied in intensity and was often evaded in practice.

Toleration and emancipation

The Toleration Act 1689, passed after the Glorious Revolution, granted freedom of worship to Trinitarian Protestant Dissenters, though civil disabilities remained. Dissenting academies provided advanced education outside Oxford and Cambridge. During the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Methodism — initially within the Church of England — greatly expanded Nonconformity. The Test and Corporation Acts were repealed in 1828, the Universities Tests Act 1871 opened degrees, and by the later nineteenth century Nonconformists were a major force in the Liberal Party and in British civic life.
