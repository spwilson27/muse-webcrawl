Bacterial conjugation

Bacterial conjugation is the transfer of genetic material between bacterial cells by direct cell-to-cell contact or by a bridge-like connection between two cells. It is one of the three principal mechanisms of horizontal gene transfer in bacteria, alongside transformation and transduction.

Mechanism

In the classic system studied in Escherichia coli, a donor cell carrying a conjugative plasmid — the fertility factor or F factor — produces a hair-like appendage called a pilus. The pilus attaches to a recipient cell and draws the two cells together. One strand of the plasmid DNA is then nicked and transferred into the recipient through a mating bridge, where it is copied to restore double-stranded DNA. Both cells end up carrying the plasmid, converting the recipient into a potential donor.

Significance

Conjugation was discovered in 1946 by Joshua Lederberg and Edward Tatum, who showed that genetic markers could be exchanged between bacterial strains. Because plasmids frequently carry genes for antibiotic resistance, virulence factors or metabolic capabilities, conjugation is a major driver of the rapid spread of antibiotic resistance among pathogenic bacteria and a key process in bacterial evolution.

Related processes

Conjugation differs from transformation, in which bacteria take up free DNA from the environment, and from transduction, in which DNA is carried between cells by bacteriophages. Some elements that transfer by conjugation are integrated into the chromosome, producing Hfr (high frequency of recombination) strains that can transfer chromosomal genes.
