Philosophy of language

Philosophy of language is the branch of philosophy that investigates the nature of language, the relations between language, its users and the world. Its central questions include the nature of meaning, reference, truth, language acquisition and use, and the relationship between language and thought.

Theories of meaning

Gottlob Frege distinguished the sense of an expression from its reference, explaining how identity statements can be informative. Bertrand Russell's theory of descriptions analysed apparent references to non-existent objects. In the 20th century the "linguistic turn" made language central to analytic philosophy: Ludwig Wittgenstein moved from the picture theory of the Tractatus to the view that meaning is use within "language games".

Speech acts and pragmatics

J. L. Austin and John Searle developed speech act theory, analysing how utterances perform actions such as promising or ordering. H. P. Grice explained conversational implicature through a cooperative principle and maxims, founding modern pragmatics. Saul Kripke's Naming and Necessity (1972) argued that names are rigid designators referring via causal-historical chains.

Related fields

The discipline overlaps with linguistics, semantics, semiotics, cognitive science and philosophy of mind. Debates continue over compositionality, contextualism, relativism and the semantics of conditionals and vagueness.
