An antibiotic is a type of antimicrobial substance active against bacteria. It is the most important type of antibacterial agent for fighting bacterial infections, and antibiotic medications are widely used in the treatment and prevention of such infections. Antibiotics may either kill bacteria (bactericidal) or inhibit their growth (bacteriostatic). They are not effective against viruses such as those causing the common cold or influenza.

History

Although antibacterial remedies were used in ancient medicine, the modern antibiotic era began with Paul Ehrlich's arsphenamine (Salvarsan) in 1910 and Alexander Fleming's discovery of penicillin in 1928, developed into a drug in the early 1940s by Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and their team. The sulfonamide Prontosil (1930s), Selman Waksman's streptomycin (1943) and the subsequent "golden age" of discovery from soil microorganisms followed. Most classes in use today were discovered between the 1940s and 1970s.

Mechanisms of action

Antibiotics act on targets absent or structurally different in human cells: bacterial cell-wall synthesis (β-lactams, glycopeptides), protein synthesis at the ribosome (macrolides, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, oxazolidinones), DNA replication and transcription (fluoroquinolones, rifamycins), folate synthesis (sulfonamides, trimethoprim), and the cell membrane (polymyxins, daptomycin).

Resistance

Antimicrobial resistance — driven by overuse in medicine, agriculture and aquaculture — is a growing global threat. Resistant organisms include MRSA, vancomycin-resistant enterococci, and multidrug- and extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis and Gram-negative bacteria producing extended-spectrum β-lactamases and carbapenemases. The World Health Organization has called resistance one of the top global public health threats, prompting stewardship programmes and renewed interest in new agents, phage therapy and rapid diagnostics.
