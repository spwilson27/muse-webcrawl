Antihero

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The antihero: a protagonist without classic heroic virtues

An antihero (sometimes spelled anti-hero) or antiheroine is a main character in a narrative (in literature, film, TV, etc.) who may lack some conventional heroic qualities and attributes, such as idealism, courage, and morality. Although antiheroes may sometimes perform actions that most of the audience considers morally correct, their reasons for doing so may not align with the audience's morality.

An antihero typically exhibits one of the "dark triad" personality traits: narcissism, Machiavellianism, and psychopathy. Antiheroes are typically antiheroes because—in a typical structure—they are set up to fail, be victims of circumstance, and be surrounded by people who are worse than they are, making them seem relatively virtuous by comparison.

History

Early examples appear in classical literature — Homer's Thersites, the picaresque rogues of Spanish fiction, and Don Quixote have all been read as antiheroic. Lord Byron's brooding protagonists established the Byronic hero in the 19th century. In the 20th century the type became central: the hard-boiled detectives of noir fiction, Holden Caulfield in The Catcher in the Rye, and the amoral figures of existentialist literature prepared the way for the antiheroes of modern film and television.

Modern examples

Contemporary popular culture is rich in antiheroes: Clint Eastwood's "Man with No Name", Han Solo, Batman in his darker portrayals, and television protagonists such as Tony Soprano (The Sopranos), Walter White (Breaking Bad), and Don Draper (Mad Men), whose popularity in the 2000s and 2010s was widely noted as a cultural shift.
