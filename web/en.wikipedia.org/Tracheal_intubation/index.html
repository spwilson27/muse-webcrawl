Tracheal intubation

FieldEmergency medicine, anaesthesiology
PurposeSecure the airway

Tracheal intubation, usually simply referred to as intubation, is the placement of a flexible plastic tube into the trachea (windpipe) to maintain an open airway or to serve as a conduit through which to administer certain drugs. It is frequently performed in critically injured, ill, or anaesthetised patients to facilitate ventilation of the lungs and to prevent the possibility of asphyxiation or airway obstruction.

Technique

The most widely used route is orotracheal, in which an endotracheal tube is passed through the mouth and vocal apparatus into the trachea, usually under direct visualisation with a laryngoscope or with a video laryngoscope. Rapid sequence intubation uses a fast-acting sedative and a neuromuscular blocking agent to minimise the risk of aspiration. Correct placement is confirmed by capnography, auscultation, and observation of chest movement.

Indications

Indications include respiratory failure, a reduced level of consciousness with inability to protect the airway, major trauma, cardiac arrest, and the need for general anaesthesia with controlled ventilation.

Complications

Complications can include damage to the teeth or airway structures, oesophageal intubation, aspiration, hypoxia during the procedure, and, with prolonged intubation, tracheal stenosis.
