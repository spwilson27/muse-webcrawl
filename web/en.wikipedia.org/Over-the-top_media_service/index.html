Over-the-top media service

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An over-the-top (OTT) media service is a media service offered directly to viewers via the Internet. OTT bypasses cable, broadcast, and satellite television platforms — the traditional controllers or distributors of such content. The term is most commonly used for subscription-based video-on-demand (SVoD) services that offer access to film and television content, such as Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, Disney+, and Hulu, but it also covers audio streaming, messaging, and voice-calling services delivered over the public Internet.

Characteristics

OTT services are accessed through websites or dedicated applications on devices such as smartphones, tablets, smart TVs, streaming sticks, and game consoles. Because delivery uses the open Internet, providers do not need to own distribution infrastructure in each market; instead they rely on broadband connections supplied by third-party internet service providers and typically on content delivery networks to cache video close to viewers. Adaptive bitrate streaming protocols such as HLS and MPEG-DASH adjust quality to available bandwidth.

Business models

Common models include subscription video on demand (SVoD), advertising-supported video on demand (AVoD, including free services sometimes labelled FAST when organised as linear channels), and transactional video on demand (TVoD), in which viewers rent or buy individual titles. The growth of OTT video has been associated with "cord-cutting" — consumers cancelling traditional pay-television subscriptions — and with the production of original programming by streaming platforms themselves.

History

The term "over-the-top" was applied in the 2000s to services that ran on top of existing networks without the involvement of the network operator. Netflix's launch of streaming in 2007 and the spread of high-speed broadband and connected televisions in the 2010s drove mass adoption. Major media conglomerates subsequently launched their own services, fragmenting content libraries that had previously been licensed to aggregators.

Regulation and issues

OTT services raise questions of net neutrality, data consumption and bandwidth costs, content licensing across territories, and the extent to which broadcast-era regulation — such as local content quotas, taxation, and accessibility requirements — should apply to Internet-delivered media. Competition among services has produced the so-called "streaming wars", with high spending on original content and periodic consolidation.
