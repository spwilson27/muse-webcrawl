Hib conjugate vaccine

The Hib vaccine is a vaccine used to prevent infection by Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib), a bacterium that was once the leading cause of bacterial meningitis in young children, as well as a cause of epiglottitis, pneumonia, sepsis and septic arthritis.

Development and effectiveness

The first Hib vaccines, introduced in the 1980s, used the purified capsular polysaccharide polyribosylribitol phosphate (PRP) but were poorly immunogenic in infants, the group at greatest risk. Conjugate vaccines, in which PRP is chemically linked to a protein carrier such as tetanus toxoid or a meningococcal outer-membrane protein, solved the problem and were licensed from 1987 onward. They are more than 95% effective against invasive Hib disease; after their introduction, incidence in countries with routine immunisation fell by over 99%.

Use

The World Health Organization recommends that Hib vaccine be included in all routine infant immunisation programmes, commonly delivered in combination products such as the pentavalent (DTwP-HepB-Hib) or hexavalent vaccines. Typical schedules give doses at 2, 4 and 6 months with a booster in the second year of life, or a 3-dose primary series without booster in many low-income countries supported by Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance. Side effects are generally mild — local redness, swelling or low fever.
