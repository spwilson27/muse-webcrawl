Hardanger fiddle
Norwegian: hardingfele
Origin: Hardanger region, Norway

The Hardanger fiddle (Norwegian: hardingfele) is a traditional Norwegian stringed instrument, similar to the violin but with four or five additional understrings that resonate sympathetically, giving it a distinctive shimmering, echoing tone. It is the national instrument of Norway and is central to Norwegian folk dance music.

Construction

Hardanger fiddles are richly decorated with mother-of-pearl inlay, black pen-work designs called rosing, and a carved dragon or lion's head scroll. The oldest known example, made by Ole Jonsen Jaastad, dates to 1651. The sympathetic strings run beneath the fingerboard and are tuned to resonate with the melody strings, which are played in many different tunings (skordatura), some with evocative names such as "troll tuning".

Music and tradition

The instrument accompanies dances such as the springar, gangar, and halling, and its repertoire of slåtter was an important influence on Edvard Grieg. The legendary 19th-century virtuoso Ole Bull began on the instrument, and the fiddle was once viewed by the church as a sinful "devil's instrument". Today it is taught in folk-music schools and celebrated at the annual Landskappleik competition.
