Face perception

Face perception is an individual's understanding and interpretation of the face. Here perception implies the presence of consciousness and hence excludes automated facial recognition systems. Although facial recognition is found in other species, this article focuses on facial perception in humans.

The perception of facial features is an important part of social cognition. Information gathered from the face helps people understand another person's identity, what they are thinking and feeling, anticipate what they will do, recognize their emotions, connect with them, and communicate through them. From a young age, people are "hardwired" to recognize faces and facial expressions.

Development

Newborn infants show a preference for face-like stimuli within hours of birth, and the ability to discriminate faces develops rapidly through the first year of life. Infants preferentially attend to their mother's face and, later, to faces of their own ethnic group — a phenomenon thought to underlie the other-race effect seen in adults, in which people are generally better at recognizing faces of their own race than of other races.

Neural mechanisms

Face perception is supported by a distributed network of brain regions, most notably the fusiform face area (FFA) in the fusiform gyrus, the occipital face area (OFA) and the superior temporal sulcus (STS). Damage to these areas can cause prosopagnosia, the inability to recognize faces. The N170 event-related potential is a neural marker associated with the structural encoding of faces.

Configural and holistic processing

Faces appear to be processed holistically: people are sensitive to the configuration of features rather than to features in isolation. Evidence comes from the face inversion effect — faces are much harder to recognize upside down than other objects — and from the composite face effect, in which the top and bottom halves of different faces are difficult to identify when aligned.

Social perception

From faces, observers rapidly infer traits such as trustworthiness, dominance and competence, though such inferences may not be accurate. Facial expressions of emotion — happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust and surprise — are a major channel of nonverbal communication, and their interpretation is shaped by context and culture.
