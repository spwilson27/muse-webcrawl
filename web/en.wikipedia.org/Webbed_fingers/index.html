Webbed fingers

[Image: Webbed fingers]

Webbed fingers or webbed toes is the common name for syndactyly affecting the fingers or toes. In this condition, two or more digits are fused together by soft tissue, and occasionally by bone. It is the most common congenital malformation of the limbs, occurring in roughly 1 in every 2,000 to 3,000 live births.

Classification

Syndactyly may be simple, involving only soft tissue, or complex, involving fusion of bone as well. It may be complete, with webbing extending to the fingertips, or incomplete, extending only partway. When the condition is associated with other abnormalities it may form part of a syndrome, such as Apert syndrome or Poland syndrome.

Treatment

Webbed fingers are often corrected surgically in childhood, typically between six months and two years of age, especially when the fusion involves digits of unequal length, such as the thumb and index finger, since the tethering can distort growth. Surgery involves separating the digits, often with skin grafts, and reconstructing the web space. Mild incomplete webbing that does not impair function is sometimes left untreated.

In other animals

Webbing between the toes is normal in many animals, such as ducks, otters and frogs, where it aids swimming. In humans, webbed digits are generally considered an anomaly, although they are usually harmless apart from their appearance and minor functional effects.
