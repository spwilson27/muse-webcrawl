Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour

Camillo Paolo Filippo Giulio Benso, Count of Cavour, Isolabella and Leri (10 August 1810 – 6 June 1861), generally known simply as Cavour, was an Italian statesman and a leading figure in the movement toward Italian unification (the Risorgimento). He was one of the leaders of the Historical Right and prime minister of the Kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia — a position he maintained, except for a six-month resignation, throughout the Second Italian War of Independence and Giuseppe Garibaldi's campaigns to unite Italy. After the declaration of a united Kingdom of Italy, Cavour became its first prime minister, dying in office only three months later.

Early career and domestic policy

A younger son of a Piedmontese noble family, Cavour trained as an army engineer, resigned his commission, travelled in Britain and France, and built a reputation as a modernising agriculturalist on his estate at Leri. He founded the newspaper Il Risorgimento in 1847 and entered parliament after the 1848 constitution (the Statuto Albertino). As minister of agriculture, commerce and finance (1850–52) he negotiated free-trade treaties, reformed the finances, promoted railways and banking, and passed the Siccardi-era anticlerical measures separating church and state — his formula was "a free church in a free state".

Diplomacy and unification

Becoming prime minister in November 1852, Cavour sought to raise Piedmont's standing by joining Britain and France in the Crimean War (1855), earning a seat at the Congress of Paris (1856), where he internationalised the Italian question. In July 1858 he secretly met Napoleon III at Plombières and secured a French alliance against Austria, in exchange for Nice and Savoy. The war of 1859 (Magenta, Solferino) brought Lombardy to Piedmont; when Napoleon III made a separate peace at Villafranca, Cavour resigned in fury, but returned in January 1860 to engineer plebiscites annexing Tuscany, Emilia and the Romagna — ceding Nice and Savoy to France by the Treaty of Turin.

In 1860 Cavour covertly abetted Garibaldi's Expedition of the Thousand to Sicily and Naples while publicly disavowing it, then sent Piedmontese troops into the Papal States (Castelfidardo) to intercept Garibaldi before he could attack Rome and provoke French intervention. The Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed on 17 March 1861, with Turin as capital and Rome and Venetia still outstanding. Cavour died that June, reportedly of malarial fever aggravated by repeated bloodletting, at the age of fifty — weeks into office as the first prime minister of the united kingdom.

Legacy

Historians rank Cavour with Garibaldi, Mazzini and King Victor Emmanuel II as one of the four "fathers of the fatherland"; his contribution was the diplomatic and parliamentary machinery that made unification a European settlement rather than a merely revolutionary one. Streets, squares, a battleship and Italy's later aircraft carrier Cavour bear his name.
