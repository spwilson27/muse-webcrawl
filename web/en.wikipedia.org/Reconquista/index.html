The Reconquista (Spanish and Portuguese for "reconquest") was the series of campaigns by Christian states to recapture territory in the Iberian Peninsula from the Muslim (Moorish) polities that had ruled much of it since the Umayyad conquest of 711. Traditionally dated from the Battle of Covadonga (718 or 722) to the fall of the Nasrid Emirate of Granada on 2 January 1492, it spanned nearly eight centuries and shaped the emergence of Spain and Portugal.

Background

After Muslim forces overran the Visigothic Kingdom, small Christian communities survived in the northern mountains. The victory of the Asturian leader Pelagius (Pelayo) at Covadonga became the founding legend of the Asturian monarchy and, in later historiography, the starting point of the Reconquista.

Major phases

Christian expansion advanced in fits and starts amid shifting alliances that often crossed religious lines. Toledo fell to Alfonso VI of León and Castile in 1085, prompting intervention by the Almoravids and later the Almohads from North Africa. The decisive victory of the combined Christian armies at Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212 broke Almohad power. In the following decades Ferdinand III of Castile took Córdoba (1236) and Seville (1248), James I of Aragon conquered Mallorca and Valencia, and Portugal completed its southward expansion with the Algarve (1249). Only the Emirate of Granada survived, as a Castilian vassal until its final conquest by Ferdinand II and Isabella I in 1492.

Society and warfare

The frontier fostered distinctive institutions: military orders such as those of Santiago, Calatrava and Alcántara; charters (fueros) attracting settlers; and a culture of coexistence and conflict among Christians, Muslims and Jews. Mudéjars (Muslims under Christian rule) and Mozarabs played important economic and cultural roles, though tolerance eroded over time, culminating in forced conversions and expulsions.

Historiography

The very concept of a single eight-century "reconquest" is largely a construction of 19th-century nationalist historiography; medieval actors more often pursued local ambitions, tribute and dynastic claims, and modern historians debate the continuity and ideological coherence of the process. The term remains in general use for the overall movement of Christian expansion in Iberia.
