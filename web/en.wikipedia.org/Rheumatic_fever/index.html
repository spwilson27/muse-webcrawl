Rheumatic fever

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Acute rheumatic fever (ARF) is an autoimmune inflammatory disease that can develop as a complication of inadequately treated strep throat or scarlet fever caused by infection with group A Streptococcus. It typically develops two to four weeks after the throat infection and can involve the heart, joints, skin, and brain. Damage to the heart valves, known as rheumatic heart disease (RHD), is the most serious long-term consequence.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually appear 2–4 weeks after a streptococcal throat infection and include fever, painful and swollen joints (migratory polyarthritis), involuntary muscle movements (Sydenham's chorea), a painless rash known as erythema marginatum, and subcutaneous nodules. Inflammation of the heart (carditis) occurs in roughly half of cases and can present with a new heart murmur, chest pain, or heart failure.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on the revised Jones criteria, which combine major manifestations (carditis, arthritis, chorea, erythema marginatum, subcutaneous nodules) and minor manifestations (fever, arthralgia, elevated inflammatory markers, prolonged PR interval) together with evidence of a preceding group A streptococcal infection, such as a positive throat culture or rising antistreptolysin O titre.

Treatment and prevention

Treatment includes antibiotics such as penicillin to eradicate remaining streptococci, anti-inflammatory drugs (aspirin or corticosteroids) for arthritis and carditis, and long-term antibiotic prophylaxis to prevent recurrences, which can worsen valve damage. Prompt antibiotic treatment of strep throat greatly reduces the risk of a first attack. Rheumatic heart disease remains a major cause of cardiovascular death in low- and middle-income countries, affecting tens of millions of people worldwide.
