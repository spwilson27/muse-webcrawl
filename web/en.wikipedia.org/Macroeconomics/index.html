Macroeconomics is a branch of economics that deals with the performance, structure, behaviour, and decision-making of an economy as a whole — regional, national, or global. Along with microeconomics, it is one of the two most general fields in economics. Macroeconomists study topics such as output and Gross Domestic Product (GDP), unemployment, inflation and prices, economic growth, and the effects of monetary and fiscal policy.

History

The field took shape in the 1930s with John Maynard Keynes's General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), written in response to the Great Depression, which argued that aggregate demand drives output and employment and that economies can settle below full employment. Later developments included the Phillips curve, Milton Friedman's monetarism and critique of discretionary policy, the rational expectations revolution associated with Robert Lucas, and new classical and new Keynesian schools, whose partial synthesis dominates mainstream teaching and central-bank practice.

Main topics

Macroeconomics examines long-run economic growth and its determinants (capital accumulation, labour, technology); short-run fluctuations known as the business cycle; unemployment, distinguishing frictional, structural and cyclical types; inflation and its control; and the balance of payments and exchange rates in open economies. Standard models include the IS–LM model, aggregate demand–aggregate supply, the Solow growth model, and modern dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models.

Policy

Governments and central banks use fiscal policy (spending and taxation) and monetary policy (interest rates and money supply) to stabilise output and prices — objectives often embodied in central-bank mandates such as the US Federal Reserve's dual mandate of maximum employment and stable prices.
