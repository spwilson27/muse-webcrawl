Optics

Dispersion of light through a prism, a classic demonstration in optics.

Optics is the branch of physics that studies the behaviour and properties of light, including its interactions with matter and the construction of instruments that use or detect it. Optics usually describes the behaviour of visible, ultraviolet, and infrared light, and because light is an electromagnetic wave, other forms of electromagnetic radiation such as X-rays and radio waves exhibit similar properties.

History

The study of optics began in the ancient world with lenses and mirrors in Egypt and Mesopotamia, and was developed by Greek philosophers. The medieval scholar Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen), whose Book of Optics (c. 1021) established the modern intromission theory of vision, is often called the father of optics. In the seventeenth century Johannes Kepler explained the retinal image, Willebrord Snellius discovered the law of refraction, and Isaac Newton demonstrated that white light is composed of colours. The wave theory of Christiaan Huygens and the later work of Young, Fresnel, and Maxwell culminated in the understanding of light as an electromagnetic wave, while the photoelectric effect and quantum theory revealed its particle character as the photon.

Branches

Geometrical optics treats light as rays that travel in straight lines and bend at interfaces according to reflection and refraction. Physical optics addresses wave phenomena such as interference, diffraction, and polarisation. Quantum optics studies the interaction of photons with atoms and underlies technologies such as the laser.

Applications

Optical science underpins lenses, mirrors, telescopes, microscopes, cameras, fibre-optic communications, lasers, and many medical and industrial instruments.
