Noir fiction is a genre of crime fiction characterised by cynicism, moral ambiguity, and a bleak, pessimistic view of human nature. Its protagonists are often criminals, victims, or compromised anti-heroes trapped in situations that spiral beyond their control. The term comes from the French roman noir ("black novel"), also the name of Gallimard's Série Noire imprint, which published translations of American crime writers from 1945.

Characteristics

Noir is distinguished from the classic whodunit by its focus on the criminal's perspective and psychological disintegration rather than puzzle-solving. Settings are typically corrupt urban environments; plots turn on greed, lust, and betrayal; endings are rarely happy. Style tends toward terse, unsentimental prose, though writers such as Raymond Chandler added a lyrical, wisecracking voice in works like The Big Sleep (1939). James M. Cain's The Postman Always Rings Twice (1934) and Double Indemnity (1936) exemplify the genre's doom-laden plots.

History and influence

Noir grew out of the hardboiled school published in pulp magazines such as Black Mask during the Prohibition and Depression eras. Many novels were adapted into the films now called film noir, whose visual style borrowed the genre's name in return. Later writers — Jim Thompson, Patricia Highsmith, Elmore Leonard — and the contemporary "neo-noir" movement extended noir into suburban, Southern, and international settings. Scholars debate whether noir is a genre, a tone, or a period style, but its influence on crime writing and cinema is undisputed.
