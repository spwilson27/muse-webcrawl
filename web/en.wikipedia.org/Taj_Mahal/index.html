Taj Mahal

The Taj Mahal (/ˌtɑːdʒ məˈhɑːl/; Hindi: ताज महल, lit. 'Crown of the Palace') is an ivory-white marble mausoleum on the right bank of the Yamuna River in Agra, Uttar Pradesh, India. It was commissioned in 1632 by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan (reigned 1628–1658) to house the tomb of his favourite wife, Mumtaz Mahal, who had died in 1631 giving birth to their fourteenth child. The tomb is the centrepiece of a 17-hectare (42-acre) complex that also includes a mosque, a guest house, and formal gardens bounded on three sides by a crenellated wall.

History

Construction began in 1632, with the main mausoleum largely complete by 1643 and the entire complex by about 1653, employing some 20,000 artisans and craftsmen under a board of architects led, according to tradition, by Ustad Ahmad Lahauri. White marble was quarried at Makrana and inlaid with 28 types of precious and semi-precious stones using the pietra dura technique. Shah Jahan was deposed by his son Aurangzeb in 1658 and confined to Agra Fort; on his death in 1666 he was buried in the Taj beside Mumtaz Mahal.

Architecture

The tomb stands on a square plinth and consists of a central chamber crowned by a large onion dome approximately 35 metres high, flanked by four smaller domed chambers (chhatris) and four slender minarets about 40 metres tall at the corners. The facade is perfectly symmetrical along both axes — an arrangement broken only by Shah Jahan's later cenotaph. Inside, an octagonal marble screen encloses the cenotaphs of Mumtaz Mahal and Shah Jahan; the actual graves lie in a plainer chamber below. Calligraphy by Amanat Khan, including verses from the Quran, adorns the great gateway and tomb facades, crafted so that the lettering appears uniform in size despite its height.

Garden and setting

The complex is set within a charbagh, a quadrilateral garden divided by waterways into four parts in the Timurid-Persian tradition, symbolising the four flowing rivers of the Islamic paradise. The mausoleum's position at the northern end of the garden, rather than its centre, makes the Yamuna itself part of the design.

Tourism and conservation

The Taj Mahal attracts several million visitors annually and was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983, described as "the jewel of Muslim art in India". Air pollution from nearby industry and traffic has yellowed the marble, prompting periodic mud-pack cleaning treatments and the creation of the Taj Trapezium Zone restricting emissions. In 2007 it was named among the New7Wonders of the World.
