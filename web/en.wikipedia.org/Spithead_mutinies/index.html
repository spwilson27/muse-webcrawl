Spithead and Nore mutinies

The Spithead and Nore mutinies were two major mutinies by sailors of the British Royal Navy in 1797. They were not violent insurrections but, in the modern term, essentially strikes, undertaken to obtain better pay and working conditions; they nonetheless alarmed the government because Britain was at war with Revolutionary France. The mutinies are named for the anchorages where they began: Spithead, near Portsmouth in Hampshire, and the Nore, a sandbank anchorage in the Thames Estuary.

Spithead

The Channel Fleet at Spithead mutinied on 16 April 1797. Sailors' pay had not risen since 1653 and was eroded by inflation; demands included increased pay, better provisions (a full 16 ounces to the pound), care for the wounded, and removal of unpopular officers. The mutineers elected delegates — most prominently Valentine Joyce — and maintained discipline and loyalty to the war effort. After negotiation involving Admiral Lord Howe, the Admiralty conceded the demands and a royal pardon was issued; the fleet returned to duty in mid-May.

The Nore

The mutiny at the Nore, beginning 12 May under "President" Richard Parker, made broader demands, including more equitable prize money, leave, and changes touching arrears and pressed men. Isolated and blockaded by the authorities, it collapsed in June. Parker and about two dozen others were hanged, and others were flogged, imprisoned or transported. The events fed government fears of revolutionary contagion and led to the Incitement to Mutiny Act 1797; the pay concessions won at Spithead were nonetheless retained.
