Charles Curtis (January 25, 1860 – February 8, 1936) was an American attorney and Republican politician from Kansas who served as the 31st vice president of the United States from 1929 to 1933 under President Herbert Hoover. A member of the Kaw Nation through his mother, Curtis was the first person with acknowledged Native American ancestry to reach the second-highest office in the United States, and remains the highest-ranking enrolled tribal member ever to serve in the federal government.

Early life

Orphaned of his mother at age three, Curtis was raised partly by his maternal grandparents on the Kaw reservation in Kansas, where he spoke Kansa and French before English, and partly by his paternal grandparents in Topeka. He rode as a jockey in his youth, studied law, and was admitted to the bar in 1881, later serving as prosecuting attorney of Shawnee County.

Senate career

Curtis served in the U.S. House of Representatives from 1893 to 1907 and in the Senate from 1907 to 1913 and again from 1915 to 1929, rising to Senate Majority Leader. He authored the Curtis Act of 1898, which extended allotment policy to the Five Civilized Tribes in Indian Territory and dissolved their tribal governments — legislation criticized for accelerating the loss of Native land, despite Curtis's own heritage. He was a leading candidate for the Republican presidential nomination in 1928 before accepting the vice-presidential spot on Hoover's ticket.

Vice presidency and later life

As vice president, Curtis presided over the Senate during the onset of the Great Depression. The Hoover–Curtis ticket lost the 1932 election to Franklin D. Roosevelt and John Nance Garner. Curtis then practiced law in Washington, D.C., where he died in 1936.
