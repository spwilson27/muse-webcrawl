Gas to liquids

Image placeholder: a commercial GTL plant

Gas to liquids (GTL) is a refinery process to convert natural gas or other gaseous hydrocarbons into longer-chain hydrocarbons such as gasoline or diesel fuel. Methane-rich gases are converted into liquid synthetic fuels, either via direct conversion or via syngas as an intermediate, for example using the Fischer–Tropsch or Mobil processes.

Process

In the most common route, natural gas is first reformed with steam and/or oxygen to produce synthesis gas (syngas), a mixture of hydrogen and carbon monoxide. The syngas is then fed to Fischer–Tropsch reactors, where catalysts (usually cobalt- or iron-based) assemble the gas into long-chain hydrocarbons resembling crude wax, which is hydrocracked and refined into products such as ultra-low-sulfur diesel, kerosene, naphtha, and lubricants.

Plants and economics

Large-scale GTL plants include Shell's Bintulu plant in Malaysia (the first commercial GTL plant, opened 1993) and Pearl GTL in Qatar, the world's largest GTL facility, as well as Sasol's Oryx GTL in Qatar and plants in Nigeria and Uzbekistan. GTL allows monetisation of remote or "stranded" gas reserves and of gas that would otherwise be flared, producing premium, clean-burning fuels that can use existing distribution infrastructure. However, the plants are extremely capital-intensive, the process is energy-hungry, and its carbon footprint is higher than that of conventional petroleum refining — factors that have limited GTL's deployment despite decades of technical success.
