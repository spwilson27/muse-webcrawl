Propaganda in China refers to the use of propaganda by the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) and the government of the People's Republic of China to shape public opinion and behaviour. The Chinese word xuānchuán (宣傳), officially rendered "publicity" in the name of the party's Publicity Department, carries a neutral-to-positive connotation of disseminating information and ideology, in contrast to the pejorative sense "propaganda" often has in English.

System and institutions

The CCP's Central Propaganda Department (Publicity Department) supervises ideology, media, publishing, film, and education. Major state media — the Xinhua News Agency, China Central Television, the People's Daily, and China Radio International — operate under its guidance and function explicitly as the "throat and tongue" (hóushé) of the party. The system couples positive promotion of official narratives with censorship: the Great Firewall blocks foreign platforms and content, domestic platforms self-censor under regulatory pressure, and paid internet commentators (the so-called "50 Cent Party") steer online discussion. Since the 1990s, "patriotic education" in schools and media has anchored legitimacy in national rejuvenation and the "century of humiliation" narrative rather than in Marxist economics alone.

History

The CCP developed sophisticated propaganda techniques during the Yan'an period (1935–1947), when Mao Zedong's rectification movement and the mass line forged a disciplined communication apparatus using newspapers, theatre troupes, and study sessions. After 1949, propaganda saturated daily life through campaigns, posters, loudspeaker networks, and the cult of Mao, reaching its extreme during the Cultural Revolution, when the Little Red Book of Mao's quotations was printed in over a billion copies and model operas dominated culture. The reform era after 1978 de-emphasised class struggle, commercialised much of the media, and shifted emphasis to economic achievement and nationalism — while retaining party control of editorial lines, especially after the 1989 Tiananmen crackdown prompted the patriotic education campaign.

External propaganda

Since the 2000s China has invested heavily in external communication — CGTN, Xinhua's global expansion, China Daily supplements in foreign newspapers, Confucius Institutes, and social-media campaigns — to "tell China's story well" and build "discourse power." These efforts, together with United Front work, have drawn scrutiny from foreign governments, some of which have required Chinese state outlets to register as foreign agents. Scholars debate the effectiveness of Chinese propaganda at home and abroad, noting both sophisticated agenda-setting and substantial public cynicism and circumvention.
