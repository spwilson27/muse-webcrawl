Library science

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Library science (often termed library studies or library and information science, LIS) is an interdisciplinary field that applies the practices, perspectives, and tools of management, information technology, education, and other areas to the tasks of collecting, organizing, preserving, and providing access to information resources in libraries. The field concerns how information is created, evaluated, selected, classified, stored, retrieved, and disseminated, as well as how people seek and use information.

History

Systematic thinking about libraries dates to early manuals such as Gabriel Naudé's Advis pour dresser une bibliothèque (1627). The first formal school for librarians was founded by Melvil Dewey at Columbia University in 1887; Dewey also devised the Dewey Decimal Classification (1876). In the 20th century, figures such as S. R. Ranganathan in India — whose five laws of library science (1931) remain widely quoted — and the documentation movement associated with Paul Otlet broadened the field's intellectual foundations. From the mid-20th century, computing transformed cataloguing (MARC records), circulation systems, and search, and many library schools renamed themselves schools of "information" or joined iSchools.

Scope and subfields

  • Cataloguing and classification: bibliographic description and subject organization using standards such as RDA, MARC, Dewey Decimal and Library of Congress classifications, and subject headings.
  • Collection development: selecting and acquiring books, journals, media, and electronic resources.
  • Reference and user services: helping users find and evaluate information; information literacy instruction.
  • Preservation and archives: conservation of physical materials, digitization, and digital preservation.
  • Information retrieval and organization: indexing, metadata, ontologies, and search system design.
  • Library management and policy: administration, intellectual freedom, privacy, and copyright.

Education and profession

In North America, professional librarians generally hold a master's degree from a programme accredited by the American Library Association (ALA). Professional associations such as the ALA, IFLA (International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions), and CILIP in the UK set ethical codes and standards. Librarians work in public, academic, school, and special libraries (law, medical, corporate), as well as in archives, museums, publishing, and information technology roles.
