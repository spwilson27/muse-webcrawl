Schrödinger's cat is a thought experiment devised by the Austrian physicist Erwin Schrödinger in 1935, during his correspondence with Albert Einstein, to illustrate what he saw as the absurdity of applying the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics to everyday objects. It remains a famous entry point into the measurement problem in quantum physics.

The thought experiment

A cat is sealed in a steel chamber with a device containing a tiny amount of a radioactive substance, so small that within one hour perhaps one atom decays, but also, with equal probability, perhaps none. If a decay occurs, a Geiger counter triggers a relay that releases a hammer, smashing a flask of hydrocyanic acid and killing the cat. Under a straightforward application of the Copenhagen interpretation, the quantum system—atom plus apparatus plus cat—exists in a superposition of states until the box is opened, and the cat is described as simultaneously alive and dead. Only when an observer looks inside does the wave function "collapse" into one outcome. Schrödinger intended this conclusion as a reductio ad absurdum of the interpretation, not as a claim about actual cats.

Interpretations

Different interpretations of quantum mechanics answer the puzzle differently. In the Copenhagen view, measurement by the apparatus (or observer) collapses the superposition. In the many-worlds interpretation, both outcomes occur in branching universes, and no collapse happens. Objective-collapse theories propose that superpositions of sufficiently large systems collapse spontaneously. The theory of quantum decoherence explains why macroscopic superpositions are not observed in practice, because interaction with the environment rapidly destroys interference, though decoherence alone does not select a single outcome.

In culture

The cat has become a cultural icon of quantum weirdness, appearing in literature, television, and humour—usually in the simplified form "the cat is both alive and dead until you look," which omits Schrödinger's critical intent. Physicists also speak of "Schrödinger's cat states" for experimentally prepared superpositions of mesoscopic systems.
