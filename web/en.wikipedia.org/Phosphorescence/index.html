Phosphorescence

Phosphorescence is a type of photoluminescence related to fluorescence. When exposed to light (radiation) of a shorter wavelength, a phosphorescent substance will glow, absorbing the light and reemitting it at a longer wavelength. Unlike fluorescence, a phosphorescent material does not immediately reemit the radiation it absorbs. Instead, a phosphorescent material absorbs some of the radiation energy and reemits it for a much longer time after the radiation source is removed.

In simple terms, phosphorescence is a process in which energy absorbed by a substance is released relatively slowly in the form of light. This is in some cases the mechanism used for glow-in-the-dark materials which are "charged" by exposure to light. These materials exist in two forms—fluorescent and phosphorescent—with phosphorescence being longer lasting.

Mechanism

In most cases, photoluminescence occurs when a substance is exposed to light of a certain wavelength, and electrons are excited to a higher energy state. In fluorescence, the electrons return quickly (in about 10−8 seconds) to the ground state, emitting a photon. In phosphorescence, the excited electrons undergo intersystem crossing to a triplet state, whose return to the ground state is spin-forbidden and therefore much slower, taking anywhere from milliseconds to hours.

Materials

Common pigments used in phosphorescent materials include zinc sulfide and strontium aluminate. Use of zinc sulfide for safety related products dates back to the 1930s. Strontium aluminate based materials have become common in the 21st century, because they are brighter and last longer than zinc sulfide.

The term phosphorescence was coined from the element phosphorus, which glows in the dark through chemiluminescence rather than true phosphorescence.
