State Shinto

State Shinto (国家神道, Kokka Shintō) was Imperial Japan's ideological use of the Japanese folk religion of Shinto between the Meiji Restoration of 1868 and the end of World War II in 1945. The Imperial Japanese government required reverence toward the kami, the Emperor, and the Japanese state at Shinto shrines, while treating this compulsory veneration as a patriotic civic duty rather than a "religion" — a legal fiction that allowed the state to mandate participation while formally guaranteeing religious freedom under the Meiji Constitution.

Origins

After the Meiji Restoration, the new government sought to unify the country around the Emperor. In 1868 it ordered the separation of Shinto from Buddhism (shinbutsu bunri), and in the following decades shrines were ranked, registered, and placed under state administration. The 1889 constitution stated that Japanese subjects could enjoy freedom of religious belief only "within limits not prejudicial to peace and order, and not antagonistic to their duties as subjects," and official doctrine held that shrine rites were patriotic ceremonies standing above religion.

Institutions

Key institutions of State Shinto included the Grand Shrine of Ise, dedicated to the imperial ancestress Amaterasu; Yasukuni Shrine in Tokyo, dedicated to the spirits of Japan's war dead; and thousands of shrines throughout Japan and its colonies, where schoolchildren and officials were expected to participate in rituals. Priests at major shrines became government appointees, and offerings at Ise and Yasukuni were made in the Emperor's name.

Abolition

Following Japan's surrender in 1945, the Allied occupation issued the Shinto Directive (15 December 1945), which abolished government sponsorship, support, perpetuation, control, and dissemination of Shinto. The 1947 Constitution of Japan established complete separation of religion and state, and shrines became independent religious organizations under the Association of Shinto Shrines.
