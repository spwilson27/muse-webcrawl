The Nasrid dynasty (Arabic: بنو نصر, Banū Naṣr) was the last Muslim dynasty in the Iberian Peninsula, ruling the Emirate of Granada from 1232 to 1492. Founded by Muhammad I (Ibn al-Ahmar), the dynasty ruled a territory that at its greatest extent included Granada, Málaga, and Almería, surviving as the last remnant of al-Andalus long after the other Muslim states of Iberia had fallen to the Christian Reconquista.

History

Muhammad I established himself in Granada in 1238 and, recognising Castilian strength, became a vassal of Ferdinand III of Castile, even assisting him in the conquest of Seville. The Nasrids maintained their state for two and a half centuries through diplomacy, tribute payments (parias), and shifting alliances with Castile, Aragon, and the Marinids of Morocco. Internal succession disputes and civil wars were recurrent features of the dynasty's later history.

Culture

Under the Nasrids, Granada became a great centre of Islamic art, learning, and commerce. Their enduring monument is the Alhambra, the palace-fortress above Granada, enlarged by Yusuf I and Muhammad V with masterpieces such as the Court of the Lions. The dynasty's motto, "There is no victor but God" (wa lā gāliba illā Allāh), is inscribed throughout the palace.

Fall

In 1482 the Catholic Monarchs Ferdinand and Isabella launched the final Granada War. Divided by civil war between Abu l-Hasan, his son Muhammad XII (known to the Spanish as Boabdil), and al-Zagal, the emirate fell city by city. On 2 January 1492 Boabdil surrendered Granada, ending Muslim rule in Iberia.
