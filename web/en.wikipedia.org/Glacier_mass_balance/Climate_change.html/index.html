Glacier mass balance and climate change

Glacier mass balance is the difference between accumulation (snowfall and other inputs of ice) and ablation (melting, sublimation and calving) over a balance year. Because glaciers gain mass mainly from snowfall and lose it mainly through surface melt, their mass balance is a sensitive, direct and largely undelayed response to climate change.

Sustained negative mass balance causes a glacier to thin and retreat; sustained positive balance causes thickening and advance. Since the mid-20th century, and especially since the 1980s, glaciers worldwide have lost mass at accelerating rates. The World Glacier Monitoring Service's reference glaciers show cumulative losses of tens of metres of water equivalent. Glacier melt contributes significantly to global sea-level rise and alters seasonal river flow relied on by hundreds of millions of people for drinking water, agriculture and hydropower.

Measurement

Mass balance is measured by the glaciological method (stakes and snow pits), geodetic methods comparing elevation models, and satellite gravimetry such as GRACE. Results are usually expressed in metres of water equivalent (m w.e.).
