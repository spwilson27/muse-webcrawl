A howitzer is a type of artillery weapon characterised by a relatively short barrel, the use of variable propellant charges, and a high trajectory that allows shells to be fired over obstacles onto targets out of direct line of sight. In modern usage, the distinction between guns and howitzers has blurred, and most contemporary pieces are described as gun-howitzers.

History

The word derives from the Czech houfnice, a fifteenth-century Hussite cannon. Early howitzers were short-barrelled weapons firing explosive shells or incendiaries at fortifications. Through the nineteenth century they became standard siege and field weapons, and in the First World War howitzers of unprecedented size — such as Germany's 420 mm "Big Bertha" — were used against fortresses, while medium howitzers fired the bulk of the war's artillery ammunition in trench warfare.

Characteristics

Howitzers fire at elevations above 45 degrees when required, lobbing shells in a steep arc so they descend almost vertically. Separate loading of shell and variable bagged charges lets crews select range and trajectory. Modern 155 mm howitzers, such as the towed M777 and self-propelled PzH 2000, M109, and CAESAR, fire high-explosive, smoke, illumination, and precision-guided rounds to ranges beyond 30 km, and over 50 km with rocket-assisted or guided projectiles.

Use

Howitzers provide the main indirect-fire support for ground forces, suppressing enemy artillery, fortifications, and troop concentrations. Self-propelled howitzers keep pace with armoured formations, while light towed pieces can be airlifted by helicopter for rapid deployment.
