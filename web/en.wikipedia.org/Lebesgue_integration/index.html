In mathematics, the Lebesgue integral, named after Henri Lebesgue, is a method of integration that extends the Riemann integral to a much larger class of functions and domains. Where the Riemann integral partitions the domain of a function into small intervals, the Lebesgue integral partitions its range, measuring the size of the sets on which the function takes given values. Lebesgue summarized the contrast with the image of counting coins: Riemann counts them one by one in the order found; Lebesgue sorts them by denomination first.

Construction

The theory begins with Lebesgue measure, which assigns lengths, areas, and volumes to a wide class of sets (measurable sets). The integral is first defined for indicator functions of measurable sets, then for simple functions taking finitely many values as weighted sums of measures, then for non-negative measurable functions as the supremum of integrals of simple functions below them, and finally for general measurable functions by decomposing into positive and negative parts.

Advantages

The Lebesgue integral has decisive technical advantages. Powerful limit theorems — the monotone convergence theorem, Fatou's lemma, and the dominated convergence theorem — justify interchanging limits and integrals under mild conditions. The space Lp of Lebesgue-integrable functions is complete, unlike the Riemann-integrable functions, making the theory central to functional analysis, Fourier analysis, and probability theory, where a probability space is precisely a measure space of total measure one and expectation is a Lebesgue integral.

History

Lebesgue presented the theory in his 1902 doctoral thesis Intégrale, longueur, aire and his 1904 lectures. The construction was extended by Johann Radon and others to general measure spaces, and related integrals (Daniell, Haar) followed.
