Growth mindset

A growth mindset is an implicit theory — a belief about the nature of human attributes — in which intelligence and other qualities are seen as malleable and capable of being developed through effort, good strategies, and help from others. It contrasts with a fixed mindset, in which such qualities are seen as stable traits. The concepts were developed by Stanford psychologist Carol Dweck and her colleagues over several decades of research on achievement and motivation, and popularized for a general audience in Dweck's 2006 book Mindset: The New Psychology of Success.

Research findings

According to Dweck's research, students who believe intelligence can grow tend to embrace challenges, persist through setbacks, and treat effort as a path to mastery, whereas those with a fixed mindset more often avoid challenges, give up easily, and interpret failure as evidence of low ability. A well-known set of experiments by Claudia Mueller and Dweck (1998) found that children praised for effort after success later chose harder tasks and enjoyed the work more than children praised for intelligence, who became more fragile after failure.

Brief "mindset interventions" — lessons teaching students that the brain forms new connections when they struggle with challenging material — have been tested at scale. A nationally representative U.S. study published in Nature in 2019 found small but reliable improvements in grades among lower-achieving ninth-graders and increased enrolment in advanced mathematics. Meta-analyses, however, find that average effects of mindset interventions on achievement are small, and critics caution against inflated popular claims, noting effects depend heavily on school context and implementation.

Applications

The idea has been widely adopted in education, sports coaching, parenting advice, and corporate training, sometimes in simplified forms Dweck herself has warned against, such as praising effort regardless of strategy or outcomes — a distortion she calls "false growth mindset".
