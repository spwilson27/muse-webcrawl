Condottiero

A condottiero (plural condottieri) was the leader of a mercenary company in Renaissance Italy. From the condotta, the contract under which they were hired, these captains and their professional troops were employed by the Italian city-states and princedoms between the thirteenth and sixteenth centuries, when Italian polities were rich but their citizens increasingly unwilling to serve as soldiers.

Role and reputation

Famous condottieri include John Hawkwood, the English captain of the White Company in fourteenth-century Florence; Francesco Sforza, who made himself Duke of Milan in 1450; and Bartolomeo Colleoni, captain-general of Venice. Cities honoured their captains with famous equestrian monuments — Donatello's Gattamelata in Padua and Verrocchio's Colleoni in Venice.

Warfare and decline

Condottieri warfare was often manoeuvre-based and comparatively sparing of life, a style famously criticised by Machiavelli in The Prince and his Art of War, who blamed mercenaries for Italy's vulnerability. The system collapsed after the French invasion of 1494, when the national armies of France and Spain, with heavier artillery and disciplined infantry, outclassed the Italian companies in the Italian Wars.
