Meta-analysis

A meta-analysis is a statistical analysis that combines the results of multiple scientific studies addressing the same question. By pooling data it can estimate an effect size with greater precision and statistical power than any single study, and can explore differences between studies. Meta-analyses are typically performed as part of a systematic review and sit near the top of the hierarchy of evidence in medicine and the social sciences.

Methods

The analyst defines eligibility criteria, systematically searches the literature, extracts effect sizes, and combines them using fixed-effect or random-effects models, weighting studies (commonly by inverse variance). Heterogeneity between studies is quantified with statistics such as I². Results are often displayed in a forest plot. Publication bias — the tendency for positive results to be published preferentially — is assessed with tools such as funnel plots, and reporting standards such as PRISMA guide the process.

History

Precursors date to Karl Pearson's pooling of typhoid inoculation studies in 1904; the term "meta-analysis" was coined by Gene Glass in 1976 in the context of psychotherapy research. The approach became central to evidence-based medicine from the 1990s with the growth of the Cochrane Collaboration.

Criticisms

Meta-analyses can be undermined by poor-quality or biased primary studies ("garbage in, garbage out"), by mixing dissimilar studies, and by selective reporting; a meta-analysis of many small biased studies can produce a spuriously precise wrong answer.
