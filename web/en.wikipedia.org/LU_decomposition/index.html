LU decomposition

LU decomposition factors a matrix into a lower triangular L and an upper triangular U.

In numerical analysis and linear algebra, LU decomposition (also called LU factorization) factors a matrix as the product of a lower triangular matrix and an upper triangular matrix: A = LU. It can be viewed as the matrix form of Gaussian elimination and is the standard method used by computers to solve systems of linear equations, invert matrices, and compute determinants. The factorization was developed in the context of work by Tadeusz Banachiewicz (1938) and others.

Definition

For a square matrix A, an LU decomposition is A = LU, where L is lower triangular with ones on its diagonal (unitriangular) and U is upper triangular. Not every matrix has such a decomposition; a sufficient condition is that all leading principal minors are nonzero. Because row exchanges are often required, in practice one uses partial pivoting: PA = LU, where P is a permutation matrix. Every nonsingular matrix admits such a decomposition. Symmetric positive-definite matrices admit the related Cholesky decomposition.

Applications

Once A = LU is computed (at a cost of about 2n3/3 floating-point operations), solving Ax = b reduces to two cheap triangular solves: forward substitution for Ly = Pb, then back substitution for Ux = y. This makes LU ideal when many systems with the same matrix but different right-hand sides must be solved. The inverse of A follows by solving against unit vectors, and det(A) equals (up to the sign of the permutation) the product of the diagonal of U. Variants such as the Doolittle and Crout algorithms differ in which factor carries the unit diagonal, and rank-revealing and block versions are used in libraries such as LAPACK.
