Comparison of regular-expression engines

A regular expression engine is software that interprets regular expressions and searches text for matches. Engines differ in the syntax they support, in their feature sets — such as capturing groups, backreferences, lookahead, and Unicode support — and in their matching algorithms. This article compares prominent engines used in programming languages, editors, and command-line tools.

Implementation approaches

Two broad families of algorithms dominate. Backtracking engines, exemplified by PCRE and the regex libraries of Perl, Python, Java, and .NET, support rich features such as backreferences and look-around assertions, but can suffer exponential runtimes on pathological patterns (catastrophic backtracking). Finite automaton engines — typically based on Thompson NFA simulation or DFA construction, as in RE2, Rust's regex crate, and the engines in Go — guarantee linear-time matching but traditionally lack backreferences and look-around.

Feature differences

Key points of variation include support for named capture groups, possessive quantifiers, atomic groups, conditionals, recursion, character class set operations, Unicode properties and grapheme clusters, and possessive behaviour around newlines. POSIX defines two simpler syntaxes — Basic Regular Expressions (BRE) and Extended Regular Expressions (ERE) — implemented by tools such as grep, sed, and awk, which avoid most extensions. Because "regular expression" dialects are not standardised, expressions often require adjustment when moved between engines.
