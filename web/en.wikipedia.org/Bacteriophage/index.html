Bacteriophage

A bacteriophage, also known informally as a phage, is a virus that infects and replicates within bacteria and archaea. Phages are among the most numerous biological entities on Earth, with an estimated 1031 particles in the biosphere.

Structure and life cycles

A typical phage consists of a protein capsid enclosing a DNA or RNA genome; many have a tail that attaches to the host cell and injects the genome. In the lytic cycle the host is destroyed as new virions are released, while in the lysogenic cycle the phage genome integrates into the host chromosome as a prophage and replicates with it.

Discovery

Phages were described by Frederick Twort in 1915 and Félix d'Hérelle in 1917, who coined the name from "bacteria" and the Greek phagein, "to devour". Phage research was foundational to molecular biology, including the Hershey–Chase experiment demonstrating that DNA is the genetic material.

Applications

Phages are used in phage therapy against antibiotic-resistant infections, in molecular cloning, and as models in genetics. See also Virus and Phage therapy.
