Walter Elias Disney (December 5, 1901 – December 15, 1966) was an American animator, film producer, voice actor, and entrepreneur. A pioneer of the American animation industry, he introduced several developments in the production of cartoons. As a film producer, he holds the record for most Academy Awards earned and nominations by an individual. He won 22 Oscars from 59 nominations, including two Golden Globe Special Achievement Awards and an Emmy Award.

Disney was born in Chicago in 1901 and developed an early interest in drawing. After serving as a Red Cross ambulance driver in France after World War I, he moved to Kansas City and then Hollywood. With his brother Roy O. Disney, he co-founded the Disney Brothers Studio (later The Walt Disney Company) in 1923. With Ub Iwerks, he developed the character Mickey Mouse in 1928, his first highly popular success; he also provided the voice for his creation in the early years. Steamboat Willie (1928) was one of the first cartoons with synchronized sound.

Feature films and expansion

Disney's studio produced the first full-length animated feature, Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937), followed by Pinocchio, Fantasia, Dumbo, Bambi, Cinderella, and Sleeping Beauty. He expanded into live-action films, television (including The Mickey Mouse Club and Disneyland anthology series), and theme parks, opening Disneyland in Anaheim, California, in 1955. Before his death he was planning the Florida Project that became Walt Disney World, including the Experimental Prototype Community of Tomorrow (EPCOT).

Death and legacy

Disney, a heavy smoker, died of lung cancer in Burbank on December 15, 1966, ten days after his 65th birthday. He remains a central figure in the history of animation and American popular culture, regarded as a cultural icon. The company he co-founded grew into one of the largest mass-media and entertainment conglomerates in the world.
