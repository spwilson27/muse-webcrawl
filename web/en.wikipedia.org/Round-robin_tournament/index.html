Round-robin tournament

A round-robin tournament is a competition format in which each contestant meets every other contestant in turn. A round-robin contrasts with an elimination (knockout) tournament, in which participants are dropped after a loss. In a single round-robin, every participant plays every other once; in a double round-robin they meet twice — often once at home and once away, as in most football league seasons. With n contestants, a single round-robin requires n(n−1)/2 matches.

Scheduling

Round-robin schedules can be generated by the circle method: fix one contestant, arrange the others in a circle, and rotate them each round, yielding n−1 rounds for an even number of contestants (a "bye" is added for odd numbers). The Berger tables used in chess are a classic example. Chess round-robins are among the most prestigious events in the game, and the format is also standard in esports leagues, curling, and group stages of international football.

Advantages and disadvantages

The format guarantees many matches, ranks all participants on a substantial body of results, and is generally regarded as the fairest way to determine a winner: the champion has faced everyone and luck of the draw is minimised. Its drawbacks are the large number of fixtures, the possibility of "dead" matches once outcomes are decided, tie-breaking complexity (handled by criteria such as head-to-head results, goal difference, or Sonneborn–Berger scores in chess), and vulnerability to collusion in final rounds.

Variations

Many competitions combine a round-robin group stage with a knockout phase — the FIFA World Cup being the best-known example. In chess, round-robin results are often measured against expected scores for norm and rating purposes.
