Occitan literature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Occitan literature is the body of writing in the Occitan language of southern France and neighbouring regions, spanning more than a thousand years. It is most famous for the lyric poetry of the medieval troubadours, whose art of courtly love profoundly influenced European literature, from the Sicilian School and Dante to the German Minnesang.

Medieval flowering

The earliest known troubadour is William IX, Duke of Aquitaine (1071–1127). Later masters such as Bernart de Ventadorn, Arnaut Daniel (praised by Dante as the finest craftsman of the vernacular) and Bertran de Born developed forms including the canso, sirventes and tenso. Women poets, the trobairitz, such as the Comtessa de Dia, also composed. The Albigensian Crusade of the early 13th century devastated the southern courts and scattered the tradition.

Decline and revival

As French became the language of administration and prestige, Occitan writing declined, though it never vanished. The 19th century brought a revival led by the Félibrige movement and Frédéric Mistral, whose epic Mirèio (1859) helped earn him the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1904. A modern Occitan literature continues today in poetry, novels and theatre, supported by regional cultural movements.
