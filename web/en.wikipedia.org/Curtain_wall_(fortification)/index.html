Curtain wall (fortification)

A curtain wall is a defensive wall between two fortified bastions or towers on a fortified town, castle, or fortress. It is the principal enclosing wall of a fortification, spanning the interval between flanking towers, which project from the wall so that defenders could direct flanking fire along its face.

Design

Curtain walls were typically built of stone with a rubble-and-mortar core, thick enough — often several metres — to resist battering rams, mining, and stone-throwing artillery. The wall top carried a wall walk (parapet walk) protected by battlements with alternating merlons and crenels, from behind which defenders could shoot or drop missiles. Overhanging wooden hoardings, later replaced by stone machicolations, allowed missiles to be dropped on attackers at the wall's base. Arrow slits and, in later periods, gun ports pierced the wall.

Because the towers projected forward and stood taller than the curtain, each section of curtain was covered by defensive fire from its neighbours, reducing dead ground. Concentric castles, such as those built by Edward I in Wales, employed two rings of curtain walls so that the inner, higher wall could fire over the outer.

History

Enclosing walls linking towers are as old as fortified cities of the ancient Near East, and were standard in Roman, Byzantine, Islamic, and Chinese fortification. In medieval Europe, high curtain walls remained effective until the spread of gunpowder artillery in the 15th century prompted the shift toward lower, thicker walls backed by earth, angled bastions, and eventually the star fort. Curtain walls continued to enclose towns and fortresses well into the early modern period.
