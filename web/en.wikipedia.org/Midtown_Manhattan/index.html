Midtown Manhattan is the central portion of the New York City borough of Manhattan. It serves as the city's primary central business district and is home to some of the city's most famous buildings, including the Empire State Building, the Chrysler Building, and the United Nations headquarters. Generally, Midtown is considered to extend from 14th Street to 59th Street, between the Hudson River and the East River.

Overview

Midtown is the largest central business district in the world and is the workplace of roughly 400,000 people on a typical weekday. Its skyline, dense with office towers built during the 20th century, includes iconic structures such as Rockefeller Center, Grand Central Terminal, and One Vanderbilt. The neighborhood also contains Times Square, the Broadway theatre district, and Bryant Park.

History

Development of Midtown accelerated in the late 19th and early 20th centuries as department stores, hotels, and office buildings moved uptown from Lower Manhattan. The opening of Grand Central Terminal in 1913 and Pennsylvania Station in 1910 anchored the district, and the skyscraper boom of the 1920s produced landmarks like the Chrysler Building (1930) and Empire State Building (1931).

Districts and landmarks

Within Midtown are smaller districts including the Garment District, Herald Square, Koreatown, Murray Hill, and Hell's Kitchen on its western edge. Fifth Avenue divides Midtown into east and west halves and hosts flagship retail stores and the main branch of the New York Public Library.

Transportation

Midtown is served by Grand Central Terminal, Penn Station, and the Port Authority Bus Terminal, together with numerous New York City Subway lines, making it one of the most transit-rich areas in the United States.
