Territorial evolution of the United States

The territorial evolution of the United States describes the changes to the country's borders and internal divisions from its independence in 1776 to the present day. Beginning with the thirteen original states along the Atlantic coast, the United States expanded westward through purchase, treaty, annexation and conquest until it spanned the North American continent, and later acquired overseas territories.

Overview

The Treaty of Paris (1783) recognized American independence and granted lands east of the Mississippi River, south of Canada and north of Florida. The Louisiana Purchase of 1803 doubled the nation's size for $15 million. Florida followed in 1819. The annexation of Texas in 1845 and the Oregon Treaty of 1846 extended the republic to the Pacific Northwest, and victory in the Mexican–American War brought the Mexican Cession in 1848, including California and the Southwest. The Gadsden Purchase of 1853 completed the contiguous continental outline.

Overseas expansion followed: Alaska was purchased from Russia in 1867; Hawaii was annexed in 1898, the same year Spain ceded Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines. The U.S. Virgin Islands were bought from Denmark in 1917. Alaska and Hawaii became states in 1959, the last change to the roster of states. Internally, territories were repeatedly subdivided and carved into new states — the last major internal boundary adjustments settled long-running disputes in the early 20th century.
