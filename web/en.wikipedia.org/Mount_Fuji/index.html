Mount Fuji

Mount Fuji
Elevation: 3,775.51 m (12,389 ft) — highest in Japan
Location: Honshu, Japan (Shizuoka and Yamanashi prefectures)
Type: active stratovolcano
Last eruption: 1707–1708 (Hōei eruption)
UNESCO World Heritage Site: 2013 (cultural)

Mount Fuji (Japanese: 富士山, Fujisan) is an active stratovolcano on the island of Honshu in Japan, with a summit elevation of 3,775.51 m (12,389 ft), making it the country's tallest mountain. It lies about 100 km (62 mi) southwest of Tokyo and is visible from the capital on clear days. Fuji is one of Japan's "Three Holy Mountains" and a sacred site in Shinto, climbed by pilgrims for centuries, and has been a UNESCO World Heritage cultural site since 2013.

Geology

Fuji is a stratovolcano formed by successive layers of basaltic lava over older volcanic centers (Ko-Fuji and Shin-Fuji), with activity extending back roughly 100,000 years and the present cone built mainly in the last 10,000. Its last confirmed eruption, the Hōei eruption of 1707, deposited ash as far as Edo (Tokyo) but produced no lava flow, instead opening three new craters on its flank. The mountain is still classified as active with a low risk of eruption; a weather station and a Shinto shrine occupy the summit, which holds Japan's highest post office during the climbing season.

Climbing and culture

Each summer, during the official July–early-September season, hundreds of thousands of people ascend via four main trails (Yoshida, Subashiri, Gotemba, and Fujinomiya) to watch the sunrise (goraikō) from the summit; overcrowding and "bullet climbing" without rest have prompted reservation systems and entry fees in the 2020s. The first recorded ascent was in 663 by the monk En no Gyōja; women were formally barred until the Meiji era. Fuji dominates Japanese art—most famously Hokusai's Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji (including The Great Wave off Kanagawa) and Hiroshige's series of the same name—and remains a national symbol of Japan, appearing on currency, company names, and the 2020 Olympics imagery.
