Particle physics or high-energy physics is the branch of physics that studies fundamental particles and forces that constitute matter and radiation. The field also studies combinations of elementary particles up to the scale of protons and neutrons, while the study of combinations of protons and neutrons is called nuclear physics.

History

The idea that all matter is composed of elementary particles dates from at least the 6th century BC. In the 19th century, John Dalton, through his work on stoichiometry, concluded that each element of nature was composed of a single, unique type of particle. The word atom, after the Greek word atomos meaning "indivisible", has since then denoted the smallest particle of a chemical element, but physicists soon discovered that atoms are not the fundamental particles of nature, being composed of electrons, protons and neutrons.

The discoveries of the 1930s and 1940s, including the positron, muon, pion and neutrino, expanded the particle "zoo", and quantum field theory provided the framework for understanding them. The quark model, proposed in 1964 by Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig, organized the proliferating hadrons, and deep inelastic scattering experiments at SLAC confirmed quarks' physical reality.

The Standard Model

The Standard Model is the theory describing three of the four known fundamental forces (electromagnetic, weak and strong interactions, excluding gravity) and classifying all known elementary particles. It was developed in stages throughout the latter half of the 20th century. The discovery of the Higgs boson at CERN's Large Hadron Collider in 2012 confirmed the mechanism by which particles acquire mass and completed the model's particle content.

The elementary particles include six quarks (up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom), six leptons (electron, muon, tau and their neutrinos), the gauge bosons (photon, W and Z bosons, gluons) and the Higgs boson.

Experimental particle physics

Experimental particle physicists use particle accelerators to collide beams at high energies, and large detectors to record the products. Major laboratories include CERN in Europe, Fermilab in the United States, KEK in Japan and IHEP in China.

Open questions

Despite its success, the Standard Model is incomplete: it does not incorporate gravity, does not explain dark matter or dark energy, and leaves neutrino masses and the matter–antimatter asymmetry partly unexplained. Theories beyond the Standard Model, such as supersymmetry and string theory, are active research topics, though none has yet been experimentally confirmed.
