Pyrex

Pyrex (trademarked as PYREX in its original form and pyrex for later consumer lines) is a brand name for glassware introduced by Corning Inc. in 1915, originally made of borosilicate glass notable for its low thermal expansion and resistance to thermal shock. The first Pyrex products were laboratory and kitchen glassware — beakers, flasks, baking dishes, and measuring cups — that could move directly between heat sources and cold surfaces without cracking, and the brand became a generic household name for ovenproof glass.

History

Corning developed its borosilicate glass, initially marketed as Nonex, for railroad signal lanterns, battery jars, and laboratory ware. Legend credits Bessie Littleton, wife of Corning physicist Jesse Littleton, with suggesting kitchen applications after using a cut-down battery jar as a cake pan; Corning launched the Pyrex bakeware line in 1915, its name reportedly derived from "pie" plus the "-ex" suffix used on other Corning glass products. Borosilicate Pyrex became standard scientific glassware worldwide and a fixture of American kitchens, including colourful opal-glass lines such as the Primary Colors mixing bowls of the 1940s and patterned designs like "Gooseberry" and "Butterprint", now sought-after collectibles.

Composition changes

From the 1940s, and comprehensively after Corning sold its consumer products division in 1998, much American consumer Pyrex (lowercase "pyrex") was made instead of tempered soda-lime glass, which is mechanically stronger against impact but less resistant to thermal shock. Scientific and European products sold under the brand have continued in borosilicate. The change prompted recurring public debate, including consumer-magazine testing and reports of dishes shattering from sudden temperature changes; manufacturers advise avoiding extreme thermal transitions, preheating ovens, and not placing hot glass on wet or cold surfaces.

Uses

Beyond the kitchen and laboratory, Pyrex glass was used for telescope mirror blanks — most famously the 200-inch Hale Telescope mirror at Palomar Observatory, cast by Corning in 1934 — and for insulators, sight glasses, and industrial piping.
