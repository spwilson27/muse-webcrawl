Selective Service System

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nearly all male U.S. citizens and residents aged 18–25 must register with the Selective Service System.

The Selective Service System (SSS) is an independent agency of the United States government that maintains a database of men potentially subject to military conscription (the draft). Under the Military Selective Service Act, nearly all male citizens and male immigrant non-citizens between the ages of 18 and 25 are required to register. Although the United States has maintained an all-volunteer military since 1973, registration continues so that a draft could be resumed in a national emergency.

History

The modern system derives from the Selective Service Act of 1917, enacted during the First World War, and the Selective Training and Service Act of 1940, the first peacetime draft in U.S. history. Conscription operated during the Second World War, the Korean War, and the Vietnam War, where a lottery system introduced in 1969 determined the order of call-up by birthdate. Draft inductions ended in 1973. President Jimmy Carter reinstated registration in 1980 in response to the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, and registration has remained in place since, administered through the Selective Service System organized with state directors and local boards.

Registration and consequences

Men must register within 30 days of their 18th birthday, online, by mail, or at post offices. Failure to register is a felony, though prosecutions have been rare since the 1980s; non-registrants may be denied federal student aid, federal employment, and certain state benefits, and immigrant men who fail to register may jeopardize naturalization. Conscientious objector status would be adjudicated by local boards if a draft were reinstated.

Debates

The male-only registration requirement has been repeatedly challenged as unconstitutional sex discrimination. In Rostker v. Goldberg (1981), the Supreme Court upheld it because women were then excluded from combat roles. After combat roles opened to women, a federal district court in 2019 declared male-only registration unconstitutional, but the Fifth Circuit reversed on the ground that only the Supreme Court could revisit Rostker; the Supreme Court declined to review the case in 2021, noting Congress was actively considering the question.
