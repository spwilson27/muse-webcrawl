Secondary succession

Secondary succession is the process of ecological succession that occurs in an area where a previously existing community has been removed or disturbed, but where soil and some organisms remain. It contrasts with primary succession, which begins on newly exposed surfaces lacking soil.

Process

After a disturbance such as fire, logging, cultivation or flood, fast-growing pioneer herbs and grasses colonise first, followed by shrubs and then trees, gradually re-establishing a mature community. Because seeds, roots and soil nutrients survive, secondary succession is typically much faster than primary succession — taking decades to a couple of centuries rather than many centuries.

Examples

Classic examples include old-field succession in the eastern United States, where abandoned farmland passes through broomsedge and pine stages toward oak–hickory forest, and forest regrowth after wildfire, studied extensively at Mount St. Helens after its 1980 eruption and in Yellowstone after the 1988 fires.
