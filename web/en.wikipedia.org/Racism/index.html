Racism

Segregation-era signage in the United States, an example of institutionalised racism in law.

Racism is the belief that groups of humans possess different behavioral traits corresponding to inherited characteristics and can be divided based on the superiority of one race or ethnicity over another. It may also mean prejudice, discrimination, or antagonism directed against other people because they are of a different race or ethnicity. Modern variants of racism are often based in social perceptions of biological differences between peoples, and these views can take the form of social actions, practices or beliefs, or political systems in which different races are ranked as inherently superior or inferior to each other.

While the concepts of race and ethnicity are considered to be separate in contemporary social science, the two terms have a long history of equivalence in popular usage and older social science literature. "Racism" and "racial discrimination" are often used to describe discrimination on an ethnic or cultural basis, independent of whether these differences are described as racial.

Definitions and forms

The United Nations Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (1965) defines racial discrimination as any distinction based on race, colour, descent, or national or ethnic origin which impairs the equal enjoyment of human rights. Scholars commonly distinguish interpersonal racism from institutional or structural racism, in which policies and practices of organisations and states produce unequal outcomes even without individual intent, and from scientific racism, the pseudoscientific doctrines of the 18th to 20th centuries that claimed biological evidence for racial hierarchies.

History

Although hostility between ethnic groups is ancient, racism as an ideology crystallised during the era of European colonialism and the Atlantic slave trade, when writers and scientists devised classifications of humankind that placed Europeans above other peoples. Thinkers such as Arthur de Gobineau popularised theories of racial inequality in the 19th century. In the 20th century, racist ideologies underpinned Nazi Germany's persecution and genocide of Jews and others in the Holocaust, the apartheid system in South Africa, and legally enforced segregation in the United States. After 1945, the scientific consensus rejected biological racial hierarchies — genetics shows greater variation within than between conventionally defined racial groups — and most states formally outlawed racial discrimination, though its legacies and informal practices persist.

Anti-racism

Opposition to racism has taken the form of abolitionism, the civil rights movement, anti-colonial struggles, and anti-apartheid activism, as well as international law and national legislation on equality and hate crime. Contemporary debates concern issues such as systemic inequality, affirmative action, immigration, and the teaching of racial history in schools.
