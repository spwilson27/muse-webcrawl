Penal Laws (Ireland)

The Penal Laws in Ireland (Irish: Na Péindlíthe) were a series of laws imposed in an attempt to force Irish Catholics and Protestant dissenters (such as Presbyterians) to accept the established Church of Ireland. Enacted mainly between 1695 and 1728 after the Williamite victory in Ireland, they excluded Catholics from public life and land ownership for more than a century.

Provisions

The laws barred Catholics from voting, sitting in Parliament, holding public office, serving as officers, practising law, or bearing arms. The Banishment Act (1697) expelled regular clergy and bishops. The Act to Prevent the Further Growth of Popery (1704) restricted Catholic land purchase and inheritance: Catholic estates were to be divided equally among all sons (gavelkind) unless an heir conformed, and Catholics could not buy land or lease it for more than 31 years. Education abroad was forbidden, and Catholic schools were outlawed, giving rise to illegal "hedge schools".

Enforcement and effects

Enforcement was uneven and decayed over the 18th century, but the laws devastated the Catholic landowning class: the Catholic share of Irish land fell from about 22% in 1688 to roughly 5% by the late 18th century. Presbyterians in Ulster also suffered disabilities, spurring emigration to North America.

Repeal

Relief Acts from 1778 onward progressively dismantled the code: Catholics recovered rights to lease and inherit land, vote (1793), and enter most professions. The final major disabilities were removed by the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829 ("Catholic Emancipation"), won by Daniel O'Connell's mass movement.
