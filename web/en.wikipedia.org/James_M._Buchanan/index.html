James M. Buchanan

James McGill Buchanan Jr. (October 3, 1919 – January 9, 2013) was an American economist known for his work on public choice theory, for which he received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1986. Buchanan's work initiated the field of "politics without romance": the application of economic reasoning — self-interested, rational actors — to politicians, bureaucrats and voters rather than assuming public-spirited government. He co-authored, with Gordon Tullock, The Calculus of Consent (1962), a founding text of public choice.

Life and career

Buchanan was born in Murfreesboro, Tennessee, and raised on a farm during the Great Depression. He studied at Middle Tennessee State Teachers College, served in the U.S. Navy during the Second World War, and earned his doctorate from the University of Chicago in 1948, where he absorbed both Frank Knight's economics and, after a postdoctoral stay in Italy, the Italian tradition of public finance. He held posts at the University of Tennessee and Florida State before joining the University of Virginia, where with Gordon Tullock he founded the Thomas Jefferson Center for Studies in Political Economy. After moving to Virginia Tech — where the Center for the Study of Public Choice was established in 1969 — he moved the centre to George Mason University in 1983.

Contributions

Buchanan insisted on methodological individualism and contractarianism: the state is not a benevolent agent but an arena in which self-interested individuals act under rules; rational analysis must therefore focus on the rules themselves ("constitutional economics"). The Calculus of Consent analysed collective decision-making costs and defended unanimity at the constitutional level as the benchmark of legitimacy. Other major works include The Demand and Supply of Public Goods (1968), The Limits of Liberty (1975) and, with Richard Wagner, Democracy in Deficit (1977), which argued that Keynesian policy norms had destroyed the old balanced-budget constitution and structurally biased democracies toward deficits. His 1986 Nobel citation honoured "his development of the contractual and constitutional bases for the theory of economic and political decision-making."
