Faroese (føroyskt mál) is a North Germanic language spoken as a first language by about 69,000 Faroe Islanders and some 30,000 people elsewhere, chiefly in Denmark. It is one of five languages descended from Old West Norse spoken in the Middle Ages; the others are Icelandic and Norwegian and the extinct Norn and Greenlandic Norse. Faroese and Icelandic, its closest extant relative, are not mutually intelligible in speech, but the written languages resemble each other closely.

History

The language of the Norse settlers of the Faroe Islands, who arrived around the 9th century, was Old West Norse. Under Danish rule, Danish became the language of administration, church, and education, and Faroese survived mainly as an oral language, preserved in ballads (kvæði) and folk tales. In 1846 Venceslaus Ulricus Hammershaimb devised an etymological orthography based on Old Norse, which remains the standard written form. Faroese was permitted in schools in 1937, replaced Danish as the church language in 1938, and was recognized as the principal language of the Faroe Islands under the Home Rule Act of 1948.

Grammar

Faroese is an inflected language with three grammatical genders (masculine, feminine, neuter), four cases (nominative, accusative, dative, genitive), and two numbers. Nouns, adjectives, and pronouns decline, while verbs conjugate for tense and mood but not for person and number in the present tense. The alphabet includes the letter ð (edd), inherited from Icelandic usage, which in Faroese represents a glide or is silent depending on context.

Vocabulary

An official language policy favours coining new Faroese words from native roots rather than borrowing international terms, producing words such as telda ("computer", from a verb meaning "to count") and flogferð ("air travel"). Despite this purism, Danish loanwords remain common in everyday speech.
