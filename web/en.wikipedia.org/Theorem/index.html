Theorem

In mathematics, a theorem is a statement that has been proved, or can be proved. The proof of a theorem is a logical argument that uses the inference rules of a deductive system to establish that the theorem is a logical consequence of the axioms, and previously proved theorems.

In mainstream mathematics, the axioms and the inference rules are commonly left implicit, and, in this case, they are almost always those of Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with the axiom of choice (ZFC), or of a less powerful theory, such as Peano arithmetic. Generally, an assertion that is explicitly called a theorem is a proved result that is not an immediate consequence of other known theorems. Moreover, many authors qualify as theorems only those results with the most important results, and use the terms lemma, proposition and corollary for less important theorems.

Nature and terminology

In mathematical logic, theorems are usually formulated in a formal language and a set of inference rules specific to the logic. In this context, a theorem is a well-formed formula that can be derived from the axioms by applying the rules of inference. Related terms include proposition, lemma (a subsidiary or intermediate theorem), corollary (a consequence that follows readily), and conjecture (a statement believed true but not yet proved, such as those that remain famous unsolved problems).

Famous theorems

Well-known theorems include the Pythagorean theorem, Fermat's Last Theorem, the fundamental theorem of calculus, the fundamental theorem of algebra, and Gödel's incompleteness theorems. It has been estimated that over a quarter of a million theorems are proved every year.

Etymology

The word theorem comes from the Greek theōrēma, meaning "spectacle", "subject of contemplation", or "that which is looked at", related to theoria, from which "theory" also derives.
