Multilateralism

In international relations, multilateralism is the principle of participation of three or more countries in an activity, particularly co-operation conducted through international institutions, treaties and shared rules. It stands in contrast to bilateralism, in which states deal with one another one-to-one, and to unilateralism, in which a state acts alone.

Definition and characteristics

Political scientist John Ruggie identified three defining features of multilateral arrangements: indivisibility among the participants (for example, collective security, in which an attack on one is treated as an attack on all), generalised principles of conduct applying equally to members, and diffuse reciprocity, in which benefits are expected over the long term rather than in each transaction. Institutions such as the United Nations, the World Trade Organization and regional bodies like the European Union and ASEAN exemplify multilateralism in practice.

History and challenges

Multilateral diplomacy grew with the Concert of Europe after 1815, the League of Nations after the First World War, and the dense institutional order built after 1945. Since the early twenty-first century multilateralism has faced strain from great-power rivalry, trade disputes, withdrawals from agreements, and debates over reform of bodies such as the UN Security Council — prompting renewed discussion of how international co-operation can be sustained.
