Lies, damned lies, and statistics

"Lies, damned lies, and statistics" is a phrase describing the persuasive power of quantitative evidence, particularly the use of statistics to bolster weak arguments. It is often attributed to the British prime minister Benjamin Disraeli, though the attribution is uncertain.

Origin

The saying was popularized by Mark Twain, who in his autobiographical writings (published 1906) attributed it to Disraeli: "There are three kinds of lies: lies, damned lies, and statistics." No evidence of the phrase has been found in Disraeli's works, and earlier variants were used by other writers in the 1890s, including the politician Leonard Courtney, who quoted it in 1895.

Usage

The phrase is commonly invoked to express scepticism about statistics offered in public debate, especially where figures are selected, framed, or presented misleadingly. It gave its title to Joel Best's book Damned Lies and Statistics (2001) on the social construction of statistical claims, and echoes Darrell Huff's classic How to Lie with Statistics (1954).

Interpretation

Statisticians note the phrase is double-edged: while statistics can mislead, careful statistical reasoning is also the main tool for exposing false claims. The saying is therefore used both by critics of misleading data and by those defending rigorous quantitative methods against blanket distrust.
