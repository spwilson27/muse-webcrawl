Electoral college

An electoral college is a set of electors who are selected to elect a candidate to particular offices. Often these represent different organisations, political parties or entities, with each elector entitled to cast a prescribed number of votes. The term is most widely associated with the United States Electoral College, which elects the president and vice president, but similar bodies exist or have existed in many other countries.

Examples

In the United States, the Electoral College consists of 538 electors apportioned among the states; a candidate needs 270 electoral votes to win the presidency. In Germany, the Federal Convention (Bundesversammlung), composed of members of the Bundestag and an equal number of state delegates, elects the federal president. In India, the president is elected by an electoral college of members of Parliament and state legislatures, using weighted votes and the single transferable vote. The cardinals of the Roman Catholic Church form a college that elects the pope in a conclave.

Historical examples

The term originates with the prince-electors (Kurfürsten) of the Holy Roman Empire, who formally elected the emperor from the 13th century onward. Finland elected its president through an electoral college until direct elections were introduced in 1994; France's Senate is still elected by local electoral colleges (grands électeurs).

Assessment

Supporters of electoral colleges argue they can require broad geographic support, encourage deliberation and insulate the choice of a head of state from transient passions. Critics note that indirect election can contradict the popular vote and dilute democratic accountability.
