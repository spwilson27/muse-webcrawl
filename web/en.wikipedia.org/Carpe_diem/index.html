Carpe diem

Carpe diem is a Latin aphorism, usually translated "seize the day", taken from book 1 of the Roman poet Horace's work Odes (23 BC). The full line reads carpe diem, quam minimum credula postero — "Seize the day, put very little trust in tomorrow." The phrase urges enjoyment of the present rather than anxious concern with the future.

Origin

The phrase closes the eleventh ode of Horace's first book, addressed to Leuconoe. Horace advises her not to consult Babylonian astrology, to accept whatever comes, and to strain the wine now, since life is short. Literally, carpe means to harvest or pluck, as of ripe fruit, so the phrase carries an agricultural sense of gathering the day while it is ripe.

Related themes

The sentiment is related to the Epicurean emphasis on present pleasure, to the Greek concept of kairos (the opportune moment), and to later poetic traditions such as ubi sunt, memento mori, and the "gather ye rosebuds" motif of English cavalier poetry, notably Robert Herrick's "To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time" (1648).

In popular culture

The phrase was memorably popularised for modern audiences by the 1989 film Dead Poets Society, in which an English teacher urges his students to "seize the day" and make their lives extraordinary. It remains one of the most widely quoted Latin expressions in English.
