Law of excluded middle

In logic, the law of excluded middle (Latin: principium tertii exclusi) states that for every proposition, either the proposition or its negation is true: P ∨ ¬P. There is no third, middle possibility.

History and debate

The principle is rooted in Aristotle, though his discussion of future contingents ("tomorrow's sea battle") has been read as qualifying it. It is a cornerstone of classical logic. In the early 20th century L. E. J. Brouwer and the intuitionists rejected it for infinite domains, since a proof of P ∨ ¬P would require a method to decide which; intuitionistic and constructive logics therefore omit it, and double-negation elimination fails in them. Related but distinct is the principle of bivalence in semantics.
