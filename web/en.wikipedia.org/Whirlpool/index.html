A whirlpool is a body of rotating water produced when opposing currents meet, or when a current flows past an obstruction. Small whirlpools form whenever water is draining from a basin or tub; powerful natural whirlpools arise in straits and rapids where tidal currents rush over an irregular seabed. In common usage, any rotating current with a downdraft — a vortex whose axis points downward — is called a whirlpool, and in the ocean a large system of rotating currents is also known by the related term gyre.

Formation

When two currents run in opposite directions, or when a strong current meets a shoreline projection or a sudden change in depth, the water begins to rotate; the rotation concentrates into a vortex with a funnel-shaped depression at its centre, and water spirals downward along its axis. Tidal whirlpools appear and disappear with the tide, reaching maximum strength part-way through the ebb or flood, and vanishing at slack water.

Notable whirlpools

The Saltstraumen maelstrom in Norway, driven by one of the strongest tidal currents in the world, is often called the most powerful whirlpool on Earth. Nearby, the Moskstraumen off the Lofoten Islands — the original "maelstrom" — was described, with much exaggeration, by Edgar Allan Poe and Jules Verne. The Naruto whirlpools in Japan's Naruto Strait can reach 20 m (66 ft) across. Scotland's Gulf of Corryvreckan, between Jura and Scarba, produces waves and whirlpools audible for miles, and the Old Sow off Eastport, Maine, is the largest whirlpool in the western hemisphere.

Dangers

Small whirlpools are harmless curiosities, but powerful ones can capsize or swamp small boats, and swimmers can be dragged down by the downdraft. In mythology and literature whirlpools were personified by monsters such as Charybdis in the Strait of Messina, said to swallow and belch forth the sea three times a day.

In everyday settings

The familiar bathtub vortex that forms over a drain is a small whirlpool driven by the geometry of the basin and residual motion of the water; contrary to popular belief, its direction of rotation is determined by local conditions and basin shape, not, at such small scales, by the Coriolis effect of the Earth's rotation.
