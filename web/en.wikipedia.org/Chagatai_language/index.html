Chagatai (also spelled Chaghatai, Jagatai, or Chaghatay) is an extinct Turkic literary language of Central Asia, once widely spoken and used as a written lingua franca across the region from the 15th century until the early 20th century. It is named after Chagatai Khan, the second son of Genghis Khan, whose ulus covered much of Central Asia. Native speakers referred to it simply as Turki ("Turkic") or Sart in some contexts.

Development

Chagatai developed from the Karluk branch of Turkic, descended from the Middle Turkic literary languages of the Karakhanid era, and crystallized into a classical literary idiom in the Timurid courts of Herat and Samarkand in the late 15th century. Its greatest literary figure was the poet and statesman Ali-Shir Nava'i (1441–1501), whose works — including the Muhakamat al-Lughatayn ("Judgment Between the Two Languages"), arguing the merits of Turkic against Persian — established Chagatai as a prestigious language of poetry and prose alongside Persian.

The memoirs of Babur, founder of the Mughal Empire (the Baburnama), were written in Chagatai, as was a vast literature of history, biography, and Islamic scholarship. Abu al-Ghazi Bahadur Khan of Khiva wrote his genealogical histories of the Turks in Chagatai in the 17th century.

Decline and legacy

Chagatai remained the standard written language of Central Asia into the 19th century, but in the early Soviet period language reforms replaced it: in 1921–1928, Soviet linguists codified modern Uzbek and Uyghur as separate literary languages based on local dialects, and Chagatai fell out of use. Its modern descendants are Uzbek and Uyghur, both members of the Karluk (southeastern) branch of Turkic. Chagatai texts are written in a Perso-Arabic script, and today the language is studied by scholars of Turkic and Central Asian history.
