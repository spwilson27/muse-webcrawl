Renaissance

The Renaissance (UK: /rɪˈneɪsəns/, US: /ˈrɛnəsɑːns/) is a period of history and a European cultural movement covering the 15th and 16th centuries. It marked the transition from the Middle Ages to modernity and was characterised by an effort to revive and surpass the ideas and achievements of classical antiquity. The movement first emerged in the Italian city-states — above all Florence — before spreading across Europe.

Overview

The term derives from the French renaissance, "rebirth". The 19th-century historian Jacob Burckhardt, in The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy (1860), popularised the idea of the period as a decisive break from the medieval world, emphasising individualism and secularism. Later historians have debated how sharp that break truly was, pointing to earlier "renaissances" (Carolingian, Ottonian, and of the 12th century).

Origins in Italy

Florence's wealth from banking and the wool trade, Medici patronage, competition among city-states, and the arrival of Greek scholars and manuscripts — accelerated by the fall of Constantinople in 1453 — fed a revival of classical learning known as Renaissance humanism, anticipated by Dante, Petrarch and Boccaccio in the 14th century (the Trecento).

Ideas and achievements

Humanists studied ancient texts in their original languages, valuing rhetoric, history and moral philosophy. In art, linear perspective (codified by Brunelleschi and Alberti), anatomy and naturalism transformed painting and sculpture. Gutenberg's movable-type printing press (c. 1440) spread Renaissance ideas rapidly. The period also saw voyages of exploration, advances in astronomy culminating in Copernicus's heliocentric model (1543), and new political thought in Machiavelli's The Prince.

Northern Renaissance

North of the Alps, the movement took distinctive forms: Erasmus's biblical scholarship, Dürer's prints, the Franco-Flemish school of music, and Tudor England's flowering under Shakespeare. Historians distinguish these national Renaissances while noting their constant exchange with Italy.

Decline

The Sack of Rome (1527), the Counter-Reformation, the Italian Wars and shifting trade routes weakened Italy's primacy; by the early 17th century the movement had given way to Mannerism and the Baroque, and intellectually to the Scientific Revolution.
