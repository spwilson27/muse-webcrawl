Ernst Waldfried Josef Wenzel Mach (18 February 1838 – 19 February 1916) was an Austrian (Moravian-born) physicist and philosopher, who contributed to the physics of shock waves. The ratio of the velocity of a fluid flow to the local speed of sound is named the Mach number in his honour. As a philosopher of science, he was a major influence on the Vienna Circle and on logical empiricism, and through Mach's principle Mach anticipated in some ways Einstein's theory of general relativity.

Life and work

Mach studied at Vienna and held chairs at Graz, Prague, and the University of Vienna (from 1895), where he established experimental physics and the study of motion and shock waves. His shadowgraph and Schlieren photographs documented supersonic flow past bullets. His book The Science of Mechanics (1883) offered an influential critique of Newton's absolute space and time.

Philosophy

In epistemology, Mach held an empiricist, anti-metaphysical view: physical theories are economical descriptions of complexes of sensations, and hypotheses must be grounded in what is given in experience ("empirio-criticism"). His ideas influenced the neopositivists and Albert Einstein, who adopted the name "Mach's principle" in 1918 for the conjecture that inertia originates in interactions with distant matter of the universe, although Mach himself resisted the atomic theory and was sceptical of atoms. The Mach bands illusion in visual perception bears his name as well.
