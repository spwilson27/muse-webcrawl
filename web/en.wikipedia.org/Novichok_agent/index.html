Novichok (Russian: Новичок, "newcomer" or "novice") is a group of nerve agents developed by the Soviet Union and Russia between 1971 and 1993 under the FOLIANT programme. They are organophosphorus compounds that inhibit acetylcholinesterase, and some are claimed to be several times more potent than VX.

Development and disclosure

The programme aimed partly at agents that could be produced from common industrial chemicals and evade NATO detection and treaty verification. Chemists Vil Mirzayanov and Lev Fyodorov publicly revealed the programme in the early 1990s; Mirzayanov was charged with treason before the case was dropped.

Known uses

Novichok agents gained worldwide attention with the 2018 poisoning in Salisbury, England, of former Russian officer Sergei Skripal and his daughter Yulia, which the UK attributed to Russia; a local resident, Dawn Sturgess, later died from exposure. In 2020 the OPCW confirmed a Novichok-type agent in samples from Russian opposition figure Alexei Navalny.

Treaty status

In 2019 member states voted to add Novichok families to Schedule 1 of the Chemical Weapons Convention — the first update of the treaty's banned-chemicals list.
