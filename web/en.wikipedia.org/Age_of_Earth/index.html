Age of Earth

Age4.54 ± 0.04 billion years
MethodRadiometric dating of meteorites and terrestrial lead isotopes

The age of Earth is estimated at 4.54 ± 0.04 billion years. This dating is based on evidence from radiometric dating of meteorite material — which formed at the same time as the Solar System — and is consistent with the ages of the oldest known terrestrial and lunar samples.

Historical estimates

Early estimates came from scripture and natural philosophy: Archbishop James Ussher famously calculated a creation date of 4004 BC from biblical genealogies. In the 18th and 19th centuries, naturalists such as Buffon and Lord Kelvin estimated ages from Earth's cooling, arriving at tens of millions of years — too young, because they did not know about radioactivity or mantle convection. Geologists, reading the rock record, argued for much longer timescales.

Radiometric dating

The discovery of radioactivity in 1896 provided a reliable clock. In the 1950s, Clair Cameron Patterson measured lead isotope ratios in the Canyon Diablo iron meteorite and derived an age of about 4.55 billion years, published in 1956 — essentially the modern value. Because Earth's oldest rocks have been recycled by plate tectonics, the oldest terrestrial materials are zircon crystals from Jack Hills, Australia, dated to about 4.4 billion years; meteorites provide the age of the planet's formation itself.

Context

Earth formed by accretion from the solar nebula shortly after the Sun ignited. Its age is slightly younger than the oldest calcium–aluminium-rich inclusions in meteorites (4.567 billion years), which mark the beginning of the Solar System, and overlaps the formation of the Moon in a giant impact roughly 4.5 billion years ago.
