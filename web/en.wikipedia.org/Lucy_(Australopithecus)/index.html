Lucy, formally AL 288-1 and known in Ethiopia as Dinknesh ("you are marvellous"), is a collection of several hundred pieces of fossilised bone representing roughly 40 percent of the skeleton of a female of the hominin species Australopithecus afarensis. Discovered in 1974 in the Afar Region of Ethiopia, Lucy is one of the most complete early hominin skeletons known and became a key piece of evidence for early bipedalism.

Discovery

On 24 November 1974, palaeoanthropologist Donald Johanson and graduate student Tom Gray found Lucy's remains at the locality AL 288 in the Hadar Formation. The specimen was nicknamed "Lucy" after the Beatles song "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds", which was playing in camp that evening.

Description

Lucy stood about 1.1 m (3 ft 7 in) tall and weighed about 29 kg (64 lb). Her skeleton combined ape-like features — a small braincase, prognathic face and relatively long arms — with unmistakably human-like features of the pelvis, knee and spine indicating habitual upright walking. The valgus angle of her femur, the shape of her pelvis and the curvature of her phalanges suggested a mixture of bipedal locomotion and some retained climbing ability.

Significance

Lucy demonstrated that human ancestors walked upright well before the evolution of large brains, overturning the earlier expectation that encephalisation came first. Her species, named Australopithecus afarensis in 1978, also includes the Laetoli footprints and the "First Family" assemblage from AL 333. The original fossils are held at the National Museum of Ethiopia in Addis Ababa.
