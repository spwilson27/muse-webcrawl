Television antenna

A television antenna (or TV aerial, in British English) is an antenna designed for use with a television receiver to receive over-the-air broadcast television signals in the very high frequency (VHF) and ultra high frequency (UHF) bands. Antennas range from simple indoor "rabbit ears" dipoles and loop antennas, to large outdoor arrays mounted on roofs and masts. Although cable, satellite, and streaming displaced antennas in many households, they remain the standard means of receiving free-to-air digital terrestrial television, and enjoyed renewed popularity with cord-cutting.

Types

Indoor antennas include the telescoping VHF dipole known as "rabbit ears" (often swivelled and extended for best reception), a UHF loop, and modern flat amplified panels placed near windows. Outdoor antennas are typically directional: the Yagi–Uda array — a driven element, reflector, and series of directors — and the broadband log-periodic antenna dominate rooftop installations, often pointed at a transmitter site and sometimes fitted with a rotator. Gain increases with directionality and height, and a masthead amplifier can overcome cable losses. Because digital signals exhibit a "cliff effect" — a clean picture until reception fails abruptly — antenna siting matters as much as with analogue reception, which degraded gracefully into snow and ghosting.

Principles

Antenna elements are sized to the wavelength of the band: a half-wave dipole for VHF channel frequencies is metres long, while UHF elements are tens of centimetres, so combined VHF/UHF designs and wideband UHF arrays are common. Analogue-era antennas remain usable for digital broadcasts (ATSC, DVB-T/T2, ISDB-T) because the carrier frequencies are largely unchanged; repacking of the UHF band for mobile services (e.g., the 600 and 700 MHz bands) has shifted some channels lower, favouring antennas optimised for the reduced range.
