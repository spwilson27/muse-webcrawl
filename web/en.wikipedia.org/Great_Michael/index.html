Great Michael

Type: Carrack (great ship)

Launched: 12 October 1511, Newhaven, Scotland

Builder: Ordered by James IV of Scotland

Fate: Passed to France after 1513; last recorded 1536

Michael, popularly called the Great Michael, was a carrack — a great ship — of the Royal Scottish Navy. She was the largest ship built at the time she was launched in 1511 and was commissioned by King James IV of Scotland as the centrepiece of his ambitious naval programme.

Construction

The ship was built at Newhaven, near Edinburgh, with construction beginning around 1507 under the direction of master shipwrights including Martin Martin and, later, French craftsmen. Contemporary accounts say her construction consumed timber from forests across Scotland — traditionally "all the woods of Fife" — supplemented by imported timber from France, Norway and the Baltic. She was launched on 12 October 1511 and completed in 1512.

Great Michael was about 73 m (240 ft) long overall including her beak, displaced roughly 1,000 tons, and carried a crew of around 300 sailors, 120 gunners and up to 1,000 soldiers. Her armament included 24 heavy cannon and numerous lighter guns, making her one of the most powerful warships in Europe — comparable to England's Mary Rose and Henry Grace à Dieu, the latter built partly in response.

Service

Under the command of James IV's admiral Andrew Barton and later the Earl of Arran, Michael cruised in support of Scottish interests and Scotland's French allies. After James IV was killed at the Battle of Flodden in 1513, the expense of maintaining the ship proved too great for the regency government, and she was sold to France in 1514. She served in the French navy and was last recorded in 1536 at Brest; her ultimate fate is unknown, though she is believed to have been allowed to rot at Brest.

Legacy

Great Michael remains a symbol of the ambitions of James IV's Renaissance court. A reminder of her size is preserved in the traditional saying that she cost Scotland a fortune, and her story is retold at the port of Newhaven, where a pub and local memorial keep her memory alive.
