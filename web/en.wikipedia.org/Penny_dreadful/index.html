Penny dreadful

The penny dreadful was a type of inexpensive popular literature produced in the United Kingdom during the 19th century. Sold for a penny, these serialised stories were aimed principally at working-class adolescent boys and were published in weekly parts, each typically eight to sixteen pages.

History

Penny dreadfuls emerged in the 1830s, following the success of cheap publications such as the Penny Magazine. Publishers like Edward Lloyd produced vast numbers of sensational titles. The name "penny dreadful" was originally applied by disapproving critics to what they regarded as lurid, low-quality fiction.

The most famous penny dreadful characters include Sweeney Todd, who first appeared in The String of Pearls (1846–47), the highwayman Dick Turpin, whose exploits were popularised in Rookwood, and the boy detective Sexton Blake. Varney the Vampire (1845–47) was one of the longest and most influential of the early titles.

Content and themes

Typical subjects included crime, adventure, romance, the supernatural, pirates, highwaymen and Gothic horror. Stories were written quickly by poorly paid authors, sometimes under multiple pseudonyms, and were illustrated with crude woodcut images designed to catch the eye of purchasers.

Decline and legacy

By the 1890s the penny dreadful was being displaced by halfpenny "story papers" such as Alfred Harmsworth's Halfpenny Marvel and Union Jack, which were marketed as more wholesome. The format influenced later British comics and popular fiction generally. Moral campaigners frequently blamed penny dreadfuls for juvenile crime, a criticism echoed in later debates about comics and video games.
