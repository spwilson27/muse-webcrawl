Cryoscopy

Cryoscopy is the measurement of the freezing point of a solvent or solution, used in particular to determine the molar mass of a dissolved substance. It rests on the colligative phenomenon of freezing-point depression: when a non-volatile solute is dissolved in a solvent, the freezing point of the solution is lower than that of the pure solvent, and the depression is proportional to the molal concentration of solute particles.

Principle

The freezing-point depression ΔTf is given by ΔTf = Kf · b · i, where Kf is the cryoscopic constant of the solvent, b is the molality of the solution, and i is the van 't Hoff factor. By measuring ΔTf for a solution containing a known mass of solute, the molar mass of the solute can be calculated.

Method

The classical technique uses a Beckmann thermometer, which can resolve temperature differences of a few thousandths of a degree. The solvent is cooled slowly with stirring until it begins to freeze, and the plateau temperature is recorded. The measurement is repeated after adding a weighed amount of solute. Camphor, with its exceptionally large cryoscopic constant, was historically popular in the Rast method for micro-scale determinations.

Applications

Cryoscopy is used to determine molar masses of organic compounds, to check the purity of substances, and in the dairy industry, where the freezing point of milk is measured to detect added water.
