Usury

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Moneylending at interest was condemned as usury by medieval religious authorities.

Usury (from Latin usura, "use") originally meant the charging of any interest on a loan. In modern usage it denotes the practice of making unethical or immoral monetary loans that unfairly enrich the lender, especially loans carrying an exorbitant or illegally high rate of interest. The term is applied in modern law in many jurisdictions to interest rates above a statutory maximum.

History

Ancient world

Interest-bearing loans predate coinage; in ancient Mesopotamia customary rates ran to 20 percent on silver and 33⅓ percent on grain. Greek and Roman philosophers were hostile: Plato disapproved of interest, and Aristotle argued in the Politics that money "breeding" money was unnatural. Roman law limited rates at various times, and Justinian's code fixed maxima between roughly 4 and 12 percent depending on the borrower.

Religious prohibitions

Judaism: the Torah forbids charging interest on loans to fellow Israelites (Exodus 22:25; Deuteronomy 23:19–20 permits interest on loans to foreigners), a distinction that shaped medieval practice.

Christianity: drawing on Luke 6:35 ("lend, expecting nothing in return") and canon law, the medieval Church prohibited usury absolutely for clergy and, from the Councils of the 12th and 13th centuries, for laypeople as well; unrepentant usurers could be denied Christian burial. Scholastics such as Thomas Aquinas argued that charging interest was charging twice for the same thing. In practice, credit was supplied through evasions and alternatives — bills of exchange, partnerships, rent-charges (census), and the "triple contract" — and by Jewish and Lombard lenders operating under licence. From the 16th century, reformers including Calvin accepted moderate interest, and Catholic doctrine gradually narrowed the prohibition to excessive rates.

Islam: the Quran prohibits riba (usually understood as interest) in strong terms (e.g., 2:275–279). Islamic finance therefore structures credit through profit-sharing, leasing and mark-up sale arrangements rather than interest-bearing loans, an industry that grew rapidly from the 1970s.

Usury laws

Many modern states cap interest rates on some or all consumer loans. In the United States, usury law is mainly state law, with caps varying widely and major exceptions — notably, the Supreme Court's 1978 decision in Marquette National Bank v. First of Omaha allowed national banks to export the rate of their home state, weakening state caps. Payday lending, credit cards and rent-to-own schemes have been frequent subjects of usury litigation and legislation. Other countries, such as Canada (criminal rate of interest, historically 60 percent effective annual rate) and many EU states, maintain general maxima or doctrines against unconscionable rates.

Economic views

Mainstream economics treats interest as the price of credit — compensating lenders for time preference, inflation risk and default risk — and generally finds that binding rate ceilings reduce the supply of legal credit to high-risk borrowers, sometimes driving them to illegal lenders. Defenders of caps respond that consumer credit markets are prone to behavioural exploitation and that limits protect vulnerable borrowers from debt traps.
