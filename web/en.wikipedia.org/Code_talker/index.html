A code talker was a person employed by the military during wartime to use a little-known language as a means of secret communication. The term is now usually associated with the United States soldiers during the world wars who used their knowledge of Native American languages as a basis to transmit coded messages. In particular, there were approximately 400 to 500 Native Americans in the United States Marine Corps whose primary job was to transmit secret tactical messages using codes built upon their native languages.

World War I

During the First World War, Choctaw soldiers in the U.S. Army's 36th Division were used to transmit messages in their language over field telephones during the Meuse-Argonne Offensive in 1918, confounding German listeners. Cherokee soldiers were also used for similar purposes.

Navajo code talkers of World War II

The most famous code talkers were the Navajo speakers recruited by the U.S. Marine Corps in 1942, following a proposal by Philip Johnston, a civil engineer who had grown up on the Navajo reservation. The original 29 recruits developed a code in which Navajo words stood for military terms and letters — for example, the Navajo word for "turtle" represented a tank, and "iron fish" a submarine. At the Battle of Iwo Jima, six Navajo code talkers transmitted more than 800 messages without error. Major Howard Connor later stated that the Marines would never have taken Iwo Jima without the Navajo code talkers. The Navajo code was never broken, and the programme remained classified until 1968.

Other groups and recognition

Comanche code talkers served with the U.S. Army in Europe, including at Utah Beach on D-Day, and Meskwaki, Hopi, and other tribal members also served. In 2001, the original 29 Navajo code talkers were awarded Congressional Gold Medals, with silver medals for the others who served. The Code Talkers Recognition Act of 2008 extended recognition to additional tribes.
