Oolong

Oolong (烏龍茶, "dark dragon tea") is a traditional Chinese tea produced through a process that includes withering the leaves and partial oxidation before curling and twisting. Oolongs occupy a spectrum between unoxidised green tea and fully oxidised black tea, with oxidation levels commonly ranging from about 8% to 85%, giving an unusually wide range of flavour.

Production

Production involves wilting the freshly picked leaves in the sun, bruising them by shaking or rolling to promote oxidation at the leaf edges, and firing to halt the process. Depending on style, the leaves are then rolled into tight balls (as for many Taiwanese and Anxi teas) or twisted into long strips (as for Wuyi rock teas). Roasting is an important finishing step for many styles.

Major styles

  • Wuyi rock tea (yancha) from Fujian's Wuyi Mountains, including Da Hong Pao ("Big Red Robe").
  • Anxi Tieguanyin ("Iron Goddess of Mercy"), a lightly oxidised, floral ball-rolled tea.
  • Taiwanese oolongs, including high-mountain (gaoshan) teas, Dong Ding, and Oriental Beauty (Dongfang Meiren), whose flavour depends on leafhopper-bitten leaves.
  • Dancong teas from Guangdong, prized for mimicking particular fragrances.

Brewing and culture

Oolong is closely associated with the gongfu method of brewing in small pots with many short infusions, in which the same leaves are steeped repeatedly. Caffeine content is moderate, and the tea is traditionally credited in China with various health benefits.
