Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

Asymmetric septal hypertrophy on echocardiography.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM, or HOCM when obstructive) is a condition in which a portion of the heart becomes thickened without an obvious cause, resulting in the heart being less able to pump blood effectively. The thickening most often involves the interventricular septum. Symptoms vary from none to feeling tired, leg swelling, shortness of breath, chest pain, or fainting. Complications include heart failure, an irregular heartbeat, and sudden cardiac death.

Cause and pathophysiology

HCM is most commonly inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, caused by mutations in genes encoding sarcomeric proteins such as MYH7 (β-myosin heavy chain) and MYBPC3 (myosin-binding protein C). It affects roughly 1 in 500 people, making it the most common inherited heart disease. Under the microscope, affected myocardium shows myocyte hypertrophy and disarray with interstitial fibrosis. In about two-thirds of patients the hypertrophied septum and abnormal mitral-valve motion obstruct outflow from the left ventricle (systolic anterior motion), producing a dynamic pressure gradient that worsens with exertion or dehydration.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually by echocardiography showing unexplained left-ventricular wall thickness of 15 mm or more (13–14 mm is borderline), supported by electrocardiography, cardiac MRI, and genetic testing. HCM is a leading cause of sudden death in young athletes, which has driven debate about pre-participation screening.

Treatment

Treatment includes beta blockers or verapamil for symptoms, avoidance of dehydration and (in obstructive disease) pure vasodilators, and the cardiac myosin inhibitor mavacamten. Patients at high risk of sudden death receive an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator. Refractory obstructive cases may be treated by surgical septal myectomy or alcohol septal ablation. Family screening is recommended because of the hereditary nature of the condition.
