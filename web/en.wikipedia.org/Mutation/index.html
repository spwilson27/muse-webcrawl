Mutation

In biology, a mutation is an alteration in the nucleotide sequence of the genome of an organism, virus, or extrachromosomal DNA. Mutations result from errors during DNA replication, mitosis, or meiosis, or from damage to DNA caused by mutagens such as radiation or certain chemicals.

Types

Point mutations change a single nucleotide: substitutions may be silent, missense, or nonsense. Insertions and deletions add or remove bases and can shift the reading frame. Large-scale mutations include gene duplications, inversions, translocations, and whole-chromosome changes such as aneuploidy.

Causes

Spontaneous mutations arise from replication errors and the chemical instability of DNA. Induced mutations are caused by physical mutagens (UV light, X-rays) or chemical mutagens (base analogues, alkylating agents). Cells possess extensive DNA repair systems that correct most lesions before they become permanent.

Effects

Most mutations are neutral. Some are harmful, causing genetic disorders or cancer; a minority are beneficial, providing the raw material for adaptation. Together with recombination, mutation is the ultimate source of all genetic variation, and hence of evolution by natural selection.

Mutation rates

Rates vary widely among organisms, from roughly one error per hundred million bases per generation in humans (after repair) to far higher rates in RNA viruses, which lack proofreading.
