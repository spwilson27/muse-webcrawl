The Divine Comedy (Italian: Divina Commedia) is an Italian narrative poem by Dante Alighieri, begun around 1308 and completed in 1320, a year before his death. Widely considered the pre-eminent work in Italian literature and one of the greatest works of world literature, it is an imaginative vision of the Christian afterlife that helped establish the Tuscan dialect as the standardized Italian language.

Structure

The poem consists of three parts or cantiche — Inferno, Purgatorio, and Paradiso — each of 33 cantos, plus an introductory canto, for a total of 100. Dante the pilgrim is guided down through the nine circles of Hell by the Roman poet Virgil, up the seven terraces of Purgatory, and finally through the heavens of Paradise by Beatrice, his idealized beloved. The verse form, terza rima, interlocking three-line stanzas, was Dante's invention.

Legacy

The work combines theology, philosophy, astronomy, and biting political commentary on Dante's Florence, placing popes, rivals, and classical and biblical figures in their eternal stations. It has inspired artists from Botticelli and Blake to Rodin and Gustave Doré, whose 19th-century engravings remain its most famous illustrations.
