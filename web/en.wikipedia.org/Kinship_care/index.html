Kinship care

Kinship care
Also known asKin care, relative care, family and friends care
CarersGrandparents, aunts/uncles, siblings, other relatives
FieldChild welfare / social work

Grandparents are the most common kinship carers.

Kinship care is the full-time care, nurturing, and protection of a child by relatives or other adults with a significant emotional bond to the child (sometimes called "fictive kin"), when the child cannot live with their parents. It takes place both informally, by private family arrangement, and formally, when a child welfare agency places a child with relatives as an alternative to foster care with strangers.

Types

  • Informal (private) kinship care – arranged by the family without state involvement; by far the largest category. An estimated several million children in the United States alone live in such arrangements.
  • Formal kinship care – the child is in public care and placed with approved relatives, who receive support and supervision similar to foster carers.
  • Kinship guardianship – a legal arrangement giving the carer parental authority, sometimes subsidised (e.g., the U.S. Guardianship Assistance Program).

Why children enter kinship care

Children may be raised by kin because of parental death, illness or disability, substance misuse, incarceration, mental health problems, poverty, military deployment, or abuse and neglect. Grandparents form the majority of kinship carers, often taking on childcare in later life with little preparation.

Outcomes and support

Research generally finds that children in kinship care experience greater placement stability, maintain family, cultural, and community connections, and report feeling loved and secure at rates equal to or better than stranger foster care, with comparable safety and behavioural outcomes. Kinship carers, however, are on average older, poorer, in worse health, and receiving less financial support, training, and services than non-relative foster carers, and many report strain from legal uncertainty, housing overcrowding, and navigating benefits systems. Policies increasingly emphasise "kin-first" placement preferences — for example the U.S. Fostering Connections to Success and Increasing Adoptions Act of 2008 and the UN Guidelines for the Alternative Care of Children (2009), which prioritise care within the extended family.
