Political communication

Political communication is the study and practice of the creation, exchange and effects of information and messages in political contexts. As an academic field within communication studies and political science, it examines how political actors, the media, and citizens produce, disseminate and respond to political content, and how communication shapes political processes such as elections, policymaking and public deliberation.

Key actors

Political communication is commonly analysed in terms of three main sources: political actors (parties, candidates, governments and interest groups), the news media, and citizens. Scholars such as Jay Blumler and Michael Gurevitch framed the field around the interactions among these three, while Brian McNair defined political communication as purposeful communication about politics, encompassing the discourse of politicians, media coverage of politics, and public discussion of political matters.

Topics

Major research topics include agenda setting, framing, priming, political advertising, campaign communication, political rhetoric, mediatization, propaganda, and the effects of digital and social media on political engagement and polarisation. The rise of the internet has shifted the field toward studying hybrid media systems, in which older broadcast logics interact with networked, participatory communication.

History

The study of political persuasion reaches back to classical rhetoric, but political communication emerged as a distinct scholarly field in the mid-twentieth century with studies of propaganda and election campaigns, such as the Erie County studies of Paul Lazarsfeld and colleagues in the 1940s, and later work on television and image-based politics.
