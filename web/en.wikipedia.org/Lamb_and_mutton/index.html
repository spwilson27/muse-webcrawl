Lamb and mutton

Sheep meat is one of the most common meats around the world, taken from the domestic sheep. It is generally divided into three categories largely depending on the age of the sheep: lamb, hogget and mutton.

Lamb is the meat of sheep in their first year. Hogget, widely used in Britain, Australia and New Zealand, is the meat of a sheep in its second year, or the animal itself. Mutton is the meat of a mature sheep, at least two years old; it is darker, stronger-flavoured and tougher than lamb, and in South Asian and Caribbean English the word often refers to goat meat.

Classification

Definitions vary by country. In the United States, "lamb" covers most sheep meat marketed, and mature sheep meat is rarely eaten. In Britain, Australia and New Zealand, age categories are more strictly applied, with lamb having at most one pair of permanent incisor teeth, hogget two pairs, and mutton more.

Consumption

Sheep meat is central to the cuisines of the Mediterranean, the Middle East, Central Asia, Australia and New Zealand. Consumption per capita has declined in many Western countries since the mid-twentieth century but remains high in countries such as Mongolia, Iceland and New Zealand. Classic dishes include roast lamb, lamb chops, moussaka, kebabs, and slow-cooked mutton curries.

Nutrition

Like other red meats, lamb and mutton are rich in protein, iron, zinc and vitamin B12, but also relatively high in saturated fat; leaner cuts and trimming reduce fat content.
