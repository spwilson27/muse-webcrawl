American literature

[Image placeholder]

American literature is literature written or produced in the United States of America and in the colonies that preceded it. The American literary tradition is part of the broader tradition of English-language literature, but also includes literature produced in Spanish, French, and other languages by American authors. For most of its early history, American literature was dominated by British writers and colonial writing from the New England region.

Colonial and early national writing

Early American writing includes the journals of John Smith, the histories of William Bradford, and the sermons and poetry of Puritan New England — Anne Bradstreet, Edward Taylor, and later Jonathan Edwards. The Revolutionary era produced the political writing of Benjamin Franklin, Thomas Paine's Common Sense (1776), and the Federalist Papers. Washington Irving, with The Sketch Book (1819–20) and tales such as "Rip Van Winkle" and "The Legend of Sleepy Hollow," was among the first American writers to win an international reputation, alongside James Fenimore Cooper and his Leatherstocking Tales.

The American Renaissance

The mid-19th century saw a flowering often called the American Renaissance: Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau articulated Transcendentalism; Nathaniel Hawthorne wrote The Scarlet Letter (1850); Herman Melville published Moby-Dick (1851); Walt Whitman issued the ever-expanding Leaves of Grass (1855); Emily Dickinson wrote nearly 1,800 poems, almost all published posthumously. Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852) was the best-selling novel of the century and fuelled abolitionist sentiment.

Realism and regionalism

After the Civil War, Mark Twain — especially Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884) — established a distinctly American vernacular prose. William Dean Howells championed literary realism, practised also by Henry James, whose novels examined the clash of American and European cultures. Regional and local-colour writing flourished in the work of Sarah Orne Jewett, Kate Chopin, and Charles Chesnutt, while naturalists such as Stephen Crane, Theodore Dreiser, and Jack London portrayed lives shaped by heredity and environment.

Modernism and the twentieth century

The early 20th century brought modernist poetry (T. S. Eliot, Ezra Pound, Wallace Stevens, William Carlos Williams) and the prose of the "Lost Generation": F. Scott Fitzgerald's The Great Gatsby (1925), Ernest Hemingway's spare novels, and William Faulkner's Yoknapatawpha saga. The Harlem Renaissance produced Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, and Jean Toomer. After 1945, major figures include Ralph Ellison, Saul Bellow, Flannery O'Connor, Toni Morrison, Thomas Pynchon, and the poets of the Beat Generation and the New York School.

Contemporary literature

Contemporary American literature is notably diverse, encompassing the work of writers from many ethnic and regional backgrounds, from Morrison and Alice Walker to Junot Díaz, Ocean Vuong, and Louise Erdrich, alongside genre fiction and graphic narrative that have gained wide critical recognition.
