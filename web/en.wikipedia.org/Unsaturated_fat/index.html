Unsaturated fat

An unsaturated fat is a fat or fatty acid in which there is at least one double bond within the fatty acid chain. A fatty acid with one double bond is monounsaturated, while one with more than one is polyunsaturated. The double bonds introduce kinks in the hydrocarbon chains, preventing the molecules from packing tightly, so unsaturated fats tend to be liquid at room temperature — as in olive oil and most vegetable oils — whereas saturated fats are typically solid.

Chemistry

Most naturally occurring double bonds in unsaturated fatty acids are in the cis configuration, in which the hydrogen atoms lie on the same side of the bond, bending the chain. Industrial partial hydrogenation can produce trans fats, in which the hydrogens are on opposite sides, straightening the chain; trans fats are associated with adverse health effects and have been largely eliminated from food supplies in many countries. Examples of unsaturated fatty acids include oleic acid (monounsaturated) and linoleic and alpha-linolenic acids (polyunsaturated omega-6 and omega-3 fatty acids).

Health effects

Dietary guidelines generally recommend replacing saturated fats with unsaturated fats to reduce LDL cholesterol and cardiovascular disease risk. Polyunsaturated omega-3 fatty acids, found in oily fish, flaxseed and walnuts, are essential nutrients the body cannot synthesise. The Mediterranean diet, rich in olive oil, nuts and fish, is frequently cited as evidence of health benefits from predominantly unsaturated fat intake, though research into specific effects continues.

Sources

Rich sources include avocados, nuts, seeds, olive and canola oils, and fatty fish such as salmon and mackerel. Because their double bonds are reactive, polyunsaturated fats are more prone to oxidation and rancidity than saturated fats.
