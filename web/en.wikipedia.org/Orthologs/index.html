Orthologs

Orthologs are genes in different species that evolved from a common ancestral gene by speciation. The term was introduced by Walter Fitch in 1970 to distinguish this relationship from paralogs — genes related by duplication within a genome. Together orthologs and paralogs are called homologs.

Identification

Because orthologs separated only by speciation, they usually retain the same function in the different organisms, which makes orthology central to comparative genomics: annotating a newly sequenced genome largely means transferring functional information from orthologs in model organisms. Computational identification methods include reciprocal best hits in sequence searches, phylogenetic tree reconciliation, synteny (conserved gene order), and graph-based clustering tools such as OrthoFinder, InParanoid and the eggNOG and OrthoDB databases.

Subtypes

Where a gene duplicated after speciation, one species' gene may correspond to several genes in another (one-to-many orthology, giving co-orthologs or in-paralogs). Xenologs arise from horizontal gene transfer, and ohnologs are paralogs produced by whole-genome duplication, a concept named after Susumu Ohno.

Applications

Ortholog catalogues underpin phylogenetic profiling, the study of gene family evolution, and biomedical research: most human disease genes have identifiable orthologs in model organisms such as mouse, zebrafish, fruit fly and yeast, enabling functional experiments.
