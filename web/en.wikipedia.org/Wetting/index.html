Wetting

Wetting is the ability of a liquid to maintain contact with a solid surface, resulting from intermolecular interactions when the two are brought together. The degree of wetting (wettability) is determined by a balance between adhesive forces between the liquid and the solid and cohesive forces within the liquid itself.

Contact angle

Wetting is commonly quantified by the contact angle θ formed at the liquid–solid–gas interface, described by Young's equation, which relates the interfacial tensions of the three phases. A contact angle less than 90° indicates good wetting (the liquid spreads over the surface), while an angle greater than 90° indicates poor wetting (the liquid beads up). Surfaces with contact angles above ~150° for water are termed superhydrophobic; the lotus effect is a well-known natural example.

Spreading and dynamics

A liquid spreads completely when its spreading coefficient is positive. Real surfaces complicate wetting through roughness and chemical heterogeneity, described by the Wenzel and Cassie–Baxter models, and contact angles often show hysteresis between advancing and receding values. Dynamic wetting matters in coating, printing, and oil recovery.

Applications

Wetting is fundamental to detergency, soldering and brazing, lubrication, pesticide spraying, waterproofing textiles, microfluidics, and enhanced oil recovery. Surfactants are used to modify wetting behaviour by reducing surface tension, promoting spreading and penetration.
