Dual inheritance theory

Dual inheritance theory (DIT), also called gene–culture coevolution or cultural evolution, is a theory in the social and behavioural sciences which posits that human behaviour is a product of two different and interacting evolutionary processes: genetic evolution and cultural evolution. Genes and culture interact in a feedback loop: genes affect the capacities that allow humans to learn from one another, while cultural practices create selection pressures that can change the distribution of genes in a population.

The theory was developed principally by the anthropologists Robert Boyd and Peter J. Richerson, whose 1985 book Culture and the Evolutionary Process laid much of its mathematical foundation, and by the population geneticists Marcus Feldman and Luigi Luca Cavalli-Sforza, who had begun modelling cultural transmission in the 1970s.

Overview

In dual inheritance theory, culture is defined as socially learned information — knowledge, beliefs, values and practices — that is transmitted between individuals through teaching, imitation and other forms of social learning. Because such information can be passed on, varied and selected, it evolves in a manner formally analogous to genetic evolution, although the mechanisms of transmission differ.

Cultural transmission may be vertical (from parents to children), oblique (from older generations to unrelated younger individuals, such as teachers), or horizontal (between peers). Transmission is also subject to biases, including content bias (some ideas are intrinsically more memorable or appealing), prestige bias (imitating successful or high-status individuals) and conformity bias (adopting the most common behaviour in a group).

Gene–culture coevolution

The best-known empirical example of gene–culture coevolution is the spread of lactase persistence alongside dairy farming. Cultural practices of keeping cattle and drinking milk into adulthood created an environment in which genetic variants allowing adults to digest lactose conferred a fitness advantage; these variants then rose in frequency in dairying populations in Europe, Africa and the Middle East. Other proposed cases include the yam cultivation practices of West Africa, which altered mosquito breeding habitats and thereby increased selection for the sickle-cell trait as protection against malaria, and the thesis that cooking changed the selection pressures on human dentition and gut anatomy.

Relation to other fields

Dual inheritance theory is closely related to evolutionary psychology, human behavioural ecology, niche construction theory and memetics, though its proponents have criticised memetics for lacking rigorous mathematical models and an adequate account of how cultural information is represented and transmitted. DIT models are used in anthropology, archaeology, economics and psychology to explain phenomena from the demographic transition to the evolution of cooperation and institutions.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether cultural change is sufficiently Darwinian to be modelled with population-genetic tools, noting that cultural traits are not discrete replicators and that intentional design plays a large role in cultural change. Proponents reply that the theory requires only that variation, transmission and selection occur, not that culture replicate with gene-like fidelity.
