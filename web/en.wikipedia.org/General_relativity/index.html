General relativity is a theory of gravitation developed by Albert Einstein between 1907 and 1915, published in its final form in 1916. According to general relativity, the observed gravitational attraction between masses results from the warping of space and time by those masses. It generalises special relativity and Newton's law of universal gravitation, providing a unified description of gravity as a geometric property of spacetime.

Overview

The theory grew from the equivalence principle: the observation that inertial and gravitational mass are identical, so that a person in free fall feels weightless, and no local experiment distinguishes a uniform gravitational field from uniform acceleration. Einstein's "happiest thought" (1907) led, with the mathematics of Riemannian geometry developed with Marcel Grossmann, to the field equations presented in November 1915:

Gμν + Λgμν = (8πG / c⁴) Tμν

These relate the curvature of spacetime (described by the Einstein tensor Gμν, from the metric tensor gμν) to the energy and momentum of matter and radiation (the stress–energy tensor Tμν). Freely falling bodies follow geodesics of the curved spacetime; as John Archibald Wheeler summarised, "spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve."

Predictions and tests

General relativity explained the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion (43 arc-seconds per century) that Newtonian theory could not. Its prediction that starlight grazing the Sun is deflected by 1.75 arc-seconds was famously confirmed by Eddington and Dyson's expeditions during the solar eclipse of 29 May 1919. Further confirmed predictions include gravitational time dilation (Pound–Rebka experiment; built into GPS corrections), gravitational lensing, frame-dragging, the expansion of the universe, black holes (imaged by the Event Horizon Telescope in 2019), and gravitational waves, directly detected by LIGO in 2015 from the merger of two black holes (event GW150914).

Applications and status

The theory underlies modern cosmology (the ΛCDM model), neutron-star and black-hole astrophysics, and satellite navigation. It remains the standard theory of gravity, while its incompatibility with quantum mechanics at the Planck scale motivates research into quantum gravity. Einstein's cosmological constant Λ, introduced in 1917 for a static universe and later removed, returned as the leading explanation of the accelerating expansion discovered in 1998, attributed to dark energy.
