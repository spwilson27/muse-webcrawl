South Sea Bubble

The South Sea Bubble was a British economic bubble that occurred in 1720, centred on the South Sea Company, a joint-stock company founded in 1711 to trade (mainly in slaves) with Spain's South American colonies and to manage part of the British national debt.

In 1720 the company proposed to take over most of the government's debt, offering shares to the public. A speculative frenzy, fed by insider dealing, bribery of politicians, and promotional publicity, drove the share price from about £128 in January 1720 to over £1,000 by June. Numerous copycat "bubble companies" — some with absurd or fraudulent schemes — also floated. The price then collapsed, falling back to around £100 by the end of the year and ruining thousands of investors, including Isaac Newton, who remarked that he could "calculate the movement of the stars, but not the madness of men".

Aftermath

A parliamentary inquiry exposed extensive corruption; the Chancellor of the Exchequer was imprisoned and several directors disgraced. Chancellor Robert Walpole restored confidence, restructured the debt, and effectively became Britain's first prime minister. The Bubble Act 1720, passed at the company's urging to suppress rival ventures, restricted joint-stock formation in Britain for a century. The episode remains a classic case study of speculative mania, alongside the Dutch tulip mania and the Mississippi Bubble in France, which collapsed the same year.
