The United States Sanitary Commission was a private relief agency created by federal legislation on 18 June 1861, signed by President Abraham Lincoln, to support sick and wounded soldiers of the US Army during the American Civil War. Modelled partly on the British Sanitary Commission of the Crimean War, it raised the equivalent of hundreds of millions of modern dollars in money and supplies and is regarded as a forerunner of the American Red Cross.

Origins

After the war began, women's aid societies and physicians pressed for a coordinated body to inspect camps, advise on hygiene and supplement the Army Medical Bureau. The Unitarian minister Henry Whitney Bellows led the campaign in Washington; despite initial resistance from the military bureaucracy, the Commission was authorized in June 1861. Landscape architect Frederick Law Olmsted became its general (executive) secretary and organized its operations.

Activities

Commission inspectors surveyed army camps for sanitation — ventilation, drainage, cooking, water supply — and pressed reforms that reduced disease, historically the war's biggest killer. Its network of some 7,000 local aid societies collected bandages, clothing, food and medical supplies, distributed through depots and hospital transports, including hospital ships and trains. The Commission ran soldiers' homes and lodges, a hospital directory so families could locate wounded men, a pension and back-pay claims service, and battlefield relief after engagements such as Gettysburg and Antietam. Its great fundraising Sanitary Fairs — beginning in Chicago in 1863 — raised millions.

Women were central to the organization: Mary Livermore and Jane Hoge directed the Northwestern Branch in Chicago, and thousands of local societies were run by women, expanding their public roles. A rival, the Christian Commission, focused on soldiers' spiritual welfare alongside relief.

Legacy

The Commission dissolved in 1866, its work documented in Charles Stillé's official history. Its professional, data-driven approach influenced American public health, hospital reform and organized philanthropy, and its personnel and model fed directly into the founding of the American Red Cross in 1881.
