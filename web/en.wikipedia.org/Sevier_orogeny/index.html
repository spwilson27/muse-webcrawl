Sevier orogeny

Thrust faulting of the Sevier belt

The Sevier orogeny was a mountain-building event that affected large areas of western North America from about 160 million years ago (Jurassic) to about 50 million years ago (Eocene). It is named for the Sevier River of central Utah. The orogeny produced a classic fold-and-thrust belt — a long, thin-skinned zone of thrust faults and folds running roughly north–south through modern Montana, Idaho, Wyoming, Utah, Nevada, and Arizona.

Causes

The Sevier orogeny resulted from the convergence of the Farallon Plate with the North American Plate along the western margin of the continent: compression transmitted into the plate interior stacked sedimentary strata eastward along gently dipping thrust faults above a basal décollement. Unlike the later Laramide orogeny, which involved basement-cored uplifts far inland, Sevier deformation chiefly involved the thick sedimentary cover of the Cordilleran miogeocline, which was shortened by as much as 100 kilometres or more in places.

Features

Major thrust systems include the Paris-Willard, Crawford, Absaroka, and Hogsback thrusts in the Idaho-Wyoming-Utah thrust belt, and the Keystone and other thrusts of southern Nevada. Loading by the thrust sheets depressed the crust to the east, creating a foreland basin — the Western Interior Basin — which was flooded by the Western Interior Seaway during the Cretaceous, and into which enormous volumes of synorogenic sediment (such as the Sevier-derived conglomerates) were shed.

The Sevier event overlapped in time and space with magmatism of the Sierra Nevada batholith to the west, and its easternmost deformation was progressively overprinted by the Laramide orogeny. Today the former thrust belt underlies much of the Basin and Range and Wasatch Front topography of the western United States.
