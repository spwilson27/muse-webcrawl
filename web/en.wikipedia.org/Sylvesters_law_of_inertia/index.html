Sylvester's law of inertia

Portrait of James Joseph Sylvester (1814–1897).

Sylvester's law of inertia is a theorem in matrix algebra about the invariants of the quadratic form associated with a real symmetric matrix under congruence transformations. It was discovered by James Joseph Sylvester in 1852.

Statement

Let A be a real symmetric n × n matrix. Any matrix of the form STAS with S invertible is called congruent to A. The theorem states that the number of positive entries, the number of negative entries, and the number of zero entries in the diagonal form of the quadratic form are the same for all congruent diagonal matrices. These counts form the inertia of A, often written as the triple (n+, n−, n0) — the numbers of positive, negative, and zero eigenvalues. The difference n+ − n− is the signature of the form.

Consequences

Two real symmetric matrices of the same order are congruent if and only if they have the same inertia; equivalently, every real quadratic form can be reduced by an invertible change of variables to a canonical form consisting only of terms +x2, −x2, and 0, with uniquely determined counts. The theorem is fundamental in the classification of quadratic forms, the definition of signature for bilinear forms (used, for example, in the metric signatures of differential geometry and relativity), and the theory of definiteness in optimization.

Proof ideas

A common proof proceeds by contradiction: if two diagonalizations gave different numbers of positive coefficients, one can construct a nonzero vector on which the quadratic form would be simultaneously positive and non-positive, using a dimension-counting argument on the spans of the relevant coordinate directions.
