WI-38 is a diploid human cell line composed of fibroblasts derived from lung tissue of a three-month-gestation female fetus. Developed by Leonard Hayflick and Paul Moorhead at the Wistar Institute in Philadelphia in 1962 — the "WI" denotes the institute and "38" the specimen number — the line became one of the most widely used normal human cell strains in biomedical research.

History

Working with fetal cells in the early 1960s, Hayflick demonstrated that normal human fibroblasts in culture divide only a finite number of times — about 50 population doublings — before entering senescence. This limit, now known as the Hayflick limit, overturned the prevailing belief that cells in culture were inherently immortal and founded the modern study of cellular aging.

Applications

Because WI-38 cells are free of contaminating viruses and have a normal (diploid) chromosome complement, they became a preferred substrate for producing human virus vaccines. Vaccines developed or manufactured using WI-38 include those against rubella, rabies, varicella, measles, and hepatitis A; Hayflick estimated that vaccines made in WI-38 have been given to more than a billion people. The rubella vaccine developed by Stanley Plotkin using WI-38 is credited with eliminating congenital rubella syndrome in many countries.
