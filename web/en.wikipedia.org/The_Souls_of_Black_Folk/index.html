The Souls of Black Folk is a 1903 work of American literature by W. E. B. Du Bois. A collection of fourteen essays blending sociology, history, autobiography, and fiction, it is a landmark of African-American literature and an early work in the field of sociology. It introduced into American thought concepts that remain central, above all "double consciousness" and the prediction that "the problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color-line."

Contents and themes

The famous first chapter, "Of Our Spiritual Strivings," describes double consciousness — "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others" — and the metaphor of the Veil separating Black Americans from the white world. Each chapter is headed by paired epigraphs: a stanza of European poetry and a bar of music from an African-American spiritual, which Du Bois celebrates in the closing chapter, "The Sorrow Songs," as America's great folk gift.

Other chapters include a history of the Freedmen's Bureau; "Of Mr. Booker T. Washington and Others," Du Bois's celebrated critique of Booker T. Washington's program of accommodation, demanding full civil and political rights; sociological studies of Black life in the rural South; an elegy for Du Bois's infant son, "Of the Passing of the First-Born"; a portrait of the minister and educator Alexander Crummell; and the short story "Of the Coming of John."

Background and reception

Several essays had appeared earlier in the Atlantic Monthly and other journals, growing out of Du Bois's scholarly work, including his Atlanta University studies and The Philadelphia Negro (1899). The book was reviewed respectfully in its day, sold steadily, and grew in stature through the twentieth century; it helped define the intellectual terms of the civil rights era and is now universally canonized, appearing in countless editions and syllabi.
