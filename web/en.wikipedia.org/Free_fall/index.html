Free fall

In Newtonian physics, free fall is any motion of a body where gravity is the only force acting upon it. In the atmosphere near Earth, objects also experience air resistance, so the idealised "free fall" of elementary mechanics strictly applies in a vacuum; in more general usage, and in aeronautics, the term also describes falling through air after jumping from an aircraft, before a parachute opens.

Physics

Neglecting air resistance, all objects near Earth's surface accelerate downward at the same rate, g ≈ 9.81 m/s², regardless of their mass — a fact famously associated with Galileo and demonstrated on the Moon by Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott with a hammer and a feather, which struck the surface simultaneously. Under general relativity, a freely falling body follows a geodesic and experiences no proper acceleration: an observer in free fall feels weightless, which underlies Einstein's equivalence principle and the experience of astronauts in orbit, whose spacecraft and occupants are in continuous free fall around the Earth.

With air resistance

In air, drag grows with speed until it balances weight, at which point a falling person reaches a terminal velocity of roughly 53 m/s (about 190 km/h) in a belly-to-earth position. Skydivers exploit drag by changing body position and deploying parachutes to reduce descent speed to a safe landing rate.
