Saint George

Saint George (Greek: Γεώργιος, Geṓrgios; died 23 April 303), also George of Lydda, was a Christian martyr traditionally said to have been a soldier in the Roman army. According to legend, he was a member of the Praetorian Guard of the emperor Diocletian and was executed for refusing to renounce his Christian faith during the Diocletianic Persecution. He became one of the most venerated saints in Christianity, famed in medieval romance as the dragon-slaying patron of soldiers, and is the patron saint of England, Georgia, Catalonia, and many other places and professions.

Life and martyrdom

Little is historically certain about George's life. The earliest accounts describe a soldier of high rank who confessed his faith before the emperor, distributed his wealth to the poor, endured elaborate tortures, and was beheaded. The principal shrine grew at Lydda (Lod) in Palestine, where a church over his reputed tomb became a major pilgrimage site; devotion to him spread rapidly through the Eastern church and, via returning Crusaders, throughout Western Europe.

The dragon legend

The famous story of George and the dragon, popularised by Jacobus de Voragine's Golden Legend (thirteenth century), tells how a dragon plagued a pagan city — variously located at Silene in Libya — demanding tribute of sheep and then of youths chosen by lot. When the lot fell on the king's daughter, George arrived, wounded the dragon with his lance, led it into the city with the princess's girdle, and slew it after the people agreed to be baptised. The tale is generally read as an allegory of the triumph of faith over evil, and echoes older myths of dragon-slaying heroes and of Perseus rescuing Andromeda — whose rescue was also localised near Lydda.

Patronage and legacy

George's red cross on a white field became the banner of England, where he displaced Edward the Confessor as patron saint during the later Middle Ages; St George's Day is celebrated on 23 April. He is likewise patron of Georgia — whose name in many languages derives from his — of Catalonia, where his day (Sant Jordi) is marked with gifts of books and roses, and of numerous cities, orders of chivalry, and military units. The Order of the Garter, England's highest order of chivalry, is dedicated to him. In the Islamic world, George is sometimes identified with the figure al-Khidr, and shared shrines in Palestine reflect the saint's cross-religious appeal.
