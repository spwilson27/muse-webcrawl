Nara period

The Nara period (奈良時代, Nara jidai) of Japanese history spans the years 710 to 794 CE. It begins with Empress Genmei's relocation of the capital to Heijō-kyō (present-day Nara) and ends when Emperor Kanmu moved the capital to Heian-kyō (Kyoto), beginning the Heian period.

Government and society

The period saw the consolidation of the ritsuryō state, modelled on Tang Chinese law and bureaucracy, with the Taihō and Yōrō codes organising administration, taxation and land allotment. Buddhism, promoted by the state, flourished under Emperor Shōmu, who commissioned provincial temples and the Great Buddha of Tōdai-ji (dedicated 752). Influence from Tang China also brought advances in writing, art and city planning; Heijō-kyō was laid out on a grid modelled on Chang'an.

Culture

Nara-period literature produced Japan's earliest surviving histories and poetry: the Kojiki (712), Nihon Shoki (720), the Fudoki provincial gazetteers, and the Man'yōshū anthology of some 4,500 poems. Treasures of the era are preserved in the Shōsō-in repository. Late in the period, the political power of Buddhist clergy — exemplified by the monk Dōkyō's bid for influence under Empress Shōtoku — alarmed the court and contributed to the decision to move the capital away from Nara.
