Aboriginal land rights in Australia

Key events: Wave Hill walk-off (1966), Woodward Royal Commission, Mabo decision (1992)

Key laws: Aboriginal Land Rights (Northern Territory) Act 1976; Native Title Act 1993

Aboriginal land rights in Australia are the rights and interests of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in land, recognised through legislation and court decisions after colonisation proceeded on the legal fiction of terra nullius — land belonging to no one — despite tens of thousands of years of Indigenous occupation.

History

Early campaigns included the 1963 Yirrkala bark petitions and the 1966 Wave Hill walk-off, in which Gurindji stockmen led by Vincent Lingiari struck for equal wages and return of their land. The protest led Prime Minister Gough Whitlam to pour soil into Lingiari's hands in 1975, symbolically returning part of the Gurindji country. The Woodward Royal Commission then recommended a land rights scheme, resulting in the Aboriginal Land Rights (Northern Territory) Act 1976, the first Australian law allowing claims based on traditional ownership.

Native title

In Mabo v Queensland (No 2) (1992), the High Court rejected terra nullius and recognised native title where Indigenous peoples maintain a continuing connection to land under traditional law and custom. Parliament responded with the Native Title Act 1993, creating a claims and future-acts regime. The 1996 Wik decision held that native title could coexist with pastoral leases, prompting controversial amendments in 1998. Land rights statutes in the states, and native title determinations, have together returned or recognised Indigenous interests over a substantial share of the continent, though claimants must meet demanding tests of continuity.
