Mencius (Chinese: 孟子, Mèngzǐ; 372–289 BC) was a Chinese Confucian philosopher, often regarded as the most important thinker of the tradition after Confucius himself. He was an itinerant sage of the Warring States period who advised rulers and systematised Confucius's teachings in the book that bears his name, the Mengzi, one of the Confucian Four Books.

Life

Born in the small state of Zou, Mencius is traditionally said to have been raised by his mother, whose celebrated diligence — she moved house three times to find a good environment for his education — made her an exemplar of motherhood in Chinese culture. He travelled among the courts of Qi, Liang and other states, urging rulers to govern benevolently, but met with limited success and spent his last years teaching disciples.

Philosophy

Mencius taught that human nature is inherently good: people possess four innate "sprouts" (duan) — compassion (the sprout of benevolence), shame (of righteousness), deference (of propriety) and approval/disapproval (of wisdom) — which, like seeds, need nourishment to mature into full virtues. His famous illustration is the instinctive alarm anyone feels on seeing a child about to fall into a well.

Political thought

In politics Mencius held that the welfare of the people comes first: "The people are the most important, the altars of soil and grain come next, and the ruler is least." A ruler who governs tyrannically forfeits the Mandate of Heaven, and Mencius defended the deposition — even killing — of a wicked king, arguing that such a man was a mere "brigand" rather than a true ruler. He advocated light taxes, care for the elderly, and ensuring every family had land enough to live (the well-field ideal).

Legacy

Through the Mengzi's inclusion in the Four Books by Zhu Xi in the Song dynasty, Mencius's view of human nature became orthodox in imperial China, in contrast to Xunzi's doctrine that nature is bad and must be reshaped by education.
