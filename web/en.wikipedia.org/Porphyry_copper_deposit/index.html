Porphyry copper deposit

Porphyry copper deposits are large, low-grade copper ore bodies formed by hydrothermal fluids associated with porphyritic intrusions — typically stocks of diorite to granodiorite composition emplaced above subduction zones. Despite copper grades commonly below 1 per cent, their enormous tonnages (hundreds of millions to billions of tonnes) make them the world's most important source of copper, supplying roughly 60–70 per cent of global production, together with significant molybdenum, gold, and silver.

Formation and characteristics

As a water-rich magma crystallises at depths of a few kilometres, exsolved magmatic fluids rise into the cooling stock and surrounding wall rocks, depositing disseminated and stockwork-vein sulphides — chiefly chalcopyrite and bornite — through a characteristic concentric pattern of hydrothermal alteration. A potassic core (biotite and K-feldspar) is typically surrounded by phyllic (quartz–sericite–pyrite), argillic, and propylitic zones, a zoning pattern formalised by Lowell and Guilbert in 1970 from the San Manuel–Kalamazoo deposit in Arizona. Near the surface, weathering can produce an enriched supergene blanket of chalcocite that greatly improves a deposit's economics.

Major deposits

The greatest porphyry districts lie along the Andes of Chile and Peru — Escondida, the largest copper mine in the world, Chuquicamata, and Collahuasi — and in the southwest United States (Morenci, Bingham Canyon), Indonesia (Grasberg), and the Philippines, Mongolia (Oyu Tolgoi), and Iran. Because grades are low, porphyries are mined by bulk open-pit or block-caving methods and are highly sensitive to copper prices and energy costs.
