Sub-replacement fertility

Sub-replacement fertility is a total fertility rate (TFR) that leads to each new generation being less populous than the older, previous one in a given area. The United Nations Population Division defines the replacement level as approximately 2.1 children per woman in developed countries (slightly higher where mortality is greater), taking into account the sex ratio at birth and deaths before the end of childbearing age.

Prevalence

Sub-replacement fertility is now the norm across most of the developed world and much of the developing world: by the early 2020s, countries containing more than half of the world's population — including China, India (at the national level), the United States, Brazil, and nearly all of Europe — had fertility rates below replacement. Some of the lowest rates are found in East Asia; South Korea's TFR fell below 0.8 in the early 2020s, the lowest recorded for any large country.

Causes

Demographers attribute the decline to the demographic transition and to factors including urbanisation, rising female education and labour-force participation, the cost of housing and child-rearing, delayed marriage and childbearing, secularisation, and widespread access to contraception.

Consequences

Where fertility remains below replacement for extended periods, populations eventually decline (absent immigration) and age structures shift toward older populations, raising old-age dependency ratios and straining pension and healthcare systems. Governments in countries such as Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Hungary, and China have adopted pro-natalist policies, with limited success in raising fertility to replacement levels.
