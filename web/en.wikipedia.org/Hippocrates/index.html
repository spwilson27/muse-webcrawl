Hippocrates

Hippocrates of Kos (c. 460 – c. 370 BC), also known as Hippocrates II, was a Greek physician of the classical age who is considered one of the most outstanding figures in the history of medicine. He is traditionally referred to as the "Father of Medicine" in recognition of his lasting contributions to the field, such as the use of prognosis and clinical observation, systematic categorization of diseases, and the theory of humoralism.

Life

Little is known with certainty about Hippocrates' life. He was born on the island of Kos, off the Anatolian coast, into a family of physician-priests (the Asclepiads) who claimed descent from the healing god Asclepius. He is mentioned by his contemporaries Plato and Aristotle, traveled widely through Greece practicing medicine, and is said to have died at Larissa in Thessaly at an advanced age.

Hippocratic medicine

Hippocrates is credited with being the first to believe that diseases were caused naturally, not because of superstition and gods. He separated the discipline of medicine from religion, arguing that disease was not a punishment inflicted by the gods but the product of environmental factors, diet, and living habits. His school emphasized careful clinical observation, detailed case histories (preserved in the Epidemics), and prognosis — predicting the course of an illness.

The Hippocratic Corpus

The Hippocratic Corpus (Corpus Hippocraticum) is a collection of some 60 medical texts in the Ionic Greek dialect, traditionally attributed to Hippocrates but almost certainly written by several authors over more than a century. It includes treatises such as On the Sacred Disease (which argues epilepsy has natural causes), Airs, Waters, Places (on environment and health), On Fractures, the Aphorisms, and the famous Hippocratic Oath — an ethical oath historically taken by physicians, pledging to practice medicine honestly and to do no harm.

Humoral theory

Hippocratic medicine is associated with the theory of the four humours — blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile — whose balance or imbalance in the body was held to explain health and disease. Though superseded by modern medicine, the framework dominated Western medical thinking for more than two thousand years.

Legacy

Hippocrates' insistence on observation, rational explanation and professional ethics laid foundations still honored in medicine; versions of the Hippocratic Oath are still sworn by graduating physicians today. Anatomical and clinical terms associated with him include the Hippocratic face (the sunken appearance of the dying) and digital clubbing, sometimes called "Hippocratic fingers."
