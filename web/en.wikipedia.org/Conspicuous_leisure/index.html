Conspicuous leisure

Conspicuous leisure is a concept introduced by the economist and sociologist Thorstein Veblen in The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899). It denotes the non-productive use of time for the purpose of displaying wealth and social status — the demonstrable ability to live without working.

Veblen's analysis

Veblen argued that in stratified societies, exemption from productive labour becomes an honorific mark of status. Conspicuous leisure is displayed through time-consuming pursuits that have no productive purpose but signal refinement: elaborate manners and etiquette, mastery of dead languages and obsolete knowledge, formal dress unsuited to work, and cultivated tastes. Wives and servants could perform "vicarious leisure" on behalf of the household head, demonstrating his wealth through their idleness and elaborate dress.

Relation to conspicuous consumption

Conspicuous leisure is the counterpart of Veblen's better-known concept of conspicuous consumption — the wasteful display of goods. Veblen held that as societies industrialise, consumption of goods tends to displace leisure as the primary status signal, because in anonymous urban settings goods are more visible than one's daily schedule. Modern commentators note countervailing trends, in which busyness itself becomes a status symbol.

Criticism

Critics have questioned the universality of Veblen's account and note that leisure pursuits may have intrinsic, charitable or communal value, not merely signalling functions.
