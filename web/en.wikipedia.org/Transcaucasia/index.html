Transcaucasia

The South Caucasus (Transcaucasia), south of the Greater Caucasus range.

Transcaucasia, also known as the South Caucasus, is a geographical region on the border of Eastern Europe and Western Asia, south of the Greater Caucasus Mountains. It roughly corresponds to the territory of the modern-day republics of Armenia, Georgia, and Azerbaijan, between the Black Sea to the west and the Caspian Sea to the east.

The name "Transcaucasia" is a Latin rendering of the Russian Zakavkazye, meaning "[the land] beyond the Caucasus," reflecting the perspective of the Russian Empire, which ruled the region from the early 19th century until 1917–18.

History

The region has been a crossroads of empires—Persian, Roman, Byzantine, Arab, Mongol, Ottoman, and Russian—and is home to some of the world's oldest Christian states: Armenia adopted Christianity in 301 AD and Georgia followed in the 4th century. Azerbaijan, in the east, is a predominantly Shia Muslim, Turkic-speaking republic closely tied historically to Persia.

After the Russian Empire collapsed in 1917, Armenia, Georgia, and Azerbaijan briefly formed the joint Transcaucasian Democratic Federative Republic in 1918 before declaring separate independence. By 1922 all three had been incorporated into the Soviet Union as the Transcaucasian Socialist Federative Soviet Republic, which lasted until 1936, when it was divided into three separate Soviet republics. All three regained independence in 1991.

Geography

Transcaucasia is bounded by the Greater Caucasus to the north and the Lesser Caucasus highlands to the south. The Kura River drains the central lowlands eastward to the Caspian Sea, while the Rioni drains westward to the Black Sea. The region is known for extreme geographic diversity, from the subtropical Colchian coast of Georgia to the semi-desert of the Azerbaijani lowlands and the alpine Armenian plateau.

Modern politics

Since 1991 the region has been marked by conflicts over Nagorno-Karabakh (between Armenia and Azerbaijan), Abkhazia, and South Ossetia (in Georgia, sharply escalating in the 2008 Russo-Georgian War). The South Caucasus is also a critical energy corridor, carrying Caspian oil and gas westward through pipelines such as the Baku–Tbilisi–Ceyhan pipeline, bypassing Russia and Iran.
