Black body

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A black body or blackbody is an idealized physical body that absorbs all electromagnetic radiation that falls on it, regardless of frequency or angle of incidence. In thermal equilibrium it emits radiation — called black-body radiation — whose spectrum depends only on the body's temperature, not on its composition. The term was introduced by Gustav Kirchhoff in 1860.

Black-body radiation

As temperature increases, a black body glows first dull red, then orange, white and blue-white; the peak of the emission spectrum shifts to shorter wavelengths according to Wien's displacement law, and the total emitted power per unit area grows with the fourth power of absolute temperature, as described by the Stefan–Boltzmann law. Stars, including the Sun, approximate black-body emitters, and the cosmic microwave background is the most precise natural black-body spectrum known.

Planck's law and quantum theory

Classical physics failed to predict the observed spectrum (the "ultraviolet catastrophe"). In 1900 Max Planck derived the correct spectrum — Planck's law — by postulating that energy is exchanged in discrete quanta, an insight that launched quantum mechanics. Real materials emit a fraction of black-body radiation characterised by their emissivity, a ratio between 0 and 1; bodies that are not black are often modelled as "grey bodies".

Approximations

A practical approximation to a black body is a small hole in an insulated cavity: radiation entering the hole is almost completely absorbed, and the radiation emerging is characteristic of the cavity's temperature. Such cavity radiators are used as calibration sources in radiometry and infrared thermometry.
