Tychonic system

The Tychonic system (or Tychonian system) is a model of the Solar System published by the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe in 1588 in his book De Mundi Aetherei Recentioribus Phaenomenis. It combines what Tycho considered the mathematical benefits of the Copernican system with the philosophical and physical benefits of the Ptolemaic system: Earth remains stationary at the center of the universe, the Sun and Moon orbit Earth, but the other five known planets orbit the Sun.

Rationale

Tycho rejected heliocentrism partly because he could detect no stellar parallax — if Earth moved, the stars should appear to shift over the year, and the implied distances and sizes of the stars seemed absurd to him — and partly because he believed a moving Earth contradicted physics and scripture. His hybrid model reproduced the appearances, including the phases of Venus observed later by Galileo, since Venus orbits the Sun in the Tychonic scheme as well. Geometrically, the Tychonic and Copernican systems are equivalent descriptions of planetary positions relative to Earth, differing in the choice of reference point.

Influence

The Tychonic system became the leading alternative to Copernicanism in the late 16th and early 17th centuries, favored by many Jesuit astronomers and by those who rejected Earth's motion on religious or physical grounds. It was effectively displaced only after Johannes Kepler's laws of planetary motion, Isaac Newton's theory of gravitation, and the eventual detection of stellar aberration (1729) and stellar parallax (1838) established Earth's motion on firm empirical ground.
