Ion trap

An ion trap is a combination of electric and/or magnetic fields used to capture charged particles — known as ions — often in a defined region for experiments in physics and chemistry. Ion traps may be used as precision spectrometers, mass spectrometers, atomic clocks, and as the hardware platform of trapped-ion quantum computers.

Types

The Penning trap confines ions with a static magnetic field plus an electrostatic quadrupole field, enabling extremely long storage times; it is used for precision measurements of fundamental constants and antimatter studies. The Paul trap (quadrupole ion trap) uses a radio-frequency oscillating field to create a time-averaged confining potential and underlies the quadrupole ion trap mass analyser. Linear Paul traps confine ions along an axis in a two-dimensional RF field and are the standard geometry for quantum computing with strings of ions.

Wolfgang Paul and Hans Georg Dehmelt received half of the 1989 Nobel Prize in Physics for developing ion trap techniques.

Applications

In mass spectrometry, ion traps store ions, select them by mass-to-charge ratio, and eject them for detection, enabling multiple stages of tandem mass spectrometry in one device. In quantum information, individually laser-cooled ions in Paul traps serve as qubits with long coherence times and high-fidelity gates; commercial systems from companies such as IonQ and Quantinuum use this architecture. Ion traps also underpin the most accurate optical atomic clocks and tests of fundamental symmetries.

Operation

Trapped ions are typically cooled — by laser cooling to the motional ground state for precision work — manipulated with lasers or microwaves, and read out by state-dependent fluorescence. Because Earnshaw's theorem forbids confinement by static electric fields alone in all directions, traps rely either on magnetic forces (Penning) or time-varying fields (Paul) to achieve three-dimensional confinement.
