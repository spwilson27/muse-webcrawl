Gender performativity

Gender performativity is a concept developed by the philosopher Judith Butler, most fully in their 1990 book Gender Trouble and refined in Bodies That Matter (1993). It holds that gender is not a fixed inner essence that a person expresses, but something constituted — brought into being — through the repeated, socially regulated performance of acts, gestures, and styles over time.

Theory

Drawing on J. L. Austin's speech-act theory of performatives, phenomenology, and poststructuralism, Butler argues that gendered behaviour cites and reiterates cultural norms; the repetition creates the illusion of an abiding gendered self behind the acts. "Performativity" is distinguished from theatrical "performance": it is not a voluntary act by a pre-existing subject, but a compulsory repetition of norms that produces the subject itself. In Gender Trouble, Butler proposed drag as an example that exposes the imitative structure of all gender, revealing gender as a kind of persistent impersonation that passes as the real.

Reception and criticism

The theory became central to queer theory and feminist philosophy. Critics have argued it underplays material embodiment (a charge Butler addressed in Bodies That Matter), is overly linguistic or deterministic, or is difficult to reconcile with the lived experience of gender identity. Butler further developed related ideas in works on precarity, assembly, and nonviolence.
