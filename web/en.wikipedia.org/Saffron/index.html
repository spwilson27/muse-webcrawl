Saffron

Saffron (pronounced SAF-rən) is a spice derived from the flower of Crocus sativus, commonly known as the "saffron crocus". The vivid crimson stigma and styles, called threads, are collected and dried for use mainly as a seasoning and colouring agent in food. The saffron crocus was slowly propagated throughout much of Eurasia and was later brought to parts of North Africa, North America, and Oceania.

Saffron's taste and iodoform-like or hay-like fragrance result from the phytochemicals picrocrocin and safranal. It also contains a carotenoid pigment, crocin, which imparts a rich golden-yellow hue to dishes and textiles. Saffron is the world's most expensive spice by weight, because each flower produces only three threads and roughly 150,000 flowers are needed to make one kilogram of dried saffron, all harvested by hand.

History and production

Saffron was documented in Assyrian botanical references of the 7th century BC and has been traded and used for over four millennia. Iran produces about 90 percent of the world's saffron, with other major producers including Spain, India (Kashmir), Greece, Afghanistan, and Morocco. Beyond cuisine — where it flavours dishes such as paella, biryani, and risotto alla milanese — saffron has a long history in traditional medicine, dyeing, and perfumery.
