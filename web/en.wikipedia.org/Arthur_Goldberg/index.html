Arthur Goldberg

Arthur Joseph Goldberg (August 8, 1908 – January 19, 1990) was an American statesman and jurist who served as United States Secretary of Labor (1961–1962), as an associate justice of the Supreme Court of the United States (1962–1965), and as US Ambassador to the United Nations (1965–1968).

Career

Born to Russian Jewish immigrant parents in Chicago, Goldberg became a leading labour lawyer, serving as general counsel of the Congress of Industrial Organizations and of the United Steelworkers, and helping negotiate the AFL–CIO merger of 1955. President Kennedy appointed him Secretary of Labor in 1961 and nominated him to the Supreme Court in 1962, succeeding Felix Frankfurter. On the Warren Court he was a reliable liberal vote; his concurrence in Griswold v. Connecticut (1965), grounding a right to privacy partly in the Ninth Amendment, is especially remembered, as was his majority opinion in Escobedo v. Illinois (1964).

In 1965 President Lyndon B. Johnson persuaded Goldberg to resign from the Court to succeed the late Adlai Stevenson as Ambassador to the United Nations, where he helped draft UN Security Council Resolution 242 after the Six-Day War. He later regretted leaving the Court. Goldberg ran unsuccessfully for Governor of New York in 1970, losing to Nelson Rockefeller, and served as ambassador-at-large under President Carter, leading the US delegation to the 1977 Belgrade CSCE conference.
