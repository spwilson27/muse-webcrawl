Renaissance sculpture is the sculpture of the European Renaissance, roughly 1400 to 1600, beginning in Italy and marked by a revival of classical Greco-Roman forms, a new naturalism grounded in the study of anatomy and perspective, and the emergence of the free-standing nude and the monumental civic statue. Sculptors became celebrated individual artists rather than anonymous craftsmen, and sculpture was regarded by many contemporaries as the most demanding of the visual arts.

Origins

The conventional starting point is the competition of 1401 for the bronze doors of the Florence Baptistery, won by Lorenzo Ghiberti over Filippo Brunelleschi. Ghiberti's second set of doors, the Gates of Paradise (1425–1452), used perspective and the low, painterly relief called rilievo schiacciato to create deep illusionistic scenes. Nicola Pisano had anticipated classical revival in the previous century, drawing on Roman sarcophagi.

Donatello and the Early Renaissance

Donatello transformed the art: his bronze David (c. 1440s) was the first free-standing nude statue since antiquity; his equestrian monument to Gattamelata in Padua revived the Roman imperial type; and his marble and wood figures display an unprecedented psychological intensity and knowledge of anatomy. Andrea del Verrocchio, Luca della Robbia (famed for glazed terracotta), and Desiderio da Settignano carried Florentine sculpture to a high polish of grace and naturalism.

High Renaissance and Mannerism

Michelangelo dominated the High Renaissance: the Pietà (1498–99), the colossal marble David (1501–04) in Florence, the Moses and the Medici Chapel figures in the New Sacristy. His later, deliberately unfinished Slaves introduced a heroic, twisting figure — the figura serpentinata — taken up by Mannerist sculptors such as Giambologna, whose Rape of the Sabine Women was designed to be read from every angle. Benvenuto Cellini, goldsmith and author of a famous autobiography, produced the bronze Perseus for Florence and virtuoso works for the French court.

Themes and technique

Renaissance sculptors worked in marble, bronze (cast by the lost-wax method), wood and terracotta. Anatomical study, sometimes including dissection, informed the rendering of the body; contrapposto revived classical balance. Subjects ranged from biblical figures and saints to portraits, tombs, fountains and, increasingly, classical mythology for private patrons such as the Medici.
