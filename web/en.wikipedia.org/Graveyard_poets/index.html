Graveyard poets

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Graveyard Poets, also known as the Churchyard Poets or the Graveyard School, were a group of mid-18th-century British poets whose work is characterised by melancholy meditation on mortality, death, and the transience of human life, often set in churchyards and among ruins. They are generally regarded as precursors of the Romantic movement and an influence on the later Gothic revival.

Characteristics

Graveyard poetry dwells on tombs, skulls, epitaphs, and the levelling power of death, which reduces kings and peasants alike to dust. Written typically in blank verse or heroic couplets, the poems favour nocturnal settings, solitude, and contemplation of ruins. The mood is one of gloomy reflection, though usually framed within orthodox Christian consolation: earthly glory fades, but the soul endures.

Principal poets and works

The school is often traced to Thomas Parnell's A Night-Piece on Death (1722) and Edward Young's Night-Thoughts (1742–1745), a long blank-verse meditation that became a European bestseller. Robert Blair's The Grave (1743) is the poem from which the school takes its name. The most famous work associated with the movement is Thomas Gray's Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard (1751), whose lines on the "rude forefathers of the hamlet" and the paths of glory leading "but to the grave" remain among the best known in English poetry. Other associated figures include James Hervey, whose prose Meditations among the Tombs (1746) was enormously popular, and the Scots poet Robert Blair's contemporaries such as James Thomson in his more sombre passages.

Influence

The graveyard mode fed directly into the sensibility of Romanticism, the Gothic novel, and later meditative poetry. Its imagery of ruins and moonlit churchyards became a stock feature of European literature, influencing writers from Wordsworth and Coleridge to the German Sturm und Drang movement.
