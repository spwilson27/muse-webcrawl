The Brothers Karamazov


Cover of the first edition (1880).

The Brothers Karamazov (Russian: Братья Карамазовы, Brat'ya Karamazovy) is the final novel by Russian author Fyodor Dostoevsky. Dostoevsky spent nearly two years writing it; it was published as a serial in The Russian Messenger from January 1879 to November 1880, and he died less than four months after its publication. Set in 19th-century Russia, the novel is a passionate work of philosophical debate, morality, faith, doubt, and free will, framed by the story of the murder of the landowner Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov and the respective roles of his three sons — Dmitri, Ivan, and Alexei — in the crime.

Plot summary

Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov, a vulgar and miserly landowner, has three sons by two marriages: the passionate army officer Dmitri, the intellectual Ivan, and the pious novice Alexei (Alyosha), as well as a neglected illegitimate son, the epileptic lackey Smerdyakov. Dmitri and his father quarrel violently over an inheritance and over the former serf woman Grushenka. When Fyodor Pavlovich is found murdered, suspicion falls on Dmitri, who is arrested and tried. Ivan is tormented by his doctrine that "if there is no immortality, everything is permitted", and in a famous chapter he relates to Alyosha the prose poem of the Grand Inquisitor. After Smerdyakov confesses to Ivan and hangs himself, Dmitri is nevertheless convicted by the jury and sentenced to Siberia; the novel ends with Alyosha encouraging the schoolboys who have gathered around the stone in memory of the dead child Ilyusha.

Themes

The novel explores the existence of God, the problem of evil and innocent suffering, free will versus moral responsibility, faith against reason, and the spiritual condition of Russia. Its arguments are distributed among the characters rather than resolved by the narrator, making it a prime example of the "polyphonic" novel described by critic Mikhail Bakhtin.

Influence and reception

The Brothers Karamazov is widely considered one of the supreme achievements in world literature. It influenced thinkers including Freud, Einstein, Wittgenstein, and Pope Benedict XVI; Freud called it "the most magnificent novel ever written". The Grand Inquisitor chapter is frequently anthologized and studied on its own.
