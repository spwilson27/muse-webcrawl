Rare Earth hypothesis

In planetary astronomy and astrobiology, the Rare Earth hypothesis argues that the origin of life and the evolution of biological complexity such as sexually reproducing, multicellular organisms on Earth (and, subsequently, human intelligence) required an improbable combination of astrophysical and geological events and circumstances. The hypothesis was articulated by palaeontologist Peter D. Ward and astronomer Donald E. Brownlee in their book Rare Earth: Why Complex Life Is Uncommon in the Universe (2000).

Argument

Ward and Brownlee contend that microbial life may be common in the universe, but complex life requires a long chain of favourable conditions: a terrestrial planet in the galactic and circumstellar habitable zones, a large moon stabilising axial tilt, plate tectonics and a magnetic field, a Jupiter-like planet shielding the inner system from comets, a "just right" atmosphere and ocean chemistry, and the avoidance of sterilising catastrophes over billions of years. The coincidence of all these factors, they argue, makes complex life extremely rare — an answer to the Fermi paradox.

Criticism

Critics respond that many of the claimed requirements may not be necessary, that Earth-like conditions may be more common than assumed — exoplanet surveys show rocky planets in habitable zones are plentiful — and that the argument is hard to test statistically from a sample of one. The hypothesis stands in opposition to the principle of mediocrity, which expects Earth to be a typical rocky planet.
