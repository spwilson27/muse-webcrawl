The theory of relativity usually encompasses two interrelated physics theories by Albert Einstein: special relativity and general relativity, proposed and published in 1905 and 1915, respectively. Special relativity applies to all physical phenomena in the absence of gravity. General relativity explains the law of gravitation and its relation to the forces of nature. It applied to the cosmological and astrophysical realm, including astronomy.

Special relativity

Special relativity is based on two postulates: the laws of physics are invariant in all inertial frames of reference, and the speed of light in vacuum is the same for all observers, regardless of the motion of the light source. Its consequences include the relativity of simultaneity, time dilation, length contraction, and the equivalence of mass and energy expressed by the famous equation E = mc². It replaced the Newtonian notions of absolute space and absolute time with a unified spacetime, formalised geometrically by Hermann Minkowski.

General relativity

General relativity generalises special relativity and refines Newton's law of universal gravitation, providing a unified description of gravity as a geometric property of space and time, or four-dimensional spacetime. In particular, the curvature of spacetime is directly related to the energy and momentum of whatever matter and radiation are present. The theory's predictions — including the bending of light by the Sun, the perihelion precession of Mercury, gravitational redshift, and gravitational waves — have been confirmed by observations and experiments.

Impact

The theory transformed theoretical physics and astronomy during the 20th century, superseding a 200-year-old theory of mechanics created primarily by Isaac Newton. It introduced concepts including 4-dimensional spacetime, the relativity of simultaneity, kinematic and gravitational time dilation, and length contraction, and underlies technologies such as GPS, which must correct for relativistic effects to remain accurate.
