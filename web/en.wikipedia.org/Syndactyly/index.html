Syndactyly

A hand with syndactyly (webbed fingers).

Syndactyly is a condition wherein two or more digits are fused together. It occurs normally in some mammals, such as the siamang and diprotodont marsupials, but is an uncommon congenital condition in humans. The term comes from Greek syn ("together") and daktylos ("finger"). Syndactyly is one of the most common congenital limb differences, occurring in roughly 1 in 2,000–3,000 live births, and more often in males.

Classification

Syndactyly may be simple (only soft tissue joined) or complex (bones of adjacent digits also fused); complete (fusion extends to the fingertips) or incomplete (partial fusion); and complicated when associated with extra bones or other abnormalities. The most common pattern is fusion of the middle and ring fingers, or of the second and third toes — the latter often requiring no treatment.

Causes

During normal development the digits separate by programmed cell death (apoptosis) in the tissue between them, around the sixth to eighth week of gestation; syndactyly results when this separation fails. It may occur as an isolated trait — sometimes inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern — or as part of syndromes such as Apert syndrome, Down syndrome, Poland syndrome, and Holt–Oram syndrome. Several genetic loci and genes (including HOXD13) have been associated with different syndactyly types.

Treatment

When fusion impairs function or threatens uneven growth — especially involving the thumb or index finger, or border digits of unequal length — surgical separation is performed, typically in infancy or early childhood. Surgery uses zig-zag incisions and often skin grafts to reconstruct the web space; complex cases may need staged procedures.
