The Gregorian calendar is the calendar used in most parts of the world today. It went into effect in October 1582 following the papal bull Inter gravissimas issued by Pope Gregory XIII, which introduced it as a modification of, and replacement for, the Julian calendar. The principal change was to space leap years differently so as to make the average calendar year 365.2425 days long, more closely approximating the 365.2422-day tropical year determined by the Earth's revolution around the Sun.

The reform

The Julian calendar drifted by about 11 minutes per year, which by 1582 had displaced the date of the spring equinox by ten days, disturbing the computation of Easter. The Gregorian reform, designed by the physician Aloysius Lilius and refined by Christopher Clavius, removed ten days from October 1582 and ruled that century years would be leap years only if divisible by 400.

Adoption

Catholic countries such as Spain, Portugal and Italy adopted the calendar immediately in 1582. Protestant and Orthodox states followed much later: Britain and its colonies in 1752, Russia in 1918 and Greece in 1923. The calendar is now the international standard for civil purposes (ISO 8601).

Structure

The calendar consists of twelve months of 28 to 31 days, organized in a 400-year cycle of exactly 146,097 days — a whole number of weeks — so the calendar repeats perfectly every 400 years. Its mean error is about one day in 3,030 years relative to the mean solar year.
