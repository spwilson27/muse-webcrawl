Fleet marriage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Fleet marriage was a common form of clandestine marriage in England before the Marriage Act 1753 came into force. Such marriages were conducted, for fees, by clergymen — often imprisoned debtors or disgraced ministers — within the precincts of the Fleet Prison in London and the surrounding "Rules of the Fleet," an area subject to special jurisdiction where normal church discipline was difficult to enforce.

Background

Before 1754, a marriage in England required only the exchange of consent before witnesses in the presence of a clergyman in holy orders; banns, licences, parental consent, and a church ceremony were canonical expectations but irregularities did not invalidate the union. Fleet parsons exploited this: couples could marry quickly, secretly, and cheaply, at any hour, without questions. At the practice's height in the 1740s, several thousand couples a year married in the Fleet area — by some estimates nearly half of all London marriages.

Motives and abuses

Couples sought Fleet marriages for privacy, haste (including pregnancy), evasion of parental opposition, or concealment from creditors. The trade also enabled bigamy, forced marriages of heiresses, and fraud; registers were sometimes forged or backdated. "Fleet parsons" touted for business in the streets around the prison, and marriage houses and taverns in the Rules specialised in the trade, keeping registers that survive today as genealogical sources.

Abolition

The scandal of clandestine marriages led to Lord Hardwicke's Marriage Act 1753, effective 25 March 1754, which required marriages in England and Wales (Jews and Quakers excepted) to follow the publication of banns or a licence, in a church, with a register — ending the Fleet marriage trade overnight. Couples seeking to evade the new rules later crossed into Scotland, where Gretna Green became famous.
