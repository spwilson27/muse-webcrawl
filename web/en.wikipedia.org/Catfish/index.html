Catfish (order Siluriformes) are a diverse group of ray-finned fish named for their prominent barbels, which resemble a cat's whiskers. They range from the tiny parasitic candiru of the Amazon to the Mekong giant catfish, which can reach 3 metres and 300 kg. Catfish occur on every continent except Antarctica, mostly in fresh water, and make up roughly one in twenty of all vertebrate species.

Characteristics

Unlike most fish, catfish lack scales; their skin is naked or covered in bony plates. They have up to four pairs of barbels packed with taste buds — some species have taste buds over their entire bodies — and an acute sense of smell and hearing enhanced by the Weberian apparatus connecting the swim bladder to the inner ear. Many species bear sharp, sometimes venomous, spines on the dorsal and pectoral fins. Several groups (electric catfish, some pimelodids) have unusual specialisations; the walking catfish can move over land using its pectoral fins.

Ecology and behaviour

Most catfish are bottom-dwelling nocturnal feeders, though some are mid-water predators, algae grazers or parasites. Parental care is unusually common among fish: males of many species guard eggs and fry, and sea catfish males mouthbrood their eggs. Armoured catfish (loricariids), including the familiar aquarium plecos, scrape algae and wood; their bristlenose species are staples of the aquarium trade.

Importance to humans

Catfish are farmed worldwide — channel catfish in the United States, pangasius (basa/tra) in Vietnam, and clarias in Africa and Asia — forming a major source of inexpensive protein. In the southern US, fried catfish is a cultural staple. Conversely, introduced walking catfish and other species have become invasive in Florida and elsewhere. The word "catfish" has also acquired a modern slang meaning: a person who adopts a false online identity, popularised by the 2010 documentary Catfish.
