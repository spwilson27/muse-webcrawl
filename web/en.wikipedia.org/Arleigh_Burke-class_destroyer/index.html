Arleigh Burke-class destroyer

Type: Guided-missile destroyer
Operator: United States Navy
Lead ship: USS Arleigh Burke (DDG 51), commissioned 1991
Displacement: about 9,200–9,700 tons (later flights)
Length: 505–509.5 ft (153.9–155.3 m)
Armament: Mk 41 VLS (90 or 96 cells), 5-inch gun, torpedoes, CIWS

The Arleigh Burke class of guided-missile destroyers (DDGs) is a United States Navy class of destroyer built around the Aegis Combat System and the SPY-1D multifunction phased-array radar. The class was named for Admiral Arleigh Burke, the most famous American destroyer officer of World War II. It is the most numerous class of American surface combatants in production, with more than seventy ships delivered and further vessels building.

Development

The class was designed in the 1980s to provide a more affordable multi-mission complement to the Aegis cruisers, with lessons from the Falklands War and the attack on USS Stark incorporated into an all-steel hull and extensive survivability features. The lead ship, USS Arleigh Burke (DDG 51), was commissioned on 4 July 1991. Ships are built by Bath Iron Works in Maine and Huntington Ingalls Industries in Mississippi.

Flights and capabilities

Successive "Flights" improved the design: Flight II added improved electronics; Flight IIA (from DDG 79) added a dual helicopter hangar, lengthened hull, and 96 vertical launching system cells; Flight III (from DDG 125) introduces the SPY-6 air and missile defense radar for ballistic missile defence. The ships carry Standard, ESSM, Tomahawk, and ASROC missiles in their Mk 41 launchers, a 5-inch gun, and embarked MH-60 helicopters.

Service

Arleigh Burke-class destroyers have conducted Tomahawk strike operations in Iraq, the Balkans, Libya, and Syria, performed ballistic missile defence patrols, and escorted carrier strike groups worldwide. USS Cole (DDG 67) was severely damaged by a suicide boat bombing in Aden in 2000, killing seventeen sailors, and was repaired and returned to service. In 2017, the collisions of USS Fitzgerald and USS John S. McCain with merchant ships prompted fleet-wide reviews of seamanship and training.
