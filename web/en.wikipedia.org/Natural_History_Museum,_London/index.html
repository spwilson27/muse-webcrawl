The Natural History Museum in London is a museum of natural history on Cromwell Road in South Kensington, exhibiting a vast range of specimens from the life and earth sciences. It is one of three major museums on Exhibition Road, alongside the Science Museum and the Victoria and Albert Museum, and is home to some 80 million items, including specimens collected by Charles Darwin.

History

The museum's core collection originated with the physician Hans Sloane, whose holdings formed the basis of the British Museum in 1753. As the natural history collections outgrew Bloomsbury, the superintendent Richard Owen campaigned for a separate building. Alfred Waterhouse designed the famous Romanesque building, notable for its terracotta façade decorated with reliefs of animals, which opened in 1881. The museum became independent of the British Museum in law in 1963 and formally in 1992.

Collections and galleries

The museum is renowned for its dinosaur skeletons; for many years a cast of a Diplodocus nicknamed "Dippy" stood in the central Hintze Hall, replaced in 2017 by the skeleton of a blue whale named Hope. Galleries cover evolution, human biology, minerals, volcanoes and earthquakes, and British wildlife. The Darwin Centre houses spirit collections (specimens preserved in alcohol) and research facilities.

Research

Several hundred scientists work at the museum on taxonomy, biodiversity, and planetary science. The museum is a non-departmental public body sponsored by the Department for Culture, Media and Sport, and admission is free.
