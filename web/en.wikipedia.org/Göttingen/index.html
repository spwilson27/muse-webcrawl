Göttingen (German: [ˈɡœtɪŋən]) is a university town in Lower Saxony, Germany, on the Leine river. First mentioned in 953 as the village of Gutingi, it received town rights around 1230 and was a member of the Hanseatic League. Göttingen is best known worldwide for its university, founded in 1734, which made the town one of the great centres of mathematics and physics.

The university

The University of Göttingen (Georg August University) was founded by King George II of Great Britain, who was also Elector of Hanover. Its scholars have included Carl Friedrich Gauss, Bernhard Riemann, David Hilbert, Emmy Noether, Max Born, Werner Heisenberg and Otto Hahn; more than 40 Nobel Prize winners have studied or taught in Göttingen. In 1937 the dismissal of Jewish and dissenting academics under the Nazis — following the 1933 purge — ended the town's golden age in mathematics, an episode symbolised by Hilbert's reported remark that mathematics in Göttingen no longer existed.

The town

The old town, encircled by a medieval wall, contains half-timbered houses and the Gänseliesel fountain in front of the old town hall — the city's emblem, traditionally kissed by newly graduated doctoral students. Göttingen was largely undamaged in the Second World War and today hosts several Max Planck Institutes and a large student population.

Transport

Göttingen lies on the Hanover–Würzburg high-speed railway and the A7 motorway, roughly midway between Hanover and Kassel.
