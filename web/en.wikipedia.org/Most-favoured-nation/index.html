Most-favoured-nation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In international economic relations and international politics, most-favoured-nation (MFN) is a status or level of treatment accorded by one state to another in international trade. The term means the country which is the recipient of this treatment must nominally receive equal advantages as the most favoured nation given by the country granting such treatment. In effect, a country that has been accorded MFN status may not be treated less advantageously than any other country with MFN status by the promising country.

In trade law

Under the rules of the World Trade Organization (WTO), a member country cannot discriminate between its trade partners: if a special favour (such as a lower customs duty rate) is granted to one trade partner, the same must be granted to all other WTO members. This principle is enshrined in Article I of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT 1947 and 1994) for goods, Article II of the GATS for services, and Article 4 of the TRIPS Agreement for intellectual property.

Exceptions

Important exceptions permit preferential treatment for developing countries, regional free-trade areas and customs unions (such as the European Union), and certain measures under the WTO's "enabling clause." A notable historical exception was the United States' Jackson–Vanik amendment, which conditioned MFN for non-market economies on freedom of emigration. In United States law, MFN status was renamed "permanent normal trade relations" (PNTR) in 1998.
