Langar (Sikhism)

Langar (Punjabi: ਲੰਗਰ, "kitchen") is the community kitchen of a gurdwara, the Sikh place of worship, where free vegetarian meals are served to all visitors without distinction of religion, caste, gender, economic status, or ethnicity. It is a central institution of Sikhism, expressing the principles of equality, humility, and seva (selfless service).

History

The institution is traditionally traced to Guru Nanak, who, according to Sikh tradition, used money given to him by his father to feed hungry holy men — the episode of the Sacha Sauda ("true bargain"). A celebrated story tells how Guru Angad's wife, Mata Khivi, became famed for her service in the langar. Under Guru Amar Das the practice was formalised: all visitors, including the Mughal emperor Akbar according to tradition, were required to sit on the floor and eat langar before audience with the Guru, deliberately breaking caste distinctions around food.

Practice

Meals are prepared and served by volunteers, and the food and labour are funded by donations from the congregation. Diners sit together in rows on the floor (pangat) and are served simple vegetarian food so that no one is excluded by dietary law. The langar at the Golden Temple in Amritsar is often described as the world's largest free kitchen, serving on the order of 50,000 people a day and up to 100,000 on festival days. Sikh communities also operate langars as disaster relief — serving free meals at refugee camps, after earthquakes and floods, and through charities such as Khalsa Aid.
