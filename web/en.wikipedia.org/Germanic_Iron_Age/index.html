Germanic Iron Age

The Germanic Iron Age is the name given by archaeologists to the period of northern European prehistory from about 400 BC to about AD 800 in Scandinavia and northern Germany, corresponding to the later part of the Iron Age among the Germanic peoples. In Scandinavian periodisation it follows the Pre-Roman Iron Age and is conventionally divided into an Early Germanic Iron Age (c. AD 1–400) and a Late Germanic Iron Age (c. AD 400–800), the latter overlapping with what in Sweden is called the Vendel Period and ending where the Viking Age begins.

Early Germanic Iron Age

The earlier phase coincides with the flourishing and collapse of the Roman Empire, whose frontier along the Rhine and Danube transformed Germanic societies. Trade and military service brought Roman goods — coins, glassware, bronze vessels and weapons — deep into Scandinavia, and Germanic war leaders returned with wealth and Roman-style retinues. Enormous ritual deposits of captured war gear, such as those at Illerup Ådal, Thorsberg and Nydam in Denmark and southern Sweden — including the Nydam oak boat — attest to large, organised armies and to conflicts between Germanic groups themselves. Settlements such as Vorbasse in Jutland show fenced farmsteads of longhouses with byres, grouped in stable villages.

Late Germanic Iron Age

After the fall of the western empire, gold-rich horizons of solidi, bracteates and ornate animal-style art mark the Migration Period, while hillforts multiplied in parts of Sweden and Norway amid apparent instability. A severe climatic downturn around AD 536–550, recorded in tree rings and possibly reflected in myths of the Fimbulwinter and Ragnarök, coincides with settlement abandonment in some regions. In the Vendel Period (c. 540–790 in Sweden), rich boat burials at Vendel and Valsgärde, and the great mounds at Gamla Uppsala, display the ships, helmets and weapons that anticipate Viking Age culture; the related Sutton Hoo ship burial in England shows close cross-North Sea connections. In Denmark, early trading places such as Gudme and later Ribe grew toward the first towns.

Society and culture

Germanic Iron Age society was ranked, with chieftains and, increasingly, kings ruling from central places that combined cult, craft production and exchange. The elder and younger runic alphabets spread during this period, carrying some of the earliest written Germanic language. The period closes around AD 800 as Scandinavian raiding, trading and settlement overseas ushers in the Viking Age, and as Denmark and the Frankish world consolidate into early medieval kingdoms.
