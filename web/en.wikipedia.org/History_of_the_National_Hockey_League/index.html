History of the National Hockey League

The history of the National Hockey League (NHL) begins with the league's founding in Montreal in 1917, amid the collapse of its predecessor, the National Hockey Association. From a handful of Canadian teams, the NHL grew into North America's premier professional ice hockey league with 32 teams across Canada and the United States.

Founding and early years

The NHL was founded on 26 November 1917 with the Montreal Canadiens, Montreal Wanderers, Ottawa Senators, and Toronto Arenas. The Wanderers withdrew after their arena burned, but the league stabilized; the Toronto franchise became the St. Patricks and then the Maple Leafs (1927). In 1924 the Boston Bruins became the first U.S. team, beginning American expansion.

The Original Six era

By 1942, financial pressures of the Depression and war left six teams — Montreal, Toronto, Boston, Chicago, Detroit, and the New York Rangers — the "Original Six", who played exclusively against each other until 1967. The era produced dynasties in Montreal and stars like Maurice Richard, Gordie Howe, and Bobby Hull, alongside the growth of televised hockey.

Expansion and the modern era

The 1967 expansion doubled the league to 12 teams. The rival World Hockey Association (1972–1979) challenged the NHL before four WHA teams merged in. Wayne Gretzky's Edmonton Oilers dominated the 1980s; his 1988 trade to Los Angeles boosted U.S. interest. The league expanded to the Sun Belt in the 1990s, adopted a salary cap after the 2004–05 lockout season was cancelled, and reached 32 teams with Vegas (2017) and Seattle (2021). Utah joined in 2024 as the rebranded former Arizona franchise.
