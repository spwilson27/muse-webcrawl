Swahili language

Swahili, also known by its local name Kiswahili, is a Bantu language originally spoken by the Swahili people of the East African coast. It is the national language of Tanzania, Kenya and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, an official language of Uganda and Rwanda, and a working language of the African Union and the East African Community. Estimates of speakers range widely: roughly 5–16 million speak it as a first language, while the total number of speakers, including second-language users, is often estimated at 100–200 million.

History

Swahili developed over the past millennium in the coastal city-states of the Swahili Coast, where Bantu-speaking communities traded across the Indian Ocean with Arabs, Persians and later Portuguese and Indians. As a result, a substantial portion — often estimated at 20–30% — of its vocabulary derives from Arabic, with additional loans from Persian, Portuguese, German, English and Hindi. It historically was written in an Arabic-based script (Swahili Ajami); a standardised form based on the Kiunguja dialect of Zanzibar was adopted in the colonial period, together with a Latin orthography.

Grammar

Like other Bantu languages, Swahili is agglutinative and organises nouns into a system of noun classes (around 15–18 in traditional analyses), each marked by prefixes that govern agreement throughout the sentence. Verbs encode subject, tense/aspect/mood, object and root in a single word. Word order is generally subject–verb–object, and the language has five vowel phonemes and a largely open syllable structure.

Use and literature

Swahili has a centuries-old poetic tradition (utenzi epic poetry) and a modern literature of novels, plays and journalism. It is the medium of primary education in Tanzania, widely used in Kenyan media and politics, and taught internationally as Africa's most widely studied indigenous language. Words such as safari, jumbo and uhuru have entered English from Swahili.
