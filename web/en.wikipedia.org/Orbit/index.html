Orbit

In celestial mechanics, an orbit is the curved trajectory of an object such as the trajectory of a planet around a star, or of a natural or artificial satellite around a planet. Normally, an orbit refers to a regularly repeating trajectory, although it may also refer to a trajectory that is not repeating. To a close approximation, planets and satellites follow elliptical orbits, with the center of mass being orbited at a focal point of the ellipse, as described by Kepler's laws of planetary motion.

A body in orbit continually falls toward the central mass but moves fast enough to keep missing it.

Understanding orbits

An object in orbit can be understood, following Newton's cannonball thought experiment, as a projectile launched horizontally with sufficient velocity that its perpetual fall curves around the Earth rather than into it. The total mechanical energy of an orbiting body determines the shape of its path: negative energy gives a bound elliptical orbit, zero energy a parabolic escape trajectory, and positive energy a hyperbolic trajectory. Johannes Kepler showed empirically that planetary orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus; Isaac Newton later derived the elliptical shape from his law of universal gravitation.

Orbital elements

An orbit in three dimensions is conventionally specified by six orbital elements: semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination, longitude of the ascending node, argument of periapsis, and true anomaly (or mean anomaly at an epoch). For Earth satellites, orbits are classified by altitude — low Earth orbit, medium Earth orbit, and geostationary orbit — and by inclination, such as polar and Sun-synchronous orbits.

Perturbations

Real orbits are not perfect Keplerian ellipses: the gravity of other bodies, atmospheric drag, solar radiation pressure, and the oblateness of the central body cause them to precess and evolve. The two-body approximation remains the foundation of astrodynamics, the discipline concerned with spacecraft trajectories and orbital maneuvers.
