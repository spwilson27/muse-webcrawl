Neoplatonism is a school of philosophy that developed in the 3rd century CE, founded by Plotinus and developed by his successors. Though its adherents considered themselves simply interpreters of Plato, Neoplatonism was a distinctive synthesis combining Platonic metaphysics with elements of Aristotelian, Pythagorean and Stoic thought, together with a strong religious and mystical orientation.

The system of Plotinus

At the summit of reality Plotinus placed the One (τὸ ἕν), an utterly transcendent and ineffable source beyond being itself. From the One, by a process of emanation rather than creation, proceeds Nous (Intellect), which contains the Platonic Forms; from Nous proceeds the World Soul, and from Soul the material world. The philosophical life aims at the soul's "return" and mystical union with the One — described by Plotinus in famous passages of the Enneads, arranged by his student Porphyry into six groups of nine treatises.

Later developments

Porphyry systematized Plotinus's teaching; Iamblichus introduced theurgy (ritual practices aimed at divine union) and multiplied metaphysical intermediaries; Proclus, head of the Athenian Academy, produced the massively systematic Elements of Theology. In 529 CE the emperor Justinian closed the Academy of Athens, and the last Neoplatonists, including Damascius and Simplicius, briefly took refuge in Persia.

Influence

Neoplatonic ideas entered Christian thought through Augustine and the writings of Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, shaped medieval Islamic philosophy (al-Farabi, Avicenna) through the Arabic paraphrase known as the Theology of Aristotle, and were revived in the Renaissance by Marsilio Ficino, whose translations of Plato and Plotinus reshaped European intellectual life.
