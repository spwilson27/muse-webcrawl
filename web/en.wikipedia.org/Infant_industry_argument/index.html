Infant industry argument

The infant industry argument is an economic rationale for trade protectionism. The core idea is that industries in their early stages in a developing country cannot initially compete against established industries in developed countries, so they need temporary protection — through tariffs, quotas, or subsidies — until they mature, gain economies of scale, and become competitive internationally. Once the industry is strong enough to survive without help, the protection should be removed.

The infant industry argument holds that temporary protection can let new industries mature until they can compete unaided.

History

The argument is commonly attributed to Alexander Hamilton, whose 1791 Report on Manufactures urged support for young American industries, and to Friedrich List, who developed the idea systematically in The National System of Political Economy (1841) after observing German and American industry. John Stuart Mill accepted a qualified version of the argument, and it later became known as the Mill–Bastable test: protection is justified only if the industry will eventually be profitable without it and the discounted future benefits exceed the present costs of protection.

Application

The argument influenced the industrial policy of many countries. The United States protected its manufacturing sector through much of the 19th century. In the 20th century, East Asian economies such as Japan, South Korea and Taiwan used selective protection combined with export promotion to build internationally competitive industries in steel, shipbuilding, automobiles and electronics.

Criticism

Critics argue that governments are poor at picking winning industries, that protected "infants" often never grow up and instead lobby to keep protection permanently, and that protection imposes costs on consumers and downstream industries. The argument is also difficult to apply in practice: it is hard to know in advance which industries would eventually become competitive, and retaliation by trading partners can offset any gains. Mainstream trade economists generally accept the argument in theory but regard successful application as rare and demanding.
