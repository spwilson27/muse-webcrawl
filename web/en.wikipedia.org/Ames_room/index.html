Ames room

An Ames room is a distorted room that creates an optical illusion. Probably influenced by the writings of Hermann Helmholtz, it was invented by American scientist Adelbert Ames Jr. in 1934 and constructed the following year.

The illusion

When viewed through a peephole with one eye, the room appears to be an ordinary rectangular room. In fact it is trapezoidal: the walls are slanted, the floor and ceiling slope, and one back corner is much farther away than the other. As a result, a person walking from one back corner to the other appears to shrink or grow dramatically in size, because the viewer unconsciously assumes the room is rectangular and interprets the person's varying distance as a change in their height.

Uses

Ames rooms are popular demonstrations in psychology and in science museums, and the underlying technique of forced perspective has been used in film production, notably to depict characters of very different heights sharing a scene.
