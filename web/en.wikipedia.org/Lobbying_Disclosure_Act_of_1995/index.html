Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995

Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995
EnactedDecember 19, 1995 (signed by President Bill Clinton)
CitationPublic Law 104-65; 2 U.S.C. § 1601 et seq.
PurposeRegistration and disclosure of lobbyists
ReplacedFederal Regulation of Lobbying Act of 1946

The Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995 (LDA) is a United States federal law enacted to bring greater accountability and public disclosure to lobbying activities directed at Congress and the executive branch. It replaced the largely ineffective Federal Regulation of Lobbying Act of 1946 and took effect on 1 January 1996.

Requirements

The Act defines a "lobbyist" as a person employed or retained to make more than one lobbying contact on behalf of a client and who spends at least 20 percent of their time on lobbying activities for that client over a three-month period. Registrants must file with the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House, disclosing their clients, the issues lobbied, the houses of Congress and agencies contacted, and good-faith estimates of lobbying income or expenditures, in periodic reports.

Amendments

The Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007 substantially amended the LDA after the Abramoff scandal, moving reporting from semiannual to quarterly, requiring disclosure of bundled campaign contributions and certain affiliated organisations, and increasing penalties, which include civil fines and criminal liability for knowing and corrupt violations.
