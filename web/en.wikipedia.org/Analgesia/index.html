Analgesia

Analgesia is the absence of pain in response to stimulation that would normally be painful. Unlike anesthesia, which eliminates all sensation in a region or produces unconsciousness, analgesia selectively reduces or abolishes the perception of pain while leaving other sensations — and usually consciousness — intact.

Mechanisms and methods

Analgesia can be produced by drugs (analgesics) acting at various points in the pain pathway: non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) reduce peripheral sensitization by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis; opioids act on receptors in the brain and spinal cord; local anesthetics block nerve conduction, as in epidural and spinal analgesia. Non-pharmacological methods include transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), acupuncture, and psychological techniques. The nervous system also has endogenous pain-control systems, including descending inhibitory pathways and endorphins.

Clinical use

Analgesia is a central goal in the management of acute pain after surgery or injury, chronic pain, cancer pain, and labor. Multimodal analgesia — combining drugs with different mechanisms — is widely recommended to improve relief and reduce opioid requirements. Inadequate analgesia can delay recovery and contribute to chronic pain, while oversedation and opioid side effects are the principal risks of aggressive treatment.

Congenital analgesia

A rare hereditary condition, congenital insensitivity to pain, causes lifelong analgesia; sufferers are at high risk of unnoticed injuries, burns, and joint damage because pain's protective warning function is absent.
