Intimate relationship

ComponentsEmotional closeness, trust, self-disclosure, mutual care; often physical or sexual intimacy
Common formsRomantic partnerships, marriage, cohabitation, close platonic bonds
Key theoriesSternberg's triangular theory of love; attachment theory; social exchange theory
Studied inPsychology, sociology, relationship science

An intimate relationship is an interpersonal relationship that involves emotional intimacy — feelings of closeness, trust, emotional connection, and mutual self-disclosure — and often physical intimacy as well. The term is most commonly applied to romantic and marital partnerships, but it can also describe very close friendships and other bonded relationships.

Emotional intimacy — being known, accepted, and cared for — is the defining feature.

Dimensions of intimacy

Psychologists distinguish several forms of intimacy: emotional (sharing feelings and feeling understood), physical (touch, affection, sexuality), intellectual (exchanging ideas), experiential (shared activities), and spiritual. Robert Sternberg's triangular theory of love (1986) describes love as a combination of intimacy, passion, and commitment in varying proportions, yielding forms from liking (intimacy alone) to consummate love (all three).

Development and maintenance

Intimacy typically develops through reciprocal self-disclosure: partners reveal progressively more personal information and respond with understanding, validation, and care — a process social penetration theory compares to peeling an onion. Research on long-term relationships, including the longitudinal observational work of John Gottman, associates stability with high ratios of positive to negative interactions, effective repair after conflict, and "turning toward" a partner's bids for connection. Attachment styles formed in childhood — secure, anxious, avoidant — are also studied as influences on adult intimacy.

Forms and cultural variation

Intimate partnerships take many socially recognised forms: marriage, civil partnerships, cohabitation, dating relationships, and consensually non-monogamous arrangements. Norms governing their formation — from arranged marriage to individual choice mediated by dating apps — vary widely across cultures and have shifted rapidly in many societies with changing gender roles, later ages of first marriage, and legal recognition of same-sex relationships. Physical intimacy is not a universal prerequisite: platonic and aromantic or asexual partnerships may rest on emotional intimacy alone.
