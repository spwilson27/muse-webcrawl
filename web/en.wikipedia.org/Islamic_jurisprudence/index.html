Islamic jurisprudence

Islamic jurisprudence, known in Arabic as fiqh ("deep understanding"), is the human understanding and interpretation of the sharia — the divine law as revealed in the Quran and the sunna (the practice of the Prophet Muhammad). The methodology by which jurists derive rulings is called usul al-fiqh, "principles of jurisprudence".

Sources

The classical sources of law, in hierarchical order, are the Quran, the sunna (known through hadith), ijma (scholarly consensus) and qiyas (analogical reasoning). Jurists classify human actions into five categories: obligatory, recommended, neutral, disliked and forbidden.

Schools of law

Over the first centuries of Islam, distinct schools (madhhabs) crystallised. The four surviving Sunni schools are the Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali, named after Abu Hanifa, Malik ibn Anas, al-Shafi'i and Ahmad ibn Hanbal. The principal Shia school is the Ja'fari school. Each school dominates in different regions of the Muslim world.

Modern application

Today fiqh governs family law in many Muslim-majority states and underpins the growing Islamic finance industry, while criminal and commercial law are mostly codified by states. See also Islamic calendar and Islamism.
