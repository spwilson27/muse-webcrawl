Ultraviolet divergence

An ultraviolet divergence is a situation in quantum field theory in which an integral over loop momenta in a Feynman diagram diverges at high energies (short distances)—the "ultraviolet" end of the spectrum, by analogy with the ultraviolet catastrophe of classical physics.

Such divergences arise because standard quantum field theories treat particles as pointlike and interactions as occurring at a single spacetime point, so fluctuations at arbitrarily short distances contribute to computed quantities. In calculations of scattering amplitudes, loop integrals often grow without bound as the internal momentum increases.

Ultraviolet divergences are handled by regularization, which modifies the theory at high energies with a cut-off or another scheme (such as dimensional regularization), and renormalization, which absorbs the divergent pieces into redefined ("bare") parameters such as mass and charge. In renormalizable theories only a finite number of parameter redefinitions is needed. Divergences that cannot be so absorbed signal that a theory is an effective field theory valid only below some energy scale. Historically, grappling with these infinities in quantum electrodynamics led to the triumphs of renormalization in the 1940s.
