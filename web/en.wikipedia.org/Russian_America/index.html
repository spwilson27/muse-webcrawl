Russian America (Russian: Русская Америка) was the name of the Russian colonial possessions in North America from 1799 to 1867, corresponding largely to present-day Alaska, with outposts as far south as Fort Ross in California and Fort Elizabeth in Hawaii. The colony was administered by the chartered Russian-American Company under the oversight of the Russian Empire.

New Archangel (Sitka), capital of Russian America, in the 19th century.

History

Russian expansion began after Vitus Bering's voyages of 1728 and 1741 opened the North Pacific to the fur trade. Promyshlenniki — independent fur traders — moved through the Aleutian Islands hunting sea otters, often brutally exploiting the Aleut population. In 1799 Tsar Paul I granted the Russian-American Company a monopoly charter. Under Alexander Baranov, the company's energetic chief manager from 1799 to 1818, the capital was established at New Archangel (Sitka), and trading posts spread along the Alaskan coast and beyond.

Sale to the United States

By the 1860s the colony was unprofitable, sea otter stocks were depleted, and Russia feared losing the territory to Britain in any future war without compensation. Negotiations with U.S. Secretary of State William H. Seward produced the Alaska Purchase treaty of 1867: the United States bought Alaska for $7.2 million — about two cents per acre. Formal transfer took place at Sitka on 18 October 1867.

Legacy

Russian influence survives in Alaska in Orthodox churches, place names (such as Baranof Island and the Kenai Peninsula's Russian-named towns), loanwords in Native languages, and a small community of Russian Old Believers who arrived much later.
