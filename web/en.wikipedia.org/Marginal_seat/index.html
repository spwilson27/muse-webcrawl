Marginal seat

A marginal seat or swing seat is a legislative district or constituency held by a small majority in a legislative election, generally one conducted under a single-winner voting system. Marginal seats are contrasted with safe seats, which are predictably won by one party. Because a small swing can change their outcome, marginal seats attract disproportionate campaign spending, candidate visits and policy promises during elections.

Measurement

Marginality is usually expressed as the percentage majority of the winning candidate or the swing required for the seat to change hands. In the United Kingdom, seats requiring a swing of under five percentage points are commonly classed as marginal, and parties compile target-seat lists ranked by the swing needed. In Australia, the Australian Electoral Commission classifies seats as marginal when the two-party-preferred margin is under 6 per cent, fairly safe between 6 and 10 per cent, and safe above 10 per cent.

Political consequences

Concentration of campaigning on marginal seats can distort policy: governments may direct discretionary spending ("pork barrelling") toward marginal constituencies, and national campaigns are designed to appeal to "swing voters" within them. In Britain, the Ashcroft polls and constituency-level MRP models have made targeting more precise. Critics argue the focus on marginals disenfranchises voters in safe seats, whose votes rarely affect the outcome.
