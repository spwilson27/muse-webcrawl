Claude Henri de Rouvroy, Comte de Saint-Simon (17 October 1760 – 19 May 1825), better known as Henri de Saint-Simon, was a French political, economic, and socialist theorist and businessman whose thought played a substantial role in influencing politics, economics, sociology, and the philosophy of science. He is often regarded as a founder of both Christian socialism and, more broadly, of modern socialism — Marx and Engels later classed his ideas among "utopian socialism" — and his vision of an industrially organised society governed by scientists and engineers anticipates technocracy.

Life

Saint-Simon was born in Paris into an aristocratic family claiming descent from Charlemagne. He served as an officer in the American Revolutionary War, fighting at the Siege of Yorktown. During the French Revolution he renounced his title, speculated profitably in confiscated church lands, and was imprisoned during the Terror. His later years were marked by failed business ventures, poverty, and devoted writing, assisted at times by his secretary Auguste Comte, who became the founder of positivism before breaking with him.

Thought

Saint-Simon argued that history progresses through alternating organic and critical epochs, and that the feudal-theological order was giving way to an industrial-scientific one. In the new society, power should pass from the "idlers" — the nobility, clergy, and rentiers — to the "industrials": workers, managers, bankers, scientists, and artists. He proposed organising production for the benefit of the poorest and most numerous class, famously summarised in the principle later adopted by socialists: capacity should determine work and reward. In his final work, Nouveau Christianisme (1825), he recast his doctrine as a "New Christianity" whose moral core was improving the condition of the poor.

Influence

After his death his disciples — including Saint-Amand Bazard, Barthélemy Prosper Enfantin, and Olinde Rodrigues — developed Saint-Simonianism into an influential movement that promoted railways, canals (Enfantin's followers worked toward the Suez Canal), banking reform, and the emancipation of women, and influenced French economic development under the Second Empire, as well as later socialist and technocratic thought.
