Hypoxia (environmental)

Hypoxia in environmental science refers to conditions in which dissolved oxygen in water falls to levels too low to support most aquatic life, conventionally below 2–3 milligrams per litre. Hypoxic areas in oceans and large lakes have increased in frequency and extent since the mid-20th century, and are often called "dead zones" because fish and invertebrates flee or die.

Causes

The most common cause is eutrophication: nutrient runoff, chiefly nitrogen and phosphorus from agriculture and wastewater, stimulates algal blooms. When the algae die and sink, bacterial decomposition consumes oxygen faster than it can be replenished, especially where water is stratified. Natural hypoxia also occurs in oxygen minimum zones, fjords and upwelling systems, and ocean deoxygenation is being intensified by warming.

Effects and examples

Hypoxia kills sessile organisms and displaces mobile ones, altering food webs and fisheries. Well-known examples include the Gulf of Mexico dead zone at the mouth of the Mississippi River, the Baltic Sea, Chesapeake Bay and Lake Erie. Reducing nutrient loads from watersheds is the principal remedy.
