The Faroe Islands (Faroese: Føroyar; Danish: Færøerne) are a North Atlantic archipelago of 18 major islands between Iceland and Norway, an autonomous territory within the Kingdom of Denmark. The name means "sheep islands". Their rugged, treeless landscape of steep cliffs and grass-roofed villages is shaped by a subpolar oceanic climate.

History

Norse settlers arrived in the 9th century, and the islands — Christianised around 1000 — came under Norwegian and then Danish rule. A trading monopoly and Danish cultural pressure made Faroese largely a spoken language until a 19th-century national revival, led by figures such as Venceslaus Ulricus Hammershaimb, who created its modern written form. During the Second World War the islands were occupied by Britain (Operation Valentine), while Denmark was under German occupation. Home rule was granted in 1948 after a narrow independence referendum in 1946.

Politics and economy

The Faroes control most domestic matters through their parliament, the Løgting — among the world's oldest parliaments — while Denmark retains defence and foreign affairs; notably, the islands are not part of the European Union. The economy rests on fishing and salmon aquaculture, which together account for the great majority of exports. The traditional pilot-whale drive hunt (grindadráp) remains culturally important and internationally controversial.
