Restoration ecology

Restoration ecology is the scientific study supporting the practice of ecological restoration, which is the practice of renewing and restoring degraded, damaged, or destroyed ecosystems and habitats in the environment by active human intervention and action. The field emerged as a distinct discipline in the 1980s, and the Society for Ecological Restoration, founded in 1988, promotes its theory and practice.

Approaches

Restoration may involve reintroducing native species (and, where appropriate, rewilding), removing invasive species, replanting native vegetation, restoring natural disturbance regimes such as fire and flooding, rewetting drained wetlands, and reconnecting fragmented habitats. Practitioners choose a historical reference ecosystem as a guide, while recognising that climate change and novel ecosystems may make a past state unattainable.

History

Early large-scale experiments included the University of Wisconsin–Madison Arboretum's prairie restorations of the 1930s and 1940s, directed by Aldo Leopold and colleagues and often considered the birth of the field. Restoration is now applied at large scales, from mine-site rehabilitation and river restoration — such as the removal of dams — to national programmes; the United Nations declared 2021–2030 the Decade on Ecosystem Restoration.

Related concepts

Restoration ecology draws on conservation biology, population ecology, and ecological resilience theory. It is distinguished from, but overlaps with, environmental mitigation, reclamation of mined land, and habitat creation.
