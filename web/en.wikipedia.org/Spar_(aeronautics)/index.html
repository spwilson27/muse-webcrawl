Spar (aeronautics)

Spar
Function: Main structural member of a wing
Typical materials: Wood, aluminium alloy, carbon-fibre composite
Related: Rib, stringer, monocoque

In aeronautics, a spar is the principal structural member of a wing, running spanwise from the fuselage to the wingtip and carrying the bending and much of the torsional loads generated in flight. Together with ribs, stringers and the wing skin, the spar forms the wing box that gives the wing its strength and shape.

Design

Most wings have two spars: a main spar near the thickest part of the aerofoil section (roughly a quarter to a third of the chord from the leading edge) and a rear spar at about 60–70% of the chord, to which ailerons and flaps are often attached. A spar typically consists of two flanges or caps, which carry bending loads in tension and compression, joined by a vertical web that carries shear.

Materials and construction

Early aircraft used solid or laminated wooden spars of spruce or ash. Metal construction introduced extruded and milled aluminium-alloy I-beams, while modern airliners and gliders increasingly use carbon-fibre reinforced polymer spars, which offer high strength at low weight. Spars are usually designed fail-safe, with multiple load paths, and are joined across the fuselage by a carry-through structure.

False spar

A lighter subsidiary member, a false spar, may be fitted to support control surfaces or landing gear loads without forming part of the primary wing box.
