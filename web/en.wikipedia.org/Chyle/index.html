Chyle

Chyle (from the Greek chylos, meaning juice) is a milky bodily fluid consisting of lymph and emulsified fats, or free fatty acids. It is formed in the small intestine during digestion of fatty foods, and is taken up by lymph vessels known as lacteals.

Physiology

During digestion, long-chain fatty acids are packaged into chylomicrons in the intestinal mucosa and released into the lacteals rather than into the blood capillaries. The resulting milky lymph — chyle — passes through the mesenteric lymph nodes and vessels into the cisterna chyli and then up the thoracic duct, which empties into the venous circulation near the junction of the left subclavian and internal jugular veins. This route delivers dietary lipids to the bloodstream while bypassing first-pass metabolism in the liver.

Clinical significance

Leakage of chyle can occur after trauma or surgery to the neck or chest, or when lymphatic flow is obstructed by tumours or filariasis. Chyle accumulating in the pleural cavity is called a chylothorax; in the abdomen it produces chylous ascites; and loss into the urine (chyluria) is associated with lymphatic filariasis. Diagnosis is confirmed by a high triglyceride concentration in the fluid. Treatment may involve drainage, dietary modification with medium-chain triglycerides (which are absorbed directly into the portal blood), somatostatin analogues, or surgical ligation of the leaking duct.
