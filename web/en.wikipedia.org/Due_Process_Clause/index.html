Due Process Clause

Due Process Clauses
LocationFifth Amendment (federal government); Fourteenth Amendment (states)
Text (Fourteenth)"…nor shall any State deprive any person of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law…"
RatifiedFifth Amendment 1791; Fourteenth Amendment 1868
DoctrinesProcedural due process; substantive due process; incorporation of the Bill of Rights

A Due Process Clause is one of two clauses in the United States Constitution guaranteeing that governments may not deprive persons of life, liberty or property without fair procedures and adequate justification. The Fifth Amendment binds the federal government; the Fourteenth Amendment, ratified after the Civil War, imposes the same obligation on the states. The phrase descends from clause 39 of Magna Carta's promise of judgment "by the law of the land".

Procedural due process

Procedural due process requires government to follow fair procedures before depriving a person of life, liberty or property — typically notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard before an impartial decision-maker. In Mathews v. Eldridge (1976) the Supreme Court articulated a three-factor balancing test weighing the private interest, the risk of erroneous deprivation, and the government's interest.

Substantive due process

Substantive due process holds that certain fundamental rights are protected from government interference regardless of the fairness of procedures. The doctrine underpinned the Lochner era of economic liberty (c. 1897–1937) and later cases recognising rights to privacy and personal autonomy, including Griswold v. Connecticut (1965), Roe v. Wade (1973, overruled by Dobbs in 2022), Lawrence v. Texas (2003) and Obergefell v. Hodges (2015). It remains one of the most debated doctrines in American constitutional law.

Incorporation

Through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause, the Supreme Court has applied ("incorporated") most provisions of the Bill of Rights against the states, from the First Amendment in Gitlow v. New York (1925) to the Second Amendment in McDonald v. Chicago (2010) and the Excessive Fines Clause in Timbs v. Indiana (2019).
