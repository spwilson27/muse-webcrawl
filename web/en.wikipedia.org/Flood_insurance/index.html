Flood insurance

Flood insurance is a form of property insurance that covers damage to buildings and their contents caused by flooding. Standard homeowners' insurance policies in most countries exclude flood damage, so separate cover is required.

United States

In the United States, flood insurance is provided mainly through the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP), created in 1968 and administered by the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), because private insurers had largely withdrawn from the market due to adverse selection — only those at high risk bought policies. Properties in high-risk flood zones with federally backed mortgages are required to carry it. A private flood insurance market has grown alongside the NFIP in recent years.

Issues

The NFIP has been criticised for subsidised premiums that encourage building in flood-prone areas, repetitive-loss properties, and substantial debt to the US Treasury after catastrophic seasons such as Hurricane Katrina (2005) and Hurricane Harvey (2017). Reforms such as Risk Rating 2.0 aim to price policies according to individual property risk.
