Berlin International Film Festival

The Berlin International Film Festival (German: Internationale Filmfestspiele Berlin), usually called the Berlinale, is a major international film festival held annually in Berlin, Germany. Founded in West Berlin in 1951, it is one of the "Big Three" European festivals alongside Cannes and Venice, and it attracts the largest public audience of any film festival in the world, selling several hundred thousand tickets each February. Its top prize is the Golden Bear (Goldener Bär), with Silver Bears for directing, acting, and other categories.

History

The festival was founded during the Cold War as a "showcase of the free world" in the divided city, opening with Hitchcock's Rebecca. Founding director Alfred Bauer led it until 1976; long-serving later directors included Moritz de Hadeln and Dieter Kosslick (2001–2019), under whom the festival moved its hub to the redeveloped Potsdamer Platz. From 2020 the festival was led by Mariette Rissenbeek and Carlo Chatrian, and from 2024 by Tricia Tuttle. Historically, the Berlinale has been the most political of the major festivals, championing Eastern European cinema during the Cold War and independent and arthouse film thereafter.

Sections and market

Alongside the Competition, sections include Panorama, Forum (avant-garde), Generation (children and youth), Perspektive Deutsches Kino, Berlinale Shorts, and Retrospective. The European Film Market, held concurrently, is one of the world's principal film-trading events, and Berlinale Talents runs a major development programme for emerging filmmakers. The Teddy Award (since 1987), for LGBTQ-themed film, originated at the Berlinale.

Selected Golden Bear winners

Notable winners include The Wages of Fear and Cinderella (1951, audience vote), Wild Strawberries (1958), Alphaville (1965), Rain Man (1989), The Thin Red Line (1999), Spirited Away (2002), A Separation (2011), Taxi (2015), Fire at Sea (2016), Alcarràs (2022), and On the Adamant (2023).
