Hindsight bias

Hindsight bias, also known as the knew-it-all-along phenomenon or creeping determinism, is the common tendency for people to perceive past events as having been more predictable than they actually were. After an event has occurred, people often believe that they could have predicted, or even that they did predict, the outcome, even when there was little objective basis for forecasting it.

Research

The phenomenon was documented experimentally by Baruch Fischhoff in 1975: participants who were told the outcome of an event (for example, the result of a historical incident or a clinical case) rated that outcome as far more probable than participants who were not told, and instructions to ignore the outcome did not remove the effect. Later work distinguished three levels: memory distortion ("I said it would happen"), inevitability ("it had to happen") and foreseeability ("I knew it would happen").

Mechanisms

Hindsight bias is thought to arise because outcome knowledge is automatically integrated into one's knowledge base: once we know what happened, the evidence consistent with the outcome becomes more salient, causal explanations are constructed around it, and it becomes difficult to reconstruct one's earlier state of uncertainty. This "creeping determinism" makes the past look orderly and the outcome inevitable.

Consequences

The bias has practical effects in law (judging defendants and officials by outcomes they could not have foreseen), medicine (retrospective reviews of adverse events), finance (judging investors and forecasters), and history (presenting events such as wars as inevitable). It can distort learning, because if an outcome was "obvious", there seems little reason to improve one's decision processes. Debiasing techniques — such as deliberately considering how alternative outcomes might have occurred — reduce but do not eliminate the effect.
