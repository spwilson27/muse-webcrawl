Legal sources: Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Art. 13; ICCPR, Art. 12
Applies to: Nationals, refugees, displaced persons, diasporas

Right of return

The right of return is a principle in international law according to which everyone has the right to return to their own country. It is enshrined in Article 13 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights ("Everyone has the right to leave any country, including his own, and to return to his country") and in Article 12 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.

Applications

The principle is invoked in several distinct contexts: individual citizens travelling abroad; refugees and internally displaced persons returning after conflict; and collective claims by peoples displaced by war or expulsion. Specific disputes include the Palestinian right of return to homes in what is now Israel, claims of ethnic Germans expelled from Eastern Europe after 1945, and return provisions in peace settlements such as the Dayton Agreement for Bosnia.

Nationality and diaspora laws

Several states grant immigration or citizenship rights based on ancestry or ethnicity — sometimes loosely called rights of return — including Israel's Law of Return (1950) for Jews, Germany's former provisions for ethnic Germans, and Ireland's and Italy's descent-based citizenship rules. The scope of the principle, especially for descendants of displaced persons, remains contested.
