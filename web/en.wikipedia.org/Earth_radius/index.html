Earth radius

The Earth radius is the distance from the center of Earth to its surface, about 6,371 kilometres (3,959 miles) on average. Because Earth is not a perfect sphere but an oblate spheroid — flattened at the poles and bulging at the equator — the radius varies with latitude, from about 6,357 km at the poles to about 6,378 km at the equator. The unit of Earth's radius (R🜨) is also used as a standard measure in astronomy and planetary science.

Definitions

The equatorial radius (semi-major axis of the reference ellipsoid) is 6,378.137 km in the WGS 84 system. The polar radius (semi-minor axis) is 6,356.752 km. Several mean radii are used: the arithmetic mean of the axes, the authalic radius (of a sphere with equal surface area) and the volumetric radius (of a sphere with equal volume), all close to 6,371 km. Local radius also depends on elevation and on whether distance is measured to sea level or to the actual surface.

History of measurement

Eratosthenes estimated Earth's circumference around 240 BC by comparing shadows at Syene and Alexandria, obtaining a value remarkably close to the true one. Al-Ma'mun's astronomers refined the measurement in the 9th century, and the French Geodesic Missions of the 1730s–40s to Lapland and Peru confirmed Newton's prediction of polar flattening. Modern values derive from satellite geodesy and the reference ellipsoids used by GPS.

Uses

Earth radius enters formulas for surface gravity, horizon distance, map projections and orbital mechanics. In astronomy, exoplanet sizes are commonly expressed in Earth radii.
