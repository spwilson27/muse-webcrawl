Arianism is a Christological doctrine attributed to Arius (c. 256–336), a presbyter of Alexandria, Egypt. It holds that Jesus Christ, the Son of God, was begotten by God the Father at a point in time, is distinct from the Father, and is therefore subordinate to him — summarised in the Arian slogan "there was a time when he was not". The doctrine rejects the consubstantiality (homoousios) of the Son with the Father affirmed by orthodox Trinitarianism.

History

The controversy erupted around 318 in Alexandria, where Bishop Alexander and his deacon Athanasius opposed Arius. Emperor Constantine I convened the First Council of Nicaea in 325, which condemned Arius and produced the Nicene Creed declaring the Son "of one substance with the Father". The dispute nevertheless convulsed the Church and Empire for over fifty years, with emperors alternately favouring Arian or Nicene parties. The First Council of Constantinople in 381, under Theodosius I, definitively confirmed Nicene orthodoxy within the Empire.

Arian kingdoms

Through the missionary Ulfilas, who translated the Bible into Gothic, Arian Christianity was adopted by several Germanic peoples, including the Goths, Vandals, Burgundians, and Lombards, and persisted in their successor kingdoms in the West into the sixth and seventh centuries — Spain's Visigoths renounced Arianism in 589 — long after it had faded in the Roman world.

Later echoes

"Arian" has been applied polemically to later nontrinitarian positions, and modern groups such as Jehovah's Witnesses are sometimes compared with Arianism, though their doctrines differ in detail. The Arian controversy profoundly shaped Christian theology, clarifying the doctrine of the Trinity.
