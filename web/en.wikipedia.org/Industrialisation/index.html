DefinitionPeriod of social and economic change transforming an agrarian society into an industrial one

Industrialisation (or industrialization) is the period of social and economic change that transforms a human group from an agrarian society into an industrial society. It involves an extensive reorganisation of an economy for the purpose of manufacturing, with mechanized production, factory systems, and a shift of labour from agriculture to industry and services, accompanied by urbanization, population growth, and rising incomes.

The first industrialisation

Industrialisation began in Great Britain in the late eighteenth century, in what is known as the Industrial Revolution. Innovations in textiles (such as the spinning jenny and power loom), steam power, coal mining, and ironmaking concentrated production in factories in northern England and the Scottish Lowlands. From Britain the process spread to Belgium, Germany, France, and the United States during the nineteenth century.

Later waves

Other countries industrialized under different models: Meiji Japan after 1868 combined state promotion with private enterprise; the Soviet Union pursued forced industrialization through five-year plans from 1928; and after 1945 the "Asian Tigers" (South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong) used export-oriented strategies. China industrialized rapidly following economic reforms begun in 1978, becoming the world's largest manufacturer.

Effects

Industrialisation raised material living standards and life expectancy over the long run, but early phases were marked by harsh factory conditions, child labour, urban overcrowding, and pollution—conditions that prompted factory legislation, trade unions, and public health reform. Economists describe the accompanying structural change with models such as the Lewis model of surplus labour transfer. In recent decades, some advanced economies have experienced deindustrialization as manufacturing employment declines relative to services.
