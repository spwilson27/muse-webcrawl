Clavichord

The clavichord is a stringed rectangular keyboard instrument that was used widely in Europe from the late Middle Ages through the Renaissance, Baroque and Classical eras. Historically it was mostly used as a practice instrument and as an aid to composition, being too quiet for ensemble performance. It produces sound by striking brass or iron strings with small metal blades called tangents.

Mechanism

When a key is pressed, a tangent — a small blade of metal set upright at the far end of the key lever — rises to strike the string and simultaneously defines its sounding length, acting like a fret. When the key is released, the tangent loses contact and a strip of damping cloth silences the string. Because the tangent remains in contact while the note sounds, the player can influence pitch and tone with finger pressure, including a vibrato effect known as Bebung.

History

Clavichords are mentioned as early as 1404, and surviving instruments date from the 1540s. In "fretted" clavichords, pairs of tangents shared a string for notes that would not be needed simultaneously; later "unfretted" instruments gave each note its own string. The clavichord was the domestic keyboard of choice in German-speaking countries well into the 18th century; Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach was a notable advocate and virtuoso. Production declined with the rise of the piano, but the instrument was revived in the early 20th century as part of the early music movement.

Repertoire

Much keyboard music of the 17th and 18th centuries was played on the clavichord, and its intimate expressiveness made it a favourite teaching and practice instrument for composers including Johann Sebastian Bach.
