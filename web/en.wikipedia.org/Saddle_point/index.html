Saddle point

The hyperbolic paraboloid z = x² − y² has a saddle point at the origin.

In mathematics, a saddle point is a point in the domain of a function at which the derivatives (slopes) do not change sign in orthogonal directions: the point is a local maximum in one direction and a local minimum in another. It is a stationary point — the gradient is zero — but is neither a local maximum nor a local minimum. The name derives from the example of a two-dimensional surface resembling a riding saddle, which curves up in one direction and down in another.

Mathematical characterization

For a twice-differentiable function of two variables, the second derivative test identifies a saddle point when the Hessian determinant at a stationary point is negative. The classic example is f(x, y) = x² − y², whose graph is a hyperbolic paraboloid. In higher dimensions, saddle points complicate optimization: gradient descent can slow near them, a phenomenon significant in the training of neural networks, where loss surfaces in high-dimensional parameter spaces abound in saddle points. Strict-saddle assumptions and perturbed gradient methods are used to escape them.

Other uses

In game theory, a "saddle point" of a payoff matrix is an entry that is simultaneously the minimum of its row and the maximum of its column; its existence is equivalent to the two players having optimal pure strategies with equal value. In dynamical systems, a saddle point of a phase portrait attracts trajectories along one axis (the stable manifold) and repels them along another (the unstable manifold).
