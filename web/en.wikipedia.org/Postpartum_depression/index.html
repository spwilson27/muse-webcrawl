Postpartum depression (PPD), also called postnatal depression, is a mood disorder associated with childbirth. It is a form of major depression occurring during pregnancy or following delivery — clinically, peripartum onset is specified for episodes beginning during pregnancy or within four weeks after delivery, though symptoms may arise up to a year postpartum. It can affect mothers and, less commonly studied, fathers as well.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms include persistent sadness, emptiness, or anxious mood; loss of interest or pleasure; irritability; changes in sleep and appetite beyond those expected with a newborn; fatigue; difficulty concentrating; feelings of guilt or worthlessness; trouble bonding with the infant; and, in severe cases, thoughts of self-harm or harming the baby. Symptoms lasting more than two weeks, or interfering with daily functioning, warrant evaluation.

Causes and risk factors

No single cause is established. The abrupt drop in estrogen and progesterone after delivery, changes in thyroid function and stress hormones, sleep deprivation, and psychological and social stressors are all implicated. The strongest risk factor is a prior history of depression or anxiety; other factors include a difficult pregnancy or delivery, a baby with health problems, breastfeeding difficulties, financial stress, and lack of support.

Diagnosis

Screening commonly uses the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) or the PHQ-9 questionnaire, followed by a clinical interview. Medical causes such as thyroid dysfunction and anemia should be excluded.

Treatment

Mild to moderate cases often respond to psychotherapy, particularly cognitive behavioral therapy and interpersonal therapy, and to peer and family support. Antidepressants, especially selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, are used for moderate to severe illness, weighed against considerations in breastfeeding. In 2019 brexanolone became the first drug approved in the United States specifically for postpartum depression, followed by the oral drug zuranolone in 2023. Postpartum psychosis, a rare but severe related emergency, requires urgent hospital care.

Related conditions

The transient "baby blues" — mood lability, tearfulness, and anxiety in the first days after birth — affect a majority of new mothers and resolve without treatment within about two weeks; they are distinct from postpartum depression in duration and severity.
