Tintype

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A tintype portrait of a man, c. 1870, showing the small iron-plate format]

A tintype, also known as a melainotype or ferrotype, is a photograph made by creating a direct positive on a thin sheet of metal coated with a dark lacquer or enamel and used as the support for the photographic emulsion. The process was invented in the United States — Hamilton Smith patented it in 1856 — and it succeeded the ambrotype.

Process

The plate is a thin sheet of iron coated with collodion containing silver salts, sensitised in a bath of silver nitrate, exposed in the camera, and developed immediately while still wet (a variant of the wet collodion process). The resulting image is actually a very thin, underexposed negative; against the blackened plate it appears as a positive. Because the support is metal, images could be cut with scissors, mailed, and carried in pockets and albums without breaking, and plates could be prepared and finished within minutes.

History and use

Tintypes became enormously popular during and after the American Civil War: they were far cheaper than daguerreotypes or ambrotypes, so itinerant and booth photographers could sell a portrait for a few cents. Small "gem" portraits were mounted in paper sleeves or lockets. The format survived into the early 20th century at fairs and seaside resorts, long after gelatin dry plates had displaced it elsewhere, and the process has enjoyed a revival among alternative-process photographers in the 21st century.
