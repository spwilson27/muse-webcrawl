French Canadians

French Canadians (French: Canadiens français) are an ethnic group who trace their ancestry primarily to French colonists who settled in what is now Canada beginning in the 17th century, during the colonial era of New France. They constitute the main French-speaking population of Canada — roughly a fifth of the country's population reports French ancestry or French as a mother tongue — and are concentrated overwhelmingly in the province of Quebec, with significant communities in New Brunswick (Acadians), Ontario, Manitoba, and the other provinces.

History

French settlement began with Samuel de Champlain's founding of Quebec City in 1608. By the British conquest of New France in 1760, some 70,000 French colonists lived along the St. Lawrence valley; isolated from France, they developed a distinct society organized around the Catholic Church, seigneurial land tenure, and the French language. The Quebec Act (1774) preserved French civil law and Catholic rights, and French Canadians maintained their language and institutions through Confederation (1867), in which Quebec entered as a majority-francophone province.

In the 19th and early 20th centuries, high birth rates ("la revanche des berceaux") grew the population, while economic hardship drove roughly a million French Canadians to emigrate to New England mill towns. The Quiet Revolution of the 1960s secularized and modernized Quebec, and language legislation — notably the Charter of the French Language (Bill 101, 1977) — established French as Quebec's official language. Since the 1960s many Quebec francophones have identified primarily as Québécois, and two referendums on sovereignty were held, in 1980 and 1995, the latter narrowly rejecting independence.

Culture

French Canadian culture is marked by the French language (including Quebec French and Acadian French), Catholic heritage, festivals such as the Quebec Winter Carnival, traditional music, and cuisines including tourtière and poutine. Acadians, descended from colonists in the Maritimes, are often considered a distinct people within the broader French Canadian family.
