Problem of other minds

FieldPhilosophy of mind, epistemology
RelatedSolipsism, mind–body problem

The problem of other minds is a philosophical problem concerning how, or whether, one can know that other people (or animals, or machines) have minds and mental states at all. Each person has direct first-person access only to their own experiences; the experiences of others are inferred from behaviour and physical resemblance.

Formulations

The problem is often framed as an epistemological challenge: what justifies the belief that another being is conscious rather than an unfeeling automaton? It is closely connected to solipsism, the view that only one's own mind is certain to exist.

Responses

Traditional responses include the argument from analogy, associated with John Stuart Mill: since in my own case behaviour is accompanied by feelings, similar behaviour in others probably accompanies similar feelings. Twentieth-century philosophers such as Ludwig Wittgenstein questioned the framing itself, arguing that mental concepts are tied to public criteria. More recent approaches appeal to inference to the best explanation and to "theory of mind" research in cognitive science.
