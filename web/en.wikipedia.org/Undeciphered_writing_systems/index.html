Undeciphered writing systems

A writing system is undeciphered when its symbols are known but their meaning, or the language they record, cannot be reliably read. Decipherment typically requires a sufficient corpus of texts, knowledge of the underlying language or a related one, and ideally bilingual inscriptions like the Rosetta Stone that unlocked Egyptian hieroglyphs.

Notable examples

Indus script

The Indus script of the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 2600–1900 BCE) consists of a few hundred signs on seals and other objects, with inscriptions averaging only about five signs and no known bilingual text. Whether it encodes a full language at all is disputed; proposed Dravidian and Indo-Aryan readings remain unproven.

Linear A

Linear A, used by the Minoan civilization of Crete (c. 1800–1450 BCE), shares many signs with the deciphered Linear B (used for Mycenaean Greek), so many signs can be transliterated, but the underlying Minoan language remains unknown and the texts cannot be understood.

Rongorongo

Rongorongo, the glyphs carved on wooden tablets from Easter Island (Rapa Nui), may be the only independent invention of writing in Oceania. About two dozen authentic tablets survive; numerous decipherment claims have not won scholarly acceptance.

Proto-Elamite

Proto-Elamite (c. 3100–2900 BCE), from ancient Iran, is among the earliest writing systems. Its numerical signs are partially understood, but the language is unknown and the script undeciphered, though digital corpora have revived research.

Others

Other undeciphered systems include the Cretan hieroglyphs, the Byblos syllabary, the Phaistos Disc (a unique artefact), Etruscan remains only partially understood through related languages, the Isthmian (Epi-Olmec) script, the Zapotec script, the Sitovo inscription and the singular Voynich manuscript, whose status as writing, cipher or hoax is debated.

Why decipherment is hard

Key obstacles are short text lengths, unknown underlying languages with no living relatives, lack of bilingual inscriptions, uncertain sign inventories (allography), and small surviving corpora. Statistical and computational methods — including Bayesian modelling and machine learning — have assisted work on known languages but cannot substitute for linguistic evidence.

Successful decipherments

Famous successes include Egyptian hieroglyphs (Champollion, 1820s, via the Rosetta Stone), cuneiform (Rawlinson and others, via the Behistun Inscription), Linear B (Ventris and Chadwick, 1952), Maya glyphs (Knorosov and others, 20th century) and Egyptian hieratic and demotic scripts.
