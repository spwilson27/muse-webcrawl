Slide projector

A slide projector is an optical device for projecting photographic transparencies (“slides”) onto a screen. Widely used from the 1950s to the early 2000s for family photographs, education, business presentations, and art lectures, it was displaced by digital projectors and flat-panel displays.

Mechanism

The projector passes light from a high-intensity lamp through a condenser lens and the slide, then through a projection lens that focuses the magnified image on the screen. A fan cools the lamp and slide. Slides are usually 35 mm film in 2-by-2-inch cardboard or plastic mounts. Feeding mechanisms range from manual push-pull changers and straight trays to the famous circular carousel introduced by Kodak in 1961, which held 80 or 140 slides and dropped each into the gate by gravity.

History

Slide projectors descend from the magic lantern; modern forms followed the popularity of 35 mm colour reversal film such as Kodachrome. The Kodak Carousel, designed by Louis Hochman, became the standard in homes, schools, and lecture halls — its operation is famously dramatized as a symbol of mid-century family life. Kodak ended Carousel production in 2004 as digital photography took over, though slides and projectors remain in use by artists and enthusiasts, and “slide show” survives as the term for digital presentations.
