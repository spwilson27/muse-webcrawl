Portuguese discoveries

Portuguese discoveries (Portuguese: Descobrimentos portugueses) are the numerous territories and maritime routes discovered and mapped by the Portuguese during the Age of Discovery, primarily in the 15th and 16th centuries. Sponsored initially by Prince Henry the Navigator, Portuguese explorers pioneered European overseas exploration, establishing sea routes to Africa, Asia, and South America.

Background

Motivated by trade in gold and spices, the search for the legendary Christian kingdom of Prester John, and competition with Castile, Portugal began systematic voyages down the West African coast after the conquest of Ceuta in 1415. The development of the caravel made long ocean voyages practical.

Major voyages

  • 1419–1420: Madeira rediscovered and settled
  • 1427 onwards: the Azores reached
  • 1488: Bartolomeu Dias rounds the Cape of Good Hope
  • 1498: Vasco da Gama reaches India
  • 1500: Pedro Álvares Cabral reaches Brazil
  • 1519–1522: the Magellan–Elcano expedition, sailing for Spain, completes the first circumnavigation

Consequences

The discoveries led to the Portuguese Empire, the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) dividing the non-European world between Portugal and Spain, and the creation of trading posts (feitorias) from Brazil to Japan. They transformed global trade, ecology and cartography, while also bringing colonisation, disease and the transatlantic slave trade to the Americas and Africa.
