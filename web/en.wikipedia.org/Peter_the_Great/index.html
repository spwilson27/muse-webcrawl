Peter the Great

Peter I (1672–1725), most commonly known as Peter the Great, was Tsar of all Russia from 1682, and the first Emperor of all Russia from 1721 until his death in 1725. He reigned jointly with his half-brother Ivan V until 1696. Through a number of successful wars, he captured ports at Azov and the Baltic Sea, laying the groundwork for the Imperial Russian Navy; he ended the era of Sweden's status as a great power in the Great Northern War, and his victory at the Battle of Poltava in 1709 is widely regarded as one of the most decisive battles in history.

Reforms

Peter carried out a comprehensive programme of Westernisation and modernisation. He reorganised the Russian army along modern lines, built a navy from scratch, secularised schools, established the Academy of Sciences, simplified the Russian alphabet, introduced the Julian calendar, founded the first Russian newspaper, and imposed Western dress and grooming customs at court (including the famous beard tax). In 1703 he founded Saint Petersburg on the Neva delta, which became the new capital in 1712.

Foreign policy and wars

The Azov campaigns (1695–96) secured a southern outlet; the Great Northern War (1700–1721) against Sweden ended with the Treaty of Nystad, granting Russia Ingria, Estonia, and Livonia and confirming it as the dominant Baltic power. Peter also campaigned against the Ottoman Empire and Persia.

Legacy

Historians debate Peter's legacy: he transformed Russia into a major European power and modern empire, but his methods were often brutal, relying on forced labour and heavy taxation. His reforms nonetheless reshaped Russian state and society for two centuries.
