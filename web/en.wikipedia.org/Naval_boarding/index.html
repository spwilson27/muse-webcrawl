Naval boarding

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Naval boarding is an offensive tactic used in naval warfare to seize an enemy ship (or, in anti-piracy and law-enforcement contexts, to inspect a vessel) by attaching to it and having sailors or soldiers climb aboard, historically to fight the crew in hand-to-hand combat. It was the dominant method of deciding sea battles from antiquity until the early modern period.

History

Roman forces used the corvus, a spiked boarding bridge, to turn sea battles against Carthage into infantry contests during the First Punic War. Galley warfare in the Mediterranean depended on ramming followed by boarding; archers and "sea-soldiers" (marines) were embarked for that purpose. In the age of sail, ships exchanged gunfire to disable the enemy before grappling and boarding with cutlasses, pistols and axes; surrender of the ship often followed capture of its deck.

Decline

More powerful naval artillery, steam propulsion, and long-range gunnery made deliberate boarding actions rare by the late 19th century. Isolated cases persisted: U-boat crews were boarded to seize code materials in both world wars, most famously the capture of U-505 in 1944 and the seizure of Enigma material from U-110 in 1941.

Modern boarding

Today boarding is principally a visit, board, search, and seizure (VBSS) activity conducted by naval and coast guard teams against suspected smugglers, pirates, embargo-breakers, and for maritime interdiction operations, as well as by special forces in hostage-rescue and counter-terrorism missions.
