Holstentor

The Holstentor ("Holstein Gate") is a city gate marking the western boundary of the old centre of Lübeck, Germany. Built in 1464–1478 in the Brick Gothic style, it is the most famous surviving gate of the city's medieval fortifications, and together with the old town of Lübeck — a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1987 — it is one of the best-known landmarks of Germany. The gate takes its name from the region of Holstein, to which the road through it led.

Architecture

The Holstentor consists of two round towers with conical roofs flanking a central block with a stepped gable, built of red brick with decorative bands of glazed brickwork. The field side (facing outward) is heavily fortified, with thick walls up to 3.5 metres and gun ports; the city side is more ornate, with windows. Above the archway on the city side stands the Latin inscription Concordia domi foris pax — "Harmony within, peace without" — dating from 1871, replacing an older inscription beginning S.P.Q.L. ("Senatus populusque Lubecensis", after the Roman model). Because it was built on marshy ground, the gate has sunk unevenly, and its towers lean noticeably toward each other.

History

The Holstentor of 1478 replaced earlier gates on the site and formed part of a system of four successive gates guarding the western approach across the Trave. As the fortifications became obsolete, the outer gates were demolished in the 19th century, and the Holstentor itself narrowly escaped demolition: in 1863 the city council voted to preserve it by a margin of a single vote. Extensive restoration followed in the 1870s and again in the 1930s. During the Nazi era the gate was celebrated as a symbol of German power; the surrounding area was reshaped in 1934.

Museum and symbol

Since 1950 the Holstentor has housed the Museum Holstentor, presenting the history of Lübeck as queen of the Hanseatic League, with ship models, medieval weapons, torture instruments and the city's trade history. The gate appeared on the German 50-mark banknote (1960–1995 series) and on stamps and coins, and remains the emblem of Lübeck, reproduced on the packaging of the city's famous marzipan.
