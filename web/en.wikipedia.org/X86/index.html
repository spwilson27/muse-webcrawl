x86 (also known as 80x86 or the 8086 family) is a family of complex instruction set computer (CISC) instruction set architectures initially developed by Intel based on the 8086 microprocessor and its 8-bit variant, the 8088. The architecture has been implemented in processors from Intel, AMD, and others, and has dominated personal computing since the IBM PC era.

History

The 8086 was introduced in 1978 as a 16-bit processor. Successive generations expanded the architecture: the 80286 (1982) added protected mode; the 80386 (1985) introduced a 32-bit architecture with paging, retrospectively called IA-32; and the 80486 and Pentium lines added integrated floating-point units, superscalar execution, and multimedia extensions (MMX, SSE). In 1999 AMD announced a 64-bit extension, AMD64, which Intel later adopted as Intel 64; together they are known as x86-64 (or x64), the standard architecture of modern desktop and server computers.

Characteristics

x86 uses variable-length instructions and relatively few general-purpose registers compared with RISC designs, reflecting its origins in 1970s design constraints. Modern implementations decode x86 instructions into internal micro-operations, achieving performance competitive with newer architectures while retaining backward compatibility with decades of software — a compatibility widely regarded as both the architecture's greatest strength and a source of considerable complexity.

Market

x86 processors power the large majority of desktop and laptop personal computers and a large share of servers and cloud infrastructure, though ARM-based processors have gained ground in mobile devices, embedded systems, and, increasingly, laptops and data centers.
