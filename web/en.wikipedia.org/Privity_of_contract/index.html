Privity of contract

The doctrine of privity of contract is a common law principle which provides that a contract cannot confer rights or impose obligations on any person who is not a party to it. The premise is that only parties to contracts should be able to sue to enforce their rights or claim damages as such. Closely connected is the rule that a third party generally cannot be burdened by a contract to which they have not consented.

Leading cases

The doctrine was firmly established in English law in Tweddle v Atkinson (1861), where a man was unable to enforce a promise made between his father and his future father-in-law for his benefit. It was reaffirmed by the House of Lords in Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co Ltd v Selfridge & Co Ltd (1915), tying privity to the doctrine of consideration: only a person who has provided consideration may enforce the contract.

Exceptions and reform

Numerous exceptions developed at common law and in equity, including agency, assignment, trusts of the contractual promise, restrictive covenants running with land, and special regimes for insurance and negotiable instruments. Because the rule could defeat the parties' evident intention to benefit a third party, it has been reformed by statute: in England and Wales, the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 allows a third party to enforce a term that expressly benefits them or that the parties intended them to enforce. Similar reforms exist in many Commonwealth jurisdictions and in the United States, where the doctrine is expressed through the law of third-party beneficiaries.
