Myelofibrosis is a rare chronic blood cancer classified as a myeloproliferative neoplasm. In myelofibrosis, abnormal blood stem cells cause scar tissue (fibrosis) to build up in the bone marrow, displacing normal blood-forming tissue. Blood cell production shifts to the spleen and liver (extramedullary haematopoiesis), which typically become enlarged.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms include fatigue from anaemia, early satiety and abdominal discomfort from splenomegaly, night sweats, fever, weight loss, bone pain and itching. Blood counts may show anaemia with tear-drop red cells (dacrocytes) and a leukoerythroblastic film.

Causes and diagnosis

The disease is driven by acquired mutations in haematopoietic stem cells — most often JAK2 V617F (in about half of patients), CALR, or MPL — causing constitutive JAK-STAT signalling. Diagnosis rests on bone marrow biopsy showing reticulin/collagen fibrosis and characteristic megakaryocyte abnormalities, plus molecular testing. Primary myelofibrosis arises de novo; secondary myelofibrosis can evolve from polycythaemia vera or essential thrombocythaemia.

Treatment

JAK inhibitors such as ruxolitinib reduce spleen size and symptoms. Other options include fedratinib, pacritinib and momelotinib, blood transfusions and, for eligible patients, allogeneic stem cell transplantation — the only potentially curative therapy. The disease can progress to acute myeloid leukaemia in a minority of patients.
