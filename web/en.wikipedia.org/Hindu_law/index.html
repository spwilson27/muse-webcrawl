Hindu law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
A manuscript page from a Dharmaśāstra text

Hindu law, as a historical term, refers to the code of laws applied to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs in British India, derived from the Dharmaśāstra tradition. In modern usage it denotes the personal law governing Hindus in India as codified by statutes such as the Hindu Marriage Act, Hindu Succession Act, Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act and Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, all enacted in 1955–56.

Classical Hindu law

The traditional sources of Hindu law were the Śruti (revealed texts, i.e. the Vedas), the Smṛti (remembered texts, especially the Dharmaśāstras of Manu, Yājñavalkya and Nārada), commentaries and digests (such as the Mitākṣarā and Dāyabhāga), and custom. Classical law treated dharma — duty, morality, religion and law — as an integrated whole, and organised obligations by varṇa (class) and āśrama (stage of life).

Anglo-Hindu law

From 1772, when Warren Hastings' judicial plan directed that Hindus be governed by "the laws of the Shaster" in matters of inheritance, marriage, caste and religion, British courts applied textual law with the help of court pandits. The resulting body of case law, increasingly based on precedent and translations, became known as Anglo-Hindu law; it applied a more uniform and rigid version of the tradition than had prevailed in practice.

Modern Hindu law

After independence, India codified and reformed Hindu personal law through the Hindu Code Bills of the 1950s. The codified law abolished polygamy, introduced divorce, widened women's inheritance rights (expanded further by the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005, which made daughters coparceners by birth), and modernised adoption and guardianship. Hindu law today is thus largely statutory, though custom remains a recognised source in some matters.
