Henry Wadsworth Longfellow (February 27, 1807 – March 24, 1882) was an American poet and educator. His works include the epic poems The Song of Hiawatha (1855) and Evangeline (1847), the narrative poem "Paul Revere's Ride" (1860), and the first American translation of Dante's Divine Comedy. He was the most popular poet of his day, and one of the fireside poets from New England.

Life

Longfellow was born in Portland, Maine (then part of Massachusetts), and graduated from Bowdoin College in 1825 — in the same class as Nathaniel Hawthorne. After studying languages in Europe he became a professor at Bowdoin, then at Harvard College, where he taught until 1854. He settled at Craigie House in Cambridge, a mansion once used by George Washington as headquarters.

His personal life was marked by tragedy: his first wife, Mary Potter, died in 1835, and his second wife, Frances Appleton, died in 1861 after her dress caught fire. Longfellow struggled with depression afterwards but continued to write and translate.

Works

Longfellow's poetry drew on American history, landscape, and Native American and European legend. Evangeline tells of the Acadian expulsion; The Song of Hiawatha, written in trochaic tetrameter modelled on the Finnish Kalevala, recounts the life of an Ojibwe hero; The Courtship of Miles Standish (1858) dramatises the Plymouth Colony. His shorter poems — "A Psalm of Life", "The Village Blacksmith", "The Wreck of the Hesperus", and "Paul Revere's Ride" — became staples of American schoolrooms. His translation of Dante (1867) was a landmark of American letters.

Legacy

Longfellow was the first American poet to achieve international fame in his lifetime; his birthday was celebrated in schools across the United States, and after his death he became the first American writer honoured with a bust in Poets' Corner of Westminster Abbey (1884). Though his reputation declined among modernist critics, his poems remain among the most widely read in American literature.
