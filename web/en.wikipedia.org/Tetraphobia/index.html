Tetraphobia

Tetraphobia (from Greek tetra, "four", and phobia, "fear") is the practice of avoiding the number 4, a superstition most common in East and Southeast Asian cultures, including China, Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam, Malaysia, and Singapore. The avoidance arises because the word for "four" sounds similar or identical to the word for "death" in several of these languages.

Etymology and language

In Mandarin Chinese, "four" is sì and "death" is sǐ, differing only in tone; in Cantonese the words are sei3 and sei2. Japanese has shi for both (from Chinese), though the alternative reading yon is often used to avoid the association, and Korean sa likewise means both "four" and "death". Similar near-homophones occur in Vietnamese and other languages influenced by Chinese numerals.

Manifestations

  • Buildings: Many buildings in East Asia omit floors numbered 4, 14, 24, and sometimes all floors from 40 to 49, or label them differently (for example, 3A instead of 4). Some hospitals have no room 4 or ward 4.
  • Products and prices: Goods may be sold in sets avoiding four; phone numbers, licence plates, and addresses containing many 4s are less desirable and can be cheaper, paralleling the premium paid for numbers containing the lucky digit 8.
  • Military and aviation: Some East Asian militaries avoid the digit in unit designations and tail numbers; airlines in the region have sometimes omitted row 4 references or seat lettering associated with the number.
  • Gifts and hospitality: Sets of four gifts are avoided in some contexts, and table settings in hospitals and care homes may be arranged to spare patients the number.

Cultural parallels

Tetraphobia is frequently compared with the Western avoidance of the number 13 (triskaidekaphobia) and of Friday the 13th. Conversely, the number 8 is considered especially lucky in Chinese culture because its name resembles the word for prosperity, and 9 was historically associated with the emperor and longevity in Japan and China.
