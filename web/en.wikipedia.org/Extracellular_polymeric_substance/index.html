Extracellular polymeric substance

Extracellular polymeric substances (EPSs) are natural polymers of high molecular weight secreted by microorganisms into their environment. Composed mainly of polysaccharides, together with proteins, lipids and extracellular DNA, EPSs form the structural matrix of biofilms, holding the cells together and attaching them to surfaces.

Composition and function

The EPS matrix is typically 50–90% of the organic matter of a biofilm. Its polysaccharides vary widely between species: alginate in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, cellulose in many enteric bacteria, and levan or dextran in others. The matrix retains water and nutrients, concentrates enzymes close to the cells, facilitates horizontal gene transfer, and shields the community from antibiotics, disinfectants, desiccation and grazing.

Significance

Because EPS makes biofilm bacteria far more tolerant of antimicrobial treatment than free-floating cells, it is central to problems such as chronic wound infections, biofouling of ships and pipelines, and membrane fouling in water treatment. Conversely, EPS production is exploited industrially: xanthan gum, gellan and dextran are commercial food and pharmaceutical thickeners, and the flocculation of activated sludge in wastewater treatment depends on EPS.
