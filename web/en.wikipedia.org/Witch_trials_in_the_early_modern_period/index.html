Witch trials in the early modern period

The witch trials of the early modern period were a series of prosecutions of people accused of witchcraft in Europe and European colonies between roughly 1400 and 1782, reaching their peak between about 1560 and 1630. Scholars estimate that around 40,000–60,000 people were executed, the large majority of them women.

Origins

The concept of the witch as a member of a devil-worshipping sect who flew to sabbaths and made pacts with demons crystallised in the 15th century, notably in the Alpine trials and in Heinrich Kramer's treatise Malleus Maleficarum (1486). Witchcraft prosecutions combined learned demonology with popular beliefs in harmful magic (maleficium).

Course and procedure

Trials occurred in waves, often during periods of religious conflict, war, plague or economic stress. Accused people — frequently older women, healers, or social outsiders — could be subjected to torture to extract confessions and the names of accomplices, fuelling chain-reaction panics such as the Würzburg and Bamberg trials in Germany and the Salem witch trials in New England (1692–93). Convicted witches were usually hanged in England and America, or strangled and burned in much of continental Europe and Scotland.

Decline

From the later 17th century, growing scepticism among judges and intellectuals, tighter rules of evidence, and centralisation of legal systems ended mass trials. The last executions in Europe occurred in the late 18th century; Anna Göldi, executed in Switzerland in 1782, is often called the last witch executed in Europe. Witchcraft remains a capital offence or a source of violent persecution in some parts of the world today.
