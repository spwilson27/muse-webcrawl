In Greek mythology, the Minotaur (Ancient Greek: Μινώταυρος, "the Bull of Minos") was a monster with the head and tail of a bull and the body of a man. He dwelt at the centre of the Labyrinth, an elaborate maze-like construction designed by the architect Daedalus and his son Icarus on the command of King Minos of Crete. The Minotaur was eventually killed by the Athenian hero Theseus.

Myth

Minos, competing with his brothers for the throne of Crete, prayed to Poseidon to send him a snow-white bull as a sign of divine support, promising to sacrifice it in the god's honour. Minos kept the magnificent Cretan Bull instead, and in punishment Poseidon caused Minos's wife, Pasiphaë, to fall in love with the animal. With the help of Daedalus's wooden cow, Pasiphaë conceived the Minotaur, whose personal name was Asterion.

Unable to sustain himself on ordinary food, the creature became savage and devoured humans. Minos imprisoned him in the Labyrinth. After Minos's son Androgeos was killed in Athens, Minos waged war and imposed a tribute: every year (or every nine years, in some versions) Athens had to send seven youths and seven maidens to be devoured by the Minotaur. In the third sending, Theseus volunteered, sailed to Crete, and with the help of Minos's daughter Ariadne — who gave him a ball of thread to retrace his path — entered the Labyrinth, killed the Minotaur, and led the Athenians out.

Interpretations

The myth has been read as reflecting Minoan Crete's maritime power, its bull cult and bull-leaping rituals (visible in the palace art of Knossos), and a possible historical tribute exacted from mainland communities. In classical art the Minotaur appears frequently from the 6th century BC, typically in combat with Theseus.

In later culture

The Minotaur has been a durable figure in Western art and literature: Dante places him in the Inferno guarding the circle of the violent; Picasso made the creature a personal emblem in works such as the Minotauromachy; and the figure recurs in modern fiction, film, and games, usually as an emblem of brute appetite imprisoned within a maze of human making.
