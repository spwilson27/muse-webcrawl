Christianisation of Anglo-Saxon England

The Christianisation of Anglo-Saxon England was the process by which the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms of early medieval England converted from Anglo-Saxon paganism to Christianity, a transformation that unfolded between the late 6th and the late 7th centuries, with its final stages extending into the 680s.

The Gregorian mission of 597 began the formal conversion of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms

The missions

The process began with the Gregorian mission: in 597 Augustine of Canterbury, sent by Pope Gregory the Great, arrived in the Kingdom of Kent and baptized King Æthelberht. A second stream of conversion came from the Irish church: from the monastery of Iona, Aidan founded Lindisfarne in 635 and evangelized Northumbria under King Oswald. Differences between the Roman and Irish traditions — notably over the dating of Easter and the form of the tonsure — were settled in favor of Roman practice at the Synod of Whitby in 664.

Conversion of the kingdoms

Conversion generally proceeded from the top down: missionary bishops targeted kings, whose baptism was followed, sometimes intermittently, by that of their courts and peoples. Relapses occurred — Kent, Essex, East Anglia, and Northumbria all saw periods of apostasy after the deaths of Christian kings. The last pagan Anglo-Saxon king, Arwald of the Isle of Wight, was killed in 686 when Cædwalla of Wessex conquered the island, an event Bede presents as completing the conversion of the English kingdoms.

Consequences

Christianisation brought literacy, Latin learning, church organization, and monumental stone architecture to Anglo-Saxon England. Monasteries such as Wearmouth-Jarrow, where the Venerable Bede wrote his Ecclesiastical History of the English People — the principal source for the conversion period — became centers of scholarship. Pagan practices persisted in folk custom for generations and were repeatedly condemned in law codes and penitentials even as the institutional conversion was completed.
