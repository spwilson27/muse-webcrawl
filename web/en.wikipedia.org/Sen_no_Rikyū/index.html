Sen no Rikyū

Sen no Rikyū (千利休, 1522 – April 21, 1591), also known simply as Rikyū, is considered the historical figure with the most profound influence on chanoyu, the Japanese "Way of Tea", particularly the tradition of wabi-cha. He was also the first to emphasize several key aspects of the ceremony, including rustic simplicity, directness of approach and honesty of self. Originating from the Sengoku period and the Azuchi–Momoyama period, these aspects of the tea ceremony persist. Rikyū is known by many names; for consistency, he will be referred to as Rikyū in this article.

There are three iemoto (schools) of Japanese tea directly descended from Rikyū: the Omotesenke, Urasenke, and Mushakōjisenke, all of which are dedicated to passing forward the teachings of their mutual family founder, Rikyū.

Early life

Rikyū was born in the city of Sakai, in present-day Osaka Prefecture, the son of a warehouse owner and fishmonger named Tanaka Yohei. He studied tea under the Sakai tea master Kitamuki Dōchin and later under Takeno Jōō, from whom he inherited the aesthetic of wabi—simplicity and imperfection. As a young man he took the Buddhist name Sōeki.

Tea master to the warlords

Rikyū became tea master to Oda Nobunaga and, after Nobunaga's death in 1582, to Toyotomi Hideyoshi, accompanying him on campaigns and at the Grand Kitano Tea Ceremony of 1587, where all tea practitioners were invited to participate. He designed the Tai-an teahouse at Myōki-an temple—a two-tatami room regarded as the archetype of the small rustic tea room (sōan)—and promoted the use of rough Raku tea bowls. Hideyoshi bestowed on him the name Rikyū and the title koji.

Death

In 1591, for reasons that remain debated—slights to Hideyoshi's vanity, political intrigue, or doctrinal dispute—Hideyoshi ordered Rikyū to commit seppuku. He died at his residence in Kyoto, composing death poems in Chinese and Japanese. His final tea gathering, held the day before his death, is one of the most famous episodes in tea history.

Legacy

Rikyū's principles of harmony (wa), respect (kei), purity (sei), and tranquility (jaku), drawn from his teacher's tradition, became the philosophical foundation of chanoyu. His seven rules ("Rikyū's Seven Precepts") are still taught in all schools descended from him.
