Confit

Confit (French pronunciation: [kɔ̃fi], from the French verb confire, "to preserve") is any type of food that is cooked slowly over a long period as a method of preservation. In meat cookery the term describes food — classically duck or goose legs from southwest France — that is salted, then poached slowly in its own rendered fat and stored covered in that fat.

Meat confit

To prepare confit de canard, duck legs are cured with salt (often with garlic, thyme and bay) for a day or more, then rinsed and submerged in melted duck fat and cooked at a low temperature — around 85–95 °C — for several hours until very tender. Cooled and stored under the fat, traditionally in earthenware crocks, the meat keeps for weeks or months. Before serving, the legs are reheated, frequently crisped in a hot pan or oven.

Fruit and other confits

In French usage confit also covers fruit preserved in sugar syrup (fruit confit, the basis of candied fruit) and condiments such as onion confit, in which vegetables are cooked down slowly in oil, butter or their own juices until jammy. Modern chefs have extended the technique to garlic, tomatoes, salmon cooked in olive oil, and many other ingredients, using "confit" loosely for anything poached gently in fat.

History

The method arose as a practical preservation technique in Gascony and the Périgord before refrigeration: the fat seal excluded air, and salt inhibited spoilage. Confit of duck and goose became emblematic dishes of southwest French cuisine, along with cassoulet, into which confit is often folded.
