Ancel Benjamin Keys (26 January 1904 – 20 November 2004) was an American physiologist who studied the influence of diet on health. He hypothesised that dietary saturated fat causes cardiovascular disease, and is credited with promoting the Mediterranean diet through his research and bestselling diet books. He founded the Laboratory of Physiological Hygiene at the University of Minnesota and directed major research efforts including the Minnesota Starvation Experiment and the Seven Countries Study.

Early life and early work

Keys earned degrees in economics and political science and then biology at Berkeley, and a PhD in oceanography and biology, before taking a second PhD in physiology at Cambridge. In the 1930s he led high-altitude physiology expeditions and, for the US Army during the Second World War, developed the K-ration, the lightweight field ration named after him.

Minnesota Starvation Experiment

In 1944–45, Keys directed the Minnesota Starvation Experiment, in which 36 conscientious objectors volunteered for semi-starvation, to study the physical and psychological effects of famine and guide relief feeding after the war. The results were published in The Biology of Human Starvation (1950).

Diet–heart research

From the 1950s Keys argued that blood cholesterol and dietary saturated fat were major causes of coronary heart disease. His Seven Countries Study, launched in 1958 and following some 12,763 men in 16 cohorts across seven countries, reported strong associations between saturated fat intake, serum cholesterol, and heart disease. With his wife Margaret, a biochemist, he popularised the Mediterranean diet in books such as Eat Well and Stay Well (1959), and appeared on the cover of Time magazine in 1961. His work strongly influenced dietary guidelines in the United States and elsewhere.

Criticism and legacy

Critics have charged that Keys selectively used country data and downplayed the role of sugar, pointing to his rivalry with the British physiologist John Yudkin, who blamed sugar. Defenders note that the broader evidence base for reducing saturated fat has held up reasonably well, while modern nutrition science has complicated the simple diet–heart picture. Keys retired to Pioppi, Italy — a village associated with the Mediterranean diet — and died in 2004, two months short of his 101st birthday.
