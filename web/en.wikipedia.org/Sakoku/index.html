Sakoku

Sakoku (Japanese: 鎖国, "chained country") was the isolationist foreign policy of the Japanese Tokugawa shogunate under which, for a period of 214 years from 1633 to 1853, Japan's relations and trade with other countries were severely limited and nearly all foreign nationals were barred from entering the country, on pain of death. Ordinary Japanese were likewise forbidden to leave.

The edicts

Between 1633 and 1639 the shogunate issued a series of edicts (the Kaikin maritime prohibitions) restricting foreign contact. Motivations included preventing the spread of Christianity, which the regime saw as subversive after the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–38; controlling foreign trade and the wealth of the southwestern domains; and consolidating Tokugawa authority. The Portuguese were expelled in 1639, and the Spanish had been excluded earlier.

Limited contact

Sakoku did not mean total isolation. Japan maintained regulated trade and diplomacy through four gateways: with the Dutch East India Company and Chinese merchants at Nagasaki (the Dutch confined to the artificial island of Dejima), with Korea via Tsushima domain, with the Ryukyu Kingdom via Satsuma domain, and with the Ainu via Matsumae domain in Ezo. Through the Dutch, Japan received European scientific and medical knowledge in the tradition known as Rangaku ("Dutch learning").

End of the policy

Foreign pressure mounted in the nineteenth century, with Russian, British, and American ships seeking provisions and trade. In 1853–54 Commodore Matthew Perry of the United States Navy arrived with warships and compelled the Convention of Kanagawa, opening Japanese ports to American vessels. Similar treaties with other powers followed, and the Tokugawa regime collapsed in the Meiji Restoration of 1868. Historians today debate the term "sakoku" itself, noting that Japan's foreign engagement, though restricted, was structured and deliberate rather than hermit-like.
