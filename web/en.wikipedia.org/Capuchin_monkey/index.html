Capuchin monkey

The capuchin monkeys are New World monkeys of the subfamily Cebinae, native to Central and South America. They are named after the Order of Friars Minor Capuchin, whose brown robes and hoods resemble the monkeys' colouring. Capuchins are considered among the most intelligent of the New World monkeys and are well known for their use of tools.

Taxonomy

Two genera are generally recognised: the gracile capuchins, Cebus, and the robust or tufted capuchins, Sapajus, which were separated on morphological and genetic grounds. Species include the white-headed capuchin (Cebus capucinus), the tufted capuchin (Sapajus apella), and the blond capuchin (Sapajus flavius), the latter critically endangered.

Description

Capuchins are small to medium-sized monkeys, weighing roughly 1.5 to 5 kilograms, with partly prehensile tails about as long as the body. Their fur ranges from black and brown to cream on the face, neck, and shoulders.

Behaviour

Capuchins live in troops of up to several dozen animals led by a dominant male. They are omnivorous, eating fruit, nuts, insects, small vertebrates, and eggs. Wild tufted capuchins in Brazil use stones as hammers and anvils to crack hard nuts — one of the best-documented examples of tool use in non-ape primates — and the behaviour appears to be socially learned and centuries old.

Relationship with humans

Capuchins have long appeared in entertainment as organ grinders' monkeys, and have been trained as service animals for people with disabilities. Their intelligence and trainability also make them common subjects in cognitive research.
