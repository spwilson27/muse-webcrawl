Anaglyph 3D

Anaglyph 3D is the stereoscopic 3D effect achieved by encoding each eye's image in different (usually chromatically opposite) colors, such as red and cyan. When viewed through "anaglyph glasses" with matching color filters — one lens red, the other cyan — each eye sees only its intended image, and the brain fuses the two views into a single three-dimensional scene.

History

The method was pioneered in the 1850s by Wilhelm Rollmann and Joseph D'Almeida and popularized in the late 19th century for printed images and magic-lantern slides. Anaglyphs enjoyed a major vogue in the 1950s for comic books and for some 3D films, and later found uses in scientific imaging, including NASA terrain imagery from Mars missions.

How it works

A stereo pair is filtered so that the left-eye view contains only the red channel and the right-eye view contains green and blue (cyan). The colored filters of the glasses block the opposite image from each eye. Because the method depends on color separation, it distorts color fidelity and can suffer from "ghosting" when the filters imperfectly block the other channel, and from retinal rivalry. Black-and-white originals reproduce best; images with strong red or cyan content work poorly.

Applications

Anaglyphs remain the cheapest way to present 3D on ordinary print or screens without special hardware, and are still used in textbooks, geology and microscopy imagery, and novelty publications.
