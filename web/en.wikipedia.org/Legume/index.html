Legume

A legume is a plant in the family Fabaceae (also called Leguminosae), or the fruit or seed of such a plant. In botany, the legume fruit is a dry pod that splits along two seams to release its seeds; in agriculture and nutrition, the term refers especially to the edible seeds—beans, peas, lentils, chickpeas, peanuts, and soybeans—collectively called pulses when harvested dry. The Fabaceae is one of the largest families of flowering plants, with about 19,000 species.

Nitrogen fixation

Most legumes live in symbiosis with nitrogen-fixing bacteria called rhizobia, which inhabit nodules on their roots and convert atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia usable by the plant. This makes legumes invaluable in crop rotation and intercropping: they enrich soil fertility and reduce the need for synthetic fertiliser, a role exploited since antiquity and formalised in systems such as the Norfolk four-course rotation.

Uses

Legumes have been cultivated for at least 10,000 years; lentils, peas, chickpeas, and fava beans were among the founder crops of Neolithic agriculture in the Fertile Crescent, and common beans, peanuts, and soybeans were domesticated independently in the Americas and East Asia. They are rich in protein, fibre, and micronutrients, and form dietary staples worldwide. Economically important products include soybean oil and meal, peanut products, alfalfa and clover forage, timber trees such as acacias and rosewood, and ornamentals such as wisteria and lupins.

Nutrition

Dry pulses typically provide 20–30% protein by weight, complementing cereal grains whose amino acid profile is weak in lysine. They are low in fat (except oilseeds like soybean and peanut) and have a low glycaemic index. Some legumes contain antinutrients such as lectins and phytic acid, largely removed by soaking and cooking; raw kidney beans are toxic without thorough boiling.
