Public prosecutor

[Placeholder image: A prosecutor addressing the court]

A public prosecutor is an official of the state or other public authority charged with conducting criminal prosecutions on behalf of the public. Unlike a private attorney hired by a client, the public prosecutor represents the community, and in many legal traditions is obliged to pursue justice rather than victory alone—including duties to disclose exculpatory evidence and to decline cases that lack sufficient proof. The prosecutor's office decides whether to bring charges, what charges to file, and often how cases are resolved through plea bargaining.

Role and functions

Typical functions include directing or supervising police investigations (in civil-law systems), presenting evidence in court, examining witnesses, arguing for conviction and sentence, and handling appeals. In inquisitorial systems, prosecutors (such as the French procureur de la République or the German Staatsanwalt) form part of a hierarchical public ministry and may supervise investigating judges. In common-law systems, prosecution services (such as England and Wales's Crown Prosecution Service, created in 1986) or district attorneys in the United States—usually locally elected—exercise wide discretion in charging.

History

Public prosecution developed as states replaced private prosecution and composition systems. France's Napoleonic Code of Criminal Instruction (1808) placed the ministère public at the centre of prosecution, a model exported across Europe and Latin America. In England, prosecutions were historically brought by victims or the police until the Prosecution of Offences Act 1985 established an independent Crown Prosecution Service. In the United States, elected local prosecutors became characteristic in the 19th century, while federal prosecutions are conducted by United States Attorneys appointed under the Department of Justice.

Independence and ethics

The office carries distinctive ethical burdens: the prosecutor must balance zeal with fairness, disclose evidence favourable to the defence (the Brady rule in the United States), avoid conflicts of interest, and refrain from pursuing charges for improper motives. Debate surrounds prosecutorial discretion, plea bargaining's dominance (over 90 per cent of U.S. convictions result from guilty pleas), wrongful convictions caused by suppression of evidence, and the degree of political independence prosecutors should enjoy from governments.
