Carolingian art

Periodc. 780 – c. 900
RegionFrankish (Carolingian) Empire
PatronCharlemagne and his successors
Key mediaIlluminated manuscripts, ivory, metalwork, fresco

Carolingian art comes from the Frankish Empire in the period of roughly 120 years from about 780 to 900 — the "Carolingian Renaissance" — during the reigns of Charlemagne and his immediate heirs. It was produced by and for the court circle and a group of associated monasteries, and consciously revived classical Mediterranean, Early Christian, and Byzantine styles while also developing innovations that set the basis for Romanesque and Gothic art.

An evangelist portrait from a Carolingian Gospel book.

Illuminated manuscripts

The most surviving works are illuminated manuscripts. Court scriptoria produced luxurious Gospel books, psalters, and sacramentaries, such as the Godescalc Evangelistary (781–783), the earliest known work of the court school, the Ebbo Gospels with their animated, sketchy brushwork, and the Utrecht Psalter, whose lively pen drawings imitate a Late Antique model. The period also standardized the legible script known as Carolingian minuscule, the ancestor of modern lowercase type.

Other media

Carolingian workshops produced fine ivory carvings, goldsmith work such as the cover of the Lindau Gospels, bronze casting (including the equestrian statuette of a king, possibly Charlemagne, now in the Louvre), and large-scale fresco cycles of which fragments survive at Mals and elsewhere. The Palatine Chapel at Aachen, modelled on Byzantine and Ravenna precedents, is the outstanding surviving Carolingian building.

Legacy

By reviving classical book production and naturalistic figure styles, Carolingian artists created much of the visual vocabulary of medieval Western art; when production declined amid the invasions and civil wars of the late 9th century, the monastic centres preserved its achievements for the Ottonian revival that followed.
