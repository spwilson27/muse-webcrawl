Shōchū (Japanese: 焼酎) is a Japanese distilled beverage. It is typically distilled from rice (kome), barley (mugi), sweet potatoes (satsuma-imo), buckwheat (soba), or brown sugar (kokutō), though it is sometimes produced from other ingredients such as chestnut or sesame. Unlike sake, which is brewed, shōchū is distilled, usually in a single pot-still run for the premium honkaku ("authentic") style.

Production

Fermentation uses kōji mold — white, yellow, or black kōji — to convert starches to sugars before yeast fermentation and distillation. Kyushu, especially Kagoshima and Ōita prefectures, is the heartland of production; sweet-potato shōchū from Kagoshima may be labelled "Satsuma shōchū" as a protected regional name.

Serving

Shōchū is drunk neat, on the rocks, diluted with hot water (oyuwari) or cold water, or mixed in cocktails such as the chūhai. Its alcohol content is commonly around 25%, lower than whisky or vodka but stronger than wine or sake.
