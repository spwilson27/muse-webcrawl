Merger arbitrage

Merger arbitrage, also known as risk arbitrage, is an event-driven investment strategy that capitalises on the spread between a target company's share price after a takeover announcement and the price offered by the acquirer. It is a staple strategy of hedge funds and specialised arbitrage desks.

How it works

When a cash takeover is announced at, say, $50 per share, the target typically trades slightly below the offer — for example at $48 — reflecting the time value of money and the risk the deal fails. The arbitrageur buys at $48 and, if the deal completes, receives $50, earning the spread. In stock-for-stock deals the arbitrageur buys the target and short-sells the acquirer's shares in the exchange ratio, locking in the spread provided completion occurs on the announced terms.

Risks

The strategy's returns are small but relatively steady, while its losses can be sudden and large: if a deal breaks — because of antitrust objections, financing problems, shareholder votes or a deteriorating target business — the target's price may fall back to or below its pre-announcement level. This asymmetric profile is sometimes compared to collecting pennies in front of a steamroller. Arbitrageurs therefore analyse regulatory risk, deal protections such as break fees, shareholder sentiment and the strategic importance of the acquisition.

Role in markets

Merger arbitrageurs provide liquidity to shareholders who prefer to sell immediately rather than wait for completion, and the size of the spread is a market-based estimate of the probability and timing of closing. Famous practitioners include early twentieth-century speculators and, later, dedicated event-driven funds.
