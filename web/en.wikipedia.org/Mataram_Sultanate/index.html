Mataram Sultanate

The Mataram Sultanate (Javanese: ꦤꦒꦫꦶ ꦩꦠꦫꦩ꧀, Nagari Mataram) was the last major indigenous Islamic state in Java, ruling much of the island from the late sixteenth century until 1755. Founded by Danang Sutawijaya, who took the title Panembahan Senapati, it reached its greatest extent under Sultan Agung (r. 1613–1645) and was ultimately partitioned by the Dutch East India Company (VOC) into the courts of Surakarta (Solo) and Yogyakarta.

Kotagede, Mataram's first capital, preserves the royal cemetery of its founders.

Rise

Mataram began as a fief granted to Ki Ageng Pamanahan for services to the Sultanate of Pajang; his son Sutawijaya (Senapati) threw off Pajang's overlordship and built a new kingdom in south-central Java with its capital at Kotagede. Senapati and his son Panembahan Hanyakrawati expanded Mataram's territory by diplomacy and conquest over the rival principalities of Java.

Sultan Agung

Under Sultan Agung, Mataram conquered most of Java: Surabaya and the eastern salient submitted by 1625, and the kingdom taxed the rich rice lands and north-coast ports. Agung twice besieged Dutch Batavia (1628 and 1629) but failed to take it, the failures a turning point in relations with the VOC. He also reshaped Javanese culture — creating the Javanese calendar by fusing Saka and Hijri reckoning, patronising literature such as the Sastra Gendhing, and styling himself "Sultan". His tomb at Imogiri remains a pilgrimage site.

Decline and partition

After Agung's death in 1645, his successor Amangkurat I moved the capital to Plered and ruled with increasing brutality, provoking rebellions, notably the Trunajaya revolt (1674–1681), which drove the king from his palace; the VOC intervened in exchange for territorial and commercial concessions. Further succession wars in the eighteenth century made Mataram a Dutch client. In 1755, by the Treaty of Giyanti, the sultanate was divided between Pakubuwono III at Surakarta and Mangkubumi — who took the title Hamengkubuwono I — at Yogyakarta, a partition whose successor courts survive in Indonesia today.
