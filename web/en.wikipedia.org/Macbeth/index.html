Macbeth (full title The Tragedy of Macbeth) is a tragedy by William Shakespeare, estimated to have been first performed in 1606. It dramatises the damaging physical and psychological effects of political ambition on those who seek power. The shortest of Shakespeare's tragedies, it tells the story of a brave Scottish general named Macbeth who receives a prophecy from a trio of witches that one day he will become King of Scotland.

Macbeth and Banquo meet the three witches, who hail Macbeth as future king.

Plot summary

Consumed by ambition and spurred to action by his wife, Lady Macbeth, Macbeth murders King Duncan and takes the Scottish throne for himself. He is then wracked with guilt and paranoia. Forced to commit ever more murders to protect himself from enmity and suspicion, he soon becomes a tyrannical ruler. The bloodbath and consequent civil war swiftly take Macbeth and Lady Macbeth into the realms of madness and death: Lady Macbeth dies, apparently by suicide, and Macbeth is killed by Macduff — a man, as the witches' equivocal prophecies had promised, "not of woman born" in the usual way, having been delivered by Caesarean section. Malcolm, Duncan's son, becomes king.

Sources and themes

Shakespeare's source was Holinshed's Chronicles (1587), though he altered the story considerably, partly in compliment to King James I, a descendant of Banquo in the chronicle tradition and a patron of Shakespeare's company. The play explores ambition, fate and free will, equivocation, and the corrupting power of unchecked desire. Many actors consider the play cursed and avoid naming it inside a theatre, calling it "the Scottish play" instead.
