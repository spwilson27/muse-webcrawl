Loaded language

Loaded language (also called loaded terms, emotive language or high-inference language) is rhetoric that uses words and phrases with strong positive or negative connotations beyond their literal meaning, in order to influence an audience. Loaded language appeals to emotion rather than argument, and is a staple of propaganda, advertising and polemical political speech.

Examples and mechanisms

Describing the same person as a "freedom fighter" or a "terrorist", or the same policy as "pro-life" or "anti-choice", frames the subject before any evidence is presented. Words such as "regime", "inhumane", "stupid" or "corrupt" carry evaluative baggage; so do flattering terms such as "heroic", "common sense" or "reform" when applied to one's own side. Because the connotation does much of the persuasive work, loaded language can smuggle a conclusion into the very description of an issue — a technique related to begging the question.

Loaded questions

A related device is the loaded question, which contains an unjustified assumption: "Have you stopped cheating on your taxes?" presupposes past cheating, so either a yes or no answer incriminates the respondent. Loaded questions are a classic example of a complex question fallacy.

Neutral alternatives

Journalistic and encyclopedic style guides generally advise replacing loaded words with neutral vocabulary — saying "said" rather than "admitted" or "claimed", and attributing value judgements to their sources rather than stating them in the editorial voice.
