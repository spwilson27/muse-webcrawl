Jean Bernard Léon Foucault (18 September 1819 – 11 February 1868) was a French physicist best known for his demonstration of the Earth's rotation with the Foucault pendulum, a device he first exhibited in 1851. He also made accurate measurements of the speed of light, discovered eddy currents (also called "Foucault currents"), and contributed to the development of the gyroscope, which he named.

Early life

Foucault was born in Paris, the son of a publisher. Initially educated at home, he began medical studies but abandoned them — reportedly owing to an aversion to blood — for physics. Largely self-taught, he developed remarkable experimental skill, first working as assistant to Alfred Donné on microscopy lectures and with Hippolyte Fizeau on the intensity of sunlight and early photography (daguerreotypy).

The speed of light

In 1850 Foucault used a rotating-mirror apparatus — improving on Fizeau's toothed-wheel method — to show that light travels more slowly in water than in air, a result that strongly supported the wave theory of light over the corpuscular theory. In 1862 he determined the speed of light to be about 298,000 km/s, within about 0.6 per cent of the modern value.

The pendulum

In February 1851 Foucault announced that a long pendulum, free to swing in any vertical plane, would appear to have its plane of swing rotate — a direct, tabletop-scale demonstration of the rotation of the Earth. After a first observation in his cellar and a demonstration at the Paris Observatory, he staged the celebrated public experiment beneath the dome of the Panthéon, using a 28-kilogram bob suspended on a 67-metre wire. The pendulum's plane rotates at a rate depending on latitude (about 11 degrees per hour in Paris), and its swing knocked over small piles of sand arranged on the floor, to the audience's amazement. Foucault pendulums are now displayed in science museums around the world, including at the United Nations headquarters.

Other work

Foucault invented the gyroscope in 1852, using it in attempts to detect the Earth's rotation. He described eddy currents induced in conductors moving in magnetic fields, developed the Foucault knife-edge test for telescope mirrors (still used by mirror-makers), devised photometric methods, and improved the regulators (governors) of arc lamps and steam engines. From 1855 he was physicist at the Imperial Observatory in Paris.

Death and legacy

Foucault died in Paris in 1868, probably of multiple sclerosis, at the age of 48. His name is one of the 72 names of scientists and engineers inscribed on the Eiffel Tower.
