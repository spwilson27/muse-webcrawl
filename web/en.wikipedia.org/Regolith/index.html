Regolith is a blanket of unconsolidated, loose, heterogeneous superficial deposits covering solid rock. It includes dust, broken rocks, and other related materials and is present on Earth, the Moon, Mars, some asteroids, and other terrestrial planets and moons.

Etymology

The term was coined in 1897 by the American geologist George P. Merrill, from the Greek words rhegos (blanket) and lithos (rock). Merrill defined it as the layer of loose mantle material resting upon bedrock, produced largely by weathering.

On Earth

Terrestrial regolith includes soil, saprolite (chemically weathered bedrock), alluvium, and other unconsolidated material near the surface. Its thickness ranges from a few centimetres to hundreds of metres in deeply weathered tropical terrains. Regolith hosts most terrestrial groundwater, supports plant life, and is a major source of construction materials and ore deposits, including lateritic nickel and bauxite.

Lunar regolith

The Moon's surface is covered by a layer of regolith typically 4–5 metres thick in the maria and up to 10–15 metres in the highlands. It formed over billions of years by micrometeorite impacts that pulverised the surface rock. Lunar regolith contains fragments of rock, mineral grains, and glassy agglutinates welded by impact melting. It has no organic matter or free water, is highly abrasive, and posed engineering challenges during the Apollo programme; it is also studied as a potential source of oxygen, metals, and helium-3.

Martian regolith

Mars is covered by a widespread layer of dust and broken rock produced by impact cratering, wind action, and chemical weathering. Martian regolith is rich in iron oxides, giving the planet its red colour, and contains perchlorate salts detected by landers. It is of interest both as a construction and radiation-shielding material for future human missions and as a possible habitat for past or present microbial life.

Importance

Regolith science matters for agriculture, civil engineering, mineral exploration, groundwater management, and planetary exploration. For spaceflight, in-situ resource utilisation (ISRU) concepts propose using local regolith to make building materials, extract water and oxygen, and shield habitats from radiation.
