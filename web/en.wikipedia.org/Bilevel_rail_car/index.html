Bilevel rail car

A bilevel car (American English) or double-decker train (British English and Canadian English) is a type of rail car that has two levels of passenger accommodation as opposed to one, increasing passenger capacity — in theory up to 90% on a given train length. In some countries such vehicles are commonly referred to as dostos, from the German Doppelstockwagen.

Design

Bilevel designs vary: some have two full decks, others a main deck with intermediate entry level and split upper and lower seating areas — the Bombardier BiLevel design common in North American commuter service — while "gallery" cars used around Chicago have an open mezzanine upper level overlooking the main floor. True double-deck trains require generous loading-gauge clearances, which is why Britain, with its restricted tunnels and bridges, has almost never used them.

History

Double-deck carriages appeared in France as early as the 19th century, and in the United States the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad introduced gallery cars in 1950. Amtrak's Superliner cars, introduced in the late 1970s, brought bilevel comfort to long-distance trains in the western United States. Double-deck trains are now standard on many busy commuter and regional networks in Germany, France, the Netherlands, Australia (Sydney), and Israel.

Advantages and drawbacks

Bilevel cars add capacity without lengthening trains or platforms, but they complicate accessibility, lengthen station dwell times because passengers funnel through limited stairways and doors, and may exceed the loading gauge on older routes.
