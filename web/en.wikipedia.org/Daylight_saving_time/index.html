Daylight saving time

Daylight saving time (DST), also called summer time in some regions, is the practice of advancing clocks during the warmer months so that darkness falls at a later clock time. Typically, clocks are set forward by one hour in spring and set back by one hour in autumn. The practice is intended to make better use of daylight in the evenings, though its benefits and drawbacks remain debated.

History

Although Benjamin Franklin humorously suggested in 1784 that Parisians could save candles by rising earlier, the modern scheme was first proposed by New Zealand entomologist George Hudson in 1895 and promoted in Britain by William Willett from 1907. DST was first implemented nationally by Germany and Austria-Hungary in 1916, during the First World War, as a coal-saving measure, and was quickly adopted by other belligerents including Britain and the United States.

Practice around the world

As of the 2020s, DST is used in most of North America and Europe and in parts of South America, Oceania and the Middle East, covering roughly a third of the world's population; most countries near the equator and in Asia and Africa do not observe it. In the United States it runs from the second Sunday in March to the first Sunday in November; in the European Union, from the last Sunday in March to the last Sunday in October. Several jurisdictions, including Russia, Turkey and Iceland, have abandoned seasonal clock changes and remain on one time year-round.

Effects and debate

Studies of DST's energy savings find small or inconsistent effects, since reduced lighting use is offset by increased heating and air conditioning. Research has found modest increases in traffic accidents and heart attacks immediately following the spring transition, attributed to sleep disruption, while evening daylight is associated with reduced crime and more outdoor activity. These findings fuel recurring legislative proposals to abolish the clock changes, either by permanent standard time or permanent daylight time.
