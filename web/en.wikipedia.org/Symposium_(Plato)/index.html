The Symposium (Ancient Greek: Sympósion) is a philosophical text by Plato, dated c. 385–370 BCE. It depicts a friendly contest of extemporary speeches given by a group of notable Athenian men at a banquet, each offering an encomium in praise of Eros, the god of love and desire. The participants are the tragic poet Agathon (the host), the comic playwright Aristophanes, the physician Eryximachus, Pausanias, Phaedrus, and Socrates. It is among Plato's most celebrated dialogues and a foundational text for the philosophy of love.

The speeches

  • Phaedrus praises Eros as the oldest of the gods and the inspiration of noble sacrifice.
  • Pausanias distinguishes "common" Aphrodite Pandemos from "heavenly" Aphrodite Urania, and bodily from spiritual love.
  • Eryximachus extends Eros to a universal harmony of opposites, as in medicine and music.
  • Aristophanes tells the famous myth of originally double human beings, split by Zeus, whose halves forever seek reunion — an origin-story for sexual orientation.
  • Agathon praises Eros as the youngest, most beautiful, and most virtuous of gods.
  • Socrates, reporting the teaching of the priestess Diotima, argues that Eros is neither god nor mortal but a spirit intermediate between them, born of Resource and Poverty; love is desire for the good and for immortality, culminating in the "ladder of love" ascending from one beautiful body to Beauty itself.

The dialogue closes with the drunken arrival of Alcibiades, who delivers an encomium not of Eros but of Socrates himself, likening him to a Silenus figure — ugly without, divine within.

Legacy

The Symposium gave rise to the notion of "Platonic love" (a later, Renaissance coinage, especially via Ficino's commentary) and remains central to discussions of desire, beauty, and philosophy as a way of life.
