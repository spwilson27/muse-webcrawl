G.I. Bill

The G.I. Bill, formally the Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, is a United States law that provided a range of benefits for returning Second World War veterans, known as G.I.s. Signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on 22 June 1944, it is regarded as one of the most significant pieces of social legislation in American history, helping to build the post-war middle class.

Benefits

The Act funded tuition and living expenses for veterans attending high school, college, or vocational school; provided low-cost mortgages guaranteed by the federal government; offered low-interest loans to start businesses or farms; and gave up to a year of unemployment compensation (the "52–20 clause": $20 per week for 52 weeks). By 1956, roughly 7.8 million veterans had used the education benefits, and millions more bought homes with VA-backed loans.

Impact and criticisms

The G.I. Bill dramatically expanded higher education and suburban homeownership, and scholars credit it with creating much of America's post-war prosperity and professional workforce. However, its benefits were administered through local and state institutions, and discriminatory practices — including segregation in colleges and redlining in mortgage lending — meant Black veterans and other minority veterans were often denied equal access, contributing to racial wealth gaps. Women veterans also faced obstacles in claiming benefits.

Later bills

The name has been applied to successor programmes for later generations: the Korean War G.I. Bill (1952), the Vietnam-era Veterans' Readjustment Benefits Act (1966), the Montgomery G.I. Bill (1984), the Post-9/11 G.I. Bill (2008), which added housing allowances and transferable benefits, and the Forever G.I. Bill (2017), which removed the 15-year limit on using benefits for recent veterans.
