SOS

SOS, known in Morse code as · · · – – – · · ·, is a Morse code distress signal used internationally. It was first adopted by the German government in radio regulations effective 1 April 1905, and became the worldwide standard under the second International Radiotelegraphic Convention, which signed it in 1906 and made it effective in 1908. SOS remained the maritime radio distress signal until 1999, when it was replaced by the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System.

History

The signal was chosen because its Morse pattern — three dots, three dashes, three dots, run together as a single procedural signal — is unmistakable and easy to send and receive, even under poor conditions or by an untrained operator. It replaced earlier conventions such as the Marconi company's CQD. Popular backronyms such as "Save Our Souls" and "Save Our Ship" were attached to the letters only later; the signal was never an abbreviation. The sinking of RMS Titanic in 1912 famously involved both CQD and SOS transmissions.

Modern use

Although formal maritime use ended with the phase-out of Morse radiotelegraphy, SOS survives as a universal visual distress signal: three short flashes, three long flashes, and three short flashes from a torch or signal mirror are recognised worldwide as a call for help.
