Stereochemistry

Stereochemistry, a subdiscipline of chemistry, studies the spatial arrangement of atoms within molecules and the effects of that arrangement on the molecules' chemical and physical properties. A branch of stereochemistry is chirality, which deals with molecules that are not superposable on their mirror images.

History

The field originated with observations of optical activity: Jean-Baptiste Biot showed in 1815 that certain organic substances rotate polarized light, and Louis Pasteur in 1848 manually separated mirror-image crystals of sodium ammonium tartrate, founding molecular chirality. In 1874 Jacobus Henricus van 't Hoff and Joseph Le Bel independently proposed the tetrahedral carbon atom, explaining how stereoisomers arise.

Stereoisomerism

Stereoisomers have the same molecular formula and the same sequence of bonded atoms (constitution) but differ in the three-dimensional orientations of their atoms in space. Enantiomers are non-superposable mirror images of each other, identical in most physical properties but interacting differently with other chiral substances — which is why the two enantiomers of a drug can have very different biological effects, a fact made tragically famous by thalidomide. Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images, and include cis–trans (E/Z) isomers around double bonds.

Notation and methods

Absolute configuration at a stereocentre is described by the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog (R/S) system, double bond geometry by the E/Z system, and relative configuration historically by D/L and erythro/threo labels. Conformational analysis, developed by Odd Hassel and Derek Barton (Nobel Prize 1969), studies the shapes molecules adopt by rotation about single bonds and their energy differences.

Importance

Stereochemistry is crucial in pharmacology, biochemistry, and materials science, because living systems are themselves chiral and respond differently to different stereoisomers. Modern asymmetric synthesis — recognized by the 2001 Nobel Prize to Knowles, Noyori, and Sharpless — allows chemists to produce single enantiomers on an industrial scale.
