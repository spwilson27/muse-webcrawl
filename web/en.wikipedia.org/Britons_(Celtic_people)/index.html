Britons (Celtic people)

The Britons, also known as the Celtic Britons or ancient Britons, were the Celtic peoples who inhabited Great Britain from at least the Iron Age into the early Middle Ages. They spoke the Common Brittonic language, ancestral to modern Welsh, Cornish, and Breton, and were organised into numerous tribal kingdoms such as the Catuvellauni, Iceni, Brigantes, and Silures. Their descendants survive chiefly in the peoples of Wales, Cornwall, and Brittany, whose emigrant Britons colonised Armorica in the early medieval period.

Roman Britain

Greek and Roman writers described the Britons, and Julius Caesar invaded in 55–54 BC before the emperor Claudius began the full conquest in AD 43. Over the following decades most of lowland Britain became the Roman province of Britannia; resistance included the revolt of Boudica of the Iceni in AD 60–61, which destroyed Colchester, London, and St Albans before Roman forces suppressed it. Roman rule brought towns, roads, villas, and Hadrian's Wall, but Brittonic culture and language persisted, especially in the upland north and west.

After Rome

When Roman administration ended around 410, the Britons fragmented into competing kingdoms. Anglo-Saxon settlement and expansion from the fifth century pushed Brittonic polities westward and northward — to Wales, Cornwall, Cumbria, and Strathclyde — a period preserved in the traditions of figures such as Vortigern and Ambrosius Aurelianus and the semi-legendary King Arthur. The Britons' languages and institutions endured in Wales and Cornwall, while mass migration across the Channel created Brittany, "Little Britain," in what is now northwestern France.
