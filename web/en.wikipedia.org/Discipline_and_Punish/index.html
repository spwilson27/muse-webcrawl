Discipline and Punish

Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the Prison (French: Surveiller et punir: Naissance de la prison) is a 1975 book by the French philosopher Michel Foucault. It is an analysis of the social and theoretical mechanisms behind the changes that occurred in Western penal systems during the modern age, based on historical documents from France.

Argument

The book opens with a gruesome contrast: the public torture and execution of the regicide Robert-François Damiens in 1757, and, eighty years later, a minute timetable for a house of young prisoners. Foucault asks why punishment shifted so completely, so quickly, from spectacular violence against the body to the hidden, regulated discipline of the soul.

He argues the prison was not adopted primarily for humanitarian reasons. Rather, punishment became part of a wider "disciplinary" technology of power — hierarchical observation, normalising judgement, and examination — that also spread through schools, barracks, hospitals, and factories. Drawing on Jeremy Bentham's Panopticon, a prison design in which inmates never know whether they are being watched and so police themselves, Foucault presents panopticism as the emblem of modern disciplinary society.

Influence

The book introduced key concepts of Foucault's theory of power — biopower, disciplinary institutions, and the carceral system — and has been enormously influential in criminology, sociology, philosophy, and surveillance studies. Critics have questioned its historical accuracy and its sweeping claims, but it remains a foundational text on the modern prison and the politics of punishment.
