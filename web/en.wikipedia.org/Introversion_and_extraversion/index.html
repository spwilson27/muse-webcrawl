Introversion and extraversion

Introversion and extraversion (commonly spelled extroversion) are central concepts in personality theory. Extraversion tends to be manifested in outgoing, talkative, energetic behaviour, whereas introversion is manifested in more reflective and reserved behaviour. Rather than a binary type, the traits form a continuum, and most people fall somewhere in the middle — sometimes described as ambiverts.

History

The terms were popularised by Carl Jung in his 1921 work Psychological Types, where introversion described an attitude oriented toward inner experience and extraversion an attitude oriented toward the external world. Hans Eysenck later grounded extraversion in cortical arousal theory, proposing that introverts have higher baseline arousal and therefore avoid overstimulation. The trait became one of the Big Five personality dimensions, encompassing facets such as sociability, assertiveness, activity, and positive emotionality.

Measurement and correlates

Extraversion is measured by inventories such as the NEO-PI-R and the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire. It correlates with reward sensitivity, positive affect, larger social networks, and leadership emergence. Introversion is associated with preference for quieter environments and deep, focused work; it is distinct from shyness, which involves anxiety about social judgement.

Biology and culture

Twin studies suggest moderate heritability. Dopaminergic reward pathways have been implicated in extraverted behaviour. Cultural norms vary: individualistic cultures often value extraversion more highly, and average trait levels differ modestly between nations.
