RJR Nabisco, Inc., was an American conglomerate formed in 1985 by the merger of Nabisco Brands and the R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Company. It combined leading cigarette brands — Camel, Winston, and Salem — with famous food brands such as Oreo, Ritz Crackers, and Chips Ahoy!. The company is best remembered for its 1988 leveraged buyout (LBO) by the private equity firm Kohlberg Kravis Roberts & Co. (KKR), then the largest buyout in history, immortalized in the book and film Barbarians at the Gate.

Formation

R.J. Reynolds, founded in 1875 in Winston-Salem, North Carolina, had diversified into foods and other industries as Reynolds Industries. In 1985, under chief executive F. Ross Johnson, it merged with Nabisco Brands — itself the product of the National Biscuit Company and Standard Brands — to create RJR Nabisco. Johnson, a flamboyant dealmaker, moved the headquarters to Atlanta and then New York, and spent lavishly on corporate perks and ventures such as the "Premier" smokeless cigarette, which consumed hundreds of millions of dollars and failed.

The 1988 leveraged buyout

In October 1988, Johnson proposed a management buyout of RJR Nabisco at $75 per share. The announcement triggered a celebrated bidding war between Johnson's group (backed by Shearson Lehman Hutton) and KKR, led by Henry Kravis and George Roberts. KKR won with a bid of $109 per share, valuing the deal at about $25 billion (roughly $31 billion including assumed debt) — by far the largest LBO ever at the time. The contest, featuring extravagant fees, bruised egos, and frantic Wall Street maneuvering, was chronicled by Bryan Burrough and John Helyar in Barbarians at the Gate: The Fall of RJR Nabisco (1989), later adapted as an HBO television film.

Later history

Burdened with debt, RJR Nabisco sold off many assets, including its international tobacco business (sold to Japan Tobacco in 1999) and Del Monte and other food units. In 1999, under pressure from investor Carl Icahn and facing tobacco litigation, the company split: the food business, Nabisco, was spun off and subsequently acquired by Philip Morris (Kraft) in 2000, while the domestic tobacco business became R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Holdings. The RJR Nabisco name thus disappeared, ending one of the most famous corporate sagas of the 1980s.
