Jerome

Jerome (Latin: Eusebius Sophronius Hieronymus; c. 347 – 30 September 420), also known as Saint Jerome, was a Christian priest, confessor, theologian and historian from Dalmatia. He is best known for his translation of the Bible into Latin, known as the Vulgate, which became the standard Latin text of Scripture in the Western Church for more than a thousand years.

Life

Born at Stridon, on the border of Dalmatia and Pannonia, Jerome studied in Rome under the grammarian Aelius Donatus and became a master of Latin, Greek and Hebrew. After travels through Gaul and the East, he spent several years as a hermit in the desert of Chalcis in Syria. He was ordained a priest at Antioch and later served as secretary to Pope Damasus I in Rome, who commissioned his revision of the Latin Bible.

After Damasus's death, Jerome settled in Bethlehem in 386, founding a monastery where he lived for the rest of his life, writing commentaries, translations and polemical works, and corresponding with a wide circle of students and patrons, including the Roman noblewoman Paula.

Works

Jerome's Vulgate was largely a fresh translation from the Hebrew Old Testament and the Greek New Testament. His other writings include biblical commentaries, the De viris illustribus catalogue of Christian authors, chronicles, and hundreds of letters, which are a major source for the social and religious life of his age.

Legacy

Jerome is recognised as a saint and Doctor of the Church in Roman Catholicism and is venerated in Eastern Orthodoxy and Anglicanism. He is the patron saint of translators, librarians and encyclopedists. In art he is often depicted as a cardinal, or as a half-clad hermit in the desert accompanied by a lion.
