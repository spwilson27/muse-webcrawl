Brown rat

The brown rat (Rattus norvegicus), also known as the common rat, street rat, sewer rat, wharf rat, Hanover rat, or Norway rat, is a widespread species of common rat. One of the largest muroids, it is a brown or grey rodent with a head-and-body length of up to 28 cm (11 in) and a tail slightly shorter than that. It weighs between 140 and 500 g (4.9 and 17.6 oz).

Origin and distribution

The brown rat likely originated in northern China and neighbouring areas. It has now spread to all continents except Antarctica, and is the dominant rat in Europe and much of North America. Despite its scientific name meaning "Norwegian rat", the species did not come from Norway; the name was coined by John Berkenhout in 1769 on the mistaken assumption that the rats had arrived in England on Norwegian ships.

Biology and behaviour

Brown rats are nocturnal, excellent swimmers and diggers, and live in hierarchical social colonies occupying burrow systems. They are omnivorous generalists, eating almost anything. Females are highly fecund: a litter averages about eight pups, and a female can produce several litters a year, with a gestation of about three weeks.

Relationship with humans

The brown rat is a major agricultural and urban pest, consuming and contaminating stored food, damaging structures by gnawing, and spreading diseases including leptospirosis, rat-bite fever, and (via fleas) historically plague — although plague is more closely associated with the black rat. Domesticated brown rats (fancy rats) are kept as pets, and albino strains are the standard laboratory rat used in biomedical research.
