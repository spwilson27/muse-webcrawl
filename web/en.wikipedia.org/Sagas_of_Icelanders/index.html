The Sagas of Icelanders (Icelandic: Íslendingasögur), also known as the family sagas, are a group of Old Norse prose narratives written in Iceland in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. They recount the lives of the first generations of Icelandic settlers and their descendants, with most of the action set in the period from the settlement of Iceland around 870 to about 1030, the so-called Saga Age.

Content and themes

The roughly forty surviving sagas follow individual families across several generations, interweaving genealogy, feuds, lawsuits, love affairs, voyages and killings. Although written centuries after the events they describe, they combine oral tradition with the literary artistry of anonymous authors. Recurring concerns include honour, fate, loyalty and the workings of feud in a commonwealth without an executive authority.

Major sagas

  • Egils saga — the life of the Viking warrior-poet Egill Skallagrímsson
  • Njáls saga — the longest and most celebrated, centred on Njáll Þorgeirsson and Gunnar Hámundarson
  • Laxdæla saga — a sweeping family chronicle with strong female characters
  • Eyrbyggja saga, Grettis saga, Gísla saga and Hrafnkels saga

Manuscripts and preservation

The sagas survive in vellum manuscripts such as Möðruvallabók and in later paper copies; many were recorded by Icelandic scribes and preserved in farmhouses for centuries before being collected by scholars such as Árni Magnússon.
