Thrombolites are clotted, accretionary sedimentary structures formed in shallow water by the trapping, binding and cementation of sediment by microbial communities, especially cyanobacteria. From the Greek thrombos ("clot"), the name reflects their internal structure of irregular clots (mesoclots) rather than the fine layers seen in stromatolites.

Structure and formation

A thrombolite consists of millimetre- to centimetre-scale clots of calcified microbial filaments embedded in sediment and cement. Photosynthesising microbes precipitate calcium carbonate as they grow, slowly building domes and columns that may reach a metre or more across. Because their internal fabric is clotted rather than laminated, thrombolites are distinguished from stromatolites (layered) and dendrolites (dendritic) within the broader group of microbialites.

Distribution

Fossil thrombolites became abundant in the late Neoproterozoic and early Cambrian seas, where they formed reefs, and are used as indicators of ancient shallow-marine environments. Living examples are rare: notable sites include Lake Clifton and Lake Richmond in Western Australia, Laguna Bacalar in Mexico, the Bahamas, and Green Lake in New York State. The Lake Clifton thrombolites, which grow in a brackish lake fed by calcium-rich groundwater, are listed as a threatened ecological community.

Significance

Microbialites are among the oldest evidence of life on Earth, and their study informs the search for biosignatures on Mars. Living thrombolites are vulnerable to changes in water level, salinity and nutrient runoff.
