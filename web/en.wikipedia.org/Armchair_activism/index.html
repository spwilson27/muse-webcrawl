Armchair activism

Armchair activism is a pejorative term for forms of activism that require little personal effort, risk or commitment, typically performed from the comfort of one's home. Closely related terms include slacktivism (a portmanteau of slacker and activism), clicktivism for support expressed through mouse clicks, and "hashtag activism" for campaigning through social-media tags.

Forms

Activities described by the term include signing online petitions, liking or sharing campaign posts, changing a profile picture to display a cause's symbol, posting hashtags, and making small donations by text message. Critics argue such actions may substitute for more consequential engagement — attending demonstrations, volunteering, organizing, or sustained political work — and can serve mainly to signal the participant's moral identity to others, a behaviour sometimes called virtue signalling.

Debate

Scholars and activists disagree about the value of low-cost online participation. Studies suggest that token public support can sometimes reduce subsequent meaningful contributions (a "licensing" effect), but that private token support, or online actions embedded in a broader campaign, can act as a gateway to deeper involvement. Movements such as the ALS Ice Bucket Challenge raised very large sums through shareable online participation, while awareness campaigns like Kony 2012 were criticized for vast online reach with limited real-world impact.
