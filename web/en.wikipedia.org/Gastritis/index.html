Gastritis is inflammation of the lining of the stomach. It may occur as a short episode (acute gastritis) or be of long duration (chronic gastritis). Symptoms include upper abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, bloating and indigestion, though many cases cause no symptoms at all. The term is often loosely applied to any upper abdominal discomfort, but strictly it is a histological diagnosis made by examining the stomach mucosa.

Causes

The most common cause worldwide is infection with the bacterium Helicobacter pylori, usually acquired in childhood, which produces chronic inflammation that can progress to mucosal atrophy and intestinal metaplasia. Other causes include non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and aspirin, which inhibit protective prostaglandins; heavy alcohol use; autoimmune gastritis, in which antibodies attack parietal cells, destroying acid production and intrinsic factor and causing vitamin B12 deficiency (pernicious anaemia); bile reflux after gastric surgery; and severe physiological stress in critically ill patients, which can cause erosive gastritis and stress ulcers.

Diagnosis and classification

Upper endoscopy allows direct inspection and biopsy of the stomach lining; gastritis is classified by cause, distribution (antral, corpus or pangastritis) and histological features under the updated Sydney System. H. pylori can be detected by biopsy urease testing, histology, the urea breath test or stool antigen tests.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the cause: H. pylori is eradicated with combination therapy — typically a proton-pump inhibitor (PPI) with two antibiotics (e.g. clarithromycin and amoxicillin) for 14 days, or bismuth quadruple therapy where resistance is common. Acid suppression with PPIs or H2-receptor antagonists, stopping NSAIDs and alcohol, and treating erosions address other forms; autoimmune gastritis requires lifelong vitamin B12 replacement. Chronic atrophic gastritis warrants surveillance because of an increased risk of gastric cancer.
