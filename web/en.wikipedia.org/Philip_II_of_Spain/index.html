Philip II (Spanish: Felipe II; 21 May 1527 – 13 September 1598) was King of Spain from 1556, King of Portugal from 1580, and, as husband of Mary I, King of England and Ireland from 1554 to 1558. Son of Emperor Charles V, he ruled one of the largest empires in history — the first on which it was said the sun never set — at the height of Spanish power.

Reign

From his father Philip inherited Spain, the Netherlands, Spanish Italy and the American colonies; he governed as a tireless administrator from Madrid, building the palace-monastery of El Escorial as his seat and mausoleum. He was a staunch champion of Catholicism against Protestantism and Islam: his reign saw the Dutch Revolt (from 1568), which he never succeeded in ending; the victory over the Ottomans at Lepanto (1571); and war with Elizabethan England, culminating in the failed Spanish Armada of 1588. In 1580 he claimed the Portuguese crown, uniting the Iberian peninsula and its overseas empires until 1640.

Administration and legacy

Despite enormous silver revenues from Potosí and Zacatecas, Philip's wars drove the crown to repeated suspensions of payments. A meticulous, suspicious and deeply pious ruler — nicknamed "Philip the Prudent" — he centralised government through councils and paperwork. Historians continue to debate his responsibility for Spain's later decline and his image in the Protestant "Black Legend," contrasted with Catholic portraits of a model monarch.
