Higgs boson

Type: Elementary particle (scalar boson)
Symbol: H0
Mass: ~125.25 GeV/c²
Discovered: 4 July 2012, CERN (LHC)
Theorised: 1964

The Higgs boson is an elementary particle in the Standard Model of particle physics, produced by the quantum excitation of the Higgs field — a field present throughout the universe whose interaction with other particles gives them mass through the Higgs mechanism. It is named after Peter Higgs, one of six physicists who proposed the mechanism in 1964.

Theory

In the Standard Model, the W and Z bosons and the fermions must acquire mass without breaking gauge invariance; this is achieved by spontaneous symmetry breaking of the electroweak interaction, mediated by a scalar field with a nonzero vacuum expectation value (about 246 GeV). The observable remnant of the field is a massive scalar particle — the Higgs boson — whose couplings to other particles are proportional to their masses. Unlike all other known elementary particles, it has zero spin, no electric charge, and no colour charge.

Discovery

After decades of searches at LEP and the Tevatron, the ATLAS and CMS experiments at CERN's Large Hadron Collider announced on 4 July 2012 the discovery of a new boson with a mass near 125 GeV/c² consistent with the Higgs boson; the result reached the five-sigma significance standard. François Englert and Peter Higgs received the 2013 Nobel Prize in Physics for the theoretical prediction.

Properties and research

The measured mass is about 125.25 GeV/c², and the particle decays within roughly 10−22 seconds into pairs of photons, W and Z bosons, tau leptons, and bottom quarks, among other channels. Precision measurements of its couplings so far agree with Standard Model predictions, and physicists continue to probe for deviations, possible additional Higgs bosons, and the Higgs self-coupling, which would confirm the shape of the Higgs potential.
