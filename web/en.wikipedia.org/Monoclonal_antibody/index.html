A monoclonal antibody (mAb) is an antibody produced by a single clone of B cells, so that every molecule is identical and binds the same epitope on its target antigen. Monoclonal antibodies are used as medicines for cancer, autoimmune disease, and infection, and as essential tools in diagnostics and laboratory research.

Production

The classic method, devised by Georges Köhler and César Milstein in 1975 (for which they shared the 1984 Nobel Prize), fuses antibody-producing mouse B cells with immortal myeloma cells to create hybridomas, which are screened for the desired specificity and grown in bulk. Modern therapeutic antibodies are mostly made by recombinant DNA technology in cultured cells such as CHO cells, and are engineered to be chimeric, humanised, or fully human to reduce immune reactions against the drug itself.

Medical uses

Monoclonals transformed several fields of medicine. Examples include rituximab (against CD20 on B cells, for lymphoma and autoimmune disease), trastuzumab (HER2-positive breast cancer), adalimumab (anti-TNF, for rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease), and the checkpoint inhibitors pembrolizumab and nivolumab, which unleash T cells against cancer. Monoclonals have also been developed against infections including Ebola and COVID-19, and against targets such as PCSK9 (cholesterol) and CGRP (migraine). Their drug names carry the stem "-mab".

Limitations

Antibodies are large proteins: they must usually be injected or infused, are expensive to manufacture, and can provoke infusion reactions or immune responses. They also generally cannot cross cell membranes, restricting them to extracellular targets.
