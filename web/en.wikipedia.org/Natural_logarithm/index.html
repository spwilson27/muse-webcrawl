Natural logarithm

The natural logarithm of a number is its logarithm to the base of the mathematical constant e, where e is an irrational and transcendental number approximately equal to 2.718281828459. The natural logarithm of x is generally written as ln x, loge x, or simply log x when the base is implicit.

Definition and properties

The natural logarithm can be defined as the inverse function of the exponential function: ln(ex) = x. It also equals the area under the hyperbola y = 1/t from 1 to x, that is, an integral. Key properties include ln(xy) = ln x + ln y, ln(x/y) = ln x − ln y, and ln(xr) = r ln x. Its derivative is 1/x.

History

The concept arose in the 17th century through work on quadrature of the hyperbola by Grégoire de Saint-Vincent and Alphonse Antonio de Sarasa, and was developed by Nicholas Mercator, who coined the term "natural logarithm" (log naturalis) in 1668. Leonhard Euler later established e and the exponential function at the centre of analysis, giving the natural logarithm its modern prominence.
