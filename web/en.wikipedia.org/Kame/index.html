Kame

A kame is a mounded deposit of sand and gravel formed at the edge of a melting glacier. Kames are created when sediment carried by meltwater streams accumulates in openings, crevasses or depressions on or against the ice, or is dumped from supraglacial streams; when the supporting ice melts away, the sediment settles into an irregular hill or mound on the landscape.

Formation

As a glacier retreats, meltwater laden with debris may fill a hole in the ice or collect in a delta where a stream enters a proglacial lake ponded against the ice margin. When the ice disappears, these deposits are left behind as steep-sided, hummocky hills. Unlike eskers, which are long sinuous ridges, kames are shorter, irregular mounds; groups of them produce characteristic "kame and kettle" topography of hills and pond-filled hollows left by melted ice blocks.

Distribution and use

Kames are common in formerly glaciated regions such as Scotland (the word comes from the Scots kame, a comb or crest), northern England, Canada and the northern United States. Their stratified sand and gravel is often quarried for aggregate. The deposits preserve a record of meltwater drainage near the end of the last glaciation and are studied by geomorphologists reconstructing ice-sheet retreat.
