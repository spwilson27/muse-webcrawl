Vulcanization

Vulcanization (British English: vulcanisation) is a range of chemical processes for converting natural rubber or related polymers into more durable materials by heating them with sulfur, peroxides, or other agents that form cross-links between polymer chains. The process hardens the rubber, making it far more resistant to heat, cold, abrasion, and solvents, and it underlies the modern rubber industry, from tyres to seals and hoses.

History

Raw natural rubber is soft and sticky when warm, brittle when cold, and prone to degradation — defects that limited its use in the early nineteenth century. The American inventor Charles Goodyear, after years of experiments, discovered in 1839 that heating rubber with sulfur produced a stable, elastic material; he received a US patent in 1844. In England, Thomas Hancock filed a patent weeks before Goodyear's arrived, coining the name "vulcanisation" after Vulcan, the Roman god of fire. Goodyear's discovery, though commercially transformative, left him in debt, and he died in 1860; the Goodyear Tire and Rubber Company, founded decades later, was named in his honour but had no connection to his family.

Chemistry

In sulfur vulcanization, sulfur forms bridges (cross-links) between long polyisoprene chains, typically at allylic carbon positions. The process is usually accelerated with compounds such as zinc oxide, stearic acid, and organic accelerators, and retarded or tuned with activators, allowing manufacturers to control cure time and the density of cross-links. Low sulfur content gives soft, flexible rubber; high sulfur content (30–50%) produces ebonite, a hard, rigid material once used for electrical insulation and fountain pens. Peroxide and resin cures are used for synthetic rubbers that lack the unsaturation needed for sulfur curing.

Applications

Vulcanized rubber is used for tyres, conveyor belts, hoses, shoe soles, gaskets, vibration mounts, and countless other products. The inverse process, devulcanization, is an active area of research for recycling scrap rubber.
