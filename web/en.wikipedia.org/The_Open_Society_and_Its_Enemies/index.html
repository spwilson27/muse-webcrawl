The Open Society and Its Enemies

AuthorKarl Popper
Published1945
LanguageEnglish (written in New Zealand)
SubjectPolitical philosophy; historicism

The Open Society and Its Enemies is a two-volume work of political philosophy by Karl Popper, written during the Second World War while Popper was in exile in New Zealand and published in London in 1945. It is one of the most celebrated defences of liberal democracy of the twentieth century.

Argument

Popper attacks historicism — the doctrine that history develops according to discoverable laws or patterns that allow prophecy of humanity's destiny — and traces its intellectual lineage to three "enemies" of the open society: Plato (volume one, "The Spell of Plato"), and Hegel and Marx (volume two, "The High Tide of Prophecy"). Against Plato's ideal of a rigid, collectivist state ruled by philosopher-kings, Popper defends the open society: one in which institutions are subject to criticism, leaders can be removed without bloodshed, and social policy proceeds by "piecemeal social engineering" — small, testable reforms — rather than utopian blueprints.

The book also introduces the paradox of tolerance: a tolerant society must reserve the right not to tolerate the intolerant, lest tolerance itself be destroyed.

Reception and influence

The work was praised by figures including Bertrand Russell and became a foundational text of Cold War liberalism; philosopher Bryan Magee and others popularised it, and financier George Soros named his Open Society Foundations after it. Popper's reading of Plato — as a betrayor of Socrates' democratic spirit and a proto-totalitarian — remains sharply contested by classical scholars, and his treatment of Hegel and Marx has likewise drawn scholarly rejoinders. The book nevertheless remains a standard reference in debates about democracy, totalitarianism and the philosophy of history.
