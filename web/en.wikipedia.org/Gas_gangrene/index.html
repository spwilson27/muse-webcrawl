Gas gangrene

Gas gangrene (also known as clostridial myonecrosis) is a rapidly progressive, life-threatening bacterial infection that causes tissue death (necrosis), gas production within tissues, and severe systemic toxicity. It is most commonly caused by Clostridium perfringens and typically follows traumatic wounds contaminated with soil or debris.

Cause

C. perfringens and related clostridia (C. novyi, C. septicum, C. histolyticum) are anaerobic, spore-forming bacteria widespread in soil and the gut. In devitalised, poorly perfused tissue their spores germinate and release exotoxins — notably alpha toxin, a phospholipase that destroys cell membranes — producing muscle necrosis and fermentation gases (hydrogen and carbon dioxide) that dissect through tissue planes.

Signs and symptoms

Onset is usually within 24–72 hours of injury. Severe pain out of proportion to the wound, rapid swelling, bronze or purple skin discoloration, haemorrhagic blisters, a sweet or foul odour, and crepitus (crackling from subcutaneous gas) are characteristic. Fever, tachycardia, shock and multi-organ failure follow quickly without treatment.

Treatment

Gas gangrene is a surgical emergency: immediate, aggressive debridement of all necrotic tissue — sometimes amputation — plus high-dose intravenous antibiotics (typically penicillin with clindamycin) and intensive supportive care. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy is sometimes used as an adjunct. Even with treatment, mortality is substantial, especially in spontaneous cases associated with C. septicum and underlying bowel malignancy.
