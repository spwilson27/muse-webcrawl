David is a masterpiece of Italian Renaissance sculpture, created from 1501 to 1504 by Michelangelo. With a height of 5.17 metres (17 ft), the marble statue represents the Biblical hero David, a favoured subject in the art of Florence.

David was originally commissioned as one of a series of statues of prophets to be positioned along the roofline of the east end of Florence Cathedral, but was instead placed in the public square in front of the Palazzo Vecchio, the seat of civic government in Florence, where it was unveiled on 8 September 1504. In 1873 the statue was moved to the Galleria dell'Accademia, and a replica was placed in the original location.

Because of the nature of the figure it represented, the statue came to symbolise the defence of civil liberties embodied in the Republic of Florence, an independent city-state threatened on all sides by more powerful rival states and by the hegemony of the Medici family. The eyes of David, with a warning glare, are turned towards Rome where the Medici lived.

History

The block of Carrara marble had been quarried decades earlier and worked on unsuccessfully by Agostino di Duccio (1464) and Antonio Rossellino (1475), both of whom abandoned it; it lay exposed to the weather in the cathedral workshop for over 25 years before the 26-year-old Michelangelo took up the commission in 1501. He worked in near secrecy behind a wooden enclosure, and the finished statue was transported through the streets of Florence in May 1504 on a wooden frame drawn by more than forty men.

A committee that included Leonardo da Vinci and Sandro Botticelli decided the statue should stand at the entrance to the Palazzo Vecchio rather than on the cathedral. Gilded details — a tree trunk support, the sling, and a wreath — were added at the time of installation (the gilding has since worn away).

Interpretation

Unlike earlier depictions by Donatello and Verrocchio, which show David triumphant over the slain Goliath, Michelangelo chose to represent David before the battle: tense, alert, and standing in contrapposto, with his sling over his left shoulder and a rock in his right hand. The figure's proportions are not wholly naturalistic — the head and hands are enlarged, consistent with the statue's intended high placement and with Renaissance ideals of expressive emphasis. David has become one of the most recognised works of Renaissance sculpture and a symbol of human strength and youthful beauty.

Conservation and replicas

The statue has suffered several episodes of damage: rioters broke its left arm in 1527 (the fragments were recovered), a toe was struck by a hammer-wielding vandal in 1991, and periodic cleanings — most notably the controversial 2003–2004 restoration for its 500th anniversary — have debated water versus dry methods. Full-size replicas stand in the Piazza della Signoria (marble, 1910), at the Victoria and Albert Museum in London (plaster cast), and in many other locations worldwide.
