Kuleshov effect

A mental phenomenon of film editing (montage) by which viewers derive more meaning from the interaction of two sequential shots than from a single shot in isolation.

The Kuleshov effect is a film-editing (montage) effect demonstrated by Soviet filmmaker Lev Kuleshov in the 1910s and 1920s. It demonstrates that viewers derive meaning not from individual shots alone but from their juxtaposition: the same footage can convey entirely different emotions and ideas depending on the shots with which it is combined.

The experiment

In the canonical experiment described by Vsevolod Pudovkin, Kuleshov intercut a single close-up of the actor Ivan Mosjoukine wearing an expressionless face with, in turn, a bowl of soup, a dead child in a coffin, and a little girl playing with a teddy bear. Audiences praised the actor's subtle performance — his hunger, grief, and tenderness — although the shot of his face was identical in each case. The original film has not survived, but the demonstration became foundational to Soviet montage theory and to film studies generally.

Interpretation and influence

The effect shows that cinematic meaning is relational: editing creates context, emotion, and narrative that no single image contains. It underlies practices from the reaction shot to modern trailer editing, and Alfred Hitchcock — who discussed it in interviews — used it extensively, for instance in Rear Window. Psychologists and neuroscientists have revisited the effect with modern methods; studies broadly confirm that context alters the perceived emotion of faces, though the size and exact mechanism of the effect in the original demonstration remain debated.
