Revenue sharing

Revenue sharing is the distribution of revenue, the total amount of income generated by the sale of goods and services among the stakeholders of an enterprise, in the amounts of the shares they are owed. It is a business model used in a wide range of industries to align incentives between the parties that create value and the parties that provide distribution, capital, or intellectual property.

Revenue sharing should not be confused with profit sharing, which distributes a share of profits (revenue minus costs) rather than of gross revenue.

Applications

Revenue-sharing arrangements are common in franchising, licensing, affiliate marketing, music and publishing royalties, and joint ventures. In oil and gas production, governments and companies divide revenues under production-sharing agreements. In affiliate marketing, merchants pay referral partners a percentage of the revenue generated by referred customers. Musicians and authors receive a share of the revenue from the sale or streaming of their works, as negotiated in their contracts with labels and publishers.

Sports leagues

Professional sports leagues use revenue sharing to promote competitive balance. In the National Football League, national television contracts and other central revenues are divided equally among all teams, which — together with the salary cap — helps small-market teams compete with large-market ones. Major League Baseball shares a portion of local revenues into a central pool redistributed to clubs, supplemented by a competitive-balance ("luxury") tax on the highest payrolls. The English Premier League distributes international broadcast income largely equally, while domestic income mixes equal shares with merit and facility payments.

Government

Revenue sharing also describes fiscal arrangements in which one level of government transfers part of its tax revenue to another. In the United States, General Revenue Sharing (1972–1986) distributed federal funds to state and local governments with few conditions. Germany's fiscal constitution divides major tax revenues between the federation, the Länder, and municipalities, with an equalization system redistributing resources to poorer states. Similar arrangements operate in Canada, Australia, and India, where constitutionally mandated finance commissions recommend the sharing of central taxes with the states.

Online platforms

Digital platforms institutionalized revenue sharing at scale. Video sites such as YouTube share advertising revenue with creators (traditionally 55 percent to the creator for long-form video), app stores retain a commission (typically 15–30 percent) on sales while passing the remainder to developers, and crowdfunding and marketplace platforms charge percentage fees. The rates and terms of such programs are frequent subjects of public debate among creators.
