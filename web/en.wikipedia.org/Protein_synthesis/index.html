Protein synthesis

Protein synthesis is the process in which biological cells generate new proteins; it is balanced by the loss of cellular proteins via degradation or export. Protein synthesis generally consists of two major stages: transcription, in which a segment of DNA is copied into messenger RNA (mRNA), and translation, in which the mRNA sequence is read by the ribosome to assemble a chain of amino acids in the order specified by the genetic code.

Transcription

During transcription, an enzyme called RNA polymerase unwinds the DNA double helix and synthesises a complementary RNA strand using one DNA strand as the template. In eukaryotes the initial transcript (pre-mRNA) is processed — a 5' cap and poly-A tail are added and introns are spliced out — before the mature mRNA leaves the nucleus for the cytoplasm.

Translation

At the ribosome, transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules, each carrying a specific amino acid and bearing an anticodon complementary to a three-base codon on the mRNA, deliver amino acids in sequence. The ribosome catalyses peptide bond formation through its ribosomal RNA core, moving along the mRNA from the start codon (usually AUG) until a stop codon is reached and the completed polypeptide is released. The nascent chain then folds, often with the aid of chaperone proteins, and may be further modified by cleavage, phosphorylation, glycosylation or other post-translational modifications.

Regulation and errors

Cells regulate protein synthesis at every stage — gene transcription, mRNA stability, initiation of translation — allowing rapid responses to changing conditions. Errors in synthesis or folding can produce dysfunctional proteins; misfolded proteins are implicated in diseases such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's. Many antibiotics work by selectively inhibiting bacterial ribosomes.
