Secular state


States with no official religion

A secular state is an idea pertaining to secularity, whereby a state is or purports to be officially neutral in matters of religion, supporting neither religion nor irreligion. Although not all secular states are fully secular in practice, a secular state does not have an official religion or state church, and the laws of the state are not based on religious doctrine. It claims to treat all its citizens equally regardless of religion, and not to give preferential treatment to a citizen based on his or her religious beliefs.

Characteristics

Secular states typically protect freedom of religion through constitutional provisions, neither establishing a religion nor prohibiting its practice. Forms of secularism vary widely: French laïcité strictly separates religious expression from public institutions, while other models, such as that of the United States under the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment, forbid an official state religion but accommodate religious practice in public life.

History

The modern secular state emerged from the religious conflicts of early modern Europe, including the Peace of Westphalia (1648), and from Enlightenment thinking on religious toleration. Many states adopted secular constitutions during the 19th and 20th centuries, including France (1905 law on the separation of the churches and the state), Turkey under Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, and post-independence India.

Examples

Countries commonly described as secular states include France, the United States, Mexico, South Korea, Vietnam, and Nepal. Some countries maintain an established church (such as England or Denmark) and are therefore not fully secular, though they may be largely secular in governance.
