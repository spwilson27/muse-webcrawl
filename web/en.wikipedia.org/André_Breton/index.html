André Robert Breton (19 February 1896 – 28 September 1966) was a French writer and poet, best known as the co-founder, leader and principal theorist of Surrealism. His writings include the first Surrealist Manifesto (1924), in which he defined Surrealism as "pure psychic automatism".

Life and work

Born in Normandy, Breton studied medicine and psychiatry and served in a neurological ward during the First World War, where his encounters with shell-shocked soldiers and the theories of Freud deeply influenced him. After the war he joined the Paris Dada movement, editing the journal Littérature with Louis Aragon and Philippe Soupault, with whom he wrote Les Champs magnétiques (1920), an early experiment in automatic writing.

Surrealism

Breaking with Dada, Breton launched Surrealism with the 1924 Manifesto, gathering artists and writers including Max Ernst, Salvador Dalí, Man Ray and Paul Éluard. His novel Nadja (1928) and the journal La Révolution surréaliste defined the movement's exploration of dreams, chance and the unconscious. Breton's relations with the French Communist Party, which he joined in 1927, were stormy; in 1938 in Mexico he co-wrote, with Leon Trotsky and Diego Rivera, a manifesto for independent revolutionary art. Exiled in New York during the Second World War, he returned to Paris in 1946 and led a second generation of Surrealists until his death in 1966.

Legacy

Breton's forceful personality — he expelled members freely and was called the "pope of Surrealism" — kept the movement coherent for four decades. His ideas about automatism, objective chance and the marvel in everyday life influenced painting, literature, film and later cultural movements from the Situationists to magical realism.
