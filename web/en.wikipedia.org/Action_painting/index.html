Action painting

Action painting, sometimes called "gestural abstraction", is a style of painting in which paint is spontaneously dribbled, splashed, or smeared onto the canvas, rather than being carefully applied with a brush. The resulting work emphasises the physical act of painting itself as an essential aspect of the finished piece. The term was coined by the American critic Harold Rosenberg in 1952 in his essay "The American Action Painters".

Approach

Action painters rejected easel painting and preconceived composition. Jackson Pollock laid enormous canvases on the floor and poured, dripped, and flung household enamel paint using sticks, trowels, and hardened brushes, walking around and even in the canvas; he described himself as being "in" the painting. Willem de Kooning worked with violent, slashing brushwork, scraping and reworking surfaces over months. Franz Kline developed a stark black-and-white gestural style from enlarged sketches of his own drawings.

Philosophy

Rosenberg argued that for these artists the canvas became "an arena in which to act" — the painting was less a picture than the record of an event, an encounter between the artist and the materials. The movement is one of the two principal branches of Abstract Expressionism, the other being the meditative colour-field painting of Mark Rothko and Barnett Newman. Its ideas drew on Surrealist automatism, existentialism, and Jungian psychology, and it overlapped with the European art informel and tachisme.

Influence

Action painting helped shift the centre of the art world from Paris to New York after the Second World War. It influenced performance art, happenings, and later process art, and provoked a lasting critical debate between Rosenberg's existential reading and Clement Greenberg's formalist defence of the movement.
