G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), also known as seven-(pass)-transmembrane domain receptors, constitute a large protein family of receptors that detect molecules outside the cell and activate internal signal transduction pathways and, ultimately, cellular responses. The ligands that bind and activate these receptors include light-sensitive compounds, odors, pheromones, hormones and neurotransmitters, and vary in size from small molecules to peptides to large proteins.

GPCRs are involved in many diseases and are the targets of an estimated 34% of all modern medicinal drugs, including beta blockers, antihistamines and many psychiatric medications. Humans have roughly 800 GPCR genes, of which about half are olfactory receptors.

Structure and mechanism

All GPCRs share a fold of seven α-helices spanning the cell membrane, with an extracellular N-terminus and intracellular C-terminus. Ligand binding stabilises an active conformation that catalyses exchange of GDP for GTP on the α subunit of a heterotrimeric G protein, which then dissociates and modulates effectors such as adenylyl cyclase (via Gs/Gi) or phospholipase C (via Gq). Signalling is terminated by receptor phosphorylation by GRKs and binding of arrestins, which also scaffold their own signalling pathways — the basis of "biased agonism".

Classification

The GRAFS system groups vertebrate GPCRs into five families: Glutamate, Rhodopsin (by far the largest, class A), Adhesion, Frizzled/Taste2, and Secretin. Class A includes receptors for adrenaline, dopamine, serotonin, opioids and light (rhodopsin itself); class B (secretin-like) receptors bind peptide hormones such as GLP-1; class C includes metabotropic glutamate and GABAB receptors.

History

Cloning of the β2-adrenergic receptor in 1986 revealed the family; Robert Lefkowitz and Brian Kobilka received the 2012 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for studies of GPCRs, including the first crystal structures of receptors in active and inactive states.
