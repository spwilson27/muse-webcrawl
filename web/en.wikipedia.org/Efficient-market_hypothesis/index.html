Efficient-market hypothesis

The efficient-market hypothesis (EMH) is a hypothesis in financial economics that states that asset prices reflect all available information. A direct implication is that it is impossible to consistently "beat the market" on a risk-adjusted basis, since prices should only react to new information. The hypothesis was developed by Eugene Fama in the 1960s, building on earlier work on random price movements by Louis Bachelier, Paul Samuelson, and others.

Forms

Fama classified market efficiency into three forms, depending on the information set considered:

  • Weak form — prices reflect all past trading information, so technical analysis cannot yield excess returns.
  • Semi-strong form — prices adjust rapidly to all publicly available information, so fundamental analysis of public data cannot yield excess returns.
  • Strong form — prices reflect all information, public and private, so even insiders cannot profit systematically.

Evidence and criticism

Empirical studies largely support weak and semi-strong efficiency as useful approximations: most professional fund managers underperform index benchmarks over long horizons, a fact that has driven the rise of passive index investing. However, documented anomalies — such as the size and value effects and momentum — and episodes such as bubbles and crashes have led many economists and behavioural finance researchers, including Robert Shiller, to argue that prices deviate substantially and persistently from fundamental values. Fama and Shiller shared the 2013 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics despite their opposing emphases.

The hypothesis also faces a theoretical problem sometimes called the Grossman–Stiglitz paradox: if prices reflected all information perfectly, no one would be paid to gather information, and prices could not come to reflect it.
