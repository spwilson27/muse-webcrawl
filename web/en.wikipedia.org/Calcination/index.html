Calcination

Calcination is a thermal treatment process in which a solid material is heated to a high temperature in the absence or limited supply of air or oxygen. The purpose is to bring about a phase transition, thermal decomposition, or the removal of a volatile fraction. The word derives from the Latin calx (lime), after the most prominent historical application: heating limestone to produce quicklime.

Processes

Calcination reactions usually take place at or above the thermal decomposition temperature of the material. Typical objectives include:

  • removing water, including chemically bound (constitutional) water;
  • removing carbon dioxide, as in the calcination of limestone: CaCO3 → CaO + CO2;
  • removing sulfur dioxide and other volatile impurities from ores;
  • oxidising part or all of a substance, or reducing it in certain calcining regimes;
  • changing crystal structure, as in the calcination of alumina and of kaolin to metakaolin.

Industrial applications

The most common industrial calcination is the production of cement: limestone is calcined in a rotary kiln at around 1450 °C in the clinkering process, and the decomposition of calcium carbonate is a major source of industrial CO2 emissions. Other applications include the production of lime for steelmaking and construction, the activation of catalysts, the manufacture of titanium dioxide pigment, and the calcination of petroleum coke and of bauxite.

Equipment

Calcination is carried out in furnaces including rotary kilns, multiple-hearth furnaces, fluidised-bed calciners, shaft kilns and flash calciners. The solid product of calcination is often called a calcine.
