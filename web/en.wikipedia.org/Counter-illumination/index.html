Counter-illumination

Counter-illumination is a method of active camouflage seen in marine animals such as firefly squid and midshipman fish, and in the military prototype of the Yehudi lights aircraft system. It involves producing light to match an animal's background, both in brightness and in wavelength, in order to counteract the shading of the animal with light. The light is produced by photophores on the animal's underside, so that when viewed from below, the animal appears as bright as the downwelling surface light, rather than silhouetted against it.

Biological counter-illumination

In the mesopelagic zone of the ocean, a predator looking upward sees prey silhouetted against the brighter surface. Many fish, squid, and crustaceans have evolved ventral photophores that emit light matching the intensity, colour, and angular distribution of downwelling light, erasing the silhouette. Animals such as the hatchetfish and the firefly squid (Watasenia scintillans) achieve remarkably precise matches, adjusting output with depth, time of day, and water clarity. Some species use bioluminescent bacteria housed in light organs; others produce their own luciferin.

Military applications

During the Second World War, the Canadian and American navies experimented with diffuse lighting camouflage on ships, and the United States Navy's Yehudi lights project fitted aircraft with forward-facing lamps whose brightness automatically matched the sky, reducing the aircraft's visibility against it. The concept was effective but was overtaken by radar. Active camouflage research continues to draw on the principle.
