Sea level rise is an increase in the level of the world's oceans due to the effects of climate change. Two main factors contribute: the added water from melting ice sheets and glaciers, and the thermal expansion of seawater as it warms. Sea level rise between 1993 and 2018 averaged about 3.3 mm per year, and the rate is accelerating. Since 1900 global mean sea level has risen by roughly 20 cm (about 8 inches).

On a longer timescale, sea level rose at rates exceeding 40 mm/yr during the meltwater pulses that ended the last ice age, and it fell during the growth of the ice sheets. Future sea level is expected to continue rising for centuries even if temperatures stabilise, because of the slow response of the deep ocean and the ice sheets. Regional patterns differ: gravitational "fingerprints" of melting ice, ocean circulation changes, subsidence and post-glacial rebound mean some coasts — for instance parts of the US East Coast and many Pacific island states — experience rates well above the global mean.

Causes

The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (2021) attributes the observed rise since 1971 mainly (about 50%) to thermal expansion, with glacier loss contributing about 22%, Greenland ice-sheet loss 20%, Antarctic ice-sheet loss 8%, plus land water storage changes. Faster-than-expected dynamics in the West Antarctic and Greenland ice sheets are the largest source of uncertainty in high-end projections; complete loss of the Greenland ice sheet alone would raise global sea level by about 7.4 m.

Impacts

Rising seas increase coastal flooding and erosion, salinise freshwater aquifers and farmland, and damage ecosystems such as mangroves and salt marshes. Low-lying island nations such as Tuvalu and the Marshall Islands face existential risk. Around one billion people live in low-lying coastal zones; cities including Miami, New York, Shanghai, Jakarta (which is also rapidly subsiding), Rotterdam and London face escalating adaptation costs for defences, drainage and, in some places, planned retreat.

Measurement

Long tide gauge records (some reaching back to the 18th century) and, since 1993, satellite radar altimeters (TOPEX/Poseidon and the Jason series) provide the observational record. The GRACE satellites measure changes in ice mass directly by tracking Earth's gravity field.
