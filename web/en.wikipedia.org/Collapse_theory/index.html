Collapse theory

Collapse theory refers to the body of theories and models that attempt to explain societal collapse: the rapid and significant loss of an established level of social, political and economic complexity. Scholars study why complex societies — from the Classic Maya to the Western Roman Empire — sometimes disintegrate, fragment into smaller units, or lose population and institutional capacity over a short period.

Major approaches

Joseph Tainter, in The Collapse of Complex Societies (1988), argued that societies increase complexity to solve problems, but complexity yields diminishing returns; when the marginal cost of further complexity exceeds its benefits, collapse — a rapid return to a simpler level — can become an economically rational outcome. Jared Diamond's Collapse (2005) emphasised environmental factors, including ecological damage, climate change, hostile neighbours, loss of trading partners and a society's response to its problems. Other approaches stress fiscal crisis, inequality, elite overproduction, loss of legitimacy, warfare, disease and the interaction of multiple stresses ("polycrisis").

Critiques

Critics note that "collapse" is often a transformation rather than an ending: populations frequently persist, reorganise and rebuild; the fall of a state or elite culture is not necessarily a catastrophe for ordinary people, and may even improve their condition. Archaeologists also caution against monocausal explanations, since the archaeological record usually shows long, uneven declines and regional variation rather than a single sudden event.
