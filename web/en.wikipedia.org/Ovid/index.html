Ovid

BornPublius Ovidius Naso, 20 March 43 BC, Sulmo (Sulmona), Italy
DiedAD 17/18, Tomis (Constanța), Moesia
OccupationPoet
Major worksMetamorphoses, Ars Amatoria, Fasti, Heroides, Tristia

Publius Ovidius Naso (20 March 43 BC – AD 17/18), known in English as Ovid, was a Roman poet who lived during the reign of Augustus. He ranks among the three canonical poets of Latin literature, alongside Virgil and Horace, and is best known for the Metamorphoses, a vast narrative poem of mythological transformations.

Life and works

Born at Sulmo into an equestrian family, Ovid was educated in Rome for a public career but abandoned law for poetry. His early works — the love elegies of the Amores, the fictional letters of mythological heroines in the Heroides, and the witty didactic poems Ars Amatoria (The Art of Love) and Remedia Amoris — made him the most fashionable poet in Rome. The Metamorphoses, in fifteen books of hexameters, retells some 250 myths from the creation of the world to the age of Augustus, linked by the theme of change.

Exile

In AD 8 Augustus banished Ovid to Tomis, a remote town on the Black Sea (modern Constanța, Romania). Ovid attributed his fall to "carmen et error" — "a poem and a mistake" — the poem generally identified as the Ars Amatoria, offensive to Augustus's moral legislation, and the "mistake" an unknown indiscretion, possibly involving the imperial family. Appeals in his exile poetry, the Tristia and Epistulae ex Ponto, failed, and he died at Tomis in AD 17 or 18.

Influence

The Metamorphoses became the single most influential source of classical myth for medieval and Renaissance Europe, shaping Chaucer, Shakespeare, and countless painters from Titian to Bernini.
