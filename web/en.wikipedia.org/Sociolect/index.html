Sociolect

In sociolinguistics, a sociolect is a form of language, or a set of lexical items, used by a socioeconomic class, profession, age group, or other social group. It is one kind of language variety (lect), alongside the regional dialect and the individual idiolect.

Study

William Labov's pioneering studies — of department-store employees in New York City and of the speech community on Martha's Vineyard — demonstrated that variation in pronunciation correlates systematically with class, age, and aspiration, and that speakers shift style with formality. Basil Bernstein's distinction between "restricted" and "elaborated" codes was an influential, though controversial, attempt to link class-based language to schooling.

Features

Sociolects may differ in vocabulary (slang, jargon, honorifics), grammar, and accent. They mark group membership and can function as a barrier or shibboleth: professional jargons, youth slang, and varieties such as African-American Vernacular English or Multicultural London English are all analysed in part as sociolects. Speakers typically command several varieties and code-switch between them.
