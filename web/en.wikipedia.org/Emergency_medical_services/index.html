Emergency medical services

Emergency medical services (EMS) are services dedicated to providing out-of-hospital acute medical care, transport and transfer of patients with illnesses and injuries that prevent them from transporting themselves. EMS systems respond to emergency calls (911 in North America, 112 in Europe, 999 in the UK, and other numbers worldwide) and are staffed by emergency medical technicians (EMTs), paramedics, nurses and emergency physicians depending on the country and model.

Models of care

Two broad philosophies exist. The Anglo-American model emphasises rapid transport to hospital by paramedics working under medical oversight ("load and go" for most emergencies). The Franco-German model brings physician-staffed units (such as the French SAMU/SMUR) to the patient, treating extensively on scene ("stay and stabilise"). Most modern systems combine elements of both, using tiered responses — first responders, basic and advanced life support ambulances, and helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS) for critical patients.

Scope of practice

EMS clinicians assess and treat cardiac arrest, stroke, heart attack, major trauma, respiratory failure and other time-critical conditions, performing interventions from CPR, defibrillation and airway management to drug administration and, in advanced systems, pre-hospital blood transfusion or ultrasound. Care is documented and handed over to hospital emergency departments; dispatchers using triage protocols assign priority and may give telephone CPR instructions.

History

Organised ambulance services grew from military medicine (Larrey's Napoleonic ambulances volantes) and civilian systems in 19th-century cities. Modern EMS emerged in the 1960s–70s with cardiopulmonary resuscitation, mobile coronary care (pioneered in Belfast by Frank Pantridge), the US EMS Systems Act of 1973 and television-popularised paramedic programmes.
