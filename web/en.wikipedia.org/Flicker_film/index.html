Flicker film

The flicker film is a form of experimental film, prominent in structural film of the 1960s, in which the rapid alternation of frames with contrasting content — typically black and white frames — creates a stroboscopic flicker effect for the viewer.

Works and filmmakers

Key examples include Peter Kubelka's Arnulf Rainer (1960), composed entirely of black and white frames arranged in rhythmic patterns; Tony Conrad's The Flicker (1966), a 30-minute film of alternating black and clear frames that produces phantom colours and patterns in viewers' perception; and Paul Sharits's colour flicker films such as Ray Gun Virus (1966) and T,O,U,C,H,I,N,G (1968).

Effects and reception

Flicker films foreground the material basis of cinema — the discrete frame and the projector's shutter — rather than narrative or imagery, a hallmark of structural film as defined by critic P. Adams Sitney. Because rapid flashing can trigger photosensitive epilepsy, screenings and broadcasts of flicker works are often accompanied by warnings.
