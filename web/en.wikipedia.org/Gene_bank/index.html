Gene bank

A gene bank is a type of biorepository that preserves genetic material for the purpose of conserving biodiversity and ensuring the long-term availability of genetic resources. Gene banks store seeds, plant tissues, pollen, animal sperm and eggs, DNA samples, and living organisms, and they catalogue the material so that researchers, breeders, and conservationists can use it in the future.

Seed banks

The most familiar form of gene bank is the seed bank, in which seeds are dried and stored at low temperatures, often around −18 °C, to keep them viable for decades or centuries. Seeds of most crop plants are "orthodox" and tolerate this treatment, although some species have "recalcitrant" seeds that cannot survive drying or freezing and must be preserved by other methods, such as tissue culture or cryopreservation of embryos.

Well-known seed banks include the Millennium Seed Bank at Kew Gardens in the United Kingdom, which stores seeds of a large fraction of the world's plant species, and the Svalbard Global Seed Vault in Norway, which holds backup copies of seed collections from gene banks around the world in a facility built into permafrost on the island of Spitsbergen.

Purpose and use

Gene banks were established largely in response to the erosion of crop genetic diversity during the twentieth century, when modern high-yielding varieties replaced traditional landraces. They serve plant breeding by providing raw material for developing varieties with resistance to pests and diseases or tolerance of drought and other stresses, and they provide insurance against the loss of varieties in the field through war, disaster, or environmental change. Animal gene banks similarly cryopreserve semen, ova, and embryos of livestock breeds. International rules for access to and benefit-sharing from these resources are framed by agreements such as the International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture.
