Great Smoky Mountains National Park is a national park of the United States in the southeast, straddling the ridgeline of the Great Smoky Mountains, part of the Blue Ridge Mountains, which are a division of the larger Appalachian Mountain chain. The park straddles the border between North Carolina and Tennessee; the state line crosses its centre. It is the most visited national park in the United States, drawing around 13 million visitors annually — more than twice the second most visited park.

The "smoke" for which the mountains are named is a natural blue haze produced by volatile organic compounds released by the dense vegetation, which scatter blue light. The Cherokee called the area Shaconage, "land of the blue smoke."

History

The area was the homeland of the Cherokee for thousands of years; most were forcibly removed on the Trail of Tears in 1838, though the Eastern Band remained in the Qualla Boundary adjacent to the park. European-American settlers farmed valleys such as Cades Cove and Cataloochee, and by the early 20th century logging companies had cut much of the accessible forest. A grassroots campaign in Tennessee and North Carolina, supported by a $5 million donation match from John D. Rockefeller Jr., raised money to buy the land; the park was chartered in 1934 and dedicated by President Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1940. The Civilian Conservation Corps built many of its trails, bridges and campgrounds.

Nature

The park is renowned for biodiversity: over 19,000 species have been documented and scientists estimate up to 100,000 more may live there. It is the "Salamander Capital of the World," with some 30 salamander species, and protects one of the largest remaining old-growth forests in the eastern United States, extensive stands of cove hardwood forest, and the southernmost spruce-fir forests on its highest summits, including Kuwohi (Clingmans Dome) at 6,643 ft. Synchronous fireflies (Photinus carolinus) draw crowds in early summer. Reintroduced elk (2001) graze in Cataloochee, and the park's roughly 1,900 black bears are its most famous residents. Air pollution and invasive pests — including the balsam woolly adelgid and hemlock woolly adelgid — are persistent threats.

Attractions

The park has 850 miles of hiking trails, including 70 miles of the Appalachian Trail and the popular Alum Cave and Laurel Falls trails. Historic districts such as Cades Cove, with its preserved cabins, churches and grist mill, and the Mountain Farm Museum at Oconaluftee, interpret settler life. US Route 441 (Newfound Gap Road) crosses the park, and the Blue Ridge Parkway terminates at its southern entrance near Cherokee.
