Ionosphere

Altitude~48 to 965 km (30 to 600 mi)
LayersD, E, F (F1 and F2)
CauseSolar ultraviolet and X-ray radiation ionizing the upper atmosphere

The ionosphere is the layer of Earth's upper atmosphere, extending from about 48 km to 965 km altitude, that is ionized by solar and cosmic radiation. Because it contains free electrons and ions, it strongly affects the propagation of radio waves.

Layers

The ionosphere is conventionally divided into D, E, and F regions. The D region (60–90 km) appears only by day and absorbs medium- and high-frequency radio waves. The E region (90–150 km) can reflect radio waves and hosts sporadic E patches. The F region (150–500 km and above) splits by day into F1 and F2 layers; the F2 layer, the densest and most persistent, enables long-distance shortwave communication by refracting HF signals back to Earth, especially at night.

Discovery and study

The existence of a conducting upper layer was proposed to explain Marconi's transatlantic radio transmission of 1901 and diurnal variations in Earth's magnetic field; it was probed directly by radio sounding (ionosondes) in the 1920s and later by rockets, satellites, and incoherent scatter radars.

Effects

Ionospheric conditions vary with time of day, season, latitude, and the 11-year solar cycle. Disturbances such as solar flares and geomagnetic storms can disrupt HF radio, degrade GPS accuracy, and produce auroras where energetic particles precipitate into the polar ionosphere.
