Transmission line

Schematic of a balanced two-wire transmission line.

In electrical engineering, a transmission line is a specialised cable or other structure designed to conduct electromagnetic waves in a contained manner. The term applies when the conductors are long enough that the wave nature of the transmission must be taken into account—roughly, when the line length is a significant fraction of the wavelength of the signal.

Everyday examples include coaxial cable connecting a radio transmitter to its antenna, the traces on high-speed circuit boards, telephone lines, and the high-voltage lines of the power grid. At low frequencies lines behave as simple wires, but at radio frequencies they must be analysed with transmission-line theory.

Theory

A transmission line is modelled as a distributed network of series resistance R and inductance L, and shunt conductance G and capacitance C, per unit length. This model yields the telegrapher's equations, first formulated by Oliver Heaviside, whose solutions are forward- and backward-travelling waves. A key parameter is the characteristic impedance:

Z0 = √[(R + jωL) / (G + jωC)]

For a lossless line this reduces to Z0 = √(L/C), a real value—50 Ω for common coaxial cable, for example. Waves reflect from a load whose impedance differs from Z0, producing standing waves; matching the load to the line maximises power transfer and minimises distortion.

Types

  • Coaxial cable — a central conductor inside a cylindrical shield.
  • Twisted pair — two insulated conductors twisted together, used in telephony and Ethernet.
  • Microstrip and stripline — planar traces on printed circuit boards.
  • Waveguides — hollow metal tubes that guide microwaves above a cutoff frequency.
  • Overhead power lines — conductors strung on towers for bulk electric power transmission.

Applications

Transmission-line theory underlies impedance matching networks, stubs and filters in RF design, signal-integrity analysis of digital interconnects, and the modelling of long power lines, where effects such as the Ferranti effect (voltage rise at the lightly loaded receiving end) appear.
