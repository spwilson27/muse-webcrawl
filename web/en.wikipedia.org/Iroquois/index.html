Iroquois

The Iroquois (French pronunciation) or Haudenosaunee ("People of the Longhouse") are an Iroquoian-speaking confederacy of Native American peoples in northeast North America. The confederacy originally comprised five nations — the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca — living in what is now New York State; the Tuscarora joined as a sixth nation in the early eighteenth century. Formed before European contact under the Great Law of Peace, the Haudenosaunee Confederacy is among the oldest participatory democracies in the world, and its governance has often been cited as an influence on later democratic thought.

The Great Law of Peace

According to Haudenosaunee tradition, a prophet known as the Great Peacemaker (Deganawida), aided by Hiawatha and the clan mother Jigonhsasee, persuaded the warring nations to accept the Gayanashagowa, the Great Law of Peace — dated by various traditions and scholars to between the twelfth and fifteenth centuries. The Onondaga became keepers of the council fire and of the confederacy's wampum records. Fifty chiefs (sachems), nominated by clan mothers of matrilineal clans, deliberate by consensus on the Grand Council. Women held significant authority: clan mothers selected and could depose chiefs, and descent and property passed through the female line.

History

From their longhouse villages the Haudenosaunee farmed the "Three Sisters" — corn, beans and squash — and hunted and traded across the northeast woodlands. In the seventeenth century the fur trade and access to firearms drew them into the Beaver Wars against neighbouring peoples and French-allied nations. The confederacy became a pivotal diplomatic and military power between New France and the English colonies, bound to the latter by the Covenant Chain alliance. The American Revolution split the confederacy: most nations sided with the British, and the Sullivan Expedition of 1779 devastated their towns. Afterwards many Haudenosaunee resettled in Canada (notably at Grand River) and on reservations in New York, Wisconsin and Oklahoma. In the late eighteenth century the Seneca prophet Handsome Lake founded the Longhouse Religion, which endures alongside Christian churches and traditional practice.

Culture and today

Lacrosse — tewaarathon — is a Haudenosaunee creation and remains the confederacy's national sport, played internationally under its own flag. The Haudenosaunee issue their own passports, govern federally recognised and self-governing communities, and are known globally for the Mohawk ironworkers who helped build New York's skyscrapers and bridges. Language revitalisation programmes seek to sustain the six Iroquoian languages, several of which are endangered.
