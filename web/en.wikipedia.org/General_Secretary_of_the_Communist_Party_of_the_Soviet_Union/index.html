General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union

The General Secretary of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union was the top position within the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU) and, in practice, the leader of the Soviet Union for most of the country's existence. The office was established in 1922, and its holder headed the party Secretariat, which controlled appointments throughout the party and state apparatus.

History of the office

Joseph Stalin was appointed the first General Secretary in April 1922. Initially a largely administrative post, it became the seat of supreme power as Stalin used its patronage over personnel to defeat rivals after Lenin's death, and every subsequent Soviet leader held the office (or its equivalent) for the core of his rule. After Stalin's death in 1953 the title was briefly changed to First Secretary — held by Nikita Khrushchev (1953–1964) and initially by Leonid Brezhnev — before the name General Secretary was restored in 1966. Brezhnev held it until his death in 1982, followed by Yuri Andropov (1982–1984) and Konstantin Chernenko (1984–1985).

Mikhail Gorbachev became General Secretary in March 1985 and used the office to launch the reforms of glasnost and perestroika. As the party's monopoly weakened, real power shifted toward the new state presidency he assumed in 1990. After the failed August 1991 coup, Gorbachev resigned as General Secretary, the party was suspended, and the office disappeared with the dissolution of the Soviet Union at the end of 1991.

Powers

Formally the General Secretary was elected by the party's Central Committee and was only first among equals in the Politburo; in practice, control of the Secretariat and the nomenklatura system of appointments made the officeholder the effective head of the Soviet state, and foreign observers treated the General Secretary as the Soviet leader, broadly equivalent to a head of government and de facto head of state.
