Caudate nucleus

The caudate nucleus is one of the structures that make up the corpus striatum, a component of the basal ganglia in the human brain. It is a large C-shaped structure that curves around the lateral ventricle and consists of a head, body, and tail. The caudate, together with the putamen, forms the dorsal striatum and receives most of its input from the cerebral cortex.

Anatomy

The head of the caudate bulges into the frontal horn of the lateral ventricle; the body runs posteriorly along the ventricle's floor, and the tail curves down into the temporal lobe, ending near the amygdala. The caudate is separated from the putamen by the internal capsule, though gray-matter bridges cross it, giving the striatum its striped appearance.

Function

The caudate plays an important role in learning, memory, procedural and associative learning, reward processing, and goal-directed behavior. It is heavily supplied with dopamine, and it is involved in the cortico-basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical loops that link cognition and movement.

Clinical significance

Dysfunction of the caudate is implicated in Parkinson's disease and Huntington's disease — in the latter, degeneration of the caudate is a hallmark — as well as in obsessive-compulsive disorder, schizophrenia, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder.
