College admissions in the United States

College admissions in the United States is the process by which students apply to and are selected by colleges and universities. Millions of students apply each year, most commonly during the autumn of their final year of high school. Admission ranges from open enrollment at many community colleges to extremely selective processes at the most prestigious private universities, where acceptance rates can fall below five percent.

Application components

Most applications include a high school transcript and grade point average, letters of recommendation, personal essays, a record of extracurricular activities, and — depending on the institution — standardized test scores from the SAT or ACT. Many colleges use the Common Application, a shared platform, or the Coalition Application. Applicants may apply under early decision (binding), early action, regular decision, or rolling admission plans, and financial aid is commonly sought through the FAFSA form.

Evaluation

Selective institutions typically use holistic review, weighing academics alongside essays, recommendations, background, talents, and institutional priorities such as athletics and geographic diversity; preferences for children of alumni ("legacy" status) have been widely debated. Affirmative action based on race was struck down by the Supreme Court in 2023 in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard, reshaping admissions practices nationwide. The process has also been reshaped by test-optional policies adopted widely during the COVID-19 pandemic, by the 2019 "Varsity Blues" bribery scandal, and by the growth of a private admissions-counselling industry.
