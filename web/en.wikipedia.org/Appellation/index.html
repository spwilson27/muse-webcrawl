Appellation

An appellation is a legally defined and protected geographical indication used to identify where the grapes for a wine were grown, and by extension a system of rules governing production in that place. The concept is most developed in France, whose appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC) system, established in the 1930s, regulates permitted grape varieties, yields, alcohol levels and winemaking practices for hundreds of named areas such as Bordeaux, Burgundy and Champagne.

Systems worldwide

Similar systems include Italy's DOC and DOCG, Spain's denominación de origen, Germany's Qualitätswein categories, and the American Viticultural Areas (AVA) of the United States, which delimit geography but impose fewer production rules. In the European Union, the corresponding framework for foods and wines is the Protected Designation of Origin (PDO).

Purpose

Appellation rules aim to protect consumers and producers by linking a product's character to its terroir — the combination of soil, climate and tradition — and by preventing wines from elsewhere using prestigious names. Critics argue that rigid rules can stifle innovation, a debate illustrated by the Super Tuscan wines produced outside Italy's appellation rules.
