Einstein coefficients

Absorption, spontaneous emission, and stimulated emission between two energy levels

Einstein coefficients are quantities describing the probability of absorption or emission of light by an atom or molecule. They were introduced by Albert Einstein in 1916–1917 as part of his quantum theory of radiation, in which he re-derived Planck's law of black-body radiation using statistical arguments.

The three processes

Einstein considered atoms in equilibrium with a radiation field and three elementary processes between two energy levels: absorption, in which an atom absorbs a photon and moves to the higher level, with a rate proportional to the coefficient B12 and the radiation density; spontaneous emission, in which an atom in the upper level emits a photon without external influence, with a rate given by the coefficient A21; and stimulated emission, in which an incoming photon induces emission, with a rate proportional to B21.

Relations between the coefficients

Requiring equilibrium between the atoms and a Planckian radiation field yields the Einstein relations: g1B12 = g2B21, and A21/B21 is proportional to ν³, where g are the statistical weights of the levels and ν is the frequency of the transition. Stimulated emission, predicted by this analysis, later became the physical basis of the laser.
