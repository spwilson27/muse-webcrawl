Client politics

Client politics is a term in political science, developed notably by the American political scientist James Q. Wilson in his typology of politics (in Political Organizations, 1973), describing situations in which a small, organised group (the "client") receives concentrated benefits from a policy while the costs are spread thinly across a large, diffuse population. Because the beneficiaries have a strong incentive to organise and lobby while those who pay the costs barely notice them, such policies tend to be adopted and to persist even when their total costs exceed their total benefits.


Client politics in Wilson's two-by-two typology: concentrated benefits, diffuse costs.

Wilson's typology

Wilson classified political situations by how the costs and benefits of a proposed policy are distributed:

  • Client politics — concentrated benefits, diffuse costs. Example: agricultural subsidies or occupational licensing benefiting a trade at slight expense to millions of consumers.
  • Entrepreneurial politics — diffuse benefits, concentrated costs: a policy entrepreneur mobilises public opinion against an industry (e.g. environmental regulation).
  • Interest-group politics — concentrated benefits and concentrated costs: rival organised groups fight, as in labour–management disputes.
  • Majoritarian politics — diffuse benefits and diffuse costs, decided by broad ideological or party appeals, such as defence policy or general tax reform.

Dynamics and examples

Client politics is closely related to clientelism and regulatory capture: the client group (an industry, profession or agency clientele) may dominate the very regulatory body meant to oversee it, because regulators depend on it for information and because no countervailing group mobilises. Classic illustrations include farm price supports, sugar import quotas, taxi medallion systems and subsidies to particular industries — policies individually small to each taxpayer but worth millions to each beneficiary. Reform typically requires an entrepreneur to publicise the diffuse costs, or a change in circumstances that makes them visible.
