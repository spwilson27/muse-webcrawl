Magi

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Magi (singular magus; from Latin magi, from Greek magoi) were originally members of a priestly caste of ancient Media and Persia, associated with Zoroastrianism and renowned for expertise in ritual, dream interpretation and astrology. In Western tradition the word is best known from the Gospel of Matthew's account of the "wise men from the East" who visited the infant Jesus in Bethlehem, bringing gifts of gold, frankincense and myrrh. From "magus" derive the words magic and magician.

The Persian magi

Herodotus describes the magi as one of the tribes of the Medes and as indispensable officiants at Persian sacrifices and funerals, experts in the interpretation of dreams and omens. Under the Achaemenid Empire they served at court; the later Zoroastrian priesthood (modern mobeds) descends from their tradition. Greek and Roman writers variously portrayed magi as wise astronomer-priests or as charlatans, an ambivalence that shaped the later meanings of "magic".

The biblical Magi

Matthew 2 relates that magi, following a star, came to Jerusalem asking for the newborn "king of the Jews", were directed to Bethlehem, worshipped the child, presented their gifts, and returned home by another road after being warned in a dream not to report back to Herod. Matthew gives neither their number nor their names; Western tradition settled on three (from the three gifts), named Caspar, Melchior and Balthazar, and depicted them as kings — the "Three Kings" or "Three Wise Men". Their supposed relics were transferred to Cologne Cathedral in 1164, where a great golden shrine still houses them, and Epiphany (6 January) celebrates their visit in many Christian traditions.

Legacy

The Magi remain staples of Nativity imagery, carol singing and gift-giving customs, and appear in works from medieval mystery plays to T. S. Eliot's poem "Journey of the Magi" (1927). See also the article on the magician in the theatrical sense.
