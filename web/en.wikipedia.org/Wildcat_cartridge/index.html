Wildcat cartridge

A wildcat cartridge, often shortened to wildcat, is a custom-made cartridge for which ammunition and/or firearms are not mass-produced. These cartridges are often created as experiments to optimise a ballistic characteristic — such as velocity, power, or efficiency — beyond what commercial offerings provide, or to adapt an existing firearm to a new purpose. The hobbyist who creates one is called a wildcatter.

Development

A wildcat is typically formed by modifying an existing commercial case: common operations include necking a case up or down to take a different calibre bullet, blowing out the case walls or sharpening the shoulder angle to increase powder capacity (as in P. O. Ackley's "Improved" designs), or shortening the case. New cases may require fire-forming — firing a parent cartridge in the new chamber so the brass expands to the new dimensions.

Notable examples

Many now-standard cartridges began as wildcats. The .22-250 Remington, .25-06 Remington, and .35 Whelen all spent decades as wildcats before being commercialised by major manufacturers. The 6.5mm Creedmoor and the .300 AAC Blackout likewise grew out of experimental handloading communities. Other wildcats, such as the extensive families based on the .223 Remington and the Mauser cases, remain purely custom propositions.

Standardisation

For a wildcat to become a factory cartridge, its dimensions and pressure limits must be registered with bodies such as SAAMI in the United States or the CIP in Europe. Wildcatters must work without such published standards, taking care with chamber pressures because no factory load data exists.
