Mongolian music

The morin khuur (horsehead fiddle) is Mongolia's emblematic instrument

Mongolian music reflects the nomadic herding culture of the Mongolian steppe, its shamanic and Buddhist heritage, and the sounds of its landscape and animals. Its best-known elements — throat singing, the horsehead fiddle, and the epic "long song" — are recognised by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage.

Traditional forms

Khoomei (throat singing) allows a single singer to produce two or more pitches simultaneously — a deep drone with flute-like overtones — imitating wind, water, and birdsong. Urtyn duu, the "long song", is a highly ornamented, slow, free-rhythm song of vast melodic lines sung mainly by women at celebrations and on the open steppe. Short songs (bogino duu) are strophic and lively. Heroic epics such as the tale of Jangar were recited to the accompaniment of the tovshuur lute.

Instruments

The morin khuur (horsehead fiddle) — a two-stringed bowed instrument with a carved horse's head — is the national instrument, its strings and bow traditionally of horsehair. Others include the yatga zither, the limbe transverse flute, the shanz plucked lute, and the jaw harp. Music pervades festivals such as Naadam and daily herding life; contemporary Mongolian musicians, from folk-rock band The Hu — whose "hunnu rock" fuses throat singing and fiddle with heavy metal — to hip-hop artists, carry the traditions worldwide.
