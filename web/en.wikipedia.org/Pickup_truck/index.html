A pickup truck or pickup is a light-duty truck with an enclosed passenger cab and an open cargo area ("bed") with low sides and a tailgate. Originally built as utilitarian work vehicles for farms, construction, and trades, pickups have become some of the best-selling vehicles in North America and are widely used as everyday family transport, especially in the United States, Canada, Thailand, and Australia (where car-based variants are called "utes").

History

Early motorists bolted wooden "pickup boxes" onto car chassis; manufacturers responded with factory models such as the Ford Model T Runabout with Pickup Body (1925) and the Ford Model A pickup. Post-war designs — the Ford F-Series (1948), Chevrolet Advance Design, and Dodge B-series — established the modern formula of a separate steel bed and increasingly car-like interiors. From the 1970s, extended and crew cabs, four-wheel drive, and luxury trims broadened the market, and compact imports such as the Toyota Hilux and Datsun/Nissan trucks added a smaller class. In the 2020s, electric pickups including the Ford F-150 Lightning, Rivian R1T, and Tesla Cybertruck entered production.

Types and terminology

Pickups are categorised by size — compact, mid-size, full-size, and heavy-duty (three-quarter-ton and one-ton) — and by cab style: regular cab, extended cab, and crew cab. Traditional pickups use body-on-frame construction, while unibody models such as the Honda Ridgeline and Hyundai Santa Cruz trade some towing capacity for ride comfort. Bed lengths typically range from about 5 to 8 feet.

Use and culture

Full-size pickups have been the top-selling vehicles in the United States for decades; the Ford F-Series has held the title since the early 1980s. Their combination of payload, towing capacity (exceeding 30,000 lb for heavy-duty diesels), and off-road capability sustains demand in construction, agriculture, and recreation, while critics point to fuel consumption, pedestrian safety, and emissions as the vehicles have grown larger.
