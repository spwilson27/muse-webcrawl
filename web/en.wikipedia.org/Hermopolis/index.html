Hermopolis

Hermopolis (Ancient Greek: Ἑρμούπολις, "city of Hermes"), also known in Egyptian as Khmun ("Eight Town") and in Coptic as Shmounein, was a major city in antiquity, capital of the Hare nome (the 15th nome of Upper Egypt). Its remains are located near the modern town of el-Ashmunein, in the Mallawi district of Minya Governorate, Egypt.

History

Hermopolis was the cult centre of Thoth, the god of wisdom, writing, and the moon, whom the Greeks identified with Hermes. In Egyptian theology the city was associated with the Ogdoad — the eight primordial deities (four frog-headed male and snake-headed female pairs representing the chaotic waters before creation) from whose name, Khmun ("Eight"), the city's Egyptian name derives. Hermopolitan cosmogony held that the world arose from these primordial powers, and that the sun god emerged from a lotus or egg on the primeval mound at Hermopolis.

The city was already prominent in the Old Kingdom and lay near the border between Upper and Lower Egypt, which made it prosperous but also exposed: the nearby necropolis at Tuna el-Gebel served as its burial ground, and the boundary stelae of Akhenaten's new capital Akhetaten (Amarna), only a short distance away, were placed within its territory. Hermopolis remained an important religious and administrative centre through the New Kingdom, the Late Period, and the Ptolemaic and Roman eras, when a large temple precinct, agora, and basilica were built. It became a Christian bishopric in late antiquity before declining after the Arab conquest.

Remains

Little of ancient Khmun survives above ground apart from the great precinct walls and foundations of temples. The open-air museum at el-Ashmunein displays two colossal quartzite statues of baboons — sacred animals of Thoth — as well as remains of the Roman agora and a Coptic basilica built partly from pharaonic blocks. The necropolis of Tuna el-Gebel contains the tomb of Petosiris, a high priest of Thoth from the early Ptolemaic period noted for its fusion of Egyptian and Greek art, extensive animal catacombs for ibises and baboons, and the tomb of Isadora, a drowned maiden venerated in Roman times.
