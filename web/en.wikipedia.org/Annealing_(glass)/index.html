Annealing (glass)

Glassware cooling slowly in a lehr during annealing

Annealing is a process of slowly cooling hot glass objects after they have been formed, to relieve residual internal stresses introduced during manufacture. When glass cools too quickly, the outer surface solidifies and contracts while the interior is still hot; as the interior later cools and shrinks, tensile stresses develop that can leave the object fragile or cause it to crack — sometimes spontaneously, much later. Annealing holds the glass within a controlled temperature range and then cools it slowly enough that stresses relax and temperature gradients remain small.

Process

For most soda-lime glasses, the glass is held at or near its annealing point — around 514 °C for typical soda-lime glass, the temperature at which viscosity is about 1012 Pa·s and stresses relax within minutes — and then cooled slowly through the strain point (about 470 °C), below which stresses can no longer relax appreciably. The required schedule depends on the thickness and composition of the piece; thick castings may require days or weeks of controlled cooling, while thin blown ware may anneal in an hour or less.

In industrial production, annealing is performed in a long tunnel furnace called a lehr, through which ware travels on a conveyor while the temperature falls along a programmed profile. In studio glassblowing, pieces are placed in an electrically heated annealer (kiln) that follows a programmed soak-and-ramp-down cycle.

Checking for stress

Residual stress in glass produces birefringence and can be inspected with a polariscope (polarimeter), in which strained regions show coloured interference patterns under polarised light. Poorly annealed ware is a safety hazard, particularly for containers holding pressure or hot liquids.
