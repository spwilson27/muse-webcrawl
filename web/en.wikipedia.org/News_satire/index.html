News satire

Also calledFake news (satirical sense), satirical journalism
ExamplesThe Onion, The Daily Show, Private Eye, Le Canard enchaîné
PurposeCommentary and humour through imitation of news formats

News satire or satirical news is a type of parody presented in a format typical of mainstream journalism, with content that deliberately mimics the tone and structure of genuine reporting while containing humorous or exaggerated fabrications. Its primary entertainment value derives from the satire or irony of its contents.

Forms

News satire appears in print publications such as Private Eye and The Onion (founded 1988), on television in programmes like The Daily Show and Last Week Tonight, and on countless websites. Unlike straight fake news created to deceive, satire aims to amuse and critique, although careless readers sometimes share satirical stories as fact — a phenomenon documented in studies of media literacy.

History

Satirical journalism has roots in 18th-century pamphlets and the works of writers such as Jonathan Swift, whose A Modest Proposal (1729) adopted the voice of earnest economic reasoning. In the 20th century the BBC's 1957 spaghetti-tree April Fools hoax demonstrated the persuasive power of the news format itself.

Effects and criticism

Research suggests satire can inform audiences about public affairs and shape political attitudes, particularly among younger viewers. Critics debate whether it promotes cynicism or civic engagement, and whether labelling standards are needed when satire circulates on social media detached from its source.
