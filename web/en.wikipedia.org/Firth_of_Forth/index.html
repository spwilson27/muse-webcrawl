Firth of Forth

The Firth of Forth (Scottish Gaelic: Linne Foirthe) is the estuary, or firth, of several Scottish rivers including the River Forth. It meets the North Sea with Fife on its northern shore and Lothian on its southern shore. It was known as Bodotria in Roman times; in the Middle Ages it was also known as the "Scottish Sea". The firth extends roughly 63 kilometres (39 miles) from the tidal limit of the River Forth near Stirling to the Isle of May at its mouth.

Geography

Geologically, the Firth of Forth is a fjord formed by glacial action during the last ice age. Its shores include, on the south, the city of Edinburgh with its port of Leith, together with towns such as Musselburgh, Bo'ness, Grangemouth, and South Queensferry; on the north, in Fife, lie Kirkcaldy, Burntisland, Inverkeithing, North Queensferry, Rosyth, and Dunfermline inland. Islands in the firth include Inchkeith, Inchcolm, Inchgarvie, the Isle of May, Fidra, Craigleith, and Bass Rock, several of which are important seabird sanctuaries. The firth and its shores are designated as sites of special scientific interest, and the outer firth is a Special Protection Area for birds.

Bridges

The firth is famous for its crossings at Queensferry. The Forth Bridge, a cantilever railway bridge opened in 1890, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and an icon of Scotland; its distinctive red steel structure spans 2,529 metres with two main spans of 521 metres each, which were the longest cantilever spans in the world at completion. The Forth Road Bridge, a suspension bridge, opened in 1964. Its replacement for general traffic, the Queensferry Crossing, a three-tower cable-stayed bridge, opened in August 2017; at 2.7 kilometres it is among the longest cable-stayed bridges in the world. Further upstream are the Kincardine Bridge (1936), the Clackmannanshire Bridge (2008), and the Stirling bridges.

Industry and history

The firth has a long industrial history: coal mining and shale oil in the hinterland, shipbuilding, the naval dockyard at Rosyth, the port of Grangemouth and its petrochemical complex, and the Hound Point marine terminal, which exports North Sea oil loaded from tankers. Historically the firth was a major fishing ground for herring and oysters, and its shores carry many historic sites, including Inchcolm Abbey and the Antonine Wall's eastern end near Bo'ness. During both world wars the firth was heavily defended as the approaches to Rosyth dockyard.
