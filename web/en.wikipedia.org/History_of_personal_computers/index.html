History of personal computers

The history of personal computers as mass-market consumer electronic devices effectively began in 1977 with the introduction of microcomputers, although the first personal computers arrived a few years earlier as build-it-yourself kits. The arrival of the microprocessor in the early 1970s — a complete CPU on a single integrated circuit — made it possible to build computers that individuals could afford and own.

Origins and kits

Early machines such as the Kenbak-1 (1971) and the French Micral N (1973) anticipated the concept, but the January 1975 cover of Popular Electronics featuring the Altair 8800, a $439 kit based on the Intel 8080, launched the hobbyist market. Bill Gates and Paul Allen wrote a BASIC interpreter for the Altair and founded Microsoft. The Homebrew Computer Club in California nurtured many future founders.

The "1977 trinity"

In 1977 three ready-to-use machines reached consumers: the Apple II, the Commodore PET 2001 and the TRS-80 from Radio Shack. The Apple II, with colour graphics, became a success in schools and small business, helped by the spreadsheet VisiCalc (1979), the first "killer app" of the PC era.

The 8-bit home computer era

The early 1980s saw a flood of inexpensive home computers: the Sinclair ZX Spectrum (1982), the Commodore 64 (1982) — which became the best-selling single computer model of all time — the BBC Micro, the Amstrad CPC and others. Games and BASIC programming brought computing into millions of homes, especially in Europe.

The IBM PC and clones

IBM entered the market in August 1981 with the IBM Personal Computer, built quickly from off-the-shelf components and an open architecture running Microsoft's MS-DOS. Its legitimacy in business, and IBM's failure to keep the design proprietary, allowed Compaq, Dell and many others to build compatible "clones". By the late 1980s the IBM-compatible PC dominated the industry, powered by Intel x86 processors and Microsoft software — the "Wintel" standard.

The graphical user interface

Xerox PARC pioneered the graphical user interface (GUI) in the 1970s. Apple commercialised it with the Lisa (1983) and especially the Macintosh (1984), introduced with a famous Super Bowl advertisement. Microsoft answered with Windows; version 3.0 (1990) sold widely, and Windows 95 made the GUI the standard interface for PCs worldwide.

The 1990s and the Internet age

Falling prices, CD-ROM multimedia and the arrival of the World Wide Web turned the PC into a household necessity. Multimedia PCs, first-person shooters, and email drove upgrades. In 1995 shipments of PCs in the United States surpassed those of televisions for the first time. Laptops steadily improved; the IBM ThinkPad line and Apple PowerBook set standards for portable design.

Laptops and beyond

In the 2000s laptops outsold desktops in many markets. The PC's dominance was eventually challenged by smartphones and tablets after 2007, but personal computers remained essential for work, gaming and content creation. Modern PCs range from gaming desktops with powerful GPUs to ultra-thin ultrabook laptops and single-board computers like the Raspberry Pi (2012), which revived the spirit of the early hobbyist machines.
