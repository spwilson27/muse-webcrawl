The Godavari at Rajahmundry

The Godavari is India's second-longest river after the Ganges and the third-largest in India in terms of discharge and drainage area. Often termed the Dakshina Ganga ("Ganges of the South"), the river has been revered in Hindu scriptures for many millennia and is the site of the Pushkaram festival, held once every twelve years on its banks.

The Godavari rises in the Western Ghats at Trimbakeshwar near Nashik in Maharashtra, at an elevation of about 1,067 metres, only some 80 kilometres from the Arabian Sea. It nevertheless flows eastward for 1,465 kilometres across the Deccan Plateau—through Maharashtra, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, and along the borders of Chhattisgarh and Odisha—before emptying into the Bay of Bengal through a broad delta near Rajahmundry.

Tributaries

The river's principal tributaries are the Pravara, Purna, Manjira, Maner, Pranhita, Indravati, and Sabari from the left and right banks. The Pranhita, formed by the confluence of the Wainganga, Penganga, and Wardha, is the largest, contributing a major share of the basin's flow.

Dams and irrigation

Major projects on the river include the Jayakwadi Dam in Maharashtra, the Sriram Sagar Project and the Polavaram Project in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, and the Dowleswaram Barrage built by Sir Arthur Cotton in the nineteenth century, which first brought perennial irrigation to the delta. The basin supports extensive cultivation of rice, cotton, sugarcane, and pulses.

Religious significance

Nashik, on the river's upper course, is one of the four sites of the Kumbh Mela; Bhadrachalam is a major pilgrimage centre associated with Rama. According to legend, the sage Gautama brought the river down to earth to expiate a sin, giving it the name Gautami, another name for the Godavari.
