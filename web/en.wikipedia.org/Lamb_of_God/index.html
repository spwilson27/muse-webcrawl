Lamb of God (Greek: ὁ ἀμνὸς τοῦ θεοῦ, ho amnos tou theou; Latin: Agnus Dei) is a title for Jesus Christ that appears in the Gospel of John, where John the Baptist greets Jesus with the words "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29). The title interprets Jesus's death as a sacrificial offering, linked to the Passover lamb of Exodus and to the suffering servant imagery of Isaiah 53.

Theology

Early Christian writers developed the image along several lines: Christ as the true Passover lamb, whose sacrifice replaces the temple cult; Christ as the apocalyptic Lamb of the Book of Revelation, where the "Lamb who was slain" (Revelation 5:6) shares the throne of God and opens the seven seals; and Christ as the sinless, willing victim prefigured by the lamb led to slaughter in Isaiah. Sacrificial-lamb language also informs the doctrine of the Eucharist in traditions that speak of the Mass as a sacrifice.

Liturgy and art

The invocation Agnus Dei, qui tollis peccata mundi, miserere nobis ("Lamb of God, who takes away the sins of the world, have mercy on us") entered the Roman Mass in the seventh century and is sung during the fraction, before communion; it has been set to music by countless composers within complete Mass settings. In Christian art the Lamb is shown standing on a book with seven seals, bearing a cross or a resurrection banner, or bleeding into a chalice — most famously at the centre of the Van Eyck brothers' Ghent Altarpiece (1432). The Moravian Church uses the victorious Lamb as its seal.
