Cinema of Hong Kong

Cinema of Hong KongMajor studios: Shaw Brothers, Golden Harvest
Key genres: kung fu, wuxia, heroic bloodshed, mo lei tau
Notable directors: Wong Kar-wai, John Woo, Tsui Hark, Ann Hui, King Hu

The cinema of Hong Kong is one of the major film industries of the Chinese-speaking world. For much of the second half of the twentieth century, Hong Kong was the third-largest motion picture industry in the world (after Hollywood and Bollywood) and the second-largest exporter of films, with productions popular across East and Southeast Asia and among diaspora audiences worldwide.

History

Early production in the 1910s–40s was in Cantonese and Mandarin. After 1949, refugee filmmakers from Shanghai boosted the industry; the Shaw Brothers studio system dominated the 1960s with wuxia and huangmei opera films. In the 1970s Golden Harvest's kung fu films made Bruce Lee a global star, and in the 1980s Jackie Chan, Sammo Hung and the "heroic bloodshed" films of John Woo (A Better Tomorrow, The Killer) defined a new era. Stephen Chow's mo lei tau comedies dominated the 1990s box office, while Wong Kar-wai (Chungking Express, In the Mood for Love) won international art-house acclaim.

Decline and co-productions

From the mid-1990s the industry contracted under the Asian financial crisis, piracy, Hollywood competition and the shift of investment to mainland China. After the 1997 handover, the Closer Economic Partnership Arrangement encouraged Hong Kong–mainland co-productions such as Infernal Affairs (2002), later remade in Hollywood as The Departed.
