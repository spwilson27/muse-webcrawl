Article 9 of the Japanese Constitution

Article 9
The Constitution of Japan (1947)
ChapterChapter II: Renunciation of War
Adopted3 November 1946; effective 3 May 1947
Key effectRenounces war and prohibits maintaining "war potential"

Article 9 of the Constitution of Japan is the clause in Chapter II ("Renunciation of War") that outlaws war as a means to settle international disputes and prohibits the maintenance of armed forces with war potential. It is a central element of Japan's post-war pacifism and one of the most debated constitutional provisions in the world.

Text

(1) Aspiring sincerely to an international peace based on justice and order, the Japanese people forever renounce war as a sovereign right of the nation and the threat or use of force as means of settling international disputes.

(2) In order to accomplish the aim of the preceding paragraph, land, sea, and air forces, as well as other war potential, will never be maintained. The right of belligerency of the state will not be recognized.

Origins

The clause was drafted during the Allied occupation; its authorship is credited both to Prime Minister Kijūrō Shidehara, who proposed renunciation of war to General Douglas MacArthur in January 1946, and to the Government Section drafters led by Charles Kades. Similar renunciation appeared in the 1928 Kellogg–Briand Pact, which Japan had signed.

Interpretation and the Self-Defense Forces

Despite the second paragraph, Japan established the Self-Defense Forces (JSDF) in 1954, justified by government interpretation that Article 9 permits force for self-defence. The Supreme Court has largely avoided ruling on the question (the Sunakawa case, 1959). Successive governments maintained that collective self-defence was prohibited, until a 2014 cabinet reinterpretation and 2015 security legislation permitted limited collective self-defence. Amendment of Article 9 — proposed notably by Prime Minister Shinzo Abe, who suggested adding an explicit reference to the JSDF — remains politically contentious, and the article has never been amended.
