Purdue Pharma

Purdue Pharma L.P. is an American pharmaceutical company that developed and marketed the opioid painkiller OxyContin (extended-release oxycodone), launched in 1996. Owned for decades by members of the Sackler family, the company became a central figure in the United States opioid epidemic and, following mass litigation, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in 2019.

History

The business traces to the Purdue Frederick Company, founded in New York in 1892 and purchased in 1952 by the brothers Mortimer and Raymond Sackler. Purdue's early successes included the laxative Senokot and the antiseptic Betadine. Its breakthrough product was MS Contin, a controlled-release morphine, whose delivery technology (the Contin system) was adapted to oxycodone to create OxyContin. Aggressive marketing—minimizing addiction risks and promoting the drug for moderate and chronic non-cancer pain—drove annual sales above $3 billion at their peak; OxyContin generated roughly $35 billion in total revenue.

Legal proceedings

In 2007, Purdue Frederick pleaded guilty to federal misbranding charges and paid about $634 million in fines. As opioid overdose deaths mounted, thousands of lawsuits by states, cities, tribes, and individuals alleged that Purdue's deceptive marketing fueled the epidemic. Purdue Pharma filed for bankruptcy in September 2019 under a proposed settlement in which the Sacklers would contribute billions and surrender ownership, with the company restructured as a public-benefit entity. In June 2024 the U.S. Supreme Court rejected that plan because it would have released the Sackler family from personal liability without plaintiffs' consent. A revised settlement reached in 2025 provides for payments of roughly $7.4 billion by the Sacklers and the company to states, municipalities, tribes, and individuals, without granting the family a blanket release.

Legacy

The fall of Purdue and the philanthropy of the Sackler name—removed from museums and universities amid protests—has become a defining case study of corporate responsibility in the opioid crisis, chronicled in Patrick Radden Keefe's book Empire of Pain and the documentary work of Nan Goldin and the group P.A.I.N.
