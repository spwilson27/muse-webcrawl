A general-purpose machine gun (GPMG) is an air-cooled, usually belt-fed machine gun concept that can be flexibly adapted to serve as a light machine gun in the assault role, firing from a bipod, and as a sustained-fire medium machine gun mounted on a tripod or vehicle. GPMGs typically fire full-power rifle cartridges such as 7.62×51mm NATO or 7.62×54mmR from an open bolt and have detachable, quickly changeable barrels to manage heat during sustained fire.

History

The concept originated in Germany with the MG 34 of 1934, designed as a universal weapon (Einheitsmaschinengewehr) to replace the separate light and heavy machine guns of the First World War era. Its successor, the MG 42, with its stamped-steel construction, roller-locked action, very high rate of fire (about 1,200 rounds per minute) and rapid barrel change, set the template and remained in production-derived service as the MG 3 with West Germany and many allies.

Post-war designs

The Belgian FN MAG (adopted as the British L7, American M240 and in dozens of other armies) became the archetypal Western GPMG. The American M60 combined features of the MG 42 and FG 42. In the Soviet sphere, Kalashnikov's PK (1961) and its modernised PKM applied the same concept in 7.62×54mmR and are among the most widely produced machine guns in history. GPMGs serve as infantry support weapons, coaxial and pintle-mounted vehicle armament, and helicopter door guns.

Characteristics

A typical GPMG weighs 10–13 kg, has an effective range of about 800 m from the bipod and 1,500–1,800 m from a tripod using indirect or observed fire, and is crewed by two or three soldiers carrying spare barrels and belted ammunition. From the 1980s, lighter 5.56 mm squad automatic weapons (such as the FN Minimi/M249) took over part of the light role, but the GPMG's range and terminal effect kept it standard at platoon and company level.
