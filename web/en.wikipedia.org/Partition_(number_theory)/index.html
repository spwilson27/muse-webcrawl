In number theory and combinatorics, a partition of a positive integer n is a way of writing n as a sum of positive integers, where the order of the parts does not matter. For example, 4 can be partitioned in five ways: 4; 3+1; 2+2; 2+1+1; and 1+1+1+1. The number of partitions of n is denoted p(n), the partition function. By convention p(0) = 1 (the empty partition).

Generating function

Leonhard Euler showed in the 18th century that the partitions are encoded in the infinite product ∏k≥1 1/(1 − xk) = Σn≥0 p(n)xn. His pentagonal number theorem gives a recurrence for p(n) using the generalised pentagonal numbers, which allowed hand computation of values such as p(200) by MacMahon. Partitions are visualised with Ferrers (Young) diagrams, in which transposing the diagram shows, for instance, that partitions of n into at most k parts equal partitions of n with largest part at most k.

Asymptotics and congruences

In 1918 G. H. Hardy and Srinivasa Ramanujan derived an asymptotic formula showing p(n) grows roughly like exp(π√(2n/3))/(4n√3); Hans Rademacher later refined this into an exact convergent series. Ramanujan discovered striking congruences, including p(5n+4) ≡ 0 (mod 5), p(7n+5) ≡ 0 (mod 7) and p(11n+6) ≡ 0 (mod 11). Ken Ono proved in 2000 that such congruences exist for every prime modulus. Partitions remain a rich source of problems connecting combinatorics, modular forms and mathematical physics.
