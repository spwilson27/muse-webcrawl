Fort Detroit

Fort Pontchartrain du Détroit, founded by Cadillac in 1701.

Fort Detroit was a fort established on the Detroit River in what is now downtown Detroit, Michigan. It was established in 1701 by the French officer Antoine de la Mothe Cadillac as Fort Pontchartrain du Détroit, named for the Comte de Pontchartrain, France's minister of marine, and for the strait (détroit) connecting Lake Erie to Lake St. Clair. The post anchored French control of the upper Great Lakes fur trade and grew into the settlement that became Detroit.

French period

Cadillac founded the fort with about one hundred soldiers and settlers, attracting French habitants and Native American allies — Ottawa, Potawatomi, Wyandot and Ojibwe villages clustered near the palisade. The settlement survived Fox attacks in 1712 and grew along ribbon farms fronting the river. During the French and Indian War it was a key western post; in November 1760, following the fall of Montreal, Major Robert Rogers took possession for Britain.

British period and Pontiac's War

In May 1763, during Pontiac's War, the Ottawa leader Pontiac besieged Fort Detroit for months, and a British relief column was ambushed at the Battle of Bloody Run. The fort held out until diplomacy and reinforcements ended the siege, though Britain abandoned many other western posts. Under the Jay Treaty, Britain finally surrendered Detroit to the United States in 1796.

War of 1812

In August 1812 the American general William Hull, facing British general Isaac Brock and Tecumseh's forces, surrendered Fort Detroit without a serious fight — an act for which he was court-martialled and sentenced to death (later pardoned). American forces under William Henry Harrison retook the fort in September 1813 after the Battle of Lake Erie. The original fortifications were later demolished as the city grew; the site lies near the modern riverfront in downtown Detroit.
