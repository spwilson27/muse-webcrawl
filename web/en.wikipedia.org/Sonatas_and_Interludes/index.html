Sonatas and Interludes

Sonatas and Interludes is a cycle of twenty pieces for prepared piano composed by the American composer John Cage between 1946 and 1948, shortly after his studies of Indian music and philosophy. It is generally regarded as one of Cage's finest achievements and a landmark of twentieth-century piano music.

Background

Cage developed the prepared piano in 1940, placing screws, bolts, nuts, rubber, and other materials between the strings to transform the instrument into a kind of percussion orchestra controlled by a single player. Early prepared-piano works accompanied dances by Syvilla Fort and others. The Sonatas and Interludes represent the culmination of this technique.

Structure and aesthetic

The cycle comprises sixteen sonatas (arranged in groups of four) and four interludes. Its expressive framework derives from Indian aesthetics: the eight permanent emotions of the rasa tradition — the erotic, heroic, wondrous, mirthful (the "white" emotions) and anger, fear, disgust, sorrow (the "black" emotions) — tending toward the ninth, tranquility, which the final sonata evokes. The rhythmic structure is built from nested proportions, a technique Cage used in much of his music of the 1940s.

Reception

The work was championed by the pianist Maro Ajemian, to whom it is dedicated, and by David Tudor. Lou Harrison praised it as a masterpiece. It remains the most frequently performed and recorded of Cage's prepared-piano compositions.
