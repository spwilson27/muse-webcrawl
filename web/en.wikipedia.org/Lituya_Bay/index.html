Lituya Bay

Lituya Bay
Location: Alaska Panhandle, United States
Coordinates: approx. 58.6° N, 137.1° W
Type: Fjord / tidal inlet
Known for: 1958 megatsunami

Lituya Bay is a fjord on the coast of the Alaska Panhandle in the U.S. state of Alaska, within Glacier Bay National Park and Preserve. The T-shaped bay is about 14.5 km (9 mi) long and up to 3.2 km (2 mi) wide, fed by the Lituya, Crillon, and Cascade glaciers. It is famous for the 1958 Lituya Bay megatsunami, the largest wave ever reliably recorded.

The 1958 megatsunami

On 9 July 1958, a magnitude 7.8–8.3 earthquake on the Fairweather Fault triggered a rockslide of about 30 million cubic metres into the bay's Gilbert Inlet. The impact generated a wave that ran up the opposite mountainside to a height of 524 metres (1,720 feet) — taller than the Empire State Building — stripping trees and soil down to bedrock. The wave then swept through the bay, destroying three fishing boats; two people were killed, while two other boats survived, one riding the wave in a famous eyewitness account by Howard Ulrich and his son.

Earlier waves

Geological evidence and trim lines of older forest show that Lituya Bay had experienced at least four earlier giant waves — in approximately 1853–54, 1874, 1899, and 1936 — each leaving high-water marks on the bay's slopes. The bay's steep walls, deep water close to shore, and position along an active strike-slip fault make it uniquely prone to landslide-generated megatsunamis.

History and geography

The bay was named in 1786 by the French explorer Jean-François de La Pérouse, whose expedition lost 21 men in the bay's violent tidal currents. The single narrow entrance, with its treacherous tides, opens onto the Gulf of Alaska between Icy Point and La Chaussee Spit. The bay's shores are ringed by the Fairweather Range, including Mount Crillon.

Legacy

The 1958 event became the type case for "megatsunami" hazard studies, informing research on landslide-generated waves at sites such as reservoirs (where the 1963 Vajont disaster occurred) and volcanic flanks. The distinctive 1958 trim line remains visible: a sharp boundary between old forest and younger regrowth along the bay's slopes.
