Penal laws (Ireland)

The Penal laws (Irish: Na Péindlíthe) were a series of laws imposed in Ireland during the 17th and 18th centuries intended to compel Irish Catholics to accept the established Church of Ireland, and to disenfranchise and impoverish the Catholic population and, to a lesser extent, Protestant dissenters such as Presbyterians. Most of the laws were enacted after the Williamite War (1689–1691) and the Treaty of Limerick.

Provisions

The laws barred Catholics from voting (until 1793), sitting in Parliament, holding public office, serving as officers in the army, practising law, and bearing arms. The Popery Act of 1704 restricted Catholic land inheritance and purchase, requiring estates to be divided among all sons (gavelkind) unless one converted to Protestantism. Catholic clergy were required to register, and bishops and regular orders were banished; Catholic education was prohibited, giving rise to illegal "hedge schools".

Effects

The laws entrenched a Protestant Ascendancy, reduced Catholic land ownership dramatically, and were a long-standing grievance in Irish politics. Edmund Burke denounced them as "a machine of wise and elaborate contrivance… for the oppression, impoverishment and degradation of a people."

Repeal

Relief acts from 1778 onwards gradually dismantled the code, and the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829 (Catholic emancipation), won by Daniel O'Connell's campaign, removed most remaining disabilities, allowing Catholics to sit in Parliament and hold most public offices.
