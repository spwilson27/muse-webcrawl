Loneliness

Loneliness is the distressing feeling of lacking satisfying social connection

Loneliness is an unpleasant emotional response to perceived social isolation. It is commonly described as the discrepancy between the social relationships a person has and those they desire — in quantity, but more often in quality, intimacy and meaning. A person can feel lonely in a crowd or in a marriage, and conversely can be alone without feeling lonely; loneliness is thus distinct from chosen solitude.

Types and causes

Psychologists distinguish emotional loneliness, arising from the absence of a close attachment figure, from social loneliness, arising from the absence of a wider network of friends and community (Robert S. Weiss, 1973). Loneliness may be transient and situational — following bereavement, relocation or relationship breakdown — or chronic, where it becomes a persistent trait-like state associated with negative expectations of social interaction and hypervigilance to social threat, as described in the evolutionary model of John Cacioppo.

Health effects

Chronic loneliness is associated with increased risks of depression, anxiety, dementia, cardiovascular disease, weakened immune function and premature mortality; a widely cited 2010 meta-analysis by Julianne Holt-Lunstad found that weak social connection carries a mortality risk comparable to smoking and greater than obesity. Loneliness is measured in research with instruments such as the UCLA Loneliness Scale.

Prevalence and responses

Surveys suggest loneliness is common among older adults and, increasingly, among adolescents and young adults, prompting discussion of a modern "loneliness epidemic." In 2018 the United Kingdom appointed the world's first Minister for Loneliness, and in 2023 the U.S. Surgeon General issued an advisory on social connection. Interventions with the strongest evidence base target maladaptive social cognition, for example through cognitive behavioural therapy, alongside community and social-prescribing approaches.
