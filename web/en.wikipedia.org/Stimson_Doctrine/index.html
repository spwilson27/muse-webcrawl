Stimson Doctrine

The Stimson Doctrine is a policy of the United States government, announced in 1932, of non-recognition of international territorial changes effected by force. It is named after Henry L. Stimson, Secretary of State under President Herbert Hoover, who issued it in response to Japan's invasion of Manchuria.

Background and statement

Following the Mukden Incident of September 1931, Japanese forces occupied Manchuria and established the puppet state of Manchukuo. On 7 January 1932, Stimson sent identical notes to Japan and China declaring that the United States would not recognise any treaty or agreement that impaired American treaty rights in China, nor any situation brought about by means contrary to the Kellogg–Briand Pact of 1928, in which war was renounced as an instrument of national policy.

Adoption and legacy

The League of Nations adopted a similar non-recognition resolution in March 1932. Although the doctrine did not dislodge Japan from Manchuria, it established a durable principle of international law: annexations achieved by aggression would not be legitimised. It was later invoked in the refusal to recognise the Soviet annexation of the Baltic states (the Welles Declaration of 1940) and underpinned the non-recognition of territorial acquisitions after the Second World War, including, in modern times, Russia's 2014 annexation of Crimea.
