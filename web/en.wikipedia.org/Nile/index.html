The Nile is a major north-flowing river in northeastern Africa, generally regarded as the longest river in the world at about 6,650 km (occasionally measured slightly shorter than the Amazon). It drains an area of about 3.35 million km² through eleven countries and empties into the Mediterranean Sea at the Nile Delta in Egypt.

Course and tributaries

The Nile has two great tributaries meeting at Khartoum, Sudan. The White Nile, the longer and steadier stream, rises in the African Great Lakes region — its most distant source is in Burundi/Rwanda, feeding Lake Victoria. The Blue Nile begins at Lake Tana in Ethiopia and, fed by monsoon rains on the Ethiopian Highlands, historically contributed most of the water and the fertile silt of the river's annual flood. Below Khartoum the river crosses the Sahara — the only major river to do so — passing six groups of cataracts, joined at Atbara by its last tributary, and then flowing some 2,700 km without further inflow through Sudan and Egypt.

History and significance

The Nile made civilisation possible in Egypt: its predictable summer inundation deposited nutrient-rich silt that supported agriculture for over five millennia, shaping ancient Egyptian religion (the god Hapi), the calendar, and settlement, nearly all of which hugs the river's banks and delta. Herodotus called Egypt "the gift of the Nile". The search for its source preoccupied explorers for centuries — John Hanning Speke reached Lake Victoria in 1858. The Aswan High Dam (completed 1970) ended the annual flood, creating Lake Nasser and providing irrigation and hydroelectric power, at the cost of silt fertility and with effects on the delta. Sharing the waters remains contentious, notably over Ethiopia's Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile.
