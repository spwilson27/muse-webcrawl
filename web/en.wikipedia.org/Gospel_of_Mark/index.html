The Gospel of Mark (Greek: Εὐαγγέλιον κατὰ Μᾶρκον, romanized: Euaggélion katà Mârkon) is the second of the four canonical gospels and one of the three Synoptic Gospels. It is the shortest of the four, narrating the ministry of Jesus from his baptism by John the Baptist to his death, burial and the discovery of the empty tomb. It contains no infancy narrative, and in its earliest manuscripts it ends abruptly at Mark 16:8, with the women fleeing the tomb in fear and saying nothing to anyone.

Most scholars regard Mark as the earliest of the gospels, written around 65–73 AD, and hold that Matthew and Luke both used it as a source (Markan priority). Its style is fast-paced and vivid, with frequent use of "immediately" (euthys), vivid historical-present verbs, and Aramaic phrases translated for a Greek-speaking audience. A prominent theme is the so-called Messianic Secret: Jesus repeatedly silences demons and those he heals and instructs the disciples to keep his identity hidden, a motif first analysed by William Wrede in 1901.

Authorship

The gospel is anonymous. Church tradition, drawing on the early-2nd-century writer Papias (quoted by Eusebius), attributes it to John Mark, a companion of Peter, whose preaching Mark was said to have recorded in Rome. Modern scholars generally regard the author as an unknown early Christian, possibly writing for a community facing persecution.

Ending

The "longer ending" (Mark 16:9–20), which includes resurrection appearances and the command to preach to all creation, is absent from the earliest complete manuscripts (Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus) and is regarded by most textual critics as a later addition; a shorter alternative ending also circulated in some manuscripts.
