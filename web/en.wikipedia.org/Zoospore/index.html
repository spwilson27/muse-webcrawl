Zoospore

A zoospore is a motile asexual spore that uses a flagellum for locomotion in water. Also called a swarm spore, these spores are produced by some protists, bacteria, and fungi to propagate themselves. Zoospores lack a cell wall during their swimming phase and are typically released from a sporangium (in this context often called a zoosporangium); after a dispersal period they retract their flagella, encyst, and germinate into a new organism.

Zoospores occur in diverse groups: the oomycetes (water moulds, including the potato blight agent Phytophthora infestans) characteristically produce biflagellate zoospores bearing one tinsel and one whiplash flagellum, while the chytrid fungi produce zoospores with a single posterior whiplash flagellum — a trait linking fungi to their opisthokont ancestors. Green algae of genera such as Chlamydomonas and Ulothrix also reproduce via zoospores. Flagellar arrangement and ultrastructure are important taxonomic characters in these groups.

Ecological significance

Because zoospores require water for dispersal, they exploit rain, dew and soil moisture to reach new hosts. The chytrid Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis, whose zoospores infect amphibian skin, is the cause of chytridiomycosis, a disease implicated in worldwide amphibian declines.
