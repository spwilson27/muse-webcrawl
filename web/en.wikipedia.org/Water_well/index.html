A water well is an excavation or structure created in the ground by digging, driving, or drilling to access groundwater in underground aquifers. Water is drawn by pump or lifted in containers, or may flow naturally to the surface under artesian pressure. Wells are among humanity's oldest water-supply technologies and remain essential: billions of people worldwide rely on groundwater from wells for drinking water, and wells supply a large share of irrigation water, particularly in India, China and the United States.

Types

Dug wells are excavated by hand or machine, usually shallow and lined with stone, brick or concrete rings; they tap the water table and are vulnerable to surface contamination and drying. Driven wells consist of small-diameter pipe hammered into soft ground with a screened point. Drilled wells, made with rotary, percussion (cable-tool) or auger rigs, can reach deep confined aquifers; they are cased with steel or plastic pipe, screened at the water-bearing zone, gravel-packed and grouted at the surface, and fitted with submersible or jet pumps. An artesian well taps a confined aquifer under pressure so water rises in the casing, sometimes flowing without pumping.

Health and sustainability

Wells require protection from contamination: sanitary seals, setbacks from septic systems and livestock, and regular testing for bacteria, nitrates and naturally occurring contaminants such as arsenic and fluoride, which affect many aquifers, notably in South Asia. Excessive pumping can lower water tables, dry neighbouring wells, cause land subsidence and induce seawater intrusion in coastal aquifers, so many jurisdictions now license and monitor groundwater abstraction. The earliest known wells date to the Neolithic, around 10,000 years ago, including examples at Atlit Yam and in Cyprus.
