Pancreatic cancer arises when cells in the pancreas grow out of control. The most common form, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, develops from the exocrine cells that produce digestive enzymes and accounts for about 90 percent of cases; rarer neuroendocrine tumours arise from hormone-producing cells and generally have a better prognosis. Pancreatic cancer is among the deadliest major cancers, largely because it usually causes no symptoms until it has spread.

Signs and symptoms

Early pancreatic cancer is usually silent. When symptoms appear they may include pain in the upper abdomen radiating to the back, loss of appetite and weight, painless jaundice (yellowing of the skin and eyes) when a tumour in the pancreatic head blocks the bile duct, pale stools, dark urine, itching, new or worsening diabetes, depression and blood clots such as Trousseau syndrome.

Causes and risk factors

The disease is most common after age 55. Tobacco smoking is the best-established risk factor, roughly doubling risk. Other factors include obesity, heavy alcohol use, chronic pancreatitis, long-standing diabetes, diets high in processed meat, and inherited mutations such as BRCA2, BRCA1, PALB2 and the gene for hereditary pancreatitis; about 10 percent of cases have a family history. Tumours characteristically accumulate mutations in KRAS, TP53, CDKN2A and SMAD4.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis typically uses CT or MRI imaging, endoscopic ultrasound with biopsy, and the blood marker CA 19-9. Only a minority of patients are candidates for potentially curative surgery — usually the Whipple procedure (pancreaticoduodenectomy) for tumours of the pancreatic head. Chemotherapy regimens such as FOLFIRINOX and gemcitabine-based combinations, sometimes with radiation, can extend survival. Because most cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage, overall five-year survival remains only around 10–13 percent, making pancreatic cancer one of the leading causes of cancer death worldwide.
