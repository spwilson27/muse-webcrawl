Dendrochronology

Dendrochronology (from Greek dendron, "tree", khronos, "time", and logos, "study") is the scientific method of dating tree rings — the annual growth rings of trees — to the exact calendar year in which each ring was formed. By matching ring-width patterns between samples, researchers build long chronologies used to date wooden artefacts and buildings, reconstruct past climates, and calibrate the radiocarbon timescale.

Principles

In temperate climates trees add one ring per growing season, with the ring's width governed by growing conditions: wide in favourable years, narrow in drought or cold. Because trees in a region share the same weather, their ring sequences share a distinctive pattern of wide and narrow years. In crossdating, patterns from many samples are overlapped and matched — checked today with statistical correlation — so that each ring in a long composite sequence can be assigned a calendar date. The method was developed into a rigorous science by the astronomer A. E. Douglass in the early 20th century, initially to date Ancestral Puebloan ruins in the American Southwest.

Chronologies

Regional master chronologies run for millennia: bristlecone pine sequences from the White Mountains of California extend roughly 9,000 years, and European oak chronologies (built from bog oaks and historic timbers in Germany and Ireland) reach back more than 10,000 years; combining floating sequences, combined chronologies span about 14,000 years. Such chronologies anchor radiocarbon dating calibration curves and pinpoint events such as the volcanic eruption that caused the extreme weather of 536 CE.

Applications

  • Archaeology and art history — dating timbers in buildings, ships, and panel paintings (a standard tool for authenticating and dating Old Master panels).
  • Dendroclimatology — reconstructing temperature and precipitation centuries before instrumental records.
  • Ecology and hazards — dating fires, floods, avalanches, insect outbreaks, and glacier advances from ring anomalies (dendrogeomorphology).
  • Calibration — providing the absolute timescale against which radiocarbon ages are corrected.

Limitations

The method works best where climate imposes a strong single limiting factor and distinct growing seasons; it is harder in the tropics, where rings may be absent or non-annual. Missing rings and false (double) rings must be detected through careful replication, and dating applies to the wood's growth — a timber may be older than the building it was reused in.
