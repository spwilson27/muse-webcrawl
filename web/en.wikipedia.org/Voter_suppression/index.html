Voter suppression

Voter suppression is a strategy used to influence the outcome of an election by discouraging or preventing specific groups of people from voting. It is distinguished from political campaigning, which seeks to change voters' choices, in that suppression instead reduces participation itself. Tactics range from legal barriers administered by the state to intimidation and disinformation.

Methods

Historically documented methods include literacy tests and poll taxes (used in the post-Reconstruction US South against Black voters until outlawed by the Twenty-fourth Amendment and the Voting Rights Act of 1965), restrictive voter-identification laws, purges of electoral rolls, reductions in polling places or early-voting days producing long queues in targeted areas, felon disenfranchisement, gerrymandering, and misinformation about voting dates or eligibility. The line between legitimate election administration and suppression is heavily contested in courts and legislatures.

United States history

After Reconstruction, Southern states systematically disenfranchised African Americans for nearly a century. The Voting Rights Act of 1965 banned discriminatory practices and imposed federal preclearance on certain jurisdictions; the Supreme Court's 2013 decision in Shelby County v. Holder invalidated the preclearance formula, after which a number of states enacted new identification and registration rules that critics describe as suppression and supporters describe as election security.
