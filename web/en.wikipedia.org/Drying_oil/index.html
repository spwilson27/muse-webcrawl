Drying oil

A drying oil is an oil that hardens to a tough, solid film on exposure to air. The oil does not harden by evaporation of water or solvent, but by a chemical reaction in which its unsaturated fatty acid components cross-link and polymerize in the presence of oxygen (autoxidation). Drying oils are key ingredients of oil paints and many varnishes, wood finishes, and linoleum.

Examples and properties

Common drying oils include linseed (flaxseed) oil, tung oil, poppyseed oil, perilla oil, and walnut oil. Their drying ability correlates with a high iodine number, reflecting a high proportion of polyunsaturated fatty acids such as linolenic and linoleic acid. "Semi-drying" oils such as soybean and sunflower oil harden only partially. Metallic driers (cobalt, manganese, or zirconium salts) are often added to accelerate curing, as in "boiled" linseed oil.

Safety

The exothermic polymerization that cures drying oils can cause spontaneous combustion: rags soaked in linseed oil, if wadded up so heat cannot escape, can self-heat and ignite, a well-known fire hazard that requires storing used rags in sealed metal containers or drying them flat.
