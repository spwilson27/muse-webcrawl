The Venetian Lagoon (Italian: Laguna di Venezia) is an enclosed bay of the Adriatic Sea in northeastern Italy in which the city of Venice is situated. Covering about 550 square kilometres, it is the largest wetland in the Mediterranean basin and one of the most important coastal ecosystems in Europe. Around 8% of the lagoon is permanently dry land — including Venice itself and many smaller islands — while roughly 80% consists of tidal mudflats and salt marshes.

Formation and hydrology

The lagoon formed some 6,000–7,000 years ago as post-glacial sea-level rise flooded the upper Adriatic plain, depositing sediment carried by rivers such as the Po, Adige, and Brenta. It exchanges water with the sea through three inlets — Lido, Malamocco, and Chioggia. Over centuries the Republic of Venice deliberately diverted rivers away from the lagoon to prevent it silting up.

Ecology and challenges

The salt marshes (barene) support rich bird life, fish nurseries, and traditional fisheries including clam harvesting. In the 20th century, industrial development at Porto Marghera, groundwater extraction (which accelerated subsidence), dredged navigation channels, and motorboat wash caused serious erosion and pollution. Venice's periodic flooding (acqua alta) prompted construction of the MOSE mobile barrier system at the inlets, first raised in 2020.
