Single malt whisky is malt whisky from a single distillery. It is made exclusively from a mash of fermented malted barley, distilled — typically in pot stills — at one distillery, and aged in wooden casks, usually oak. Single malts are most famously associated with Scotland, where regulations require Scotch single malts to be matured in Scotland for at least three years, but they are also produced in Ireland, Japan, the United States, India, Taiwan and many other countries.

Production

Barley is malted — steeped, germinated and kiln-dried, sometimes with peat smoke — then milled and mashed with hot water to produce a sugary wort. Yeast fermentation yields a beer-like wash of about 8% alcohol, which is distilled twice (sometimes three times) in copper pot stills. The spirit is then matured in oak casks, commonly ex-bourbon barrels or sherry-seasoned casks, which contribute much of its colour and flavour.

Scotch whisky regions

Scotland's malt whisky regions are traditionally the Lowlands, Highlands (including the islands), Speyside, Islay and Campbeltown. Speyside hosts the densest concentration of distilleries, while Islay is noted for heavily peated styles. Well-known single malts include Glenlivet, Glenfiddich, Macallan, Talisker, Laphroaig and Highland Park.

Labelling

An age statement on a single malt refers to the youngest whisky in the bottle. Single cask bottlings come from one barrel, and "cask strength" bottlings are not diluted to a standard strength. Single malts differ from blended malts (mixtures of malts from several distilleries) and from single grain whiskies, which may be made from other cereals.

History

Distillation of barley spirit in Scotland and Ireland dates to at least the 15th century; the earliest written record of Scotch whisky production is from 1494. Single malts were long sold mainly to blenders and were only widely marketed as premium bottled brands from the 1960s onwards, led by Glenfiddich, before a global boom in the late 20th and 21st centuries.
