United States Army Special Forces

The United States Army Special Forces (SF), colloquially known as the Green Berets, are a special operations force of the United States Army designed to deploy and execute nine doctrinal missions: unconventional warfare, foreign internal defense, direct action, counter-insurgency, special reconnaissance, counter-terrorism, information operations, counterproliferation of weapons of mass destruction, and security force assistance. Their motto is De oppresso liber — "To free the oppressed".

Organization

Modern Special Forces trace their lineage to World War II units such as the First Special Service Force and the OSS Operational Groups. The first Special Forces group, the 10th, was activated in 1952 at Fort Bragg under Colonel Aaron Bank. Today there are five active-duty groups (1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th and 10th) and two Army National Guard groups (19th and 20th), each oriented on a geographic combatant command region. The basic unit is the twelve-man Operational Detachment Alpha (ODA or "A-Team"), each member cross-trained in a specialty — weapons, engineering, medicine, communications or intelligence — and in a regional language and culture.

History

President John F. Kennedy championed the force and authorized the green beret as its headgear in 1961 — hence the nickname. Green Berets advised South Vietnamese forces throughout the Vietnam War, trained mujahideen partners indirectly through regional allies in the 1980s, and after 2001 were among the first US forces into Afghanistan, working with the Northern Alliance. They have since been central to "by, with and through" partner-force operations against insurgencies and terrorist groups worldwide.

Selection and training

Candidates pass the Special Forces Assessment and Selection (SFAS) and then the Special Forces Qualification Course (the "Q Course"), which can take one to two years and includes small-unit tactics, specialty training, language school and the Robin Sage unconventional-warfare exercise in North Carolina.
