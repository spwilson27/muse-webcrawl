Rosie the Riveter

Rosie the Riveter is a cultural icon of World War II representing the American women who worked in factories and shipyards during the war, many of whom produced munitions and war supplies. The image — most famously J. Howard Miller's 1943 "We Can Do It!" poster and Norman Rockwell's 1943 Saturday Evening Post cover — came to symbolise women's economic power and, later, became an emblem of feminism.

Origins

The name "Rosie the Riveter" was popularised by a 1942 song written by Redd Evans and John Jacob Loeb, about a tireless assembly-line worker. The term was associated with real women, notably Rose Will Monroe, a riveter at the Willow Run bomber plant in Michigan whom the song's promoters discovered working there. In 1942 the artist J. Howard Miller was commissioned by Westinghouse Electric to produce a series of morale posters; his "We Can Do It!" image was displayed inside factories for only a short period and was little known during the war. Norman Rockwell's painting of a muscular Rosie, published on the cover of the Saturday Evening Post on May 29, 1943, was the more famous wartime image.

Women in the wartime workforce

With millions of men serving overseas, the United States government campaigned to recruit women into war industries. Between 1940 and 1945 the female percentage of the US workforce rose from 27% to nearly 37%, and by 1944 millions of women worked in the aircraft, munitions, and shipbuilding industries. Women built aircraft and ships, drove trucks, and worked in jobs previously reserved for men.

Rediscovery and feminist icon

Miller's poster was rediscovered in the 1980s and reproduced widely, becoming the image most strongly associated with Rosie today. It has been reinterpreted as a symbol of female empowerment and widely adapted in popular culture and politics. The Rosie the Riveter/World War II Home Front National Historical Park was established in Richmond, California, in 2000. In 2010, the Congressional Gold Medal was authorised for the "Rosie the Riveters" — the women who served on the home front.

Real-life Rosies

Several women have been identified as inspirations for the imagery. Geraldine Hoff Doyle claimed to be the model for Miller's poster, based on a 1942 wire photograph; later research identified the woman in that photograph as Naomi Parker Fraley. Rockwell's model was Mary Doyle Keefe, a telephone operator from Vermont.
