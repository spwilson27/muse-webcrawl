Mizrahi Jews

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mizrahi Jews (Hebrew: יהודי המיזרח, "Eastern Jews") are the descendants of local Jewish communities of the Middle East and North Africa — including Babylonian (Iraqi), Persian, Yemenite, Kurdish, Syrian, Egyptian, Moroccan and other communities — many of which trace their origins to antiquity and the Babylonian exile. The term Mizrahi (from mizrach, "east") came into widespread use in Israel after 1948 to describe Jews who immigrated from Muslim-majority countries.

History

Jewish communities existed across the region for more than two millennia, under Babylonian, Persian, Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, Arab, Ottoman and European colonial rule. Following the establishment of Israel in 1948 and amid rising tensions, pogroms and expulsion policies, the great majority of these communities emigrated — most to Israel, others to France, the Americas and elsewhere. Operations such as the airlifts of Yemenite Jews (1949–50) and Iraqi Jews (1951–52) brought entire communities to Israel within a few years.

Culture

Mizrahi Jews traditionally spoke Judeo-Arabic and other Jewish languages such as Judeo-Persian, and developed distinctive liturgical rites, religious poetry (piyyutim), cuisine and musical traditions, including the Andalusian and maqam-influenced styles. In Israel, Mizrahi music (musica mizrahit) grew from a marginalised genre into a central current of Israeli popular culture, and Mizrahi religious and cultural life is closely associated with the Shas political movement, founded to represent Sephardi and Mizrahi Haredi interests.

Relationship to Sephardi Jews

"Mizrahi" overlaps with but is not identical to "Sephardi", which properly refers to descendants of Jews expelled from Spain in 1492, many of whom settled in North Africa and the Ottoman Empire and intermarried with older local communities. In modern Israeli usage the two terms are often grouped together as "Sephardim and Mizrahim" (Edot HaMizrach, "Communities of the East").
