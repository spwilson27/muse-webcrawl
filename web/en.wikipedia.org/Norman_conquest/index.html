Norman Conquest

Norman Conquest of England
Date1066–1075
LocationKingdom of England
ResultNorman victory; William I crowned King of England

The Norman Conquest (in British history often called the Norman Conquest of England) was the 11th-century invasion and occupation of England by an army made up of thousands of Norman, Breton, Flemish, and French troops, all led by the Duke of Normandy, later styled William the Conqueror.

Background

William claimed that the childless English king Edward the Confessor had promised him the throne and that Harold Godwinson, crowned after Edward's death in January 1066, had sworn to support his claim. In September 1066 Harold first defeated a Norwegian invasion at the Battle of Stamford Bridge, then hurried south to meet William, who had landed at Pevensey on 28 September.

Battle of Hastings

The two armies met at the Battle of Hastings on 14 October 1066. Harold was killed — tradition holds by an arrow through the eye — and the English army was destroyed. William marched on London and was crowned king at Westminster Abbey on Christmas Day 1066.

Consolidation

English resistance continued for several years, culminating in the Harrying of the North (1069–70), a brutal campaign that devastated much of northern England. The last major rebellion, centred on the Isle of Ely under Hereward the Wake, was suppressed in 1071. By 1075 Norman rule was secure.

Consequences

The Conquest transformed England. The Anglo-Saxon aristocracy was almost entirely replaced by Normans; by 1086, when the Domesday Book survey was compiled, only a handful of great English landowners survived. Norman French became the language of the court and government, profoundly influencing the English language. Castles and great Romanesque cathedrals were built across the country, and England was drawn into closer political and cultural ties with continental Europe.
