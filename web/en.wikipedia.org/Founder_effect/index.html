Founder effect

In population genetics, the founder effect is the loss of genetic variation that occurs when a new population is established by a very small number of individuals from a larger population. First described by Ernst Mayr in 1942, it is a special case of genetic drift. As a result of the loss of genetic variation, the new population may be distinctively different, both genotypically and phenotypically, from the parent population from which it is derived. In extreme cases, the founder effect is thought to lead to the speciation and subsequent evolution of new species.

Illustration
A small sample of founders carries only a subset of the source population's alleles

Mechanism

When a small group colonises a new habitat — for example an island — or when a population passes through a severe bottleneck, the alleles carried by the founders are rarely a representative sample of the source gene pool. Alleles common in the source may be absent among the founders, while rare alleles may by chance be over-represented and subsequently become common. Because the founding population is small, inbreeding increases and genetic drift operates strongly over the first generations.

Serial founder effect

A serial founder effect occurs when a population migrates over long distances through a series of successive founding events, each subsampling the previous population. This pattern has been used to explain the decline in genetic diversity with distance from Africa in human populations, consistent with an origin of modern humans in Africa followed by stepwise colonisation of the rest of the world.

Examples

Island populations

Island species frequently show reduced genetic diversity compared to mainland relatives. The flora and fauna of the Galápagos and Hawaiian islands descend from very small numbers of colonists, and island birds such as the silvereye populations of the Pacific have served as classic natural experiments.

Human populations

Founder effects are important in human medical genetics. Isolated populations often have elevated frequencies of particular inherited disorders: among the Old Order Amish of Pennsylvania, for instance, Ellis–van Creveld syndrome is unusually common, tracing to the small number of founding couples. High rates of certain recessive disorders are also documented in Ashkenazi Jewish, Finnish, and French-Canadian populations, in part reflecting historical founder events and endogamy. The population of Pingelap atoll, after a typhoon in 1775 reduced survivors to about 20, shows a very high frequency of total colour blindness (achromatopsia).
