Royal charter

TypeLegal instrument
Issued byThe monarch, on advice of the Privy Council
PurposeIncorporation, grant of rights or status

A royal charter is a formal grant issued by a monarch under royal prerogative as letters patent, granting a right, power or status to an individual or a body corporate. In the United Kingdom and other Commonwealth realms, royal charters are granted by the sovereign on the advice of the Privy Council. Historically they were used to create chartered companies, boroughs, universities and colonies; today they most often incorporate professional bodies, charities and other institutions.

History

Charters have been issued in England since the medieval period. In the 16th and 17th centuries, chartered trading companies such as the East India Company (chartered in 1600) and the Hudson's Bay Company (1670) received monopolies and quasi-governmental powers overseas. Many American colonies, including Massachusetts Bay and Pennsylvania, originated as royal charters granted to proprietors or companies.

Chartered bodies

Bodies incorporated by royal charter are known as chartered bodies. They include universities such as those founded before the modern era, learned societies such as the Royal Society (1662), and professional institutions whose members may use titles such as "chartered engineer" or "chartered accountant". In 2013, a royal charter on self-regulation of the press was granted following the Leveson Inquiry.

Legal effect

A royal charter incorporates the recipient body, giving it legal personality independent of its members, and can confer privileges such as the right to use the word "royal". Amendments to a charter generally require the approval of the Privy Council. Because the prerogative is exercised on ministerial advice, the grant of a charter is in practice a decision of government.
