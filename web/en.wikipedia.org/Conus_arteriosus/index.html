Conus arteriosus

The conus arteriosus is a conical pouch formed in the upper left angle of the right ventricle in the chordate heart, from which the pulmonary trunk arises. In mammals it is usually known as the infundibulum.

Anatomy

The conus arteriosus forms the smooth-walled outflow tract of the right ventricle, funnelling blood toward the pulmonary valve. It is separated from the inflow portion of the ventricle by the supraventricular crest. Tendinous chords are not attached to the walls of the infundibulum, distinguishing it from the trabeculated inflow part of the ventricle. Its posterior surface gives rise to the pulmonary trunk, which carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs.

Development

The conus arteriosus is formed by the fusion of the bulbus cordis and the truncus arteriosus in the developing embryo. Defects in its development can produce malformations of the outflow tracts of the heart, such as tetralogy of Fallot, in which the infundibulum is abnormally narrowed (infundibular stenosis).

In other vertebrates

In fishes and amphibians, the conus arteriosus is a distinct contractile chamber containing valves that helps to propel blood into the ventral aorta and smooths the pulsatile flow from the single ventricle. In lungfishes and amphibians it may contain a spiral valve that helps direct oxygenated and deoxygenated blood into different vessels.
