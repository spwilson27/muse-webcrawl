The bassoon is a woodwind instrument in the double reed family, which plays in the tenor and bass ranges. It was developed in its modern form in the 19th century from the Baroque bassoon and the earlier dulcian (curtal). The instrument is folded back on itself: its bore is about 2.5 metres (8 feet) long folded into a body roughly 1.3 metres tall, usually made of maple.

The bassoon is known for its distinctive tone colour, wide range, variety of character, and agility. Listeners often compare its sound to that of a tenor voice. Its name derives from the Italian fagotto, from the instrument's resemblance to a bundle of sticks.

Construction

The bassoon consists of six parts: the bell joint, bass (long) joint, boot joint, wing joint, tenor joint, and the metal crook (bocal) carrying the double reed. Two fingering systems are in use: the German Heckel system, used in most of the world, and the French Buffet system, retained chiefly in France, Belgium, and parts of Latin America. The larger contrabassoon sounds an octave lower.

Repertoire and role

The bassoon is a standard member of the orchestra, wind quintet, and concert band. Famous solos include the opening of Stravinsky's The Rite of Spring, the grandfather's theme in Prokofiev's Peter and the Wolf, the second movement of Shostakovich's Ninth Symphony, and the "clown of the orchestra" solo in Paul Dukas's The Sorcerer's Apprentice. Vivaldi wrote 39 concertos for the instrument; Mozart's Bassoon Concerto (1774) is a cornerstone of the repertoire.

History

The bassoon emerged as a distinct instrument in the 17th century, used in church and court music; improvements by the Heckel family in the 19th century standardized the modern German instrument.
