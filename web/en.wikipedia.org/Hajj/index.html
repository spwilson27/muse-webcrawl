The Hajj is an annual Islamic pilgrimage to Mecca, Saudi Arabia, the holiest city for Muslims. It is a mandatory religious duty for Muslims that must be carried out at least once in a lifetime by every adult Muslim who is physically and financially capable of undertaking the journey and can support their family during their absence. The Hajj is one of the Five Pillars of Islam, alongside Shahada, Salah, Zakat, and Sawm.

Rites

The pilgrimage occurs from the 8th to the 13th day of Dhu al-Hijjah, the last month of the Islamic calendar. Pilgrims enter a state of consecration called ihram, wearing simple white garments that erase signs of wealth and status. Central rites commemorate Abraham (Ibrahim), Hagar, and Ishmael: circling the Kaaba seven times (Tawaf); walking between the hills of Safa and Marwah (Sa'i), recalling Hagar's search for water; the Day of Arafat (9 Dhu al-Hijjah), spent in prayer on the plain of Arafat, considered the spiritual climax; the symbolic stoning of pillars at Mina representing the devil; and the sacrifice of an animal at Eid al-Adha, followed by shaving the head (for men) and a farewell Tawaf.

History and organisation

Muslims trace the pilgrimage to Abraham, and its present form to Muhammad's "Farewell Pilgrimage" of 632 CE. Today Saudi Arabia administers the Hajj under a national quota system (roughly 1,000 pilgrims per million of each country's Muslim population). The gathering is among the largest annual assemblies of people in the world, posing immense logistical challenges; disasters such as the 2015 Mina crowd crush, which killed over two thousand people, have prompted repeated infrastructure expansions, including the multi-level Jamarat Bridge and the world's largest tent city at Mina.

Significance

Completing the Hajj earns the honorific Hajji and is regarded as a profound spiritual renewal—the pilgrim returns, in the traditional image, as free of sin as on the day of birth. A lesser pilgrimage, Umrah, may be performed at any time of year.
