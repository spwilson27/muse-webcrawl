Neopaganism

Neopaganism, also known as modern paganism and contemporary paganism, is a collective term for a diverse set of new religious movements influenced by or claiming to revive the pre-Christian polytheistic religions of Europe, North Africa, and the Near East. Neopagans generally celebrate seasonal festivals (often a "Wheel of the Year"), revere nature, and practise polytheistic, animistic, or pantheistic beliefs, though the movement is highly decentralised with no central authority or shared scripture.

Major traditions

Prominent neopagan traditions include Wicca, founded in mid-20th-century England by Gerald Gardner, centred on the worship of a Goddess and God and the practice of ritual magic; Heathenry, which reconstructs Germanic and Norse religion; neo-Druidism, inspired by the Celtic druids; and various reconstructionist movements reviving Hellenic, Roman, Egyptian (Kemetic), Slavic, and Baltic religions. Many practitioners are solitary or eclectic rather than affiliated with a single tradition.

History

The roots of neopaganism lie in Romanticism, 19th-century folklore studies, and occult orders such as the Golden Dawn. Wicca emerged publicly in the 1950s after the repeal of Britain's Witchcraft Act, and the movement spread widely with the counterculture of the 1960s and 1970s, absorbing influences from feminism, environmentalism, and ceremonial magic.

Demographics and recognition

Estimates of neopagan adherents worldwide vary widely, from hundreds of thousands to over a million, concentrated in the United States, the United Kingdom, and other parts of the English-speaking world, with growing communities elsewhere. Pagan groups have won legal recognition as religions in several countries, and pagan chaplains serve in some militaries, prisons, and universities.
