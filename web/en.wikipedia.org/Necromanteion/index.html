A necromanteion (Greek: νεκρομαντεῖον), or oracle of the dead, was an ancient Greek temple devoted to necromancy — the consultation of the spirits of the dead. The most famous example is the Necromanteion of Acheron in Epirus, traditionally identified with the site described by Herodotus and associated in myth with the entrance to Hades, near the confluence of the rivers Acheron, Pyriphlegethon, and Cocytus.

The Acheron site

The archaeological site on a hill near the village of Mesopotamos was excavated by Sotirios Dakaris beginning in 1958. The main complex dates to the Hellenistic period (late 4th–3rd century BC), though the cult may be older. The sanctuary burned in 167 BC during the Roman conquest of Epirus and was later reused, with a monastery of St. John built over part of the ruins in the 18th century.

Ritual practice

Pilgrims seeking to consult the dead underwent days of preparation: ritual purification, a special diet (including beans and pork), sacrifices, and incantations, led by priests through a sequence of dark rooms and labyrinthine corridors. At the climax, in an underground vaulted chamber, the supplicant was believed to encounter the shadows of the dead and receive answers to questions. Dakaris interpreted parts of the complex — including remains of a large bronze mechanism with gears and wheels — as stage machinery for raising images of the gods or lowering priests, though this interpretation remains debated.

Herodotus records that Periander, tyrant of Corinth, sent envoys to the Acheron oracle to question the ghost of his dead wife Melissa. In Homer's Odyssey, Odysseus's descent to the dead (the Nekyia) was also traditionally located in this region.

Other oracles of the dead

Other necromanteia are attested in the Greek world, including sites associated with Heraclea Pontica, Tainaron (Cape Matapan), and Cumae in Italy, the last linked with the Sibyl and the underworld journey of Aeneas in Virgil's Aeneid.
