Julia Kristeva (Bulgarian: Юлия Кръстева; born 24 June 1941) is a Bulgarian-French philosopher, literary critic, semiotician, psychoanalyst, feminist and novelist who has lived in Paris since the mid-1960s. A leading figure of post-structuralism, she coined the concept of intertextuality and developed influential accounts of language, subjectivity, abjection and melancholia.

Life and career

Kristeva was born in Sliven, Bulgaria, and studied linguistics at the University of Sofia. In 1965 she moved to Paris on a research fellowship, entering the circle around the journal Tel Quel and Roland Barthes, whose seminar shaped her early work; she married the writer Philippe Sollers in 1967. Her doctoral work, published as Revolution in Poetic Language (1974), brought her international attention. She later trained as a psychoanalyst, taught at Paris Diderot University as professor of linguistics, and has been a visiting professor at Columbia University. In 2004 she became the first recipient of the Holberg International Memorial Prize.

Key concepts

Building on Bakhtin, Kristeva proposed that every text is a "mosaic of quotations" — an absorption and transformation of other texts — coining the term intertextuality in the late 1960s. In Revolution in Poetic Language she distinguished the semiotic (rhythmic, bodily drives, linked to the pre-Oedipal chora) from the symbolic (grammar, syntax and law), arguing that poetic language renews meaning by letting the semiotic disrupt the symbolic. In Powers of Horror (1980) she developed the concept of abjection — the horror provoked by what disturbs identity and borders, such as corpses and filth. Later works explored melancholia (Black Sun, 1987), love (Tales of Love), foreignness (Strangers to Ourselves, 1988) and revolt, alongside novels including The Samurai (1990) and Murder in Byzantium (2004).

Influence

Kristeva's work has been central to literary theory, feminist thought — though her relation to feminism is ambivalent and much debated — and psychoanalytic criticism. With figures such as Barthes, Derrida and Foucault, she helped define the post-structuralist moment in French intellectual life.
