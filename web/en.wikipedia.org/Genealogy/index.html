Genealogy

Genealogy (from Greek genealogia, "the making of a pedigree") is the study of families, family history, and the tracing of their lineages. Genealogists use historical records, oral interviews, and genetic analysis to obtain information about families and to demonstrate kinship and pedigrees of their members. The results are often displayed in charts or written as narratives, and a person's recorded genealogy is known as a family tree or pedigree.

History

Interest in ancestry is ancient: genealogies appear in the Hebrew Bible, in the lineage lists of Chinese clans, and in the pedigrees of European nobility, for whom descent established rights to titles and property. In many cultures, including Māori, Icelandic, and West African griot traditions, genealogies were preserved orally for centuries. In the modern period genealogy became a popular pursuit among ordinary people, especially after the publication of Alex Haley's Roots (1976), and it is now one of the most widespread hobbies in the Western world.

Records and research

Genealogical research relies on vital records (birth, marriage, and death certificates), census returns, parish and church registers, wills and probate records, land and tax records, military service files, passenger lists, and newspapers. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, whose doctrine motivates extensive family history work, maintains the world's largest genealogical archive through FamilySearch, including the Granite Mountain Records Vault in Utah. Since the 1990s, the Internet has transformed the field: subscription databases such as Ancestry.com and free services such as FamilySearch and WikiTree hold billions of digitised records and user-built trees. Genetic genealogy, using genealogical DNA tests, has since the 2000s added a scientific tool for confirming or overturning documentary conclusions.

Standards

Professional genealogists follow proof standards — most prominently the Genealogical Proof Standard — requiring reasonably exhaustive research, full source citations, analysis and correlation of evidence, resolution of conflicts, and a soundly reasoned conclusion. Amateurs' online trees, by contrast, frequently contain errors copied from one tree to another.
