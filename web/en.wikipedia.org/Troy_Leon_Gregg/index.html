Troy Leon Gregg (1932 – July 29, 1980) was an American convicted murderer whose case, Gregg v. Georgia, decided by the Supreme Court of the United States in 1976, held that the death penalty was not inherently cruel and unusual punishment and thereby allowed states to resume executions after a four-year nationwide moratorium.

Crime and trial

In November 1973, Gregg and a travelling companion, Floyd Allen, were hitchhiking in Georgia when they were picked up by Fred Edward Simmons and Bob Moore. The following day the bodies of Simmons and Moore were found in a ditch along U.S. Route 41 in Gwinnett County; they had been shot. Gregg was convicted of murder and armed robbery and sentenced to death under Georgia's new guided-discretion capital sentencing statute.

Gregg v. Georgia

Gregg appealed, arguing that the death penalty was cruel and unusual under the Eighth Amendment. On July 2, 1976, the Supreme Court upheld his sentence by a 7–2 vote, ruling that capital punishment, applied through procedures that guide the discretion of sentencing authorities, is constitutional. The decision ended the de facto moratorium that had followed Furman v. Georgia (1972).

Escape and death

Gregg remained on death row at Reidsville State Prison. On July 28, 1980, the night before his scheduled execution, Gregg escaped with three other condemned men; they had sawed through bars and disguised themselves. The following night, Gregg was beaten to death in a bar fight in North Carolina, becoming one of the few death row inmates in the United States to escape and die before execution could be carried out.
