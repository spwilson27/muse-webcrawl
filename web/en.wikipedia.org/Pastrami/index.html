Pastrami

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pastrami on rye, the classic New York deli sandwich

Pastrami is a cured meat product, most famously made from beef, that is brined, seasoned with spices, smoked and steamed before serving. It is a signature of Jewish delicatessens in New York City, where it is traditionally sliced thick and served hot on rye bread with mustard.

Origins

Pastrami descends from the preserved meats of the Ottoman world: Turkish pastırma is air-dried, spice-crusted beef, and Romanian pastramă was originally made from goose or mutton preserved for the winter. The very word is often linked to the Romanian verb a păstra, "to keep, to preserve". Jewish immigrants from Romania brought the technique to New York in the late nineteenth century, adapting it to inexpensive cuts of beef — classically the navel plate, and later brisket — which Jewish dietary law permitted.

Preparation

The raw meat is first cured in brine with salt, nitrites, sugar and pickling spices for days or weeks. It is then desalted, coated with a coarse rub of cracked black pepper, coriander and other spices, and smoked. Before serving, the pastrami is steamed until the connective tissue softens, giving the meat its characteristic tender, juicy texture and dark reddish-brown crust. Variations include Montreal-style smoked meat, which uses a different cure and spice balance, and turkey or salmon "pastrami" seasoned in the same fashion.

In popular culture

Pastrami sandwiches from delis such as Katz's Delicatessen in New York became icons of American food culture; Katz's is also famous for the scene filmed there for the film When Harry Met Sally... (1989).
