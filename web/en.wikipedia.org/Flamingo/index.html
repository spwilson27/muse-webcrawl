Flamingo

Flamingos are a type of wading bird in the family Phoenicopteridae, the only extant family in the order Phoenicopteriformes. There are six living species, distributed in the Americas (including the Caribbean) and in Africa, Asia, and southern Europe. Flamingos are famous for their bright pink plumage, stilt-like legs, and habit of standing on one leg; they live in large, highly social flocks that may number in the thousands, as on the soda lakes of East Africa's Rift Valley.

Description and feeding

Flamingos range from about 80 to 145 cm in height; the greater flamingo (Phoenicopterus roseus) is the largest and most widespread species. Their pink or reddish colour comes from carotenoid pigments in the algae and small crustaceans they eat; without carotenoids the feathers fade to grey or white, and breeding adults may bleach nearly white while raising chicks. Flamingos are specialised filter feeders: holding their large, sharply bent bills upside down in the water, they pump water across comb-like lamellae that trap blue-green algae, diatoms, and small invertebrates such as brine shrimp. The lesser flamingo feeds almost entirely on microscopic algae in highly alkaline lakes that few other animals can exploit.

Behaviour and breeding

Flamingos perform synchronised group displays — head-flagging, wing-salutes, and marching — before breeding. Both parents build a cone-shaped mud nest in shallow water; a single chalky egg is laid, and both sexes incubate it for about a month. Chicks are fed a protein- and fat-rich secretion called "crop milk" produced in the parents' upper digestive tract, and gather in crèches watched over by a few adults. Flamingos are strongly monogamous within a season and can live 30–50 years, longer in captivity. Why they stand on one leg remains debated; proposed explanations include heat and energy conservation and drying the resting leg. Their closest living relatives are the grebes, with which they form the clade Mirandornithes.

Conservation

The Andean and puna (James's) flamingos of high South American salt flats, and the lesser flamingo, are considered near threatened or vulnerable, threatened by mining (including lithium extraction from salt flats), water diversion, disturbance at breeding colonies, and changes in lake chemistry. The Caribbean (American) flamingo has recovered in parts of its range after persecution for feathers and eggs.
