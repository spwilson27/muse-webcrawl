Sortition

In governance, sortition (also known as selection by lot, allotment, or the demarchy system) is the selection of political officials by random sampling — choosing officeholders as a representative sample of the population, rather than by election or appointment.

History

Sortition was central to classical Athenian democracy: most magistrates, the Council of 500 (Boule), and the large popular juries of the dikasteria were chosen by lot, using allotment machines called kleroteria, because the Athenians considered the lot more democratic than elections, which they associated with oligarchy and wealth. Generals and some financial officers were elected instead.

The practice persisted in later republics: Renaissance Florence and Venice used combinations of lot and scrutiny to select officials, notably in the multi-stage procedure for choosing the Doge of Venice, designed to frustrate faction and bribery.

Modern use

The most familiar surviving use of selection by lot is the trial jury. Since the late twentieth century, sortition has been revived in deliberative democracy experiments: citizens' assemblies chosen by stratified random sampling, such as British Columbia's Citizens' Assembly on Electoral Reform (2004) and the Irish citizens' assemblies, have deliberated on constitutional questions. Advocates argue that allotted bodies are descriptively representative and resistant to campaign finance and party discipline; critics question the competence and accountability of unelected officials.
