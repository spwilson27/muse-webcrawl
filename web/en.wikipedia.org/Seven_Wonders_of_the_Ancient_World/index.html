The Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, also known as the Seven Wonders of the World, is a list of remarkable constructions of classical antiquity given by various authors in guidebooks or accounts popular among the ancient Hellenic tourists. The list represents the most spectacular man-made sights of the Mediterranean and Near Eastern world as known to the ancient Greeks. Of the seven, only the Great Pyramid of Giza still survives substantially intact; the existence of one of them, the Hanging Gardens, remains unproven.

The list

The final form of the list emerged in the Hellenistic period, with antecedents in the works of Herodotus and Callimachus of Cyrene, and a surviving version attributed to Antipater of Sidon. The wonders are:

  • The Great Pyramid of Giza in Egypt (c. 2580–2560 BC), tomb of Pharaoh Khufu — the oldest wonder and the only one still standing.
  • The Hanging Gardens of Babylon (in present-day Iraq), legendary terraced gardens said to have been built by Nebuchadnezzar II; no definitive archaeological evidence has been found at Babylon, prompting some scholars to relocate them to Nineveh.
  • The Statue of Zeus at Olympia (c. 435 BC), a giant gold-and-ivory figure by Phidias, later taken to Constantinople and destroyed by fire.
  • The Temple of Artemis at Ephesus (c. 550 BC, rebuilt c. 323 BC), burned down by the arsonist Herostratus and finally destroyed in late antiquity.
  • The Mausoleum at Halicarnassus (c. 350 BC), tomb of Mausolus, satrap of Caria — the origin of the word "mausoleum."
  • The Colossus of Rhodes (280 BC), a giant bronze statue of Helios that stood only about 56 years before being toppled by an earthquake.
  • The Lighthouse of Alexandria, or Pharos (c. 280–247 BC), over 100 metres tall, destroyed by earthquakes in the 14th century AD after more than 1,600 years.

Legacy

The canon inspired countless later lists — of the medieval world, of nature, and of modern engineering — and the modern "New7Wonders of the World," chosen by a global poll in 2007. The wonders remain a byword for the greatest achievements of ancient building, and for the transience of all but one of them.
