Suggestibility

Suggestibility is the quality of being inclined to accept and act on the suggestions of others, in which false but plausible information is given and one fills in the gaps in certain memories with false information when recalling a scenario or moment. Suggestibility can also cause one to alter their behaviours and actions as though following an instruction, given by another person or oneself.

Hypnotic suggestibility

In the context of hypnosis, suggestibility refers to the degree to which a person responds to hypnotic suggestions, such as arm levitation or the inability to open one's eyes. Standardised scales, such as the Stanford Hypnotic Susceptibility Scales, measure individual differences, which are relatively stable across the lifespan. Roughly 10–15% of people are highly hypnotisable.

Interrogative suggestibility

Interrogative suggestibility, studied by Gisli Gudjonsson, is the tendency to yield to leading questions and to shift one's answers under interpersonal pressure during police interrogation. It is implicated in false confessions and unreliable witness testimony. Children and people with intellectual disabilities tend to score higher on measures of interrogative suggestibility.

Memory and misinformation

Suggestible individuals are more prone to the misinformation effect, incorporating misleading post-event information into their recollections. Research by Elizabeth Loftus demonstrated that the wording of questions can alter remembered details, contributing to the creation of false memories.

Factors

Suggestibility increases with fatigue, anxiety, uncertainty, and immaturity, and is influenced by the status and confidence of the person making the suggestion. It plays a role in placebo responses, eyewitness testimony, and recovered-memory controversies.
