Third rail

A third rail, also known as a live rail, electric rail or conductor rail, is a method of providing electric power to a railway locomotive or train, through a semi-continuous rigid conductor placed alongside or between the rails of a railway track. It is used typically in a mass transit or rapid transit system, which has alignments in its own corridors, fully or almost fully segregated from the outside environment. Third rail systems are always supplied from direct current electricity.

History

Third-rail systems are not the first means of railway electrification, but they were widely adopted in the early decades of electric traction. Early experiments date to the 1880s, including systems demonstrated by Werner von Siemens and by Thomas Edison. The first commercial third-rail line is generally considered to be the City and South London Railway, opened in 1890. The system spread rapidly to underground and elevated railways in cities such as New York, Chicago, Boston, Paris and London.

Operation

The conductor rail is supported on ceramic insulators or insulated brackets, typically at a voltage in the range of 600 to 1,500 volts DC. Trains draw power through sliding contact shoes mounted on their bogies. The running rails usually serve as the return conductor. Where the third rail must be interrupted, such as at level crossings and junctions, gaps are bridged by the train's multiple collector shoes.

Variations

Contact may be made with the top, side or bottom of the rail. Top-contact systems are the simplest but expose the live rail to weather and accidental contact; bottom-contact (underrunning) systems, used by systems such as the New York City Subway's protected segments and many modern installations, cover the rail with an insulating board for safety. Side-contact systems were used by some early lines. A related development is the fourth rail system, as used on the London Underground, which provides a dedicated return rail.

Safety

Because the conductor rail is exposed and carries lethal voltages, third-rail systems present hazards to trespassers, track workers and animals. Modern installations use protective cover boards, warning signage and fencing. Compared with overhead lines, however, third rail is cheaper to install, requires smaller tunnels, and is less visually intrusive, which has kept it in use on metro systems worldwide.
