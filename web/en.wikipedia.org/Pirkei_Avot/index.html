Pirkei Avot (Hebrew: פרקי אבות, "Chapters of the Fathers"), also known in English as Ethics of the Fathers, is a compilation of the ethical teachings and maxims of Mishnaic-era rabbis. It is a tractate of the Mishnah, located in the order of Nezikin, though unlike most tractates in that order it deals almost entirely with ethical and moral teaching rather than law.

Structure

The tractate comprises five chapters, with a sixth chapter (the Baraita of Kinyan Torah) added later and traditionally recited together with the first five. The first chapter traces the chain of transmission of the Oral Torah from Moses at Mount Sinai through the prophets and the Men of the Great Assembly down to the early rabbinic sages, grounding the sayings that follow in that chain of tradition.

"Moses received the Torah from Sinai and transmitted it to Joshua; Joshua to the elders; the elders to the prophets; and the prophets handed it down to the Men of the Great Assembly." (Avot 1:1)

Content and themes

Pirkei Avot contains many of the most famous sayings of rabbinic Judaism. Hillel the Elder is quoted: "If I am not for myself, who will be for me? But if I am only for myself, what am I? If not now, when?" Shammai taught: "Make your Torah study a fixed practice; say little and do much; and receive all men with a pleasant countenance." Rabbi Shimon ben Gamliel taught that the world stands on three things: on truth, on judgment, and on peace.

Other celebrated passages include the five disciples of Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai, the saying of Ben Zoma—"Who is wise? He who learns from all men. Who is mighty? He who subdues his evil inclination"—and the parables and numbered sayings (things created in tens, sevens, and fours) of the fifth chapter.

Liturgical use

It is customary in many Jewish communities to study one chapter of Pirkei Avot each Shabbat afternoon during the six weeks between Passover and Shavuot (in some traditions, throughout the summer), making the tractate among the most widely known and commented-upon texts of the Mishnah. It is printed in many prayer books.

Commentaries

Commentaries have been written by Maimonides (whose introduction, the Eight Chapters, is a classic of Jewish philosophy), Rabbenu Yonah of Girona, the Maharal of Prague, and many others, as well as modern translations and ethical studies.
