Pasture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cattle grazing on pasture land

Pasture (from the Latin pastus, "fed") is land used for grazing by domesticated livestock such as cattle, sheep, horses and goats. Pasture lands range from intensively managed, sown grasslands near the farm to extensive semi-natural rangelands, and they are a major category of agricultural land worldwide — pasture and meadows occupy roughly a quarter of the Earth's ice-free land surface.

Types and management

In improved pasture, farmers sow productive grasses such as perennial ryegrass and legumes such as white clover, apply fertiliser, and control grazing to keep the sward in a leafy, nutritious state. Rotational grazing moves animals between paddocks so that each area is grazed and then rested to regrow, while continuous grazing leaves stock on the same ground all season. Unimproved pasture — hill grazing, moorland, steppe and communal common land — relies on native vegetation and is stocked at lower densities.

Ecology

Permanent grasslands store substantial carbon in their soils and can support rich plant and invertebrate communities, especially where grazing is light and fertiliser use low; many wildflower meadows depend on grazing or mowing to prevent scrub encroachment. Conversely, overgrazing degrades pasture, compacting soil, encouraging unpalatable species and causing erosion, a process implicated in desertification in dry regions. Pasture management therefore balances forage production against ground cover, water quality and biodiversity.

Pasture in law and culture

Rights of pasturage — grazing on common land — were a central institution of medieval and early modern agriculture, and commons systems survive in parts of Europe, including the New Forest and the Alpine summer pastures to which cattle are still driven each year (transhumance). The word also gives English the phrase "put out to pasture" for retirement.
