Yiddish (ייִדיש, Yidish), literally "Jewish", is a West Germanic language historically spoken by Ashkenazi Jews. It developed in Central Europe around the 9th–12th centuries from Middle High German dialects, fused with substantial elements from Hebrew and Aramaic and, later, Slavic languages. Yiddish is written in the Hebrew alphabet.

History

For centuries Yiddish was the everyday vernacular (mame-loshn, "mother tongue") of Ashkenazi communities across Europe, while Hebrew remained the language of liturgy and scholarship. It developed a rich literature, theatre and press; by the early 20th century there were an estimated 11–13 million speakers, and Yiddish culture flourished in cities from Warsaw and Vilnius to New York's Lower East Side. The 1925-founded YIVO Institute standardised its scholarly study.

The Holocaust destroyed the majority of Yiddish speakers and the language's European heartland. In the Soviet Union, Yiddish culture was suppressed under Stalin, and in Israel the young state promoted Hebrew, often at Yiddish's expense.

Present day

Today most native Yiddish speakers are members of Hasidic and Haredi communities in the United States, Israel and elsewhere, where the language remains a living daily vernacular and its speaker base is growing rapidly through high birth rates. Secular interest has also revived, with university courses, festivals and translation projects; the writer Isaac Bashevis Singer won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1978 for his Yiddish fiction.

Features

Yiddish grammar is broadly Germanic, but it uses Hebrew-derived vocabulary for religious and communal concepts and Slavic loans especially in Eastern Yiddish dialects. Its influence on American English includes words such as bagel, schmooze, chutzpah and klutz.
