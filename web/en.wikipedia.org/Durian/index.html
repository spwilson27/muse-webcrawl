The durian is the edible fruit of several tree species of the genus Durio, native to Borneo and Sumatra in Southeast Asia. It is regarded in the region as the "king of fruits", famous for its large size, formidable thorn-covered husk, creamy flesh — and powerful, divisive odour.

Description

The fruit can grow to about 30 cm (12 in) long and weigh 1–3 kg. Its husk is covered in sharp spikes (the name comes from the Malay duri, "thorn"). Inside, the flesh ranges from pale yellow to deep orange-red depending on species and cultivar; it is custard-like in texture and rich in flavour. Popular cultivars include Musang King and Monthong.

Odour and taste

The smell of durian has been compared to almonds and custard by its admirers, and to rotting onions, garlic and gym socks by detractors; the naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace famously praised its flavour as worth a journey to the East to experience. Because of its penetrating odour, durian is banned in many hotels and public transport systems across Southeast Asia. The flesh is eaten fresh and used in ice cream, cakes, candies and savoury dishes; the seeds can be cooked.

Cultivation

Thailand is the largest exporter of durians, with Malaysia and Indonesia also major producers; demand from China has driven large recent expansion. The trees bear fruit seasonally and the fruit is considered ripe when it falls from the tree.
