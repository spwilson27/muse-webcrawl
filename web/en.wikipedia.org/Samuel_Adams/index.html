Samuel Adams (1722–1803) was an American statesman, political philosopher, and one of the Founding Fathers of the United States. A leader of the movement that became the American Revolution, he was a key organiser of resistance to British taxation in Massachusetts and a signer of the Declaration of Independence.

Early life

Adams was born in Boston into a prosperous family, graduated from Harvard College in 1740, and worked in his family's malt business. A cousin of John Adams, he became involved in colonial politics as a clerk and tax collector, though he was famously poor at the latter occupation.

Revolutionary activity

Adams helped organise opposition to the Stamp Act of 1765 and was associated with the Sons of Liberty. He wrote prolifically under pseudonyms, promoted the committees of correspondence, and was linked to the Boston Tea Party of 1773. He served in the Continental Congress from 1774 to 1781.

Later career

After the war Adams served as lieutenant governor of Massachusetts (1789–1793) and then as governor (1794–1797). He died in Boston in 1803. John Adams famously described him as indispensable to the Revolution.
