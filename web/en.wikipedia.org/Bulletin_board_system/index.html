Bulletin board system

A bulletin board system (BBS) is a computer server running software that allows users to connect to the system using a terminal program. Once logged in, the user can perform functions such as uploading and downloading software and data, reading news and bulletins, and exchanging messages with other users through public message boards and sometimes via direct chatting. In the early 1980s, message networks such as FidoNet were developed to provide services such as NetMail, which is similar to Internet-based email.

History

The first public dial-up BBS, the Computerized Bulletin Board System (CBBS), was created by Ward Christensen and Randy Suess in Chicago in 1978. BBSes flourished from the late 1970s through the mid-1990s, first on microcomputers with modems and a single telephone line — meaning one caller at a time on most systems — and later with multi-line and Internet-connected setups.

Culture

BBSes fostered a distinctive online culture: ANSI art, door games such as Trade Wars, demo scene releases, and local communities organized around sysops (system operators). Many systems were hobbyist-run and free; others charged subscriptions. Networks like FidoNet linked thousands of boards worldwide, exchanging mail and echomail conferences overnight by modem.

Decline and legacy

The rise of the commercial Internet and the World Wide Web in the mid-1990s rapidly displaced dial-up BBSes, though a small community of telnet- and web-accessible boards survives. BBSes are regarded as precursors of web forums, social media, and online gaming communities.
