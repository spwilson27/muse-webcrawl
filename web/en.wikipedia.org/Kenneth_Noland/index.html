Kenneth Noland

Noland's concentric "target" paintings are among the signature images of Color Field painting.

Kenneth Noland
Born: April 10, 1924, Asheville, North Carolina, U.S.
Died: January 5, 2010, Port Clyde, Maine, U.S.
Movement: Color Field painting, Post-painterly Abstraction
Known for: Target paintings, chevrons, stripe paintings

Kenneth Noland (April 10, 1924 – January 5, 2010) was an American painter and one of the best-known exponents of Color Field painting. Along with Morris Louis and Helen Frankenthaler, he developed the technique of staining thinned paint into unprimed canvas, so that colour became one with the surface rather than sitting on top of it.

Life and career

Noland served in the United States Army Air Forces during the Second World War and then studied at Black Mountain College in North Carolina under Josef Albers and Ilya Bolotowsky, absorbing Bauhaus colour theory. After a period in Paris, where he had his first solo exhibition in 1949, he settled in Washington, D.C. A pivotal 1953 visit with critic Clement Greenberg to Helen Frankenthaler's New York studio introduced him to the stain technique.

Work

Noland worked in a sequence of clearly defined formats: the concentric-circle Targets (1958–1962), the nested Chevrons of the 1960s, long horizontal Stripe paintings in the 1960s and 1970s, and later shaped and plaid canvases. His aim was a pure, impersonal art in which the image is generated directly by colour relationships. Greenberg championed him as a leading figure of Post-painterly Abstraction, and his work was shown in Greenberg's influential 1964 exhibition of that name at the Los Angeles County Museum of Art.

Legacy

Noland represented the United States at the Venice Biennale in 1964. Retrospectives of his work were held at the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum (1977) and other major institutions, and his paintings are held by museums worldwide. He spent his later years in Maine, continuing to paint until shortly before his death.
