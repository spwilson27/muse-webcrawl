Convective available potential energy

In meteorology, convective available potential energy (commonly abbreviated as CAPE) is the amount of energy a parcel of air would have if lifted a certain distance vertically through the atmosphere. CAPE is effectively the positive buoyancy of an air parcel and is an indicator of atmospheric instability, which makes it valuable in predicting severe weather. It is a form of fluid instability found in thermally stratified atmospheres in which a colder fluid is on top of a warmer one.

Definition

CAPE exists within the conditionally unstable layer of the troposphere, the free convective layer (FCL), where an ascending air parcel is warmer than the ambient air. CAPE is measured in joules per kilogram (J/kg) and is computed by integrating the parcel's positive buoyancy from the level of free convection (LFC) to the equilibrium level (EL). Any given CAPE value is specific to the parcel chosen: surface-based CAPE uses a surface parcel, while most-unstable CAPE (MUCAPE) uses the most unstable parcel in the lower troposphere.

Interpretation

Values below 0 J/kg indicate stability; 0–1,000 J/kg represents marginal instability, 1,000–2,500 J/kg moderate instability, 2,500–4,000 J/kg strong instability, and values above 4,000 J/kg extreme instability. High CAPE favours strong updrafts and is associated with thunderstorms, heavy rain, hail and, in combination with wind shear, tornadoes. However, CAPE alone does not guarantee storms: convective inhibition (CIN) may prevent parcels from reaching the LFC at all.
