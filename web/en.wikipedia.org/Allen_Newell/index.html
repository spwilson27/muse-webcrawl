Allen Newell (March 19, 1927 – July 19, 1992) was an American researcher in computer science and cognitive psychology at the RAND Corporation and at Carnegie Mellon University's School of Computer Science, Tepper School of Business, and Department of Psychology. He contributed to the Information Processing Language (1956) and two of the earliest AI programs, the Logic Theorist (1956) and the General Problem Solver (1957), with Herbert A. Simon. He was awarded the ACM's A.M. Turing Award along with Herbert A. Simon in 1975, for their contributions to artificial intelligence and the psychology of human cognition.

Biography

Newell studied physics at Stanford University, receiving his bachelor's degree in 1949, and did graduate work in mathematics at Princeton University. From 1950 to 1961 he worked at the RAND Corporation in Santa Monica, where he collaborated with Herbert Simon and Cliff Shaw on some of the first computer programs that simulated human problem solving. He received his doctorate from Carnegie Institute of Technology in 1957, with Simon as his advisor, and joined the faculty, spending the rest of his career in Pittsburgh.

Contributions

With Simon and Shaw, Newell developed the Logic Theorist, which proved theorems in Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica, and the General Problem Solver, an attempt at a universal problem-solving architecture based on means–ends analysis. Their joint book Human Problem Solving (1972) was a landmark of cognitive psychology, based on verbal protocol analysis.

Newell's later work aimed at unified theories of cognition. He developed the Soar cognitive architecture with John Laird and Paul Rosenbloom, described in his book Unified Theories of Cognition (1990), and he articulated the "knowledge level" as a level of description of intelligent systems, above the symbol level. His presidential address to the American Association for Artificial Intelligence proposed Soar as a candidate for a unified theory of cognition.

Awards and legacy

Newell received the Turing Award (1975), the IJCAI Award for Research Excellence, and the U.S. National Medal of Science (1992), awarded posthumously. The Allen Newell Award of the Association for Computing Machinery, given for career contributions bridging computer science and other disciplines, is named in his honour. He died of cancer in Pittsburgh in 1992.
