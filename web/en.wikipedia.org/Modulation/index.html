Modulation


Analog types: AM, FM, PM
Digital types: ASK, FSK, PSK, QAM
Inverse process: Demodulation

In electronics and telecommunications, modulation is the process of varying one or more properties of a periodic waveform, called the carrier signal, with a separate signal that typically contains information to be transmitted. Modulation of a sine waveform transforms a baseband message signal into a passband signal suitable for transmission over a channel, for example by radio.

Analog modulation

In amplitude modulation (AM), the amplitude of the carrier is varied in proportion to the message. In frequency modulation (FM) and phase modulation (PM), the frequency or phase of the carrier is varied instead. FM offers better noise immunity than AM and is widely used for high-fidelity radio broadcasting, while AM remains in use for long-range broadcasting.

Digital modulation

Digital modulation schemes encode bits onto a carrier by switching between discrete states: amplitude-shift keying (ASK), frequency-shift keying (FSK), phase-shift keying (PSK), and quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM), which combines amplitude and phase variations. Modern standards such as Wi-Fi, LTE, and 5G use QAM together with orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM). A device that performs both modulation and demodulation is a modem.

Why modulate?

Modulation allows signals to be transmitted efficiently (antenna sizes are practical only at higher frequencies), lets many signals share one medium through frequency-division multiplexing, and can improve resistance to noise and interference.
