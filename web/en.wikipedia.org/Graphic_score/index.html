Graphic Score

A graphic score (also called graphic notation) is a representation of music through the use of visual symbols outside the realm of traditional music notation. Graphic scores emerged in the twentieth century as composers sought to loosen the constraints of the five-line stave, introduce indeterminacy, and invite performers to interpret images, shapes and texts as music.

History

Precursors include the eye music of the Renaissance and the elaborate calligraphic scores of the ars subtilior. The modern graphic score flourished from the 1950s, particularly in the United States and Europe. Morton Feldman's Projection series (1950–51) used grids of boxes indicating register and duration. Earle Brown's December 1952 — a set of floating horizontal and vertical lines — became an iconic example of the genre.

John Cage used transparencies and points in works such as Fontana Mix and his Aria, while Cornelius Cardew composed the monumental Treatise (1963–67), a 193-page score of abstract graphics without instructions. Other noted practitioners include Roman Haubenstock-Ramati, Anestis Logothetis, Bogusław Schaeffer and Sylvano Bussotti, whose scores are often visually striking artworks in their own right.

Interpretation

Graphic scores vary widely in how much freedom they grant. Some specify a clear mapping between graphic elements and musical parameters; others are open invitations to improvise. The approach challenges the traditional work-concept in music and has influenced experimental music, free improvisation, sound art and music education, where graphic scores are widely used to engage performers without notation-reading skills.
