Universalism

Universalism holds that certain truths, values, or salvific outcomes apply to all people.

Universalism is the philosophical and theological concept that certain ideas, principles, or truths have universal application or applicability to all people. The term is used in several distinct senses across religion, ethics, and political philosophy.

Religious universalism

In Christian theology, universalism — often called universal salvation or universal reconciliation — is the doctrine that all human beings will ultimately be saved and reconciled to God, rather than suffering eternal damnation. Early proponents are associated with figures such as Origen of Alexandria and Gregory of Nyssa, who taught forms of apocatastasis, the restoration of all things. The doctrine was influential in 18th- and 19th-century America, where the Universalist Church of America organized around it; in 1961 it merged with the American Unitarian Association to form the Unitarian Universalist Association.

Moral universalism

Moral universalism holds that a system of ethics applies to everyone regardless of culture, race, religion, or nationality. It is often contrasted with moral relativism. Universalist moral frameworks underlie modern human rights law, including the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948).

Other uses

In political theory, universalism describes policies designed for the whole population rather than targeted at particular groups — for example, universal health care or a universal basic income. In linguistics and philosophy, universalism can refer to properties shared by all languages or all minds.
