Greek theatre of Syracuse

The cavea of the Greek theatre at Syracuse, Sicily.

The Greek theatre of Syracuse is an ancient theatre in the archaeological park of Neapolis at Syracuse (Siracusa), Sicily, Italy. First built in the 5th century BCE and substantially rebuilt in the 3rd century BCE under Hieron II, it is among the largest theatres of the ancient Greek world, with a cavea about 138 metres in diameter carved directly into the rock of the Temenites hill.

History

A theatre existed at Syracuse by the early 5th century BCE, when the playwright Aeschylus staged productions there and the comic dramatist Epicharmus was active at the court of Hieron I. The surviving structure dates mainly to the Hellenistic rebuilding of about 238–215 BCE, when the seating was cut into the hillside in nine wedges divided by staircases, with a broad walkway (diazoma) and inscriptions naming members of the royal family of Hieron II. Under Roman rule the theatre was adapted for Roman spectacles, and in the imperial period the orchestra was modified, possibly for aquatic displays. In the 16th century stone was quarried from the site and watermills were built in the cavea, fed by the ancient aqueduct.

Modern use

Since 1914 the theatre has hosted an annual festival of classical Greek drama organised by the Istituto Nazionale del Dramma Antico (INDA), presenting tragedies and comedies of Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, and Aristophanes before audiences of several thousand. The theatre forms part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site "Syracuse and the Rocky Necropolis of Pantalica", inscribed in 2005.
