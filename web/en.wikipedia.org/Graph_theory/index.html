Graph theory

In mathematics, graph theory is the study of graphs — mathematical structures used to model pairwise relations between objects. A graph is made up of vertices (also called nodes or points) connected by edges (also called links or lines). Graphs may be undirected, where edges link two vertices symmetrically, or directed, where edges have a direction from one vertex to another. Graph theory is a major area of discrete mathematics and computer science, with applications from network analysis and logistics to chemistry, linguistics, and sociology.

History

The subject begins with Leonhard Euler's 1736 solution to the Seven Bridges of Königsberg problem, often regarded as the first theorem of graph theory and an early seed of topology. The nineteenth century added the study of trees (Cayley), the four colour problem (posed 1852, proved by computer in 1976 as the four colour theorem), and Hamiltonian cycles. The first textbook, by Dénes Kőnig, appeared in 1936.

Basic ideas

A path is a sequence of edges joining distinct vertices; a cycle returns to its start. A graph is connected if every pair of vertices is joined by a path. A tree is a connected graph without cycles. Important problems include finding shortest paths (Dijkstra's algorithm), spanning trees, network flows, matchings, and proper vertex colourings — assigning colours so adjacent vertices differ, the minimum number needed being the graph's chromatic number.

Applications

Graphs model road and communication networks, the web's link structure (exploited by Google's PageRank), molecular structures in chemistry, social networks, scheduling and dependency problems, and routes in operations research problems such as the travelling salesman problem.
