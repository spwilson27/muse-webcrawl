Pathogenicity island

A pathogenicity island (PAI) is a distinct genetic element found in the genomes of many pathogenic bacteria. PAIs are typically large regions of DNA, often tens of kilobases long, that carry genes encoding virulence factors, including toxins, adhesins, secretion systems, and iron-uptake systems.

PAIs differ in G+C content from the rest of the chromosome, suggesting they were acquired by horizontal gene transfer, often through conjugation, transduction, or transformation. They are frequently inserted adjacent to tRNA genes and flanked by direct repeats, and they may contain mobility genes such as integrases that allow excision.

Characteristics

  • Carry one or more virulence genes
  • Present in pathogenic strains but absent or rare in closely related non-pathogenic strains
  • Distinctive base composition compared with the core genome
  • Association with tRNA loci and flanking direct repeat sequences
  • Contain genes for mobility, such as integrases and transposases
  • Range in size from about 10 to over 200 kilobases

Examples

Well-studied examples include the Salmonella pathogenicity islands SPI-1 and SPI-2, which encode type III secretion systems, and the islands of uropathogenic Escherichia coli, which carry genes for P fimbriae and hemolysin. The concept remains central to understanding how bacterial pathogens evolve from commensal ancestors.
