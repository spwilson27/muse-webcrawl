Socket wrench

A socket wrench is a type of wrench (spanner) that turns fasteners — typically nuts and bolts — using interchangeable sockets that fit over the fastener head. The most common form is the ratchet: a handle with a reversible ratcheting mechanism that lets the user tighten or loosen a fastener with repeated back-and-forth strokes without lifting the socket off the nut, greatly speeding work in confined spaces.

Construction

Sockets attach to the handle by a square drive stud retained by a spring-loaded ball detent (and sometimes a quick-release button). Standard drive sizes are 1/4 inch, 3/8 inch, 1/2 inch, 3/4 inch, and 1 inch, matched to the torque required and socket size. Sockets themselves come in metric and SAE (imperial) sizes, in 6-point and 12-point broaching (6-point grips flats more securely; 12-point allows finer positioning), in standard and deep lengths, and specialized variants such as spark plug sockets, universal (flex) sockets, impact sockets of tougher steel for use with impact wrenches, and pass-through designs. Accessories include extension bars, universal joints, adapters, torque wrenches using the same drive system, speed handles, and breaker bars.

History

Interchangeable-socket tools appeared in the nineteenth century; the modern quick-release ratchet is associated with American inventor J.J. Richardson's 1863 patent for a removable socket wrench, and firms such as Snap-on (founded 1920) popularized socket sets in automotive repair. The ratchet-and-socket system became the standard fastening tool in automotive, aviation, and industrial maintenance.

Use

The user selects a socket matching the fastener, seats it fully, and swings the ratchet handle; a selector lever reverses direction. For final tightening to specification, a torque wrench or power impact tool is used. Care is taken to keep the socket square on the fastener to avoid rounding its corners.
