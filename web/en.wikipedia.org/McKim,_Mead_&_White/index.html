McKim, Mead & White

McKim, Mead & White was an American architectural firm based in New York City. The firm came to define architectural practice, urbanism, and the standards of the American Renaissance neoclassical and Beaux-Arts styles. It was founded in 1877 by Charles Follen McKim (1847–1909), William Rutherford Mead (1846–1928), and Stanford White (1853–1906).

History and works

The firm's early work included shingle-style houses in Newport and Long Island. It rose to national prominence with the Boston Public Library (1887–1895), a landmark of Renaissance Revival design. Major commissions followed: the original Madison Square Garden (1890), Columbia University's Morningside Heights campus, the University Club of New York, the Pierpont Morgan Library, New York's original Pennsylvania Station (1910; demolished 1963), and restorations and additions to the White House under Theodore Roosevelt.

Partners and legacy

Stanford White, the firm's most flamboyant designer, was murdered in 1906 by Harry Kendall Thaw on the roof of Madison Square Garden, in one of the era's most sensational trials. After the deaths of White and McKim, the firm continued under Mead and later partners into the mid-20th century, shaping campus, civic, and club architecture across the United States. The demolition of Penn Station in 1963 galvanised the historic preservation movement in New York.
