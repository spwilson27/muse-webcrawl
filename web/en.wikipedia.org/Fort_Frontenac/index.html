Fort Frontenac

Fort Frontenac was a French trading post and military fort built in 1673 at the mouth of the Cataraqui River where Lake Ontario flows into the St. Lawrence River, at the site of present-day Kingston, Ontario, Canada. It was named for Louis de Buade de Frontenac, governor of New France, who directed its construction to control the fur trade and defend against the Iroquois Confederacy.

History

Frontenac travelled to the site in July 1673 and met with Iroquois representatives before ordering the construction of a wooden palisaded fort, initially called Fort Cataraqui. The explorer René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle, was granted the seigneury and rebuilt the fort in stone in the 1680s. The fort changed hands and was besieged several times during the Beaver Wars: the French garrison destroyed it in 1689 under Iroquois pressure, and it was rebuilt in 1695 under Frontenac's renewed direction.

French and Indian War

During the Seven Years' War, Fort Frontenac was a key French supply depot for the Ohio Country and the western posts. In August 1758 a British force under Lieutenant-Colonel John Bradstreet, moving by boat from Oswego, surprised and captured the fort, seizing large stores of supplies and French shipping on Lake Ontario. The loss crippled French logistics in the interior; the French never reoccupied the site, and the ruins passed to Britain under the Treaty of Paris in 1763.

Later use

In 1783 the site became part of the settlement of Cataraqui, soon renamed Kingston, where Loyalists established a town. During the War of 1812 the British built barracks on and around the old fort site. Today the location is occupied by Canadian Forces Base Kingston, and the modern Fort Frontenac, built in 1939–40 within the base, houses the Canadian Army Command and Staff College. Archaeological excavations have uncovered remains of the original French fortifications beneath the present parade square.
