Anti-predator adaptation

Anti-predator adaptations are mechanisms evolved by organisms to help them avoid being detected, selected, subdued or consumed by predators. They form one side of the evolutionary arms race between predators and prey, and are found in virtually every animal group, as well as in some plants.

Avoiding detection

Many prey species avoid being noticed at all through camouflage, nocturnality, living underground or in inaccessible places, and countershading. Others are detected but advertise unprofitability: bright warning coloration (aposematism) signals toxins or stings, and harmless species exploit this through Batesian mimicry.

Deterring and escaping attack

Defences against attack include armour, spines and shells; chemical weapons such as the bombardier beetle's spray or the skunk's musk; startling displays and deimatic behaviour; playing dead (thanatosis); and active escape by flight, speed or shedding a body part (autotomy). Group living brings further benefits: dilution of risk, shared vigilance, and confusion effects in swirling schools of fish or flocks of birds. Some prey even fight back or mob predators cooperatively.

Evolution

Because predation is a strong selective pressure, anti-predator traits evolve rapidly and in turn drive counter-adaptations in predators — faster pursuit, better senses, venom resistance — in an ongoing coevolutionary process.
