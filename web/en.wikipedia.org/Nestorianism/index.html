Nestorianism

Nestorianism is a Christological doctrine associated with Nestorius (c. 386–450), Patriarch of Constantinople from 428 to 431. It is generally characterised as emphasising the distinction between the human and divine natures of Jesus Christ, and it became infamous through Nestorius's objection to the title Theotokos ("God-bearer") for the Virgin Mary, preferring Christotokos ("Christ-bearer"). The doctrine was condemned as heresy at the Council of Ephesus in 431, and Nestorius was deposed and exiled.

Controversy and schism

Nestorius's preaching provoked fierce opposition from Cyril of Alexandria, whose party prevailed at Ephesus. Many modern scholars argue that Nestorius's own teaching — known in full only after the rediscovery of his Book of Heraclides in the late 19th century — was closer to orthodoxy than his condemnation implied, and that the dispute was as much about ecclesiastical politics and language as about substance.

Christians in the Persian Empire, organised as the Church of the East, upheld the Antiochene theology associated with Nestorius, and the label "Nestorian" was thereafter applied to that church — a label it has historically rejected as inaccurate and pejorative. From its base in Mesopotamia the Church of the East mounted remarkable missionary expansion along the Silk Road, establishing communities in Arabia, India (the Saint Thomas Christians), Central Asia, and Tang-dynasty China, where the arrival of the missionary Alopen in 635 is commemorated on the Nestorian Stele erected in Xi'an in 781.

Modern developments

Persecution under Timur in the 14th century devastated the church in Asia; its remnant communities survived mainly in Mesopotamia, southeastern Turkey, and India. In 1994 the Assyrian Church of the East and the Roman Catholic Church signed a Common Christological Declaration acknowledging that the ancient divisions rested largely on misunderstandings and different theological vocabularies.
