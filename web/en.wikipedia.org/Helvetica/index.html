Helvetica, also known by its original name Neue Haas Grotesk, is a widely used sans-serif typeface developed in 1957 by Swiss typeface designers Max Miedinger and Eduard Hoffmann. It is a neo-grotesque design, influenced by the famous 19th-century typeface Akzidenz-Grotesk, and became one of the most popular typefaces of the 20th century.

Originally designed for the Haas Type Foundry in Münchenstein, Switzerland, Helvetica aimed to create a neutral typeface with great clarity, no intrinsic meaning in its form, and usability across a wide variety of signage and text. Its clean lines and tight spacing made it a hallmark of the International Typographic Style, also known as Swiss Style.

History

The first version of the typeface was created in 1957 by Miedinger, a freelance designer who had earlier worked as a typesetter, together with Hoffmann, the director of the Haas foundry. They set out to design a new sans-serif that could compete with the success of Akzidenz-Grotesk in the Swiss market. The original name, Neue Haas Grotesk, was changed in 1960 to Helvetica, derived from Confoederatio Helvetica, the Latin name for Switzerland, in order to make the face more marketable internationally.

Released through the German Stempel foundry and then Linotype, Helvetica spread rapidly during the 1960s and 1970s. It was adopted for corporate identities, transport systems, and government signage around the world. In 1983 D. Stempel AG released Neue Helvetica (Helvetica Neue), a reworking with more unified weights and heights. In 2019 Monotype released Helvetica Now, a comprehensive redesign for digital use.

Characteristics

Helvetica's distinctive features include tall x-height, horizontal and vertical stroke endings, tight letter spacing, and a largely uniform stroke width. Uppercase letters are wide and drawn with tight apertures. The design has two-storey lowercase a and a distinctive tail on the Q. Its numerals are tabular by default in many cuts, supporting its use in tables and timetables.

Usage

Helvetica has been used in the logos and identities of numerous companies, including Lufthansa, American Airlines (until 2013), BMW, Jeep, and Microsoft (in early branding). It is the typeface of the New York City Subway signage system under the MTA standards developed by Massimo Vignelli and Bob Noorda. From 2007 to 2015 it was the default system font on Apple's iPhone and OS X, before being replaced by San Francisco.

Influence and criticism

Helvetica is the subject of the 2007 documentary film Helvetica by Gary Hustwit. Some designers, notably Erik Spiekermann, have criticised its ubiquity and spacing, while others praise its neutrality. Because of its dominance, Helvetica is often recommended against as a default, but it remains one of the most recognisable typefaces ever produced.
