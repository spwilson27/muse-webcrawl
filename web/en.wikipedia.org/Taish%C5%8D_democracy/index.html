Taishō democracy

Taishō democracy (Japanese: 大正デモクラシー) was a liberal and democratic movement in the political history of the Empire of Japan, prominent during the Taishō period (1912–1926) and extending into the early Shōwa era. The term describes the flowering of party politics, universal manhood suffrage activism, and liberal thought associated with figures such as the political scientist Yoshino Sakuzō, who advocated minponshugi — government in accordance with the will of the people.

Background

After the Meiji Restoration, political power rested with an oligarchy of elder statesmen (genrō). Urbanisation, mass education, and a growing press created new constituencies demanding representation. The Hibiya riots of 1905 and the Rice Riots of 1918 revealed broad popular mobilisation.

Development

The Taishō Political Crisis of 1912–13 toppled the Katsura cabinet in defence of constitutional government. In 1918 Hara Takashi became the first commoner and party politician to serve as prime minister, inaugurating an era of party cabinets that lasted, with interruptions, until 1932. After a long campaign, the General Election Law of 1925 granted suffrage to virtually all adult men, quadrupling the electorate.

Decline

The same year, 1925, saw the Peace Preservation Law, used to suppress dissent. Economic crisis, the London Naval Treaty controversy, and assassinations — culminating in the killing of Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi in the May 15 Incident of 1932 — ended party cabinets and opened the way to growing military influence in the 1930s.
