Norwegian (norsk) is a North Germanic language spoken mainly in Norway, where it is the official language, by around 5.3 million people. Together with Danish and Swedish it forms a dialect continuum of mutually intelligible Scandinavian languages, descended from Old Norse.

Two written standards

Uniquely, Norwegian has two official written forms. Bokmål ("book tongue"), used by roughly 85–90 percent of the population, developed from the Danish-influenced written language of the centuries of Danish rule. Nynorsk ("new Norwegian") was constructed in the 19th century by linguist Ivar Aasen from rural Norwegian dialects as a distinctly Norwegian standard. Norway spent much of the 20th century attempting, and eventually abandoning, a merger of the two (Samnorsk). School pupils learn both forms, and every municipality chooses one as its official standard.

Dialects and pronunciation

Spoken Norwegian is remarkably diverse: there is no standard spoken dialect, and local dialects are used even in national broadcasting and parliament. Most dialects have two tones (pitch accents) that distinguish otherwise identical words. The spoken language of the Oslo region, however, functions as a de facto standard.

Grammar and history

Norwegian grammar is relatively analytic, with two genders in Bokmål (three optionally preserved), definite articles suffixed to nouns, and verb conjugation simplified compared with Old Norse. Middle Norwegian was heavily influenced by Middle Low German through Hanseatic trade. Attempts to "Norwegianise" the written language followed independence from Denmark in 1814, producing the language controversies (språkstriden) that continue in milder form today.
