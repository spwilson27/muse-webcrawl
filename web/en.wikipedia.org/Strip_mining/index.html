Strip mining

Surface mining, including strip mining, is a broad category of mining in which soil and rock overlying the mineral deposit (the overburden) are removed, in contrast to underground mining, in which the overlying rock is left in place and the mineral is removed through shafts or tunnels. North American surface coal mining developed from the late 16th century onward, and the large-scale strip mining of coal by steam shovels and draglines expanded rapidly in the 20th century.

Types

Strip mining itself has several forms. In area mining, large rectangular pits are excavated over flat terrain. In contour mining, miners follow the outcrop of a seam around a hillside, historically leaving a highwall; a related method, mountaintop removal mining, removes the entire top of a mountain to reach coal seams. Open-pit mining extracts ore in successive benches, and dredging and placer mining recover minerals from water bodies and alluvial deposits.

Environmental effects

Surface mining profoundly disturbs land, destroying ecosystems and topsoil, generating dust and noise, and producing spoil heaps that can leach acids (acid mine drainage) into waterways. In the United States, the Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977 requires operators to reclaim mined land, restore approximate original contour, and post bonds; other countries impose similar reclamation duties, though enforcement and long-term damage remain contested. Surface mining is nevertheless generally safer for miners than underground mining and is more economical for shallow deposits.
