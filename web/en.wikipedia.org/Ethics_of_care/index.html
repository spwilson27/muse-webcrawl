Ethics of care

The ethics of care (alternatively care ethics or EoC) is a normative ethical theory that holds that moral action centres on interpersonal relationships and care, or benevolence, as a virtue. It arose in feminist philosophy in the early 1980s, in contrast to rule-based moral theories such as Kantian deontology and utilitarianism, which emphasise universal standards and impartiality.

Origins

The psychologist Carol Gilligan's In a Different Voice (1982) criticised Lawrence Kohlberg's stages of moral development for reflecting a male bias toward abstract justice reasoning, and described an alternative "care voice" oriented to relationships and responsiveness to need. The philosopher Nel Noddings developed the theory systematically in Caring: A Feminine Approach to Ethics and Moral Education (1984).

Key ideas

Care ethicists emphasise the moral salience of relationships, dependency, and context; the partiality owed to those close to us; and attentiveness, responsibility, competence, and responsiveness (Joan Tronto's phases of care). The theory has been applied in nursing and medical ethics, education, social policy, and international relations.

Criticism

Critics worry that care ethics can entrench traditional gender roles, justify parochialism, and offer little guidance for conflicts between those to whom one owes care; defenders often present it as complementary to, rather than wholly replacing, justice-based approaches.
