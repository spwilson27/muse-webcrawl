Salsa

Salsa (Spanish for "sauce") generally refers to a sauce in Mexican cuisine, as well as Tex-Mex and Southwestern American cooking, usually used as a condiment and dip, especially for tortilla chips. Most salsas are based on tomatoes or tomatillos combined with chili peppers, onion, and herbs, and range from mild to extremely hot.

Types

Common varieties include salsa roja (red sauce), made with cooked tomatoes, chili peppers, onion, garlic, and fresh coriander; pico de gallo, a fresh, uncooked mixture of chopped tomato, onion, and peppers (also called salsa fresca or salsa bandera for its red, white, and green colours of the Mexican flag); salsa verde, made green from tomatillos; salsa negra, darkened with dried chillies; and fruit-based salsa de mango. Related sauces include mole, guacamole, and chimichurri, which occupy similar roles in other cuisines.

History and popularity

Sauces of tomatoes, chillies, and ground squash seeds were known to the Aztecs, who sold them in markets described by Spanish chroniclers. The word salsa entered English usage in the mid-twentieth century as Mexican food spread in the United States. In 1991, sales of salsa surpassed those of ketchup in the US, a frequently cited marker of changing American tastes. Commercial salsas, typically cooked and jarred, became a supermarket staple, while fresh refrigerated salsas also grew in popularity. Heat levels are commonly described by the Scoville scale of the chillies used. See also Mexican cuisine and Pico de gallo.
