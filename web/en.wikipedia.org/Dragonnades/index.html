Dragonnades

The Dragonnades were a policy of persecution instituted by King Louis XIV of France in 1681 to force French Protestants (Huguenots) to convert to Catholicism. Under the policy, dragoons — mounted infantry — were billeted in Protestant households, where they were permitted to abuse the inhabitants, destroy property, and steal belongings until the household converted.

Background

The Edict of Nantes (1598) had granted Huguenots limited religious toleration. Louis XIV, seeking religious unity under the principle "one king, one law, one faith," progressively restricted Protestant rights. The intendant René de Marillac first employed troop billeting in Poitou in 1681; the tactic produced mass nominal conversions and was extended across the kingdom.

Course and consequences

Dragoons were quartered disproportionately on Protestant families; soldiers committed violence and humiliations, and many Huguenots converted under duress while others fled. The policy culminated in the Edict of Fontainebleau (1685), which revoked the Edict of Nantes entirely. An estimated 200,000 Huguenots emigrated to England, the Dutch Republic, Brandenburg-Prussia, Switzerland, and overseas colonies, a loss of skilled subjects that damaged the French economy. Persecution continued in the "Church of the Desert," and resistance erupted in the Camisard revolt in the Cévennes (1702–1704).
