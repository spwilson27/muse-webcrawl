Antifreeze

An antifreeze is an additive which lowers the freezing point of a water-based liquid. An antifreeze mixture is used in internal combustion engines and other heat transfer applications, such as HVAC chillers and solar water heaters. The purpose of antifreeze is to prevent a rigid enclosure from bursting due to expansion when water freezes.

Common types

The most common antifreeze base chemicals are ethylene glycol and propylene glycol, mixed with water and corrosion inhibitors. Ethylene glycol solutions first became available in 1926 and were marketed as "permanent antifreeze" since their high boiling points provided advantages for both summer and winter use. Propylene glycol is less toxic and is used where ethylene glycol would be inappropriate, such as in food-processing systems.

Toxicity

Ethylene glycol is poisonous to humans and animals; its sweet taste can make it attractive to children and pets. Poisoning is treated with fomepizole or ethanol, which compete for the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase. Many manufacturers add a bittering agent, denatonium benzoate, to discourage ingestion.

Biology

Some organisms produce antifreeze proteins that bind to ice crystals and inhibit their growth, allowing fish and insects to survive in sub-zero environments.
