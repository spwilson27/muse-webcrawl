Nymph

A nymph (Greek: νύμφη, nýmphē, "bride") in Greek mythology is a minor female nature deity, usually depicted as a beautiful maiden associated with a particular place or natural feature. Unlike goddesses, nymphs are generally not immortal, but they are extremely long-lived and closely tied to their locality — when their tree or spring perished, so might they.

Types

Nymphs were classified by habitat. Naiads presided over fresh water; Nereids and Oceanids over the sea and ocean; Dryads and Hamadryads over trees and forests; Oreads over mountains; and the Meliae over ash trees. Groups such as the Pleiades and Hyades were nymphs of the stars. They accompanied greater gods — Artemis was attended by a train of nymphs, and Dionysus by the maenads and the nymphs who raised him.

Myths

Nymphs figure in countless myths, often as objects of desire pursued by gods and mortals: Daphne was transformed into a laurel to escape Apollo; Echo pined for Narcissus; the river nymph Arethusa became a fountain fleeing Alpheus. Others, like Calypso and Circe (sometimes classed as goddesses), detained heroes. Nymphs could also be dangerous, dragging favourites such as Hylas beneath the water.

Legacy

The word passed into later folklore and literature for nature spirits generally, and gave English terms such as "nymphaeum" (a shrine or grotto of nymphs) and, in biology, the immature stage of insects with incomplete metamorphosis.
