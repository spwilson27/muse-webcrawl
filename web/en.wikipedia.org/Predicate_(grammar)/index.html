Predicate (grammar)

In traditional grammar a sentence divides into a subject and a predicate.

The term predicate is used in two distinct but related ways in grammar and linguistics. In the tradition derived from Aristotle, a sentence consists of a subject — what the sentence is about — and a predicate, which is everything in the sentence except the subject, centred on the main verb and its complements and adjuncts. For example, in "The cat sleeps on the mat", "The cat" is the subject and "sleeps on the mat" is the predicate.

Modern senses

In modern syntactic theory, "predicate" often denotes the verbal constituent more narrowly: the verb phrase, or the head verb together with the arguments it selects. In semantic and logical usage influenced by predicate calculus, a predicate is an expression that denotes a property or relation and takes arguments to form a proposition — so that in "Socrates is mortal", is mortal is a one-place predicate applied to Socrates. The related notion of predication covers the act of ascribing a property to a subject, and languages differ in whether predicates must contain a verb at all: many languages allow nominal or adjectival predicates, sometimes with a zero copula in the present tense.
