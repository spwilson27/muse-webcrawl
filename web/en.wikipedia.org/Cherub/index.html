Cherub

A cherub (plural cherubim; Hebrew: כְּרוּב, kərūḇ) is a type of supernatural being attested in the religions of the ancient Near East and prominent in the Hebrew Bible, Judaism and Christianity. Cherubim are depicted guarding sacred spaces and bearing the throne of God.

In the Hebrew Bible

In Genesis, cherubim with a flaming sword guard the way to the Tree of Life after the expulsion from Eden. Two golden cherubim with outstretched wings overshadow the mercy seat of the Ark of the Covenant. In the prophet Ezekiel's visions, cherubim are four-faced, four-winged creatures — with the faces of a human, a lion, an ox and an eagle — accompanying the divine chariot (merkabah); in Ezekiel 28 the cherub is associated with the king of Tyre in Eden. Psalm 18 describes God riding upon a cherub.

Angelology and later tradition

In the hierarchies of angels systematised by Pseudo-Dionysius and accepted in medieval Christian theology, cherubim form the second-highest choir of angels, below the seraphim, and are associated with knowledge and contemplation. Islamic tradition has comparable throne-bearing angels (the karūbiyyūn). The winged sphinxes and lamassu of Assyrian art are often compared with the biblical cherubim.

In art

Western art from the Renaissance onward often conflated cherubim with putti — chubby infant figures, also called "cherubs" in common usage — a depiction quite unlike the formidable biblical beings.
