Second Triumvirate

The Second Triumvirate was the political alliance formed in 43 BC between Octavian (the future emperor Augustus), Mark Antony, and Marcus Aemilius Lepidus. Unlike the informal First Triumvirate, it was legally constituted by the lex Titia as a commission "for restoring the constitution of the republic" (tresviri rei publicae constituendae) with sweeping powers for five years, later renewed.

The three triumvirs: Octavian, Mark Antony, and Lepidus.

Proscriptions and Philippi

The triumvirs inaugurated their rule with proscriptions—published lists of citizens declared outlaws who could be killed for reward and whose property was confiscated—in which some 300 senators and 2,000 equites were listed, among them Cicero. In 42 BC Antony and Octavian defeated Caesar's assassins Brutus and Cassius at the Battle of Philippi in Macedonia, after which the triumvirs divided the Roman world: Antony took the East, Octavian the West, and Lepidus Africa.

Breakdown

Relations deteriorated through wars with Sextus Pompey in Sicily and Antony's campaigns and liaison with Cleopatra in Egypt. Lepidus was stripped of power in 36 BC after a failed bid for Sicily. Open war between Octavian and Antony followed; Octavian's victory at the Battle of Actium in 31 BC and the conquest of Egypt in 30 BC left him sole ruler, and in 27 BC he took the name Augustus, conventionally marking the start of the Roman Empire.
