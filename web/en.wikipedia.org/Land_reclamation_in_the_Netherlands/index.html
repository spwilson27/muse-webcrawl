Land reclamation in the Netherlands

Land reclamation in the Netherlands has a long history. As of 2017, roughly 17% of the country's total land area had been reclaimed from sea, lakes, marshes and swamps; about a quarter of the Netherlands lies below sea level, and some 3,500 polders are drained and defended by dikes.

The Dutch saying "God made the world, but the Dutch made Holland" reflects a millennium of hydraulic engineering, from medieval monastic dike-building to the great state projects of the twentieth century.

Early polders

From about the year 1000, inhabitants of the low coastal regions built dikes and drained marshland. Windmills, developed into the iconic polder mills of places like Kinderdijk, pumped water from drainage canals into rivers and the sea. The draining of lakes (droogmakerijen) with windmill power in the seventeenth century, led by engineers such as Jan Adriaanszoon Leeghwater, created the Beemster and Schermer polders.

Zuiderzee Works

The Zuiderzee Works, authorised in 1918 under Cornelis Lely's plan, closed off the Zuiderzee with the Afsluitdijk (1932), converting it into the freshwater IJsselmeer. Over the following decades the Noordoostpolder, Eastern and Southern Flevoland were enclosed and pumped dry, creating the province of Flevoland; together the works added about 1,650 km² of land.

Delta Works

After the catastrophic North Sea flood of 1953, which killed 1,836 people in the Netherlands, the Delta Works built a system of dams, sluices and storm-surge barriers — including the Oosterscheldekering — to protect the southwestern delta.

Modern approaches

Recent policy has shifted from fighting water to "room for the river": depoldering selected areas, lowering floodplains and creating controlled storage to accommodate climate change and sea-level rise.
