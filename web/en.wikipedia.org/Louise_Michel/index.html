Louise Michel (29 May 1830 – 9 January 1905) was a French teacher and revolutionary figure during the Paris Commune of 1871. Often called the Vierge Rouge ("Red Virgin") or simply la grande citoyenne, she became one of the most prominent women of the French revolutionary left, and later a leading anarchist speaker and writer.

Early life

Michel was born at the château of Vroncourt, the daughter of a serving woman and, reputedly, the son of the château's owners, by whom she was raised and educated. She trained as a teacher but refused to swear the oath of loyalty to Napoleon III required of state teachers, instead teaching in private schools in Paris, where she became involved in radical politics and women's rights circles.

The Paris Commune

During the Siege of Paris (1870–71) Michel organised ambulance services and soup kitchens and joined the National Guard. When the Paris Commune was proclaimed in March 1871 she fought in its defence — at Issy, Neuilly and on the barricades of Montmartre — dressed, at times, in a National Guard uniform, and worked as a nurse. After the Commune's bloody suppression in May, she went into hiding but surrendered to secure her mother's release.

Trial, deportation and return

At her trial in December 1871 she defiantly demanded to be shot, declaring that since every beating heart for liberty had only a little lead in store, she claimed her share. Instead she was deported to New Caledonia, where she spent seven years; there she taught, studied Kanak culture and sided with the Kanak revolt of 1878. Amnestied in 1880, she returned to a heroine's welcome in Paris and resumed anarchist agitation, editing journals, lecturing across Europe and being imprisoned again several times, including after a bread riot demonstration in 1883.

Later years and legacy

Michel spent periods of exile in London, continued to write novels, plays, poetry and memoirs (including La Commune, 1898), and remained an indefatigable speaker until her death at Marseille in 1905. Tens of thousands attended her funeral in Paris. She remains an emblem of revolutionary commitment and women's emancipation in France; a Paris Métro station (Louise Michel, on Line 3bis) bears her name.
