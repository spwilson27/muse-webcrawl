Battle of Wakefield

Date30 December 1460
LocationWakefield, West Yorkshire, England
ResultLancastrian victory
BelligerentsHouse of Lancaster vs. House of York
CommandersDuke of Somerset, Earl of Northumberland, Lord Clifford vs. Richard, Duke of York †, Earl of Salisbury (executed), Edmund, Earl of Rutland †

The Battle of Wakefield took place at Wakefield, West Yorkshire, on 30 December 1460 during the Wars of the Roses. It was a decisive Lancastrian victory in which Richard, Duke of York — who had weeks earlier been recognized by the Act of Accord as heir to King Henry VI — was killed, together with his second son Edmund, Earl of Rutland, and the Earl of Salisbury.

Background

In October 1460 the Act of Accord settled the succession of the English crown on York and his heirs, disinheriting Henry VI's son Edward of Westminster. Queen Margaret of Anjou refused to accept the settlement and gathered a large army in the north, plundering Yorkist estates. In December, York marched from London with perhaps 5,000–6,000 men to his castle at Sandal, near Wakefield, intending to confront the Lancastrians, whose army — possibly twice his strength — was based at Pontefract.

Battle

Why York left the strong position of Sandal Castle remains debated: he may have sallied to protect foragers, fallen for a feigned retreat, or been drawn out by Lancastrian troops concealed in woods while others displayed themselves openly on Wakefield Green. Once on open ground, York's force was attacked from several sides and overwhelmed. York himself was killed in the fighting. His seventeen-year-old son Rutland was killed during the rout — legend holds that Lord Clifford slew him on Wakefield Bridge, in revenge for his own father's death at St Albans. Salisbury was captured and beheaded at Pontefract the next day.

Aftermath

The heads of York, Salisbury and Rutland were displayed on spikes on Micklegate Bar in York, the Duke's mockingly crowned with a paper crown. The Lancastrian triumph was short-lived: York's eldest son Edward, Earl of March, won the Battle of Mortimer's Cross in February 1461, joined Warwick's forces after the Second Battle of St Albans, and was proclaimed King Edward IV in March, sealing his claim with the great victory at Towton. The battle is traditionally linked to the mnemonic for the colours of the rainbow, "Richard Of York Gave Battle In Vain", though the rhyme's origin is uncertain.
