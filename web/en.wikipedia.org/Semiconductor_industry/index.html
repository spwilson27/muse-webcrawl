Semiconductor industry

The semiconductor industry is the aggregate of companies engaged in the design and fabrication of semiconductors and semiconductor devices, such as integrated circuits and transistors. It formed around 1960, once the fabrication of semiconductor devices became a viable business, and has since grown into a global industry with annual revenues exceeding US$500 billion in the early 2020s, driven further by demand for artificial intelligence accelerators.

Industry structure

The industry is organised into several business models. Integrated device manufacturers (IDMs) such as Intel and Samsung design and manufacture their own chips. Foundries (pure-play contract manufacturers) such as TSMC fabricate chips designed by others. Fabless companies such as NVIDIA, AMD, and Qualcomm design chips but outsource manufacturing. Around them sit suppliers of equipment (ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research), materials, electronic design automation (EDA) software (Synopsys, Cadence), and outsourced assembly and test (OSAT) firms.

History

The industry began with the transistor (Bell Labs, 1947) and the first integrated circuits (Kilby and Noyce, 1958–59). Silicon Valley firms such as Fairchild Semiconductor and Intel commercialised the technology in the 1960s–70s. Japan dominated memory chips in the 1980s; South Korea (Samsung, SK hynix) later took the lead in memory, while Taiwan's TSMC, founded in 1987 by Morris Chang, pioneered the pure-play foundry model that enabled the fabless revolution.

Markets and geopolitics

Logic chips (processors, GPUs, AI accelerators) and memory (DRAM and NAND flash) are the largest segments, alongside analog, discrete, and optoelectronic devices. Because advanced fabrication is concentrated in a few places — Taiwan produces the large majority of leading-edge logic — semiconductors have become a focus of industrial policy, including the US CHIPS and Science Act of 2022 and export controls on advanced chips and lithography equipment.
