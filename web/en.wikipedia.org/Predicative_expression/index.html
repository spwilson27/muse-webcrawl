Predicative expression

A predicative expression (or just predicative) is part of a clause predicate, and is an expression that typically follows a copula (or linking verb), e.g. be, seem, appear, or that appears as a second complement of a certain type of verb, e.g. call, make, name, etc. The most frequently acknowledged types of predicative expressions are predicative adjectives (also predicate adjectives) and predicative nominals (also predicate nominals).

Examples

The following sentences contain predicative expressions. The predicative expression is in bold:

  • The idea was ridiculous. — predicative adjective
  • Bob is a clown. — predicative nominal
  • She seems eager to please. — predicative adjective phrase
  • They painted the house red. — object-oriented predicative

Subject and object predicatives

A predicative expression that describes the subject of the clause is a subject predicative (or subject complement), as in "Bob is a clown". A predicative expression that describes the object is an object predicative (or object complement), as in "They made him their leader" or "She painted it green".

Predicative vs. attributive

Predicative expressions are distinguished from attributive expressions, which modify a noun directly inside a noun phrase, as ridiculous does in "the ridiculous idea". Some adjectives can occur only predicatively (e.g. afraid, asleep), while others normally occur only attributively (e.g. mere, utter).

In linguistics

The distinction between predicative and attributive uses is a standard topic in English grammar and in syntax more generally. Predicative expressions are often analysed as complements of the verb rather than as modifiers, and they agree with or are controlled by the subject or object they describe.
