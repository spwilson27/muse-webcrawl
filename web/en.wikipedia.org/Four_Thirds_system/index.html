Four Thirds system

A Four Thirds system camera. The standard was introduced by Olympus and Kodak in 2002.

The Four Thirds system is a standard for digital single-lens reflex (DSLR) cameras and lenses, created by Olympus and Kodak and announced in 2002. It takes its name from the size classification of its image sensor — a "4/3-inch type" sensor measuring about 18 × 13.5 mm — and from the 4:3 aspect ratio of the images it produces. The standard defined an open lens mount so that bodies and lenses from different manufacturers would be interchangeable.

Design goals

Unlike systems adapted from 35 mm film cameras, Four Thirds was designed from the ground up for digital capture. Its smaller sensor (with a 2× crop factor relative to 35 mm film) allowed smaller, lighter lenses, especially telephoto lenses, at the cost of greater depth of field and somewhat poorer high-ISO noise performance than larger-sensor rivals. The standard also specified a lens mount throat wide enough, and a sensor-to-flange distance short enough, to allow light to strike the sensor close to perpendicularly, which its designers argued improved edge sharpness.

Cameras and manufacturers

The first Four Thirds camera was the Olympus E-1 of 2003, a professional body. Olympus went on to produce the E-series DSLRs, ending with the E-5 in 2010. Panasonic sold Four Thirds bodies under its Lumix brand, and Leica offered the Digilux 3. Lens makers included Olympus (Zuiko Digital), Panasonic/Leica and Sigma.

Micro Four Thirds

In 2008 Olympus and Panasonic announced Micro Four Thirds, a mirrorless derivative using the same sensor size but a much shorter flange distance and no mirror box. Micro Four Thirds proved commercially successful and effectively superseded the original DSLR standard; no new Four Thirds DSLR bodies have been produced since the early 2010s, though Four Thirds lenses can be used on Micro Four Thirds bodies with adapters that preserve electronic functions.
