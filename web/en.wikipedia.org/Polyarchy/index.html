Polyarchy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Elections are one of the institutional guarantees of polyarchy

Polyarchy is a concept in political science coined by the American political scientist Robert A. Dahl to describe actually existing democratic political systems. Dahl argued that no large-scale state fully realises the ideal of democracy, and used "polyarchy" — rule by the many — to denote regimes that come closest: those with high levels of both public contestation and inclusive participation.

Criteria

In Polyarchy: Participation and Opposition (1971), Dahl set out institutional guarantees that a polyarchy requires, including: elected officials; free, fair and frequent elections; freedom of expression; access to alternative sources of information; associational autonomy (freedom to form political parties and interest groups); inclusive citizenship (nearly all adults entitled to vote and hold office); and effective control of government policy by elected officials.

Dimensions

Dahl analysed regimes along two dimensions: liberalisation (public contestation) and inclusiveness (participation). Regimes that are low on both are "closed hegemonies"; high contestation with restricted suffrage gives "competitive oligarchies"; high inclusiveness with low contestation gives "inclusive hegemonies". Polyarchies occupy the corner where both dimensions are high.

Historical development

Dahl traced how countries such as the United Kingdom, the United States and Sweden moved toward polyarchy through sequences of expanding suffrage and deepening liberalisation, and he examined the social conditions — such as dispersed power resources, literacy, pluralist traditions and the absence of a subculture with coercive backing — that made such transitions more likely and more durable.

Criticism

Critics have argued that Dahl's criteria emphasise formal institutions and may understate inequalities of wealth and influence that persist within polyarchies, as well as the exclusion of resident non-citizens from political rights. Dahl himself later explored these tensions in works such as On Democracy (1998).
