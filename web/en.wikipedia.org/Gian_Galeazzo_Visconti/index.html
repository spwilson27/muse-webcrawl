Gian Galeazzo Visconti (15 October 1351 – 3 September 1402), often known in English as Galeazzo II in older sources (though he was in fact the first Gian Galeazzo and his grandfather was Galeazzo II), was the first Duke of Milan (1395–1402). He was a ruthless and ambitious ruler who nearly unified northern and central Italy under Milanese rule at the turn of the 15th century.

Rise to power

Gian Galeazzo was the son of Galeazzo II Visconti and Bianca of Savoy. On his father's death in 1378 he shared power in the western Visconti lands (centred on Pavia) with his uncle Bernabò, who ruled Milan. In 1380 he married Caterina Visconti, Bernabò's daughter — his own cousin. In 1385, feigning a pilgrimage, he lured Bernabò into an ambush at the Porta Orientale in Milan, imprisoned him in the castle of Trezzo, and seized sole power; Bernabò died in captivity later that year, probably poisoned.

Expansion

From 1386 Gian Galeazzo embarked on rapid conquest: Verona and Vicenza (1387), Padua (1388), then in the following decade Pisa, Siena, Perugia, Assisi, and Bologna came under his control, directly or through client rulers. He bought the ducal title from the emperor Wenceslaus in 1395 for 100,000 florins, becoming the first Duke of Milan. His campaigns against Florence (1390–92 and 1397–1402) twice brought Milan close to destroying Florentine independence; Florentine humanists such as Leonardo Bruni cast the war as a struggle between republican liberty and tyranny.

Administration and patronage

A formidable administrator, Gian Galeazzo reformed Milanese taxation and law (promulgating a famous statute against the plague and founding a pioneering health office, the basis of the later quarantine system), and lavished money on building: he founded the Certosa di Pavia (1396) as the family mausoleum and gave decisive support to the construction of Milan Cathedral, begun in 1386. The University of Pavia also benefited from his patronage.

Death and aftermath

Retreating from the plague at Milan, Gian Galeazzo died at Melegnano on 3 September 1402, just as a final assault on Florence seemed imminent. His will divided the duchy between his young sons Giovanni Maria and Filippo Maria and his illegitimate son Gabriele Maria, and the great state he had assembled rapidly fragmented under regency government, not to be reassembled until Filippo Maria's maturity.
