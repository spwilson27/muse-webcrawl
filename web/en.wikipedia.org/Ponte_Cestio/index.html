Ponte Cestio

The Ponte Cestio (Latin: Pons Cestius) is an ancient Roman stone bridge in Rome, Italy, spanning the western channel of the River Tiber from the Trastevere bank to Tiber Island. It is the western continuation of the route whose eastern half is the Ponte Fabricio, the oldest bridge in Rome still in use.

History

The bridge was first built around 46–44 BC, possibly by Lucius Cestius, brother of Gaius Cestius (whose tomb is the Pyramid of Cestius), in the period after Julius Caesar's civil war. It was substantially rebuilt in AD 370 by the emperors Valentinian I, Valens, and Gratian, and rededicated as the Pons Gratiani. Much of the ancient structure was demolished and rebuilt in 1888–1892 when the Tiber channel was widened and embanked; the present bridge retains the central arch geometry of the 4th-century restoration but is largely a 19th-century reconstruction, about 54 metres (177 ft) long.

Description

The bridge consists of three arches, the central span being the largest. Unlike its twin, the Ponte Fabricio, which survives almost intact from the 1st century BC, the Cestio's fabric visible today is mostly modern masonry faced in travertine and tufa in imitation of the original. Together the two bridges give access to Tiber Island, site of the ancient Temple of Aesculapius and today of the Fatebenefratelli Hospital.
