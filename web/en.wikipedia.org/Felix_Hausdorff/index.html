Felix Hausdorff

Felix Hausdorff (November 8, 1868 – January 26, 1942) was a German mathematician, considered one of the founders of modern topology, who also made significant contributions to set theory, descriptive set theory, measure theory, and functional analysis. He is best known for the concepts of Hausdorff space, Hausdorff dimension, and Hausdorff measure, all named after him.

Life

Hausdorff was born in Breslau into a wealthy Jewish textile-manufacturing family. He studied at the University of Leipzig, earning his doctorate in 1891 with work on astronomy, then habilitated in 1895. He taught at Leipzig until 1910, and later held chairs at Greifswald (1913–1921) and Bonn (1921–1935), where he built a distinguished school of mathematics. Under the pen name "Paul Mongré" he also published philosophical essays, poetry, and a successful play. After the Nazi seizure of power, Hausdorff was progressively restricted under anti-Jewish laws, forced to retire in 1935, and stripped of his emeritus status. In January 1942, learning that deportation to an extermination camp was imminent, he died by suicide together with his wife and sister-in-law.

Work

His 1914 treatise Grundzüge der Mengenlehre (Principles of Set Theory) was the first comprehensive treatment of set theory and introduced topological and metric spaces axiomatically, including the separation axiom that now defines Hausdorff spaces. He developed the theory of partially ordered sets, proved the Hausdorff maximal principle (equivalent to the axiom of choice), formulated the Hausdorff paradox — a non-measurable decomposition of the sphere related to the Banach–Tarski paradox — and, with the concept of fractional dimension, anticipated the theory of fractals later developed by Mandelbrot.
