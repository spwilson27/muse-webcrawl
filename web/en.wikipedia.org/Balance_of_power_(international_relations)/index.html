Balance of power (international relations)

Balance of power
FieldInternational relations theory
Associated schoolRealism
Key thinkersHans Morgenthau, Kenneth Waltz

The balance of power theory in international relations suggests, as a theory-conjecture, that states may secure their survival by preventing any one state from gaining enough military power to dominate all others. If one state becomes much stronger, the theory predicts it will take advantage of its strength and attack weaker neighbours, thereby providing an incentive for those threatened to unite in a defensive coalition. Some realists maintain that a balance-of-power system is more stable than one with a dominant state, as aggression is counterproductive when there is an equilibrium of power between rival coalitions.

Theory

When threatened, states may seek safety either by increasing their own power (internal balancing, for example through military build-up) or by aggregating their capabilities with those of other states in alliances (external balancing). In neorealist theory, Kenneth Waltz argued that bipolar systems, such as the Cold War confrontation between the United States and the Soviet Union, are particularly stable, while multipolar systems have historically been the norm in Europe.

Critics and other theorists note alternatives to balancing: states may bandwagon with the stronger power, pass the buck to another state, or attempt to hide. Stephen Walt's "balance of threat" refinement argues that states balance not against power alone but against the threat posed by a combination of power, proximity, offensive capability, and perceived intentions.

History

Balance-of-power ideas were practised in Renaissance Italy among the city-states and articulated in early modern Europe, notably in the diplomacy following the Peace of Westphalia (1648). The concept guided the coalitions against Louis XIV and Napoleon, the Congress of Vienna (1815), and the Concert of Europe. After the Second World War, the term described the bipolar stand-off of the Cold War; after 1991 scholars debated how long American unipolarity would last and whether other states would balance against it.
