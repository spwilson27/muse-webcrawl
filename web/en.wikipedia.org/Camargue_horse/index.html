The Camargue horse is an ancient breed of small horse indigenous to the Camargue area in southern France, the marshy Rhône delta between the Mediterranean Sea and the two arms of the Rhône. Long considered one of the oldest breeds of horse in the world, it lives semi-feral in the delta's wetlands and is the traditional mount of the gardians, the Camargue's herdsmen, who use it to manage the region's black fighting bulls.

Characteristics

Camargue horses are relatively small, standing about 1.35–1.50 m, with a large head, powerful neck, deep chest and notably hard feet adapted to marshy ground. Foals are born black or dark brown and lighten as they mature, so adults appear white-grey. The breed is famously hardy, agile and sure-footed, able to gallop through shallow water and mud, and tolerant of the delta's mosquitoes, heat and poor grazing.

Life and management

Horses live in small herds called manades under the oversight of gardians, who round them up for branding, veterinary care and selection. The breed was officially recognised in 1978, and a studbook and breed association preserve its population, much of it within the protected Camargue Regional Natural Park.

Cultural significance

The image of white horses galloping through the delta's lagoons is one of the most famous symbols of the Camargue and of Provence more broadly, celebrated in the 1953 film White Mane (Crin-Blanc) and a fixture of regional tourism. Together with the black bulls and pink flamingos of the delta, the horses form the emblematic triad of Camargue wildlife.
