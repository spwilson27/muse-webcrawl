Jacobite rising of 1715

DateSeptember 1715 – February 1716
LocationScotland and northern England
ResultGovernment victory
Jacobite commanderEarl of Mar; James Francis Edward Stuart

The Jacobite rising of 1715 (often called "the Fifteen") was an attempt by James Francis Edward Stuart — the "Old Pretender", son of the deposed James II of England and VII of Scotland — to regain the thrones of Great Britain from George I. Led in Scotland by the Earl of Mar, it was the most substantial Jacobite rising between 1689 and 1745, but collapsed through hesitant leadership and the failure of expected French support.

Background

The Act of Settlement (1701) settled the succession on the Hanoverian line, and the Union of 1707, unpopular in Scotland, gave Jacobites a grievance around which to rally. George I's accession in 1714, and his favouring of Whigs over Tories, drove some Tories toward Jacobitism. A planned rising was forestalled by arrests, but Mar, dismissed from office, travelled to Scotland and raised the Jacobite standard at Braemar on 6 September 1715 without waiting for James.

The campaign

Mar quickly assembled an army of over 10,000 men, larger than the government force under the Duke of Argyll, and took Perth. But he delayed instead of advancing decisively. On 13 November 1715 his army fought Argyll at the Battle of Sheriffmuir: each side routed one of the other's wings, but Mar failed to exploit his advantage, and the battle proved a strategic defeat for the Jacobites. The same day, the English Jacobite force under Thomas Forster, having invaded England with a Scottish contingent, was trapped and surrendered at the Battle of Preston. James himself landed at Peterhead on 22 December, too late to change the outcome; demoralised, he and Mar abandoned Perth and sailed back to France from Montrose in February 1716.

Aftermath

Captured leaders were tried for treason; the Earls of Derwentwater and Kenmure were executed, while others, including Forster, escaped or had sentences commuted. The government passed the Disarming Act to weaken the Highland clans and strengthened its hold on Scotland. James never again led a rising in person; the final attempt came under his son Charles Edward Stuart in 1745.
