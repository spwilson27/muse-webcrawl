Single-ended signaling

Single-ended signaling is the simplest and most common way of transmitting electrical signals over wires. A transmitter drives a signal onto one conductor, and a receiver measures the voltage of that conductor relative to a shared ground reference. One conductor carries each signal, plus a common ground shared by all channels.

Characteristics

Single-ended interfaces need only one wire per signal and simple circuitry, making them cheap and compact. However, any noise on the shared ground — caused by return currents, ground loops, or external interference — appears directly in the measured signal, and crosstalk between adjacent conductors limits speed and distance.

Examples

Classic examples include RS-232 serial links, the original parallel SCSI bus, I²C buses, and coaxial audio connections. Many board-level and legacy interfaces remain single-ended for cost and simplicity.

Comparison with differential signaling

Differential signaling transmits each signal over a pair of conductors carrying equal and opposite voltages; the receiver responds to the difference, rejecting common-mode noise picked up along the way. Modern high-speed standards such as USB, HDMI, PCI Express, and Ethernet therefore use differential pairs.
