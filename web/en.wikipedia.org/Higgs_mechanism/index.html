Higgs mechanism

In the Standard Model of particle physics, the Higgs mechanism is essential to explain the generation mechanism of the property "mass" for gauge bosons. Without the Higgs mechanism, all bosons would be considered massless, but measurements show that the W+, W−, and Z0 bosons actually have relatively large masses of around 80 GeV/c². The Higgs field is a scalar field that permeates all space, and through spontaneous symmetry breaking it gives mass to the weak force carriers and, via Yukawa couplings, to elementary fermions.

History

In 1964, three teams published papers proposing the mechanism: François Englert and Robert Brout; Peter Higgs; and Gerald Guralnik, C. R. Hagen, and Tom Kibble. The idea adapted concepts from condensed matter physics, notably Philip Anderson's observation that gauge bosons in superconductors acquire effective mass. Steven Weinberg and Abdus Salam incorporated the mechanism into electroweak unification in 1967–68. Englert and Higgs received the 2013 Nobel Prize in Physics after the predicted particle was found.

How it works

The Higgs field has a "Mexican hat" potential whose minimum is not at zero field but at a nonzero value, so the vacuum spontaneously breaks the electroweak gauge symmetry SU(2)×U(1) down to the U(1) of electromagnetism. Three of the field's four components become the longitudinal polarizations of the now-massive W and Z bosons, while the fourth remains as a physical particle, the Higgs boson. The photon, associated with the unbroken symmetry, stays massless. Fermion masses arise from Yukawa interactions between fermions and the Higgs field, with each particle's mass proportional to its coupling strength.

Discovery

On 4 July 2012, the ATLAS and CMS experiments at CERN's Large Hadron Collider announced the discovery of a new boson with a mass around 125 GeV/c², consistent with the Higgs boson; subsequent measurements of its spin, parity, and couplings have matched Standard Model predictions.
