Pedal point

In music, a pedal point (also called a pedal tone, organ point, or drone) is a sustained tone, typically in the bass, during which at least one foreign (i.e. dissonant) harmony is sounded in the other parts. A pedal point sometimes functions as a "non-chord tone", placing it in the categories of suspensions, retardations, or passing tones.

Terminology

The term comes from the organ, where the player's feet can sustain a bass note on the pedal keyboard while both hands play changing harmonies above it. Pedal points are typically on the tonic or dominant scale degrees; a sustained tone on the dominant is called a dominant pedal, and one on the tonic a tonic pedal. An inverted pedal is a pedal point in an upper voice rather than the bass.

Use

Pedal points are a hallmark of Baroque counterpoint and fugue: J. S. Bach's organ works contain celebrated examples, such as the extended dominant pedal in the Toccata and Fugue in D minor's opening section and the long pedal passages in the Passacaglia in C minor. Pedal tones build tension that resolves when the harmony realigns with the sustained note.

The device persists across later styles—in the drone textures of folk music and bagpipes, in the static bass lines of minimalism, and in popular music, where a pedal tone under moving chords is a common means of building anticipation before a chorus.

Related concepts

A drone is a continuous sustained sound, of which the pedal point is a notated, functional instance within tonal harmony. Related devices include the ostinato, a repeating pattern, and the ground bass.
