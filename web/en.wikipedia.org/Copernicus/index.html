Copernicus

Copernicus most commonly refers to Nicolaus Copernicus (1473–1543), the Renaissance polymath who formulated a heliocentric model of the universe with the Sun at its centre, displacing the Earth from the position assumed by the Ptolemaic system. His book De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres), published in the year of his death, is regarded as the starting point of the Scientific Revolution.

Nicolaus Copernicus

Born in Toruń in Royal Prussia, Copernicus studied at Kraków, Bologna and Padua, and spent most of his career as a canon of Frombork Cathedral in Warmia. Besides astronomy he practised medicine, administered church estates, advised on coinage — formulating an early statement of the quantity theory of money and of what became known as Gresham's law — and defended his region during the Polish–Teutonic War. His heliocentric hypothesis, first sketched in the Commentariolus (c. 1514), explained retrograde motion naturally and reordered the planets by their periods, though he retained circular orbits and epicycles.

Other uses

  • Copernicus Programme — the European Union's Earth observation programme, operating the Sentinel satellites.
  • Copernicus (lunar crater) — a prominent impact crater on the Moon, about 93 km across.
  • 55 Cancri e, a planet formerly nicknamed Copernicus, and the star Copernicus (55 Cancri A) named in the IAU NameExoWorlds contest.
  • Nicolaus Copernicus University in Toruń and other institutions, museums and observatories named in his honour.
  • Copernicium — chemical element 112, symbol Cn, named after him in 2010.
