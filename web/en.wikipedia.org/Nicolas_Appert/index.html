Nicolas Appert (1749–1841)

Nicolas Appert (17 November 1749 – 1 June 1841) was a French confectioner and inventor who invented airtight food preservation. Appert, known as the "father of food science", described his invention as a method that would preserve food "like cream, eggs or blood" using bottles sealed and heated in a water bath. His technique anticipated canning and, although he did not understand why it worked — the germ theory of disease came decades later — it revolutionised food storage.

The prize and the method

In 1795 the French government, seeking reliable ways to feed its armies and navy, offered a prize of 12,000 francs for a new method of preserving food. After some fifteen years of experiments using glass bottles sealed with cork and wax and heated for varying times in boiling water, Appert claimed the prize in 1810, on condition that he publish his method. His book, L'Art de conserver, pendant plusieurs années, toutes les substances animales et végétales ("The Art of Preserving Animal and Vegetable Substances"), appeared the same year.

Legacy

English inventor Peter Durand patented the use of tin cans the same year, and Appert himself later adopted metal containers. Appert's factory near Paris supplied the French army and navy, but he died impoverished in 1841. The canning process is still called "appertisation" in French, and France's food industry honours him as the inventor of the canned food industry.
