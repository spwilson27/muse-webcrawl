Anatomy

Anatomy (from the Greek anatomē, "dissection") is the branch of biology concerned with the study of the structure of organisms and their parts. Anatomy is a branch of natural science that deals with the structural organisation of living things. It is an old science, having its beginnings in prehistoric times, and is inherently tied to developmental biology, embryology, comparative anatomy, evolutionary biology, and physiology.

Branches

Gross anatomy studies structures large enough to be seen with the naked eye, including surface anatomy and regional anatomy. Microscopic anatomy involves the use of optical instruments in the study of tissues (histology) and cells (cytology). Other approaches include systemic anatomy (by organ system), comparative anatomy across species, and anatomical pathology.

History

Anatomical knowledge accumulated in ancient Egypt and Greece; Herophilos and Erasistratus performed human dissections in Hellenistic Alexandria. The works of Galen (2nd century CE) dominated medicine for over a millennium, though based largely on animal dissection. The Renaissance revived human dissection, culminating in Andreas Vesalius's De humani corporis fabrica (1543), the foundational work of modern anatomy. In the 17th century William Harvey demonstrated the circulation of the blood, and the microscope opened microscopic anatomy through Malpighi and others. Modern anatomy is standardised by the Terminologia Anatomica and increasingly supplemented by medical imaging and digital anatomical models such as the Visible Human Project.

Human anatomy

Human anatomy is conventionally organised by systems: skeletal, muscular, nervous, cardiovascular, respiratory, digestive, urinary, reproductive, endocrine, lymphatic, and integumentary. Knowledge of anatomy is essential to medicine and surgery, physical therapy, and the biological sciences.
