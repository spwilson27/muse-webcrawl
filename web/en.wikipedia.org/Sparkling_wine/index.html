Sparkling wine

Sparkling wine is a wine with significant levels of carbon dioxide in it, making it fizzy. The carbon dioxide results from natural fermentation, either in the bottle (the traditional method) or in a large tank (the Charmat method), or is occasionally injected. The most famous example is Champagne, from the region of that name in France; the European Union reserves the legal term "Champagne" for that origin alone.

Production methods

Traditional method

A still base wine is bottled with yeast and sugar (the liqueur de tirage), undergoing a second fermentation in the bottle that traps CO₂. The bottles are riddled (gradually tilted and turned) to settle the dead yeast in the neck; the neck is frozen and the plug of sediment disgorged, after which a dosage of wine and sugar sets the sweetness before final corking. Champagne, Cava and most premium sparkling wines use this method, gaining complex yeasty flavours from ageing on the lees.

Charmat method

The second fermentation takes place in a pressurised stainless-steel tank, after which the wine is filtered and bottled under pressure. Faster and cheaper, it preserves fresh fruity aromatics and is used for Prosecco and many others. A third route, carbonation, simply injects CO₂ into still wine, yielding coarser bubbles.

Styles and sweetness

Sparkling wines are labelled by residual sugar: brut nature (driest), extra brut, brut (the common dry style), extra dry (confusingly, slightly sweeter than brut), sec, demi-sec and doux (sweetest). They may be white, rosé, or occasionally red (such as sparkling Shiraz from Australia or Italian Lambrusco). Major producers outside Europe include the USA, England — whose chalk-soil wines have won wide acclaim — and Tasmania and New Zealand.

Serving

Sparkling wine is served well chilled (about 6–10 °C) in a flute or tulip glass that preserves bubbles and aroma. Bottles are opened by easing the cork out quietly rather than popping it, to retain the fizz.
