Self-image

Self-image is the mental picture a person holds of themselves — the set of beliefs and impressions about one's own appearance, abilities, personality and worth. It is a component of the broader self-concept, alongside self-esteem (the evaluative, emotional component) and the ideal self (the person one wishes to be). Self-image may diverge considerably from how others perceive the individual and from objective reality.

Formation

Self-image develops through social interaction. Charles Horton Cooley's "looking-glass self" (1902) proposed that people come to see themselves as they imagine others see them; George Herbert Mead similarly grounded the self in social experience. Family feedback, peer comparison, cultural ideals and media representations all contribute. In Carl Rogers' humanistic psychology, incongruence between self-image and ideal self — and between self-image and actual experience — is a source of anxiety, and therapy aims to reduce it.

Body image

The best-studied aspect is body image: perceptions and feelings about one's physical appearance. Distorted body image is a core feature of eating disorders such as anorexia and bulimia nervosa and of body dysmorphic disorder. Research links heavy exposure to idealised media images, and more recently to filtered social-media imagery, with body dissatisfaction, though effects vary with individual vulnerability.

Change

Self-image is fairly stable but not fixed: significant life events, ageing, illness and disability can revise it, and cognitive-behavioural techniques, self-compassion training and corrective social feedback can modify distorted self-images. Positive but realistic self-image is associated with resilience and mental health, whereas persistently negative self-image features in depression and social anxiety.
