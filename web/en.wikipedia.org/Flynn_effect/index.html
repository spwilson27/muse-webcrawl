Flynn effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Flynn effect is the substantial, long-term increase in average scores on intelligence tests observed throughout much of the 20th century. It is named after New Zealand researcher James R. Flynn, who documented the phenomenon extensively; earlier observations were made by others, including a 1982 study by Richard Lynn. Gains averaged roughly 3 IQ points per decade in many countries, and were largest on fluid-reasoning tests such as Raven's Progressive Matrices.

Proposed causes

Because genetic change cannot plausibly act so quickly, the gains are attributed to environmental factors: improved nutrition and health, longer and better schooling, greater familiarity with testing, smaller families, and the increasing cognitive complexity of everyday environments — including more abstract and scientific habits of thought, which Flynn emphasised. The effect complicates comparisons of intelligence across generations, since tests must be periodically re-normed so that the average score remains 100.

Reversal

Since the 1990s, several developed countries — including Norway, Denmark, Finland, the United Kingdom, and France — have reported stagnation or decline in test scores, sometimes called the "negative Flynn effect" or anti-Flynn effect. A major Norwegian study of military conscripts found declines within families, suggesting environmental rather than compositional causes. The causes and generality of the reversal remain debated.
