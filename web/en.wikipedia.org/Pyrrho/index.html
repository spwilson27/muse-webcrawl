Pyrrho

Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360 – c. 270 BC) was a Greek philosopher of classical antiquity, credited as the founder of Pyrrhonism, the first school of philosophical skepticism. According to tradition, Pyrrho travelled to India with Alexander the Great's army and studied with the "gymnosophists", an experience said to have shaped his philosophy of tranquillity and suspension of judgment. His teachings, preserved through his pupil Timon of Phlius and later writers such as Diogenes Laertius and Sextus Empiricus, held that because things are equally indifferent and undecidable, the wise person suspends assent (epoché) and thereby attains freedom from disturbance (ataraxia).

Life

Pyrrho was born in Elis, in the northwestern Peloponnese, and is said to have begun as a painter before turning to philosophy under Bryson and Anaxarchus. He accompanied Anaxarchus in Alexander's expedition to the East (327–325 BC), meeting Indian ascetics and Persian Magi. In later life he lived quietly in Elis, held in high honour by his fellow citizens, who made him chief priest and exempted philosophers from taxes on his account. Anecdotes — likely embellished — show him maintaining imperturbable calm in storms, accidents, and pain, and being rescued by friends from dangers his indifference made him slow to avoid.

Philosophy

Pyrrho wrote nothing; his doctrine is reconstructed from Timon's summaries, especially as reported by Aristocles and preserved in Eusebius. The core teaching is threefold: things by nature are equally indifferent, unmeasurable, and undecidable; therefore our perceptions and opinions are neither true nor false; therefore we should not trust the senses, should say of each thing that it "no more is than is not", and should withhold judgment — from which tranquillity follows "like a shadow". Later Pyrrhonists such as Sextus Empiricus developed the position into a systematic practice of opposing arguments of equal strength on every question. Whether Pyrrho's stance was a metaphysical claim that reality is indeterminate, or simply a practical attitude of non-assertion, remains debated among scholars, with parallels often drawn to early Buddhism and to the Madhyamaka school.

Legacy

The adjective "Pyrrhonian" came to denote radical skepticism; the phrase "Pyrrhic victory" is unrelated, referring to King Pyrrhus of Epirus. Pyrrhonism was revived in the Renaissance through the printing of Sextus's works in the 1560s, influencing Montaigne and, through him, much of early modern philosophy, including Descartes's method of doubt and Hume's skepticism about induction.
