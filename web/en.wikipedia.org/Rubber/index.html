Rubber

Rubber is a natural or synthetic elastomer characterised by its ability to stretch substantially and recover its original shape. Natural rubber, also called India rubber or caoutchouc, is an organic polymer of isoprene harvested mainly as latex from the rubber tree, Hevea brasiliensis. Synthetic rubbers are artificial elastomers made from petroleum byproducts; together the two supply the material for tyres, seals, hoses, footwear, medical gloves and thousands of other products.

Natural rubber

Indigenous peoples of Mesoamerica used rubber for balls, containers and waterproofing thousands of years before European contact. Commercial exploitation began in the nineteenth century, when vulcanisation — heating rubber with sulfur, patented by Charles Goodyear in 1844 — produced a stable, durable material. Wild rubber extraction in the Amazon basin fuelled a boom (and notorious abuses, notably in the Congo Free State and the Putumayo) until plantation rubber from Southeast Asia, grown from seeds taken from Brazil in 1876, undercut it after 1910. Today Thailand, Indonesia and Vietnam are among the leading producers.

Production and properties

Latex is collected by tapping diagonal cuts in the bark and coagulated with acid into sheets or blocks. Raw rubber is elastic, waterproof and electrically insulating, but softens with heat; vulcanisation introduces sulfur cross-links between polymer chains that greatly improve strength and temperature stability. Fillers such as carbon black further improve abrasion resistance, which is why tyres are black.

Synthetic rubber

Shortages during the Second World War spurred the large-scale development of synthetic rubbers such as styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR), neoprene and nitrile rubber. Synthetic types now account for the majority of world production and can be tailored for resistance to oil, heat and weathering.

Uses and recycling

Roughly 70 per cent of rubber goes into tyres. Other uses include conveyor belts, gaskets, condoms, balloons, erasers and sports balls. Waste tyres pose disposal problems; rubber is increasingly recycled into crumb for playground surfaces, fuel and civil-engineering applications.
