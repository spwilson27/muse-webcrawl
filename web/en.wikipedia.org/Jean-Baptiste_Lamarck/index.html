Jean-Baptiste Lamarck

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jean-Baptiste Pierre Antoine de Monet, chevalier de Lamarck (1 August 1744 – 18 December 1829), often known simply as Lamarck, was a French naturalist, biologist, academic, and soldier. He was an early proponent of the idea that biological evolution occurred and proceeded in accordance with natural laws. He gave the term "biology" its modern meaning, and produced the first full theory of evolution, presented in his Philosophie zoologique (1809).

Life

Lamarck was born in Bazentin, Picardy, into an impoverished aristocratic family. He served in the French army during the Seven Years' War and was decorated for bravery. After leaving the army because of injury, he studied medicine and botany in Paris, publishing the three-volume Flore française (1778), which won him the patronage of the Comte de Buffon and election to the French Academy of Sciences. When the Jardin des Plantes was reorganised as the Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle in 1793, Lamarck was appointed professor of zoology in charge of insects, worms, and microscopic animals — the field then called invertebrate zoology, a term he coined along with "invertebrate" itself.

Theory of evolution

Lamarck proposed that species change over time through two forces: a tendency of life to become progressively more complex, and adaptation to environments through the use and disuse of organs, with acquired characteristics being inherited by offspring — the idea later dubbed "Lamarckism". His classic example was the giraffe, whose neck he suggested lengthened over generations through stretching to reach high leaves. Although his proposed mechanism was superseded by Darwinian natural selection and later genetics, Lamarck is recognised as the first naturalist to present a systematic, scientific theory of evolution, and his ideas stimulated later evolutionary thought. Modern research in epigenetics has revived some interest in inheritance of acquired traits, though this is distinct from Lamarck's theory.

Later years and legacy

Lamarck's Histoire naturelle des animaux sans vertèbres (1815–1822) founded much of invertebrate classification. He went blind in his final years and died in poverty in Paris in 1829. A statue of Lamarck stands at the entrance to the Jardin des Plantes, inscribed as the founder of the doctrine of evolution.
