Two-photon excitation microscopy

Two-photon excitation microscopy
Type: fluorescence microscopy
Introduced: 1990 (Denk, Strickler, Webb)
Typical excitation: near-infrared pulsed laser

Two-photon excitation microscopy is a fluorescence imaging technique in which a fluorophore is excited by the near-simultaneous absorption of two photons of roughly half the energy (twice the wavelength) needed for one-photon excitation, as first described theoretically by Maria Göppert Mayer in 1931 and demonstrated for microscopy by Winfried Denk, James Strickler and Watt Webb in 1990.

Principle

Because two-photon absorption requires extremely high photon flux, excitation occurs only in the tiny focal volume of a pulsed laser, giving intrinsic optical sectioning without a confocal pinhole. The near-infrared excitation light scatters less in tissue, allowing imaging hundreds of micrometres deep in living specimens.

Applications

The technique is widely used in neuroscience for imaging neuronal activity in living brains, in developmental biology, and in intravital imaging of tissues, with reduced photodamage outside the focal plane.
