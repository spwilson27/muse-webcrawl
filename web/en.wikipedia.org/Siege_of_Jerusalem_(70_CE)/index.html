Siege of Jerusalem (70 CE)

Date14 April – 8 September 70 CE
LocationJerusalem, Judea
BelligerentsRoman Empire vs. Jewish rebels
CommandersTitus (Roman); Simon bar Giora, John of Gischala (rebels)
ResultRoman victory; Temple destroyed

The Siege of Jerusalem of 70 CE was the decisive episode of the First Jewish–Roman War (66–73 CE). The Roman army, led by Titus, son of the emperor Vespasian, besieged the rebel-held city in the spring of 70, breached its walls after months of fighting, and destroyed the Second Temple — an event mourned in Judaism to this day on the fast of Tisha B'Av and commemorated in Rome by the Arch of Titus, whose reliefs show the Temple's menorah and trumpets carried in triumph.

Background

Revolt against Roman rule broke out in Judea in 66 CE. The rebels were divided among rival factions — led principally by John of Gischala and Simon bar Giora, with Eleazar ben Simon's Zealots holding the Temple — which fought a civil war inside Jerusalem even as the Romans advanced. Vespasian subdued Galilee and the countryside; proclaimed emperor in 69, he left his son Titus with four legions and auxiliaries, some 70,000 men, to finish the war.

The siege

Titus arrived before Jerusalem around Passover 70, when the city was crowded with pilgrims. Roman forces took the outer (third) wall, then the second wall after hard fighting, and built a circumvallation wall with forts around the entire city to starve the defenders; the resulting famine inside was horrific, and deserters caught smuggling food were crucified in view of the walls. After failed assaults and the capture of the Antonia Fortress, the Romans fought their way into the Temple precincts. On the ninth of Av (late August by most reckonings) the Temple caught fire — Josephus claims against Titus's orders — and was burned to the ground. The upper city fell in September. Josephus reports 1.1 million dead and 97,000 captives; modern historians regard the figures as exaggerated but accept catastrophic loss of life. Survivors were enslaved, dispersed, or sent to die in arenas across the empire.

Sources and aftermath

The siege is known chiefly from the detailed account of Josephus, a former rebel commander who witnessed it from the Roman side (The Jewish War, Book 6). Jerusalem was left in ruins, the Legio X Fretensis camped on the site, and the war ended with the fall of Masada in 73/74 CE. The destruction of the Temple ended the sacrificial cult and accelerated the transformation of Judaism toward its rabbinic, synagogue-based form.
