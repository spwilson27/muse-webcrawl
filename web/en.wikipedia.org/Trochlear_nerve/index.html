The trochlear nerve (cranial nerve IV) is a purely motor nerve that innervates a single muscle, the superior oblique muscle of the eye, which acts to depress, abduct, and intort the eyeball. It is named for the trochlea, the fibrocartilaginous pulley through which the tendon of the superior oblique passes. The trochlear nerve is distinctive among cranial nerves: it is the smallest cranial nerve by number of axons, it has the longest intracranial course, and it is the only cranial nerve to exit from the dorsal (posterior) side of the brainstem.

Course

Fibres arise from the trochlear nucleus in the midbrain, at the level of the inferior colliculus, and cross completely (decussate) within the superior medullary velum before emerging just below the inferior colliculus on the opposite side. The nerve then winds around the cerebral peduncle, passes between the posterior cerebral and superior cerebellar arteries (like the oculomotor nerve), pierces the dura, runs in the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus, and enters the orbit through the superior orbital fissure, outside the annulus of Zinn, to reach the superior oblique.

Clinical significance

Damage to the trochlear nerve causes paralysis of the superior oblique, producing vertical (and partly torsional) double vision that is worst when looking down and in — classically, patients have trouble descending stairs or reading, and tilt the head away from the affected side to compensate. Causes include head trauma (the nerve's long course and dorsal exit make it vulnerable), microvascular disease such as diabetes, tumours, and congenital fourth nerve palsy, a common cause of head tilt in children.
