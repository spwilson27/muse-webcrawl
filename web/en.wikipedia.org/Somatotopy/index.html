Somatotopy

Somatotopy is the point-for-point correspondence of an area of the body to a specific point on the central nervous system. Typically the area of the body corresponds to a point on the primary somatosensory cortex (postcentral gyrus), where the body is represented in an orderly map known as the sensory homunculus. A corresponding map, the motor homunculus, exists in the primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus).

Organisation

In the somatosensory cortex the body is mapped roughly upside-down, with the legs represented near the midline and the head laterally. The size of the cortical territory devoted to each body part is not proportional to the part's physical size but to the density of its sensory receptors: the lips, tongue, fingertips and face occupy disproportionately large areas, reflecting their fine tactile discrimination. Wilder Penfield's stimulation studies during epilepsy surgery in the 1930s–1950s produced the classic homunculus drawings.

Other maps

Somatotopic organisation also appears in the dorsal columns and spinothalamic tracts of the spinal cord, in the thalamus, and in the cerebellum, where multiple, fractured body maps are found. The maps are not fixed: they reorganise with learning, injury and amputation, a phenomenon related to neuroplasticity and implicated in phantom limb sensations.
