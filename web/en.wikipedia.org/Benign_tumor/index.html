Benign tumor

A benign tumor is a mass of cells (tumor) that does not invade neighboring tissue or metastasize (spread throughout the body). Compared to malignant (cancerous) tumors, benign tumors generally have a slower growth rate and their cells resemble the normal cells of the tissue of origin. Benign tumors are typically surrounded by an outer surface (fibrous sheath of connective tissue) or are contained by an epithelium.

Characteristics

Common examples of benign tumors include moles (melanocytic nevi), skin tags, lipomas (fatty lumps), uterine fibroids (leiomyomas) and adenomas of endocrine glands. "Benign" does not mean harmless in all cases: a benign tumor in a confined space, such as a meningioma inside the skull, can compress vital structures and be life-threatening. Benign tumors also do not always remain benign; some, such as colonic adenomatous polyps, are precursors of cancer.

Treatment

Many benign tumors need no treatment and may simply be monitored. Treatment is indicated when a tumor causes symptoms, cosmetic concerns, or has malignant potential, and usually consists of surgical excision, after which the tumor typically does not recur.
