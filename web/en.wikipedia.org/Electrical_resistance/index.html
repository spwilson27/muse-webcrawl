Electrical resistance and conductance

Electrical resistance is a measure of how strongly a material or device opposes the flow of electric current. The SI unit is the ohm (Ω). The reciprocal quantity, electrical conductance, is measured in siemens (S). For many materials, resistance is defined by Ohm's law, R = V/I, where V is the voltage across the object and I is the current through it.

Resistance of a conductor

For a uniform wire, R = ρL/A, where ρ is the material's resistivity, L its length, and A its cross-sectional area. Metals such as copper and silver have low resistivities, while insulators such as rubber and glass have enormously high ones. Semiconductor resistivities lie in between and can be controlled by doping.

Temperature dependence

In metals, resistance increases with temperature as lattice vibrations scatter electrons more strongly; in semiconductors and insulators, resistance usually decreases as temperature frees more charge carriers. At extremely low temperatures, superconductors exhibit exactly zero resistance below a critical temperature.

AC circuits

In alternating-current circuits, the generalization of resistance is impedance, which includes the effects of capacitance and inductance. At high frequencies the skin effect confines current to the surface of conductors, raising effective resistance.

Resistors

A resistor is a component manufactured to provide a specified resistance; resistors dissipate electrical energy as heat at a rate P = I²R (Joule heating). They are marked with standardized colour codes and are fundamental to electronic circuits for limiting current, dividing voltage, and setting time constants.
