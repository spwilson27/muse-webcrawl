Feng shui

A luopan, the magnetic compass ringed with formulae used in compass-school feng shui.

Feng shui (Chinese: 風水, literally "wind–water") is a traditional Chinese practice that originated in ancient China and claims to use energy forces — qi — to harmonise individuals with their surrounding environment. It involves arranging buildings, objects and spaces, and choosing auspicious sites for graves, dwellings and structures, in relation to landscape, orientation and time.

History and schools

Feng shui's roots lie in early Chinese astronomy and burial customs; the term appears in the Book of Burial attributed to Guo Pu (276–324 CE). Two broad schools developed: the Form School, which reads the shapes of mountains, rivers and buildings, and the Compass School, which uses the luopan compass and cosmological correlations, including the bagua (eight trigrams) and the five phases (wuxing). Historically, feng shui guided the siting of imperial tombs, palaces and entire cities such as Beijing.

Modern practice and criticism

Suppressed in mainland China during the Cultural Revolution, feng shui persisted in Hong Kong, Taiwan and overseas Chinese communities and became a global interior-design and real-estate phenomenon from the late 20th century. Scientists and sceptics classify feng shui as a pseudoscience, noting that its claims about qi and auspicious arrangement have no empirical support, though some of its traditional siting advice coincides with practical considerations of light, drainage and shelter.
