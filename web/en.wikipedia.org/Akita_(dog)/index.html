The Akita (秋田犬, Akita-inu) is a large breed of dog originating from the mountainous Akita Prefecture of northern Japan. It is a spitz-type breed historically used for hunting large game such as bear, boar and deer, and later as a guard dog and symbol of good health, happiness and longevity in Japan.

History

The breed descends from the matagi hunting dogs of the Akita region. In the early 20th century the breed was crossed with other dogs during a vogue for dog fighting, but preservation efforts led by the Society for the Preservation of the Japanese Dog restored the original type; the Akita was designated a Japanese Natural Monument in 1931. Helen Keller is credited with introducing the first Akitas to the United States in 1937. Two strains exist: the Japanese Akita (Akita Inu) and the American Akita, recognised as separate breeds by many registries.

Hachikō

The most famous Akita is Hachikō (1923–1935), who waited daily at Tokyo's Shibuya Station for his deceased owner for nearly ten years. A statue at the station commemorates his loyalty, and his story has been retold in Japanese and American films.

Temperament and care

Akitas are known for dignity, courage and loyalty, but can be aloof with strangers and dominant with other dogs; they require experienced handling, socialisation and regular grooming of their dense double coat. Common health concerns include hip dysplasia, hypothyroidism, and autoimmune disorders.
