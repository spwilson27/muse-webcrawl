Battleship Potemkin (Russian: Bronenosets Potyomkin) is a 1925 Soviet silent film directed by Sergei Eisenstein and produced by Mosfilm. It dramatises the true story of the 1905 mutiny aboard the Russian battleship Potemkin, when the crew rose against their officers during the failed Russian Revolution of 1905. The film is consistently ranked among the greatest ever made and is celebrated for revolutionising film editing.

Plot

The film is structured in five acts. Sailors refuse maggot-infested meat; their leader Vakulinchuk is killed; the crew mutinies, throws the officers overboard, and sails into Odessa, where the townspeople rally to the ship. In the fourth act, tsarist soldiers massacre civilians on the great harbour staircase — the celebrated "Odessa Steps" sequence, in which a baby carriage rolls down the steps amid the carnage. Finally, the squadron sent to destroy the Potemkin refuses to fire, and the ship sails through to cheers.

Technique and influence

Eisenstein used the film to demonstrate his theory of montage — the collision of images to create meaning — including the famous juxtaposition of a slaughtered bull and a massacred crowd, and rhythmic editing that stretches a few minutes of action into an extended sequence. Battleship Potemkin was named the greatest film of all time at the 1958 Brussels World's Fair critics' poll and has influenced filmmakers worldwide, though its status as revolutionary propaganda led to bans in several countries.
