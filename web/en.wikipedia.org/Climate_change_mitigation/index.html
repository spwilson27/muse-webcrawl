Climate change mitigation (or decarbonisation) is action to limit the greenhouse gases in the atmosphere that cause climate change. Climate change mitigation actions include conserving energy and replacing fossil fuels with clean energy sources. Secondary mitigation strategies include changes to land use and removing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.

Current climate change mitigation policies are insufficient, as they would result in global warming of about 2.7 °C by 2100, significantly above the 2015 Paris Agreement goal of limiting global warming to below 2 °C. Recent energy transition is driven by the growing cost competitiveness of wind and solar power, which together with hydropower and nuclear power can replace fossil-fuel generation.

Emission trends and targets

Global greenhouse gas emissions were around 59 gigatonnes of CO₂-equivalent in 2019. Pathways limiting warming to 1.5 °C with no or limited overshoot require global CO₂ emissions to fall by roughly 45–48% by 2030 relative to 2010 and reach net zero around 2050. Under the Paris Agreement, countries submit nationally determined contributions (NDCs) setting out their mitigation pledges.

Energy and industry

Decarbonising electricity is central: efficiency, renewable generation, grids and storage, and retirement of unabated coal. Hard-to-abate sectors — steel, cement, chemicals, aviation, shipping — require hydrogen, electrification, material efficiency, and carbon capture and storage.

Land, food, and carbon removal

Halting deforestation, restoring forests and wetlands, soil carbon management, and dietary shifts reduce emissions from agriculture, forestry and other land use. Carbon dioxide removal options include afforestation, bioenergy with carbon capture (BECCS), and direct air capture, though most remain small in scale.

Policies and economics

Policy instruments include carbon pricing (taxes and emissions trading), clean-energy standards, subsidies, and phase-out regulations. The IPCC finds that the global economic benefit of limiting warming to 2 °C exceeds mitigation costs, and that feasibility depends on governance, finance, and public acceptance.
