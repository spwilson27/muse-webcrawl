The common carp (Cyprinus carpio) is a widespread freshwater fish native to lakes and large rivers in Europe and Asia. One of the most domesticated of all fishes, it has been farmed for food for centuries and introduced worldwide; its ornamental variety is the koi of Japanese garden ponds. In many countries it is a prized angling species, while in others, notably Australia and North America, it is regarded as a damaging invasive pest.

Description

The common carp is a heavy-bodied fish with large scales, a long dorsal fin, and two pairs of barbels at the corners of the mouth. Wild fish are typically olive-brown to silvery; selectively bred forms include the mirror carp (with a few enlarged scales), the almost scaleless leather carp, and the brightly patterned koi. Carp are highly tolerant of low oxygen and turbid water, and can live for several decades, with well-documented individuals exceeding 40 years.

Biology and habitat

Carp inhabit slow-moving, weedy waters, feeding on the bottom on insect larvae, crustaceans, molluscs, and plant material, often uprooting vegetation and clouding the water as they feed. Spawning occurs in spring and early summer in shallow water, females scattering adhesive eggs over vegetation. Their hardiness lets them thrive in a wide range of climates, from temperate Europe to subtropical Asia.

Human uses and impacts

Carp aquaculture dates back to Roman times and medieval monastic ponds, and the fish remains a traditional Christmas dish in parts of Central and Eastern Europe. Coarse anglers in Britain and Europe pursue large specimen carp intensively. Conversely, introduced carp in Australia (where they dominate many Murray–Darling river systems), North America, and elsewhere are blamed for destroying aquatic vegetation, increasing turbidity, and displacing native fish, prompting costly control programmes.
