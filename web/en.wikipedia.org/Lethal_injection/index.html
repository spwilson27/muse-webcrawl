Lethal injection

Lethal injection is the practice of injecting one or more drugs into a person to cause rapid death, used principally as a method of capital punishment. It was first adopted by the U.S. state of Oklahoma in 1977 and first carried out in Texas in 1982, when Charles Brooks Jr. was executed. Since then it has become the primary method of execution in the United States and is also authorised in China, Vietnam and a small number of other jurisdictions.

Procedure and drugs

The traditional American protocol uses a sequence of three drugs: an anaesthetic (originally sodium thiopental), a paralytic agent (pancuronium bromide) and potassium chloride to stop the heart. Shortages of execution drugs — after manufacturers, especially in Europe, restricted their sale for executions — led states to adopt alternatives such as single-drug protocols or the sedative midazolam.

Controversy

Lethal injection has been challenged as cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment, most prominently in Baze v. Rees (2008) and Glossip v. Gross (2015), in which the U.S. Supreme Court upheld state protocols. Several executions described as "botched", including those of Clayton Lockett (2014) and Joseph Wood (2014), intensified debate over drug transparency, dosage and the risk of conscious suffering masked by paralytics.
