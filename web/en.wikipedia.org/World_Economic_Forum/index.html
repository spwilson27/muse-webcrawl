The World Economic Forum (WEF) is an international non-profit foundation for public–private cooperation, headquartered in Cologny, Switzerland. It is best known for its annual meeting in Davos, a resort town in the Swiss Alps, which brings together business leaders, heads of government, academics and civil-society figures to discuss global economic and social issues.

History

The Forum was founded in 1971 by German economist Klaus Schwab as the European Management Forum; it adopted its present name in 1987. The Davos meeting grew from a management symposium into a major fixture of the global political calendar, hosting roughly 3,000 participants in recent years. The WEF is funded by its member companies, typically large multinational corporations.

Activities

Beyond Davos, the Forum holds regional meetings and publishes widely cited research, including the Global Competitiveness Report and the Global Risks Report. Its initiatives have spanned climate action, the Fourth Industrial Revolution, and pandemic preparedness. Nelson Mandela and F. W. de Klerk made a joint appearance at Davos in 1992, a milestone in South Africa's transition from apartheid.

Criticism

The Forum attracts criticism for elitism and for giving corporations privileged access to policymakers; the Davos meeting draws regular protests, and anti-globalisation campaigners have long targeted it. Defenders argue it provides a rare neutral platform for dialogue between governments, business and civil society.
