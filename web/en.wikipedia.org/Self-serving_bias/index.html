Self-serving bias

A self-serving bias is any cognitive or perceptual process that is distorted by the need to maintain and enhance self-esteem, or the tendency to perceive oneself in an overly favourable manner. Most characteristically, people attribute their successes to internal factors such as ability and effort, but attribute their failures to external factors such as bad luck or the situation.

Evidence

The bias has been demonstrated in students explaining exam results, drivers assessing their own skill, athletes explaining wins and losses, and workers negotiating credit for group projects. A large meta-analysis by Mezulis and colleagues (2004), covering hundreds of studies, found the bias to be pervasive but moderated by culture: it is weaker, and occasionally reversed, in East Asian samples, and it is reduced in people with depression.

Explanations

Two families of explanation compete. Motivational accounts say people make self-serving attributions to protect self-esteem. Cognitive accounts say the bias arises from ordinary information processing — people expect to succeed, so successes seem normal and diagnostic while failures seem anomalous. The truth likely involves both.
