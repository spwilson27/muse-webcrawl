Oxidative phosphorylation

Oxidative phosphorylation (UK /ɒkˈsɪd.ə.tɪv/, US /ˈɑːk.sɪˌdeɪ.tɪv/) is the metabolic pathway in which cells use enzymes to oxidize nutrients, thereby releasing chemical energy which is used to produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP). In eukaryotes, this takes place inside mitochondria; it is the final and most productive stage of cellular respiration.

Mechanism

Electrons carried by NADH and FADH2 (from glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and fatty acid oxidation) pass through the electron transport chain, a series of protein complexes (I–IV) in the inner mitochondrial membrane. As electrons flow to the terminal acceptor, oxygen, the complexes pump protons (H+) into the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical gradient — the mechanism proposed by Peter Mitchell in 1961 and called chemiosmosis. ATP synthase (Complex V) allows protons to flow back into the matrix, using the released energy to phosphorylate ADP to ATP; its rotating structure was elucidated by Paul Boyer and John Walker (Nobel Prize 1997, shared with Jens Skou).

Yield and importance

Oxidative phosphorylation generates the great majority of ATP in aerobic organisms — commonly estimated at about 26–28 of the ~30–32 ATP produced per glucose molecule fully oxidized. Because the pathway depends on oxygen, it distinguishes aerobic from anaerobic metabolism. Uncouplers such as dinitrophenol dissipate the proton gradient as heat, and inhibitors at each complex (e.g. cyanide at Complex IV, oligomycin at ATP synthase) are classic poisons; brown adipose tissue exploits uncoupling protein 1 for thermogenesis.
