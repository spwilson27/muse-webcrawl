A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that nothing — not even light or other electromagnetic waves — can escape. The boundary of the region from which no escape is possible is called the event horizon. The theory of general relativity predicts that a sufficiently compact mass can deform spacetime to form a black hole.

History

The idea of a body so massive that even light cannot escape was proposed by John Michell in 1783. Karl Schwarzschild found the first exact solution of Einstein's field equations in 1916, and work by Oppenheimer, Chandrasekhar and others established that massive stars can collapse into such objects. The term "black hole" was popularised by John Archibald Wheeler in the 1960s.

Properties

Under the no-hair theorem, a stationary black hole is completely characterised by its mass, electric charge, and angular momentum. At the centre lies a gravitational singularity, where general relativity breaks down. Rotating (Kerr) black holes possess an ergosphere from which energy can in principle be extracted. Stephen Hawking showed in 1974 that black holes emit thermal radiation — Hawking radiation — and therefore slowly evaporate.

Observation

Black holes are detected through their interaction with surroundings: accretion disks, relativistic jets, stellar orbits, and gravitational waves from mergers, first detected by LIGO in 2015. Supermassive black holes of millions to billions of solar masses reside in most large galaxies, including Sagittarius A* at the centre of the Milky Way. In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope released the first direct image of a black hole's shadow, in galaxy M87, followed in 2022 by an image of Sagittarius A*.
