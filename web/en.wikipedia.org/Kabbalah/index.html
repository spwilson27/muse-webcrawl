Kabbalah

Kabbalah (Hebrew: קַבָּלָה, "tradition/received")
Central text: The Zohar (13th c.)
Key concepts: Ein Sof; the ten sefirot; tikkun
Major schools: Lurianic; Hasidic

Kabbalah is an esoteric method, discipline, and school of thought in Judaism. It forms the foundation of mystical religious interpretations within Judaism, seeking to explain the relationship between the unchanging, infinite God — the Ein Sof ("without end") — and the mortal, finite universe, which Kabbalists describe as emanating from God through ten creative attributes called the sefirot.

History

Jewish esotericism has roots in ancient Merkabah (chariot) mysticism and the Heichalot literature. Kabbalah as a distinct system emerged in 12th- and 13th-century Provence and Spain, where the Bahir appeared and where Moses de León published the Zohar (c. 1280), attributed pseudonymously to the 2nd-century sage Shimon bar Yochai. After the expulsion of the Jews from Spain in 1492, the Galilean town of Safed became a center of Kabbalistic creativity; there Isaac Luria (the Ari) taught a new cosmology of divine contraction (tzimtzum), the shattering of the vessels, and the repair of the world (tikkun) through observance of the commandments.

Influence

Lurianic Kabbalah profoundly shaped early modern Judaism and was popularized among the masses by 18th-century Hasidism. It also influenced Christian Cabala and Hermetic Qabalah in the West. In the modern era, Kabbalah has been the subject of academic study, notably by Gershom Scholem, and of widespread popular interest, sometimes detached from its traditional Jewish framework — a development many rabbis criticize, since classical Kabbalah presupposes full observance of Jewish law.
