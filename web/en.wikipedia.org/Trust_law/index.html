Trust law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Trust law governs the legal arrangement known as a trust, in which one party (the settlor, or grantor) transfers property to another party (the trustee) to hold and manage for the benefit of a third party (the beneficiary). The trust is a distinctive creation of English equity: legal title to the property vests in the trustee, while the equitable (beneficial) interest belongs to the beneficiary. Trusts are now used throughout the common law world and in adapted forms in many civil law jurisdictions.

Elements and creation

A valid private trust generally requires certainty of intention, subject matter, and objects (the "three certainties" of Knight v Knight). A trust may be created by declaration during the settlor's lifetime (inter vivos), by will (testamentary trust), or by operation of law. Resulting and constructive trusts arise by implication of law rather than express intention. Charitable trusts, which benefit purposes rather than ascertainable people, are enforced by public officials such as the Attorney General and enjoy various privileges, including exemption from some rules against perpetuities.

Duties of trustees

Trustees owe strict fiduciary duties: to act solely in the beneficiaries' interests, to avoid conflicts of interest and unauthorised profits, to exercise care and skill in managing and investing trust property, and to account for their administration. Beneficiaries may sue for breach of trust, trace trust property into the hands of third parties, and seek remedies such as compensation or disgorgement of profits. Modern statutes (such as the Uniform Trust Code in the United States and the Trustee Act 2000 in England and Wales) supplement the case law.

Uses

Trusts serve many purposes: estate planning and wills (including trusts for minors and "spendthrift" trusts), asset protection, charitable giving, pension funds, unit trusts and other collective investments, and commercial structures such as securitisations. Offshore jurisdictions have developed distinctive trust regimes, fuelling debate over tax avoidance and secrecy.
