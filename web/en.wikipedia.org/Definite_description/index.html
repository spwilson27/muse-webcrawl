Definite description

Bertrand Russell's theory of definite descriptions (1905) is a landmark of analytic philosophy of language.

A definite description is a denoting phrase of the form "the X" (in some languages also "that X" or a proper name, depending on the analysis), where X is a noun phrase at least in apparent form. Definite descriptions are typically analyzed by analogy with quantified expressions: "the present King of France" purports to pick out a unique individual satisfying a condition, unlike an indefinite description such as "a man." The logical analysis of such phrases has been a central problem in philosophy of language since the early 20th century.

Russell's theory

In his 1905 paper "On Denoting," Bertrand Russell proposed that definite descriptions are not referring expressions at all but "incomplete symbols" that disappear under analysis. "The present King of France is bald" is analyzed as a conjunction: there is something that is a present King of France, nothing else is, and that thing is bald. Because France has no king, the sentence comes out false rather than meaningless — dissolving puzzles about non-existent objects and negative existentials (such as "the golden mountain does not exist") without positing Meinongian objects.

Strawson's critique and later debate

P. F. Strawson, in "On Referring" (1950), argued that Russell mistook presupposition for assertion: a speaker uttering "the King of France is bald" does not assert that a unique king exists but presupposes it, so when the presupposition fails the utterance is neither true nor false. Keith Donnellan later distinguished referential from attributive uses of descriptions, and Saul Kripke argued in Naming and Necessity (1972) that proper names are rigid designators rather than disguised descriptions. Russell's analysis nevertheless remains the canonical starting point for the semantics of noun phrases.
