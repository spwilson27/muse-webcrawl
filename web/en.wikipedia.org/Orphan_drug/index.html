Orphan drug

An orphan drug is a pharmaceutical agent developed to treat a rare medical condition — an "orphan disease" — that affects so few people that industry would not recover development costs from sales without special incentives. Governments therefore grant orphan drug status benefits such as market exclusivity, tax credits, fee waivers and research assistance.

Legislation

The United States Orphan Drug Act of 1983 defines a rare disease as one affecting fewer than 200,000 people in the US, and grants seven years of market exclusivity plus tax credits for clinical testing. The European Union's Regulation (EC) No 141/2000 applies to conditions affecting no more than 5 in 10,000 people, granting ten years of market exclusivity. Japan and Australia operate similar schemes.

Impact

Before 1983 only a handful of drugs for rare diseases reached the US market; since then hundreds of orphan products have been approved, transforming treatment for conditions such as cystic fibrosis, Gaucher disease and certain rare cancers.

Criticism

Orphan drugs are frequently among the most expensive medicines in the world, with some costing hundreds of thousands of dollars per patient per year. Critics argue that exclusivity and pricing rules are sometimes exploited — for example by "salami slicing" common diseases into rare sub-populations — while defenders note the genuine cost and risk of developing treatments for tiny patient groups.
