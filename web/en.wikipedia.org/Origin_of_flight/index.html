Origin of flight

The origin of flight in birds concerns the evolutionary transition by which small feathered theropod dinosaurs became capable of powered flapping flight. Powered flight has evolved at least four times in animals — in insects, pterosaurs, birds and bats — but the avian case is uniquely illuminated by a rich fossil record from the Jurassic and Cretaceous of China and elsewhere.

Theories

Two classic hypotheses frame the debate. The arboreal ("trees-down") theory holds that ancestors of birds climbed trees and glided down, with feathers first serving gliding before flapping evolved. The cursorial ("ground-up") theory proposes that running theropods used feathered forelimbs to generate lift or stability while chasing prey or fleeing predators. A related model, wing-assisted incline running (WAIR), observed in modern chukar partridges, shows flapping can help birds ascend steep slopes, offering a functional intermediate stage. The pouncing-proavis model suggests leaping attacks from elevated perches.

Fossil evidence

Archaeopteryx (c. 150 million years ago) combines dinosaurian teeth, claws and a long bony tail with asymmetrical flight feathers. Feathered dinosaurs such as Microraptor, which bore long feathers on both arms and legs, and Anchiornis demonstrate that extensive plumage preceded flight. Feathers likely originated for insulation or display and were exapted for aerodynamics.
