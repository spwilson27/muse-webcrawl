Mudéjar

Mudéjar (from Arabic mudajjan, "tamed" or "domesticated") refers to the Muslims who remained in the Iberian Peninsula after the Christian Reconquista, living under Christian rule without converting to Christianity. The term is also — and today most commonly — applied to the distinctive artistic and architectural style, flourishing from the twelfth to the seventeenth century, that blended Islamic techniques and ornament with Christian forms.

Mudéjar art and architecture

Mudéjar architecture is characterised by extensive use of brick, horseshoe and multifoil arches, intricate geometric tilework (azulejos), carved stucco (yesería), and ornate wooden ceilings (artesonado). Christian kings and nobles commissioned Mudéjar craftsmen for palaces and churches, producing celebrated monuments such as the Alcázar of Seville, the synagogues of Toledo, and the towers of Teruel — the Mudéjar architecture of Aragon is inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

History of the Mudéjar population

Mudéjar communities lived under legal arrangements that initially tolerated their religion, law and customs in return for taxes. Conditions worsened over time; forced conversions in Castile (1502) and Aragon (1526) turned the Mudéjars into nominal Christians known as Moriscos, who were finally expelled from Spain between 1609 and 1614.
