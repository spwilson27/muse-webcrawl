Post-glacial rebound

Post-glacial rebound (also called isostatic rebound or crustal rebound) is the rise of land masses after the removal of the immense weight of ice sheets during the last glacial period. During glaciation, ice sheets kilometres thick depressed the Earth's crust; as they melted, the viscoelastic mantle began flowing back, slowly raising the land — a process still continuing thousands of years later.

Effects

Rebound is fastest where the ice was thickest. Parts of Canada around Hudson Bay and the Gulf of Bothnia in Scandinavia are rising at rates approaching 1 cm per year, and total uplift since deglaciation exceeds 300 metres in places, still recorded in raised beaches and tilting lake shorelines. Conversely, peripheral "forebulge" regions such as the U.S. mid-Atlantic coast are subsiding as the bulge collapses, compounding sea-level rise there, while global melting of ice raises the ocean — the interplay of local uplift and eustatic change determines relative sea level at any coast.

Measurement and significance

Rebound is measured by tide gauges, precise levelling, GPS, and the GRACE gravity satellites. Its rate and pattern constrain the viscosity of the Earth's mantle and the thickness of past ice sheets, and corrections for "glacial isostatic adjustment" are essential to interpreting satellite measurements of modern ice-sheet mass balance.
