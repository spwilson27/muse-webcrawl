Abel–Ruffini theorem

Field: Algebra

Stated by: Paolo Ruffini (1799), Niels Henrik Abel (1824)

The Abel–Ruffini theorem (also known as Abel's impossibility theorem) states that there is no solution in radicals to general polynomial equations of degree five or higher with arbitrary coefficients. In other words, unlike quadratic, cubic and quartic equations, the general quintic cannot be solved by a formula built only from the coefficients using arithmetic operations and root extractions.

History

Paolo Ruffini published the first attempted proof in 1799, though his argument contained a gap and was received sceptically. Niels Henrik Abel supplied a complete proof in 1824, publishing it at his own expense. Évariste Galois later developed group theory to give a precise criterion: a specific polynomial is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is a solvable group.

Interpretation

The theorem does not say that fifth-degree equations have no solutions — every polynomial has roots in the complex numbers by the fundamental theorem of algebra — nor that particular quintics cannot be solved in radicals; many special quintics can. It rules out only a universal radical formula for the general equation of degree five or more. Quintics can still be solved numerically, or in closed form using more advanced functions such as Bring radicals or elliptic functions.
