The history of anatomy traces the study of the structure of the human body from ancient Egyptian and Greek medicine to the imaging-based anatomy of today. Its progress repeatedly depended on whether societies permitted human dissection — the one reliable route to first-hand knowledge.

Antiquity

Egyptian medical papyri show practical anatomical knowledge from surgery and embalming. In Hellenistic Alexandria, Herophilus and Erasistratus performed the ancient world's only systematic human dissections (c. 300 BCE). The Roman-era physician Galen, barred from human bodies, extrapolated from apes and pigs; his authoritative writings nonetheless contained long-lived errors, such as a porous wall between the heart's ventricles.

Renaissance revolution

Public dissections returned in Italian universities; Mondino de' Liuzzi's Anathomia (1316) was the first modern dissection manual. Leonardo da Vinci produced unsurpassed anatomical drawings, unpublished in his lifetime. Andreas Vesalius's De humani corporis fabrica (1543), based on his own dissections and illustrated with superb woodcuts, corrected Galen on point after point and refounded anatomy as an observational science. Successors — Falloppio, Eustachi — mapped ever finer structures, and William Harvey demonstrated the circulation of the blood in 1628.

Modern anatomy

The microscope added tissues (Bichat) and cells; the 19th century professionalised dissection teaching, notoriously outrunning the legal supply of cadavers — the Burke and Hare murders prompted Britain's Anatomy Act 1832. Henry Gray's Anatomy (1858) became the standard text. In the 20th century X-rays, CT, MRI and endoscopy let clinicians see anatomy in the living body, while digital atlases such as the Visible Human Project (1994) rebuilt it in three dimensions.
