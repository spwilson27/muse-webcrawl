Mihna

The Mihna (Arabic: محنة, "ordeal" or "inquisition") was a period of religious persecution instituted by the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun in 833 CE, in which religious scholars were interrogated and punished for refusing to profess the doctrine of the createdness of the Quran. The policy continued under al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq and was abandoned by al-Mutawakkil around 848–851.

Background

Al-Ma'mun, influenced by the rationalist Mu'tazilite school of theology, championed the doctrine that the Quran was created by God rather than co-eternal with Him. Asserting caliphal authority over religious doctrine, in 833 he ordered judges, witnesses, and scholars across the empire to be tested (mihna) on the question; those who refused were dismissed, imprisoned, or flogged.

Ahmad ibn Hanbal

The most celebrated victim was Ahmad ibn Hanbal, founder of the Hanbali school of law, who refused to affirm the createdness of the Quran under interrogation and flogging during the reign of al-Mu'tasim. His steadfastness made him a popular hero and established the principle that religious authority rested with the scholarly community (ulama) rather than the caliph.

End and significance

Al-Mutawakkil reversed the policy between 848 and 851, releasing prisoners and restoring traditionalist scholars. The failure of the Mihna is generally seen as a turning point: no caliph again attempted to dictate doctrine, and Sunni Islam developed as a scholarly-led tradition in which the caliph's role was political rather than theological. The episode is often compared to a state-run inquisition and remains a reference point in debates about state control of religion in Islamic history.
