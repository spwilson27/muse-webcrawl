Visual attention

Visual attention is the set of cognitive mechanisms by which the visual system selectively processes some parts of the visual field at the expense of others. Because the brain cannot fully process all incoming visual information, attention acts as a filter and spotlight, prioritizing stimuli that are salient or relevant to current goals.

Types and mechanisms

Attention may be overt, accompanied by eye movements (saccades) to the attended location, or covert, shifted without moving the eyes. It can be driven bottom-up by stimulus salience (bright colours, sudden motion) or top-down by task goals, as in visual search for a target among distractors. Theories range from early-selection models (Broadbent) to late-selection and feature-integration theory (Treisman), which proposes that focused attention binds separate features into coherent objects.

Phenomena and neural basis

Classic findings include the attentional blink, inattentional blindness (failing to notice an unexpected stimulus when attention is engaged elsewhere), and change blindness. Neuroimaging and electrophysiology implicate a frontoparietal network — including the frontal eye fields, intraparietal sulcus, and superior colliculus — in orienting and sustaining visual attention, with the pulvinar and visual cortex modulating processing of attended stimuli.
