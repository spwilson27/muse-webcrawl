G. K. Chesterton

Gilbert Keith Chesterton (1874–1936)

Gilbert Keith Chesterton (29 May 1874 – 14 June 1936), known as G. K. Chesterton, was an English writer, philosopher, Christian apologist, and literary and art critic. He has been referred to as the "prince of paradox" for his habit of making serious arguments through wit, inversion, and apparent contradiction.

Life and career

Chesterton was born in Kensington, London, and educated at St Paul's School and the Slade School of Art. He worked first as an art critic and journalist before turning to full-time writing. A large and convivial man, he was famous for his public debates with his friend George Bernard Shaw, with whom he conducted a celebrated series of friendly arguments. With Hilaire Belloc he promoted the economic philosophy of distributism. Chesterton was received into the Roman Catholic Church in 1922.

Works

Chesterton wrote around 80 books, several hundred poems, some 200 short stories, 4,000 essays, and several plays. His best-known fiction is the series of detective stories featuring the priest-detective Father Brown, beginning with The Innocence of Father Brown (1911). His novels include The Napoleon of Notting Hill (1904) and The Man Who Was Thursday (1908).

His non-fiction includes Orthodoxy (1908), a personal account of his journey to Christian belief; The Everlasting Man (1925), a survey of human history written partly in response to H. G. Wells's Outline of History; and acclaimed literary studies of Charles Dickens, Robert Browning, and St Thomas Aquinas. The idea known as "Chesterton's fence" — that reforms should not be made until the reasoning behind the existing state of affairs is understood — comes from his 1929 book The Thing.
