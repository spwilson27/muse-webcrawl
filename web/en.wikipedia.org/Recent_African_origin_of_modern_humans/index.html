Recent African origin of modern humans

The recent African origin of modern humans, also known as the Out of Africa II hypothesis, is the dominant scientific model for the origin and early dispersal of anatomically modern humans (Homo sapiens). It proposes that modern humans evolved in Africa and then spread across the world beginning roughly 60,000–70,000 years ago, largely replacing earlier archaic human populations, though with limited interbreeding.

Origins in Africa

Fossils from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, dated to about 300,000 years ago, together with remains from Omo-Kibish in Ethiopia (about 233,000 years) and Herto (about 160,000 years), show that early Homo sapiens were present across Africa hundreds of thousands of years before the main dispersal. Genetic studies consistently find the greatest diversity among African populations, indicating a longer history there, and trace the ancestry of all living humans to African populations of this era.

The dispersal

Some early excursions out of Africa, such as the Skhul and Qafzeh hominins in the Levant (around 90,000–120,000 years ago), appear to have left little genetic legacy. The successful expansion that populated the rest of the world is generally dated to about 50,000–70,000 years ago, likely via a southern route across the Bab el-Mandeb strait into Arabia, then along the coasts of South and Southeast Asia to Sahul (Australia and New Guinea) by around 50,000–65,000 years ago, and into Europe by about 45,000 years ago.

Interbreeding with archaic humans

Ancient DNA has shown that the expansion was not a total replacement. Non-African genomes carry roughly 1–4% Neanderthal ancestry from interbreeding in the Middle East about 50,000–60,000 years ago, and populations in Oceania carry additional Denisovan ancestry of up to about 5%. Small amounts of archaic ancestry are also found within Africa.

Evidence

The model is supported by converging lines of evidence: the fossil record; the pattern of decreasing genetic diversity with distance from Africa (the serial founder effect); mitochondrial DNA and Y-chromosome phylogenies tracing to African ancestors (sometimes popularised as "Mitochondrial Eve" and "Y-chromosomal Adam"); and ancient DNA from early modern humans such as the Ust'-Ishim man of Siberia.

Comparison with other models

The hypothesis superseded the multiregional origin model, which proposed regional continuity from Homo erectus populations across the Old World. The current consensus is sometimes described as "mostly out of Africa" or "leaky replacement", acknowledging the small but real archaic contribution.
