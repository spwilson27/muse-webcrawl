Subjunctive mood

The subjunctive (also known as the conjunctive in some languages) is a grammatical mood, a feature of an utterance that indicates the speaker's attitude toward it. Subjunctive forms of verbs are typically used to express various states of unreality such as wish, emotion, possibility, judgment, opinion, obligation, or action that has not yet occurred; the precise situations in which they are used vary from language to language. The subjunctive is one of the irrealis moods, which refer to what is not necessarily real.

It is often contrasted with the indicative, a realis mood used to state factual information. Examples of the English subjunctive include "I suggest that he be present" (mandative subjunctive) and "If I were you" (irrealis were).

"If I were a rich man" — the irrealis subjunctive

In English

English has two main uses of the subjunctive. The mandative subjunctive uses the plain form of the verb in subordinate clauses after verbs and adjectives of demand, recommendation and necessity: "They insisted that she leave at once." It is more common in American than British English, where "should" constructions often compete ("that she should leave"). The second use is the irrealis form were for all persons in counterfactual conditions and wishes: "If I were rich…", "I wish it were Friday." Fixed formulas such as "God save the Queen", "be that as it may" and "come what may" preserve older subjunctives.

In other languages

The subjunctive is far richer in inflected languages. Latin used the subjunctive extensively in subordinate clauses and independent clauses expressing wishes and commands. Romance languages retain a full subjunctive paradigm: in Spanish and Portuguese it is required after verbs of emotion, doubt and desire and in many adverbial clauses, with present, imperfect and (in Portuguese) future subjunctive tenses. German has two subjunctive forms, Konjunktiv I (used chiefly for reported speech) and Konjunktiv II (used for counterfactuals and polite requests). French uses the subjunctive after expressions of volition, emotion and doubt, though its spoken use is narrowing.

Semantic range

Across languages, subjunctive morphology is recruited for wishes (optative-like uses), hypothetical and counterfactual conditions, indirect commands, polite requests, purpose clauses, and clauses governed by verbs of fearing, doubting and evaluating — a family of meanings unified by non-assertion: the speaker presents the situation without committing to its truth.
