Irreducible element

DefinitionNonzero non-unit that cannot be factored into two non-units

In abstract algebra, an irreducible element of an integral domain is a nonzero element that is not a unit and that cannot be written as a product of two non-unit elements. Irreducible elements are the "atoms" of factorization theory: in well-behaved rings every element factors into irreducibles, though the factorization need not be unique.

Definition

Formally, an element p of a domain R is irreducible if p is not zero, p is not a unit, and whenever p = ab for elements a, b of R, then either a or b is a unit. In the integers this recovers the notion of a prime number (apart from sign: both 5 and −5 are irreducible in ℤ).

Irreducible versus prime

Every prime element is irreducible in an integral domain, but the converse is false in general. The standard counterexample is the ring ℤ[√−5], where 6 factors both as 2 · 3 and as (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5); the factors are irreducible yet not prime, since for instance 2 divides the product (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5) without dividing either factor. In a unique factorization domain (UFD), and in particular in every principal ideal domain, the two notions coincide.

Examples

In polynomial rings over a field, irreducible elements are exactly the irreducible polynomials: linear factors over an algebraically closed field, but over ℝ the irreducible quadratics such as x² + 1 also occur. In ℤ[i], the Gaussian integers, the rational prime 5 is not irreducible since 5 = (2 + i)(2 − i), whereas 3 remains irreducible.

Factorization theory

Rings in which every nonzero non-unit factors into irreducibles are called atomic; ascending chain conditions on principal ideals guarantee this. Failure of uniqueness of factorization — measured classically by the class group in rings of algebraic integers — motivated Kummer's and Dedekind's development of ideal theory in the 19th century.
