Quantum Zeno effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The quantum Zeno effect (also known as the Turing paradox) is a feature of quantum mechanics in which the evolution of an unstable particle can be slowed or halted by measuring it frequently enough. The name, coined by E. C. G. Sudarshan and Baidyanath Misra in 1977, alludes to Zeno's arrow paradox: a watched arrow, observed at every instant, never moves.

Explanation

For short times after preparation, the survival probability of an unstable state deviates from exponential decay and behaves quadratically, P(t) ≈ 1 − (ΔH t/ħ)². If the system is measured at intervals shorter than the so-called Zeno time, each measurement projects it back onto the initial state, and the effective decay rate is suppressed. In the limit of continuous observation, the system never decays.

Anti-Zeno effect

Under other conditions, frequent measurement can instead accelerate decay; this is the quantum anti-Zeno effect, which depends on the spectral density of the environment at the measurement-induced transition frequencies.

Experiments and applications

The effect was demonstrated experimentally by Itano and co-workers in 1990 using radio-frequency transitions in trapped beryllium ions. Zeno dynamics are now used in quantum computing to protect quantum information, suppress decoherence, and implement gates by confining evolution to subspaces of the Hilbert space.
