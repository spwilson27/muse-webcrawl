Stern–Gerlach experiment

The Stern–Gerlach experiment demonstrated that the spatial orientation of angular momentum is quantized, and thus showed that atomic-scale systems have intrinsically quantum properties. Performed by Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach in Frankfurt in 1922, it passed a beam of silver atoms through an inhomogeneous magnetic field and observed that the beam split into two discrete components rather than spreading continuously — direct evidence of the quantization of angular momentum.

Background

Classical physics predicted that particles with magnetic moments passing through an inhomogeneous magnetic field should be deflected by varying amounts depending on their orientation, producing a continuous smear. The Bohr–Sommerfeld model of the atom suggested instead that only certain orientations ("space quantization") are allowed.

The experiment

Stern and Gerlach evaporated silver in an oven, collimated the atoms into a beam, and sent it through a carefully shaped magnet producing a strongly non-uniform field, collecting the atoms on a glass plate. The deposit formed two distinct bands rather than a broadened spot. Originally interpreted as proof of Bohr–Sommerfeld space quantization, the result is now understood as a measurement of electron spin: the unpaired electron in silver's ground state has spin up or spin down, each of which is deflected oppositely.

Importance

The experiment is fundamental to quantum mechanics and remains the archetypal example of a quantum measurement: the apparatus forces a system into one of a discrete set of eigenstates. Repeated Stern–Gerlach measurements in sequence illustrate the uncertainty principle and the collapse of the wavefunction. Stern later received the 1943 Nobel Prize in Physics in part for this work. Variants of the technique are used in atomic clocks, atomic beams, and modern quantum information experiments.
