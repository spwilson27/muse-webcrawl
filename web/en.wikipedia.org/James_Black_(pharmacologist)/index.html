James Black (pharmacologist)

Sir James Whyte Black (14 June 1924 – 22 March 2010) was a Scottish pharmacologist who invented two classes of blockbuster drugs: the beta blockers (propranolol) for heart disease and the H2-receptor antagonists (cimetidine) for stomach ulcers. He shared the 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Gertrude Elion and George Hitchings.

Career

Born in Uddingston, Lanarkshire, Black studied medicine at the University of St Andrews. After academic posts in Malaya and Glasgow, he joined ICI Pharmaceuticals in 1958, where he reasoned that blocking adrenaline's effects on the heart's beta receptors would reduce its oxygen demand in angina. The result, propranolol (1964), revolutionised cardiology and helped establish the rational, receptor-based approach to drug design.

Moving to Smith, Kline & French in 1964, Black applied the same logic to histamine: identifying the H2 receptor and developing cimetidine (Tagamet), launched in 1976, the first drug to treat peptic ulcers by suppressing acid — for years the world's best-selling prescription medicine.

Later life

Black held chairs at University College London and King's College London, founded the James Black Foundation, and served as chancellor of the University of Dundee (1992–2006). He was knighted in 1981 and appointed to the Order of Merit in 2000.
