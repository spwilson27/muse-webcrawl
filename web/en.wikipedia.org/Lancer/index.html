A lancer was a type of cavalryman who fought with a lance. Lances were employed by mounted troops in Assyria as early as 700 BC and subsequently by Greek, Persian, Gallic, Han Chinese, nomadic, and Roman horsemen. The weapon became the foremost tool of shock cavalry in Europe and Asia after the development of the stirrup and the framed saddle allowed riders to couch the lance and deliver the full momentum of horse and rider at the point of impact.

History

In medieval Europe the couched lance was the signature weapon of the knight, and massed lance charges decided battles for centuries before pike infantry and firearms eroded their dominance. Lancer units nonetheless enjoyed a major revival during the Napoleonic Wars: Polish lancers in French service demonstrated the weapon's reach and terror effect, and most European armies — Britain, France, Prussia, Austria, and Russia — raised lancer regiments, often called uhlans in German and Austrian service. Lancers typically also carried sabres and, later, carbines or pistols.

Decline

Lancers remained on the battlefield through the 19th century and into the First World War, but machine guns, quick-firing artillery, and barbed wire ended the era of the mounted charge. The last great lance charges took place in the opening campaigns of 1914 and in colonial warfare; most lancer regiments were converted to mechanised or armoured roles in the interwar period. Several British and Indian lancer regiments survive today as armoured units retaining the historic title.
