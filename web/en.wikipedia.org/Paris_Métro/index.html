Paris Métro

The Paris Métro (French: Métro de Paris [metʁo də paʁi]), short for Chemin de fer métropolitain, is a rapid transit system serving the city of Paris and its metropolitan area in France. It is one of the densest metro systems in the world, with 16 lines, over 300 stations, and roughly 225 kilometres of track, the great majority within the city proper — few places in central Paris are more than 500 metres from a station. Opened in 1900 for the World's Fair, it became a symbol of the city, known for its uniform architecture and its Art Nouveau entrances designed by Hector Guimard.

History

After decades of dispute between the city and the state over whether the network should serve Paris itself or link suburban railway termini, the city prevailed, and the first line — Porte Maillot to Porte de Vincennes — opened on 19 July 1900 during the Exposition Universelle, engineered under Fulgence Bienvenüe, the "father of the Métro." Most of the network was complete by the 1920s. After the Second World War, several lines switched to rubber-tyred trains running on concrete pads. Line 14, fully automated, opened in 1998, and Line 1 was converted to driverless operation in 2011–2012. The Grand Paris Express project, under construction since the 2010s, is adding four largely automated lines (15–18) and some 200 km of track, mostly in the suburbs.

Network and operation

Lines are identified by numbers 1 to 14 plus two minor lines, 3bis and 7bis. Most lines run on the right on standard-gauge track with steel wheels; five lines use rubber tyres. Trains are relatively short and stations closely spaced (averaging about 550 metres apart). Service runs from about 05:30 to after midnight (later on weekends). Fares are integrated with the wider Île-de-France network via the Navigo card and contactless payment.

Architecture and culture

Guimard's sinuous green cast-iron "Édicules" and enamelled "Métropolitain" signs became icons of Art Nouveau; a small number survive, protected as historic monuments. Underground stations are vaulted and tiled in white bevelled ceramic to maximise light. The Métro is deeply embedded in Parisian culture — from its ghost stations and the famous "ticket poinçonneur" to films such as Le Samouraï and Amélie — and its name gave French the generic word métro, adopted for subways worldwide.
