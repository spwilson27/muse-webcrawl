Chekhov's gun

Chekhov's gun (Russian: Чеховское ружьё) is a narrative principle attributed to the Russian playwright and author Anton Chekhov, according to which every element in a story must be necessary, and irrelevant elements should be removed. In its most famous formulation: if a rifle is hung on the wall in the first act, it must go off in the second or third act; if it is not going to be fired, it should not be hanging there.

Origin

Chekhov expressed the idea in several letters and recorded conversations with slight variations. In a letter of 1 November 1889 he wrote that one must not put a loaded rifle on the stage if no one is thinking of firing it. The playwright's own plays observe the principle with some subtlety: in The Cherry Orchard, for instance, the guns shown are fired only offstage or ineffectually, which critics have read both as obedience to and subversion of the rule.

Interpretations

The principle is often paired with the related concept of foreshadowing and contrasted with the red herring — a detail deliberately introduced to mislead, which serves a function of its own. Ernest Hemingway criticised a narrow reading of the rule, arguing that some unimportant details (as in his story "Big Two-Hearted River") contribute texture and verisimilitude. In modern usage the term is invoked in criticism of film, fiction and game design whenever an introduced object, ability or fact acquires a delayed payoff — or conspicuously fails to.
