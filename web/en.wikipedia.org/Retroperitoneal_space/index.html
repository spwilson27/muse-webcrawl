Retroperitoneal space

The retroperitoneal space is the anatomical region in the abdominal cavity behind the parietal peritoneum and in front of the transversalis fascia and posterior abdominal wall. It extends from the diaphragm to the pelvis and contains major vessels, kidneys, glands, parts of the alimentary tract, nerves, lymphatics, and fat.

Boundaries
Anterior: posterior parietal peritoneum
Posterior: transversalis fascia and abdominal wall
Contents: kidneys, adrenal glands, aorta, inferior vena cava, pancreas and other structures

Subdivisions

Fascial planes divide the retroperitoneum into the anterior pararenal space, perirenal space, and posterior pararenal space. The anterior pararenal space contains much of the pancreas, the retroperitoneal portions of the duodenum, and the ascending and descending colon. Each perirenal space surrounds a kidney, adrenal gland, renal vessels, and fat. The posterior pararenal space consists mainly of fat and provides a route by which fluid collections may spread.

Primarily and secondarily retroperitoneal structures

Kidneys, ureters, adrenal glands, aorta, inferior vena cava, and several nerves develop and remain behind the peritoneum. Other organs, including most of the duodenum and pancreas and parts of the colon, begin development within the peritoneal cavity but become secondarily retroperitoneal as their mesenteries fuse to the posterior wall.

Clinical significance

Because the space is deep and compliant, retroperitoneal haemorrhage may become large before producing obvious abdominal signs. Infection, pancreatic fluid, urine leaks, tumours, fibrosis, and aortic aneurysms can involve the compartment. Computed tomography is central to diagnosis, and fascial anatomy helps clinicians predict how disease spreads.
