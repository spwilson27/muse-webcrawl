François Maurice Adrien Marie Mitterrand (1916–1996) was a French statesman who served as President of France from 1981 to 1995, the longest-serving president of the Fifth Republic. A member of the Socialist Party, he was the first left-wing president of the Fifth Republic.

Early life and career

Mitterrand studied law and political science in Paris. During the Second World War he was taken prisoner by the Germans, escaped, and later joined the French Resistance. After the war he held numerous ministerial posts in the Fourth Republic, including Minister of the Interior and Minister of Justice, and became the leading figure of the non-communist left.

Presidency

Elected in 1981 and re-elected in 1988, Mitterrand's governments initially pursued an expansionary socialist programme with nationalisations, then turned to economic austerity in 1983. His presidency saw the abolition of the death penalty, decentralisation reforms, and the "Grands Projets" — monumental Parisian building works including the Louvre Pyramid and the Grande Arche de la Défense. Abroad, he was a driving force of European integration alongside German Chancellor Helmut Kohl, culminating in the Maastricht Treaty of 1992. He served two periods of "cohabitation" with right-wing governments.

Death and legacy

Mitterrand died of prostate cancer in January 1996, months after leaving office; he had concealed his illness for much of his presidency. His legacy remains debated in France, mixing praise for his European commitment and cultural projects with controversy over his wartime record and wiretapping scandals.
