Hanafi


Study of Hanafi fiqh

The Hanafi school (Arabic: الحنفية, romanized: al-Ḥanafiyya) is one of the four major schools of Islamic jurisprudence within Sunni Islam. It developed from the legal tradition of Kufa in Iraq and is named after its eponym, the eighth-century scholar Abu Hanifa (699–767). With about a third of the world's Muslims adhering to it, the Hanafi school is the largest of the four madhhabs, predominant in Turkey, the Balkans, Egypt, the Levant, Central Asia, and South Asia.

Methodology

The Hanafi school is noted for its comparatively extensive use of reasoned legal opinion (ra'y) and analogy (qiyas), as well as juristic preference (istihsan), which allows departing from strict analogy where it would produce hardship or conflict with equity. Its hierarchy of sources begins with the Qur'an and Sunnah, followed by consensus (ijma), analogy, juristic preference, and local custom (urf). Abu Hanifa's students Abu Yusuf and Muhammad al-Shaybani systematised the school's doctrine; al-Shaybani's works form the corpus known as zahir al-riwaya.

History and spread

Adopted as the official school of the Abbasid caliphate after Abu Yusuf became chief judge, Hanafi law later became the dominant school of the Ottoman and Mughal empires, whose courts applied it through codifications such as the Ottoman Mecelle (1869–1876), one of the first attempts to codify Islamic civil law. Hanafi jurisprudence has historically been described as the most flexible of the schools toward local custom and the rights and practices of non-Muslim subjects under the dhimma system.
