A control moment gyroscope (CMG) is an attitude control device generally used in spacecraft attitude control systems. A CMG consists of a spinning rotor and one or more motorized gimbals that tilt the rotor's angular momentum vector. As the rotor tilts, the changing angular momentum causes a gyroscopic torque that rotates the spacecraft, allowing large torques to be applied with little electrical power and no propellant.

Principle and types

In a single-gimbal CMG, the rotor spins at constant speed inside a gimbal that rotates about one axis; tilting produces torque about an axis perpendicular to both. Double-gimbal CMGs offer more freedom at greater complexity. Spacecraft typically fly clusters of CMGs — often four in a pyramidal arrangement — whose gimbals are steered in coordination by a steering law that combines their torques while avoiding singular configurations in which the cluster cannot produce torque in some direction.

Applications

CMGs provided attitude control for Skylab, Mir, and the International Space Station, whose four large CMGs orient the station without expending propellant, with momentum periodically "desaturated" using thrusters or gravity-gradient torques. Their high torque-to-power ratio suits large spacecraft; smaller satellites more often use reaction wheels, which exchange momentum by changing rotor speed instead of tilt.

Singularities

The central technical challenge of CMG clusters is singularity management: certain gimbal angle combinations align the individual torque directions so that no net torque can be produced along one axis. Steering laws trade small torque errors or deliberate excursions for singularity avoidance or escape.
