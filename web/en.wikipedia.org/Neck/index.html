Neck

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The neck is the part of the body on many vertebrates that connects the head with the torso. It supports the weight of the head and protects the nerves and blood vessels that carry signals and blood between the brain and the rest of the body. In humans, the neck is defined by the cervical vertebrae (C1–C7), and contains the trachea, larynx, pharynx, oesophagus, thyroid gland, carotid arteries, and jugular veins.

Anatomy

The neck is divided by anatomists into anterior and posterior triangles bounded by the sternocleidomastoid muscle. Superficial structures include the platysma and external jugular vein; deeper lie the hyoid bone, thyroid and cricoid cartilages, cervical plexus, and the scalene muscles. The cervical spine provides mobility, permitting rotation, flexion and extension of the head.

Function

Beyond support and movement, the neck is a conduit: the airway and digestive tract pass through it, the carotid and vertebral arteries supply the brain, and the spinal cord and vagus and other cranial nerves traverse it. The thyroid and parathyroid glands sit in the front of the neck and regulate metabolism and calcium balance.

Clinical significance

Neck pain is a common complaint, arising from muscle strain, cervical disc disease, or whiplash injury. Neck masses may reflect lymphadenopathy, thyroid nodules, or tumours; meningism (neck stiffness) is a classic sign of meningitis. Emergent conditions include airway obstruction, cervical spine fracture, and penetrating trauma.
