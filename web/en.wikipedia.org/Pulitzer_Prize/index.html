The Pulitzer Prizes are a set of 23 annual awards administered by Columbia University in New York City for achievements in United States newspaper, magazine, and online journalism, literature, and musical composition. The prizes were established in 1917 by provisions in the will of Joseph Pulitzer, who had made his fortune as a newspaper publisher, and are awarded yearly in April.

History

Joseph Pulitzer, a Hungarian-American immigrant who built the St. Louis Post-Dispatch and the New York World into leading newspapers, left $2 million to Columbia University in his will, half of which funded the founding of the Columbia University Graduate School of Journalism and the establishment of the prizes. The first Pulitzer Prizes were awarded on June 4, 1917, for work done during 1916.

Over the decades the categories have evolved. The original awards covered reporting, editorial writing, history, biography, drama, and fiction. Poetry was added in 1922, music in 1943, and photography categories followed. In 2024 the prize board added a memoir and autobiography category, splitting it from biography.

Administration

The prizes are administered by Columbia University under the direction of the Pulitzer Prize Board, which consists of leading editors, publishers, writers, and academics. Each year, more than 100 jurors are selected to serve on juries in the various categories; juries nominate three finalists, from which the board selects the winners by majority vote. The board retains the right to make no award in a category.

Categories

As of 2024 there are 15 journalism categories, including Public Service, Breaking News Reporting, Investigative Reporting, Explanatory Reporting, Local Reporting, National Reporting, International Reporting, Feature Writing, Commentary, Criticism, Editorial Writing, Editorial Cartooning, Breaking News Photography, Feature Photography, and Audio Reporting. The letters, drama, and music categories include Fiction, Drama, History, Biography, Memoir or Autobiography, Poetry, General Nonfiction, and Music.

The Public Service prize, awarded to a news organization rather than an individual, is considered the most prestigious; its winner receives a gold medal. Other winners receive a certificate and a cash award of $15,000 (raised from $10,000 in 2017).

Notable winners

Repeat winners include Robert Frost, who won the poetry prize four times; Eugene O'Neill, who won the drama prize four times; and novelists such as William Faulkner and John Updike, each of whom won the fiction prize twice. Newspapers with long records of Public Service gold medals include The New York Times and The Washington Post.

Criticism

The prizes have been criticized for favoring large metropolitan newspapers and East Coast institutions, and some winning works have been accused of factual errors. The board revoked the 1981 feature-writing prize awarded to Janet Cooke of The Washington Post after her story was revealed to be fabricated. Despite such controversies, the Pulitzer remains the most prominent award in American journalism and letters.
