Self-coup

A self-coup, also called an autocoup (from the Spanish autogolpe) or coup from the top, is a form of coup d'état in which a nation's head of government, having come to power through legal means, tries to stay in power through illegal means. The leader may dissolve or render powerless the national legislature and unlawfully assume extraordinary powers not granted under normal circumstances. Other measures may include annulling the nation's constitution, suspending civil courts, and having the head of government assume dictatorial powers.

Historical examples

One of the earliest recorded examples is that of Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte, who as President of France staged the coup of December 1851 against the National Assembly and proclaimed himself Emperor Napoleon III the following year. In the 20th century, examples include the March on Rome aftermath consolidation by Mussolini and Adolf Hitler's consolidation of power after appointment as Chancellor of Germany, culminating in the Enabling Act of 1933.

Notable self-coups

  • Peru, 1992: President Alberto Fujimori, with military support, dissolved Congress and the judiciary in what became known as the autogolpe of 5 April 1992.
  • Russia, 1993: President Boris Yeltsin dissolved the parliament, leading to the 1993 constitutional crisis.
  • Venezuela, 2017: The Supreme Tribunal of Justice assumed the powers of the National Assembly, an act widely described as a self-coup.
  • Guatemala, 1993: President Jorge Serrano Elías attempted a self-coup (the "Serranazo"), dissolving Congress and the Supreme Court, but it failed and he was forced from office.

Attempted self-coups

Attempted self-coups include the 1993 Guatemalan case and various episodes in which executives attempted to extend their terms or override term limits through courts or referendums of dubious legality. Scholars distinguish self-coups from democratic backsliding or "constitutional coups" in which incumbents use legal mechanisms to entrench their power.
