Centralia mine fire

The Centralia mine fire is a coal-seam fire that has been burning under the borough of Centralia, Pennsylvania, United States, since at least May 27, 1962. Its original cause is still a matter of debate. It is burning in underground coal mines at depths of up to 300 feet (90 m) over an 8-mile (13 km) stretch of 3,700 acres (15 km²). At its current rate, it could continue to burn for over 250 years. The fire caused most of the town to be abandoned: the population dwindled from around 1,500 at the time the fire started to 5 residents in 2020, and many buildings have been demolished.

Ignition and early efforts

Centralia sits atop rich anthracite deposits in the Western Middle Coal Field. In May 1962 the borough council arranged to burn trash in a landfill located in an abandoned strip-mine pit; the fire apparently ignited an exposed coal seam or reached underground workings through an unsealed opening, despite a state inspector's earlier warning that the pit needed a fire-resistant barrier. Firefighters believed they had extinguished the blaze, but it smouldered on and spread through the labyrinth of abandoned mines beneath the town. Various early attempts — digging out the coal, flushing with water and crushed rock, and trenching — failed, in part because of cost and disputes over funding and responsibility.

Discovery and evacuation

For years the fire's effects were mostly limited to smoke and noxious fumes entering basements, but events in 1979–1981 brought national attention: the mayor, John Coddington, found his underground gasoline storage tanks heated to dangerous temperatures, and on February 14, 1981, 12-year-old Todd Domboski fell into a sinkhole that opened in his grandmother's yard, surviving by clinging to a tree root as carbon monoxide-laden steam rose around him. Studies found lethal levels of carbon monoxide and low oxygen in some homes. Congress eventually appropriated more than $42 million for relocation, and most residents accepted buyouts and left in the mid-1980s. The state invoked eminent domain over the remaining properties in 1992; a handful of residents fought eviction for decades and were ultimately allowed to remain for their lifetimes. Pennsylvania Route 61's southern section buckled from the heat and was closed in the 1990s, its abandoned stretch becoming the graffiti-covered "Graffiti Highway" before being covered with earth in 2020.

Centralia today

Today Centralia is essentially a ghost town: a few houses, a church, and cemeteries remain amid grid streets, with signs warning of subsidence and gases. Steam still vents from the ground in places. The borough has served as an inspiration for fictional depictions of deserted, hellish towns, including aspects of the Silent Hill franchise. The fire continues to burn and migrate, and full extinguishment is generally considered impractical.
