The mineral pyrite (/ˈpaɪraɪt/), or iron pyrite, also known as fool's gold, is an iron sulfide with the chemical formula FeS2 (iron (II) disulfide). Pyrite is the most abundant sulfide mineral.

Pyrite's metallic luster and pale brass-yellow hue give it a superficial resemblance to gold, which led to the well-known nickname "fool's gold". The color has also led to the nicknames brass, brazzle and Brazil, primarily used to refer to pyrite found in coal.

Etymology and history

The name pyrite is derived from the Greek πυρίτης (pyritēs), "of fire" or "in fire", in turn from πῦρ (pyr), "fire". In ancient Roman times this name was applied to several types of stone that would create sparks when struck against steel; Pliny the Elder described one of them as brassy, almost certainly a reference to what is now called pyrite. By the sixteenth century the term was applied specifically to iron pyrite.

Occurrence and uses

Pyrite is found in sedimentary, metamorphic and igneous rocks, often in well-formed cubic or pyritohedral crystals, and in hydrothermal veins alongside other sulfides. Historically it was mined as a source of sulfur for sulfuric acid, and it was used in early firearms in wheellock mechanisms, where it struck sparks to ignite powder. It is now mainly of interest to collectors and as a minor ore.

Weathering

Exposed to air and water, pyrite oxidises to iron oxides and sulfuric acid. This reaction is responsible for acid mine drainage, a major environmental problem at mine sites, and for "pyrite disease" (pyrite decay), in which fossil and mineral specimens crack and crumble in humid collections.
