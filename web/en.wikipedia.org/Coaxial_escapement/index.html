Coaxial escapement

The coaxial escapement is a type of escapement for mechanical watches invented in the 1970s by the English watchmaker George Daniels. It was the first fundamentally new escapement to enter commercial production since the lever escapement of the 18th century, and it became widely known after its adoption by the Swiss manufacturer Omega in 1999. The design places two wheels on the same axis (hence "coaxial") and separates the locking and impulse functions of the escapement onto different surfaces.

Design

In a conventional Swiss lever escapement, the escape wheel gives impulse directly to the pallet stones through sliding contact, which demands lubrication and causes wear as the lubricant degrades. In Daniels's arrangement, a compound escape wheel — two toothed wheels of different sizes mounted coaxially — works with a special three-stone pallet fork and a roller carrying an impulse jewel. One wheel performs locking while the other delivers impulse radially, so the impulse is given almost tangentially, more like the chronometer detent escapement, with minimal sliding friction. Daniels set out to make an escapement that would perform well over long service intervals with virtually no lubrication of the escapement itself.

Development and adoption

Daniels developed the escapement through the 1970s, patented it, and demonstrated it in his own handmade pocket watches. After approaching several Swiss manufacturers over many years, he found a partner in Omega, then revitalising its mechanical watchmaking under the Swatch Group; the first production calibre with the coaxial escapement, the Omega calibre 2500, appeared in the De Ville line in 1999. Early versions beat at reduced rates and underwent revisions; later in-house calibres such as the 8500 (2007) were designed around the escapement from the start, running at 25,200 vibrations per hour and paired with silicon balance springs and Master Chronometer certification.

Assessment

Horologists praised the escapement's theoretical advantages — stable rate over time and longer service intervals — while noting its greater complexity and manufacturing cost relative to the lever escapement. It remains essentially exclusive to Omega, and Daniels regarded its industrialisation as the vindication of his lifetime's work in independent watchmaking.
