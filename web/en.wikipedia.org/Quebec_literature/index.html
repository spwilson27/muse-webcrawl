Quebec literature

Quebec literature is the body of writing produced in French (and, to a lesser degree, in English and Indigenous languages) in the Canadian province of Quebec. It developed from the accounts of explorers and missionaries in New France, through the patriotic verse and the rural "novel of the land" of the 19th and early 20th centuries, to the urban, experimental and politically engaged writing that followed the Quiet Revolution of the 1960s.

Origins and the 19th century

Early texts include the Relations of the Jesuit missionaries and the travel accounts of Jacques Cartier and Samuel de Champlain. After the British conquest, French-Canadian writing turned to the preservation of language and faith. The first French-Canadian novel, L'Influence d'un livre (1837) by Philippe Aubert de Gaspé fils, and his father's memoir-novel Les Anciens Canadiens (1863), stand alongside the patriotic poetry of Louis-Honoré Fréchette. The dominant form of the early 1900s was the roman du terroir, exemplified by Louis Hémon's Maria Chapdelaine (1913).

Modern literature

The postwar decades brought psychological and urban fiction: Gabrielle Roy's Bonheur d'occasion (The Tin Flute, 1945) depicted working-class Montreal, and Anne Hébert and Marie-Claire Blais won international reputations. The Quiet Revolution produced the manifesto-like writing of Hubert Aquin and the theatre of Michel Tremblay, whose Les Belles-sœurs (1968) famously used working-class Montreal French (joual) on stage. Poetry is anchored by figures such as Émile Nelligan, Gaston Miron and Saint-Denys Garneau. Contemporary Quebec writing is notable for the "migrant literature" of authors such as Ying Chen and Dany Laferrière.
