Iridium satellite constellation

Manufacturer: Motorola (original); Thales Alenia Space (NEXT)

Operator: Iridium Communications

Orbit: Low Earth orbit, ~780 km

Satellites: 66 operational + spares

The Iridium satellite constellation provides L-band voice and data coverage to satellite phones, satellite messenger communication devices and integrated transceivers, as well as two-way satellite messaging service to supported devices across the entire surface of the Earth. The constellation consists of 66 active satellites in low Earth orbit, divided into six orbital planes of eleven satellites each, plus in-orbit spares.

History

The system was conceived in 1987 by engineers at Motorola and named after the element iridium because the original design called for 77 satellites, matching iridium's atomic number. Although the design was later reduced to 66 satellites, the name was kept. Service began in November 1998, but high handset and call costs led the original company into bankruptcy in 1999. The constellation was rescued by new investors in 2000 and relaunched as Iridium Satellite LLC, later Iridium Communications.

Iridium NEXT

Between 2017 and 2019, the company replaced the entire constellation with second-generation Iridium NEXT satellites, launched by SpaceX Falcon 9 rockets from Vandenberg Air Force Base. The NEXT satellites host additional payloads, including Aireon ADS-B aircraft tracking receivers and maritime AIS receivers. The network is notable for its inter-satellite crosslinks, which route traffic in space between satellites, and for producing bright "Iridium flares" from the original satellites' reflective antennas — a phenomenon that ended as the first-generation satellites were de-orbited.

2009 satellite collision

On 10 February 2009, Iridium 33 collided with the defunct Russian satellite Kosmos 2251 over Siberia, the first-ever accidental hypervelocity collision of two intact satellites, generating thousands of pieces of space debris.
