Craniometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Craniometric measurement of a skull

Craniometry is the measurement of the characteristics of the human skull, once a principal technique of physical anthropology. Its main practical use today is descriptive and comparative — in forensic science, archaeology and the study of human variation — but in the 19th and early 20th centuries craniometric data were used to construct racial classifications and claims about intelligence and character that are now regarded as scientific racism and pseudoscience.

History

The Dutch anatomist Pieter Camper developed the "facial angle" in the 1770s. In the 19th century Samuel George Morton collected hundreds of skulls and measured cranial capacity (in works such as Crania Americana, 1839), while Paul Broca in France refined measuring instruments and founded the Société d'Anthropologie de Paris. Later practitioners such as Franz Boas showed that head shape changes within a generation of immigration, undermining claims that cranial form was a fixed racial marker.

Stephen Jay Gould's The Mismeasure of Man (1981) famously accused Morton of unconscious bias in his measurements, though later re-measurement of Morton's collection has disputed aspects of Gould's critique.

Modern use

Contemporary researchers use cranial measurements, alongside genetics, to study population history and migration, while forensic anthropologists use them to help estimate ancestry and sex of skeletal remains. The cephalic index — the ratio of skull breadth to length — remains a standard descriptive measure.
