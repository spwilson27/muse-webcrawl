Karst is a topography formed by the dissolution of soluble rocks — principally limestone, dolomite, and gypsum — by slightly acidic rainwater and groundwater. Karst landscapes are characterised by caves, sinkholes (dolines), sinking streams, large springs, and extensive underground drainage, with little surface water. The term derives from the Karst Plateau of Slovenia and Italy, where such terrain was first systematically studied.

Formation

Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil, forming weak carbonic acid that dissolves calcium carbonate along joints and bedding planes. Over time the fissures enlarge into conduits and caverns; where the roof of a void collapses, a sinkhole forms. In humid tropics, continued dissolution produces tower karst — steep limestone pinnacles such as those of Guilin in China and Ha Long Bay in Vietnam. In arid or cold climates, relict karst forms may be preserved from earlier conditions.

Significance

Karst aquifers supply drinking water to a large fraction of the world's population but are highly vulnerable to contamination because water moves rapidly underground with little filtration. Karst areas present engineering hazards from subsidence and sinkhole collapse, yet also host outstanding cave systems (such as Mammoth Cave), unique ecosystems with specialised cave fauna, and major tourist landscapes.
