Shock tactics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Shock tactics are military tactics designed to overwhelm the enemy through sudden, violent offensive action, breaking their morale and cohesion before they can mount an effective response. The shock action may be delivered by massed cavalry charges, armored assaults, or concentrated close-quarters attacks, and relies on momentum, surprise and psychological impact as much as on physical destruction.

History

Shock tactics are as old as organized warfare. Heavy infantry formations such as the Greek phalanx and the Macedonian phalanx under Alexander the Great used massed shock action, while armored cavalry — from Parthian cataphracts to medieval knights and Napoleonic cuirassiers — delivered decisive battlefield charges. The couched-lance charge of mounted knights dominated European warfare for centuries.

The spread of effective missile weapons, pike formations and eventually firearms eroded the power of the traditional cavalry charge, though shock action survived in forms such as the bayonet charge. In the First World War, stormtrooper infiltration tactics sought to restore shock by different means. In the Second World War, the German Blitzkrieg combined armored spearheads, motorized infantry and air support into a mechanized form of shock tactics; the Allied response developed similar combined-arms methods.

Modern usage

Modern doctrines such as "shock and awe" (rapid dominance) apply the principle at the operational and strategic level, using overwhelming firepower and maneuver to paralyze an opponent's ability to respond. Armored breakthrough operations, heliborne assault and concentrated precision strikes are contemporary expressions of the same idea.
