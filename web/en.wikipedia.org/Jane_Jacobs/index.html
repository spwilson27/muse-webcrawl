Jane Jacobs OC OOnt (born Jane Butzner; May 4, 1916 – April 25, 2006) was an American-Canadian journalist, author, theorist and activist who influenced urban studies, sociology and economics. Her most famous book, The Death and Life of Great American Cities (1961), argued that modernist urban planning destroyed the intricate neighbourhood life of cities, and introduced concepts such as "eyes on the street" and the social capital of mixed-use districts.

Life

Jacobs was born in Scranton, Pennsylvania, and moved to New York City in 1934, living in Greenwich Village and working as a freelance writer before joining the magazine Architectural Forum in 1952. Largely self-educated in urban matters, she became a leading critic of the "urban renewal" programmes of the era and of the powerful builder Robert Moses. Her campaigns helped stop the demolition of her own West Village neighbourhood and contributed to the cancellation of the Lower Manhattan Expressway, which would have run through SoHo and Little Italy.

Toronto

In 1968, partly to keep her sons out of the Vietnam War draft, Jacobs moved with her family to Toronto, Canada, where she quickly became involved in civic life, helping to stop the Spadina Expressway and influencing the city's comparatively neighbourhood-friendly planning. She became a Canadian citizen and wrote several more books, including The Economy of Cities (1969), Cities and the Wealth of Nations (1984), Systems of Survival (1992) and Dark Age Ahead (2004).

Ideas and legacy

Jacobs argued that thriving city districts need four generators of diversity: mixed primary uses, short blocks, buildings of varying age and condition, and sufficient density. Her work reframed cities as complex, self-organizing ecosystems and helped inspire New Urbanism and community-based planning. She died in Toronto in 2006, shortly before her 90th birthday; annual "Jane's Walks" in hundreds of cities worldwide commemorate her ideas.
