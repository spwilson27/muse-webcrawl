Vladimir I Sviatoslavich (Old East Slavic: Володимѣръ Свѧтославичь; c. 958 – 15 July 1015), also known as Vladimir the Great or Saint Vladimir, was Prince of Novgorod from 970 and Grand Prince of Kiev from 980 until his death in 1015. He is best known for the Christianization of Kievan Rus' in 988, an event that shaped the religious and cultural development of the East Slavic peoples and for which he was canonised as a saint by both the Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic churches.

Early life and rise to power

Vladimir was the youngest son of Sviatoslav I of Kiev by his housekeeper Malusha, described in the sagas as a prophetess. His uncle Dobrynya served as his tutor and most trusted advisor. After Sviatoslav's death in 972, civil war broke out between Vladimir's brothers Yaropolk and Oleg. In 977 Vladimir fled to his kinsman Haakon Sigurdsson in Norway, where he raised an army of Varangian mercenaries. Returning in 978, he marched against Yaropolk, first retaking Novgorod and then Polotsk, whose prince Rogvolod he killed before forcibly taking his daughter Rogneda as a wife. Kiev fell in 980 and Yaropolk was murdered, leaving Vladimir sole ruler of the Rus' lands from Novgorod to Kiev.

Pagan reforms

During the first years of his reign Vladimir attempted to unify the disparate Slavic, Finnic and Norse cults of his realm. In 980 he erected a temple on a hill in Kiev with idols of six gods: Perun, Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl and Mokosh. Human sacrifices are recorded in the Primary Chronicle, most famously of the Varangian Christians Theodore and John in 983. Vladimir also acquired numerous wives and, according to the chronicle, hundreds of concubines.

Conversion and Christianization

According to the famous account in the Primary Chronicle, in 986 Vladimir received envoys from Muslim Volga Bulgars, Latin Christians, Khazar Jews and a Greek philosopher, and in 987 sent his own emissaries to examine the faiths of neighbouring peoples. His envoys were overwhelmed by the beauty of the liturgy in the Hagia Sophia in Constantinople, reportedly declaring that they knew not whether they were in heaven or on earth. In 988 Vladimir seized the Byzantine city of Chersonesus in Crimea and demanded the hand of Anna Porphyrogenita, sister of the emperors Basil II and Constantine VIII. Following his baptism at Chersonesus (or, in other accounts, at Kiev or Vasiliev), he returned to Kiev and ordered the mass baptism of its inhabitants in the Dnieper River, destroying the pagan idols — Perun was dragged behind a horse and beaten before being thrown into the river. The Christianization of Kievan Rus' proceeded, at times by force, throughout his reign and those of his successors.

Later reign

Vladimir built churches, including the Church of the Tithes in Kiev, endowed with a tenth of his income, and founded schools and charitable institutions. He appointed his twelve sons to govern the principal towns of Rus', a policy that sowed the seeds of later feudal fragmentation. His coinage was among the first struck by a Rus' ruler, bearing his image enthroned. Relations with neighbouring peoples were maintained through marriages: his daughters and sons married into the royal houses of Poland, Hungary, France, Norway and Byzantium.

Death and succession

Vladimir died at Berestove on 15 July 1015 while preparing to march against his son Yaroslav of Novgorod, who had refused to pay tribute. His death was followed by a succession war among his sons, from which Yaroslav the Wise emerged victorious. Vladimir was canonised, probably in the thirteenth century, and his feast day is celebrated on 15 July in the Orthodox Church. He is the namesake of the Order of St. Vladimir and of numerous churches, and his statue overlooking the Dnieper in Kiev, erected in 1853, is a symbol of the city.
