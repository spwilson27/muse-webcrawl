Brighton Rock (novel)

Brighton Rock is a novel by English author Graham Greene, first published in 1938 in the United Kingdom by William Heinemann and in the United States as The Worst Passion-era title simply Brighton Rock by Viking Press. Set in the seaside resort of Brighton between the wars, it is a murder thriller centred on Pinkie Brown, a teenage gang leader, and the waitress Rose, who unwittingly holds evidence against him — but it is equally a theological novel about good and evil, damnation and mercy, written soon after Greene's conversion to Roman Catholicism. It is among Greene's most famous works and was famously adapted to film in 1948 and again in 2010.

Plot summary

Journalist Charles Hale, in Brighton as part of a newspaper promotion, is murdered by the razor-gang of seventeen-year-old Pinkie Brown, whose former leader Hale had betrayed. Pinkie must dispose of the evidence: a waitress, Rose, has seen something that could connect him to the crime, and Pinkie courts and then marries her — in a registry office ceremony both regard as a mortal sin — because a wife cannot be compelled to testify against her husband. Meanwhile Ida Arnold, a warm-hearted, non-religious woman who had befriended Hale, pursues her own investigation out of a belief in "right and wrong", in contrast to the Catholic categories of good and evil that govern Pinkie and Rose. Pinkie, feeling trapped, plots Rose's murder as a staged suicide; his plan collapses, and at the climax he falls to his death from the cliff edge after throwing vitriol that splashes back into his own face. The ending — among the most discussed in Greene — has Rose walking to hear a gramophone record Pinkie made for her, on which, unbeknownst to her, he recorded hatred rather than love; Greene leaves her approaching "the worst horror of all".

Themes

The novel opposes Ida's secular morality of right and wrong to the Catholic vision in which Pinkie, for all his evil, and Rose, for all her complicity, inhabit a universe where damnation and salvation are real. Greene explores the corruption of innocence, the psychology of a killer formed by slum poverty and a loveless childhood, and the possibility of mercy: the priest's closing words about "the appalling strangeness of the mercy of God" encapsulate the novel's theology. Greene later expressed mixed feelings about the book, revising the text for the Collected Edition, but critics consistently rank it among the finest English novels of the 20th century.

Adaptations

The 1948 film, directed by John Boulting with Richard Attenborough as Pinkie, is a landmark of British cinema; Greene co-wrote the screenplay and softened the ending at the producers' insistence. A stage musical adaptation premiered in 2004 (music by John Barry), and Rowan Joffé's 2010 film relocated the story to the mods-and-rockers Brighton of 1964.
