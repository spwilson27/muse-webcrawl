Sudebnik of 1497

The Sudebnik of 1497 was a collection of laws introduced by Ivan III, the grand prince of Moscow, and the first legal code for the centralized Russian state. The name derives from the Russian word sud, meaning a court or trial.

The code standardized legal procedures across the territories that Moscow had recently brought under its control, including Novgorod and Pskov, suppressing local legal customs in favour of a single system administered by the grand prince's officials. It drew on earlier sources such as the Russkaya Pravda and the Pskov Judicial Charter.

Contents

The Sudebnik regulated court organization and fees, criminal penalties for offences such as theft, murder, and bribery, and property and inheritance disputes. Its most famous provision, Article 57, restricted the right of peasants to move from one landowner to another to a period around St. George's Day (26 November) — one week before and one week after — provided they paid a fee (pozhiloe). Historians regard this as an early step toward the enserfment of the Russian peasantry.

Legacy

The 1497 code was superseded by the Sudebnik of 1550 under Ivan IV, which expanded and revised its provisions. The only surviving copy of the 1497 text was discovered in 1817.
