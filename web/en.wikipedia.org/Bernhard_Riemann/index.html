Georg Friedrich Bernhard Riemann (17 September 1826 – 20 July 1866) was a German mathematician who made profound contributions to analysis, number theory, and differential geometry. In the field of real analysis he is mostly known for the first rigorous formulation of the integral, the Riemann integral. His contributions to complex analysis include the introduction of Riemann surfaces, and his work on differential geometry laid the foundations for the mathematics of general relativity.

Life

Riemann was born in Breselenz, a village near Dannenberg, the son of a Lutheran pastor. Painfully shy and afflicted by poor health, he showed exceptional mathematical talent at school. In 1846 he entered the University of Göttingen, where Gauss taught; he also studied in Berlin under Dirichlet, Jacobi, and Eisenstein. He completed his doctorate under Gauss in 1851 with a dissertation that founded the geometric theory of complex functions. In 1854 his habilitation lecture, "On the hypotheses which lie at the bases of geometry", delivered before Gauss, introduced the concept of a manifold and the curvature tensor, and remained unpublished until 1868 — it is now regarded as one of the most important works in geometry. He became a full professor at Göttingen in 1859, succeeding Dirichlet. Riemann died of tuberculosis in 1866 at Selasca on Lake Maggiore, aged thirty-nine.

Contributions

Riemann's short career produced results of extraordinary depth: the Riemann integral and the notion of integrability; Riemann surfaces and the Riemann mapping theorem in complex analysis; the Riemann–Roch theorem in algebraic geometry; the theory of trigonometric series and the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma; and the geometry of manifolds of arbitrary dimension and curvature that Einstein later used in general relativity.

The Riemann hypothesis

His single paper on number theory, "On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude" (1859), introduced the Riemann zeta function and conjectured that all its nontrivial zeros have real part 1/2 — the Riemann hypothesis, still unproved and one of the Millennium Prize Problems, with a reward of one million dollars for its solution.
