Selim I (1470–1520), known as Selim the Grim or Selim the Resolute (Turkish: Yavuz Sultan Selim), was the ninth sultan of the Ottoman Empire from 1512 to 1520. In a reign of only eight years he roughly tripled the empire's territory, defeating the Safavid Empire in the east and conquering the Mamluk Sultanate of Egypt, Syria, and the Hejaz, thereby bringing the holy cities of Mecca and Medina under Ottoman rule and making the empire the leading power of the Islamic world.

Accession

As a prince, Selim governed Trabzon and campaigned in the Caucasus. In 1511–1512, with Janissary support, he forced his father Bayezid II to abdicate and eliminated his rival brothers, securing the throne in accordance with the Ottoman practice of royal fratricide.

Conquests

Seeing the Shia Safavids of Shah Ismail I as both a military and religious threat, Selim crushed their army at the Battle of Chaldiran in 1514 and annexed eastern Anatolia and Upper Mesopotamia. He then turned on the Mamluks: victory at Marj Dabiq (1516) delivered Syria, and victory at Ridaniya (1517) delivered Egypt, ending the Mamluk Sultanate. The Sharif of Mecca submitted, sending the keys of the holy cities; Selim also acquired relics of the Prophet Muhammad now kept in Topkapı Palace. Ottoman tradition holds that the last Abbasid caliph of Cairo transferred the caliphate to Selim, styling later sultans as caliphs.

Death and legacy

Selim died in 1520 at Çorlu, reportedly of illness (traditionally said to be a carbuncle, "the lion's claw"), while preparing further campaigns. His conquests filled the treasury, secured control of eastern trade routes, and transformed the Ottoman state into a predominantly Middle Eastern, Muslim empire; his son Suleiman the Magnificent inherited its greatest age of expansion.
