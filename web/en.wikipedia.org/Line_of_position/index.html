Line of position

A line of position (LOP) is, in navigation, a line (or, more generally, a curve) somewhere along which a vessel or aircraft is known to be located, derived from a single observation. A single LOP does not determine a position; the intersection of two or more lines of position — obtained simultaneously, or adjusted to a common time — gives a fix.

Sources of lines of position

Common sources include:

  • Bearings of charted objects: a compass bearing of a lighthouse, headland or radio beacon defines a straight LOP passing through the object.
  • Celestial observations: a sextant altitude of a star, planet, the Sun or Moon, worked by the intercept method (associated with Marcq St. Hilaire), yields an LOP approximately perpendicular to the body's azimuth. The special case of a line of equal altitude (a Sumner line) was introduced by Thomas Hubbard Sumner in 1837.
  • Radar ranges and depth contours: a measured distance from a charted object gives a circular LOP; following a soundings contour gives a rough LOP used in fog navigation.
  • Radio direction finding and hyperbolic systems such as Loran, where the line is a hyperbola of constant difference in signal arrival times.

Advancing and retarding

Because observations are rarely simultaneous, an LOP obtained earlier is advanced — moved along the vessel's track by the distance run in the interval — and a later one retarded, so that all lines refer to the same moment. The resulting crossing is sometimes called a running fix. Ideally LOPs cross at angles between 30° and 150°; three lines typically form a small triangle (a "cocked hat") whose size reflects the accuracy of the fix.

Modern practice

Although satellite navigation has displaced celestial and visual fixing in routine practice, the line of position remains a fundamental concept in maritime training and a backup method, and the term is retained in air navigation and in resection techniques on land.
