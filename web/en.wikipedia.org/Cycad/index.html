Cycad

Cycas revoluta, the sago palm, the most widely cultivated cycad.

Cycads are seed plants that typically have a stout, woody trunk crowned with large, hard, evergreen pinnate leaves. They constitute the division Cycadophyta, containing about 350 recognised species in ten or eleven genera and three families (Cycadaceae, Stangeriaceae and Zamiaceae). Although superficially similar to palms and ferns, cycads are gymnosperms most closely related to Ginkgo, and bear their reproductive organs in cones.

Description

Cycad stems may be subterranean or form trunks up to several metres tall, armoured with the persistent bases of old leaves. Leaves are pinnately compound and unfold like fern fronds. All cycads are dioecious — individual plants are either male, producing pollen cones, or female, producing seed cones. Unusually for seed plants, many species host nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria in specialised coralloid roots. Pollination is often by beetles or thrips rather than wind.

Evolution and distribution

The lineage extends back to the early Permian, roughly 280 million years ago, and cycads were abundant and widespread during the Mesozoic, sometimes called the "Age of Cycads". Living species are relicts of that diversity, confined to tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas, Africa, Asia and Australia. Molecular studies suggest, surprisingly, that most living species radiated relatively recently, within the last 10–20 million years.

Ecology and toxicity

Cycads are slow-growing and long-lived; some specimens are estimated at over 1,000 years old. Their seeds contain toxins including cycasin and the amino acid BMAA. Nevertheless, starch from stems and seeds ("sago") has traditionally been eaten after careful processing, notably by the Chamorro people of Guam — where a link between cycad consumption and the ALS-parkinsonism-dementia complex has long been investigated — and by Indigenous Australians.

Conservation

Cycads are among the most threatened groups of plants: habitat loss and over-collection for horticulture have driven many species toward extinction, and all are listed under CITES. Popular ornamentals such as the sago palm (Cycas revoluta) and cardboard palm (Zamia furfuracea) are widely grown outside their native ranges.
