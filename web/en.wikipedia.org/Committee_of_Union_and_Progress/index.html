The Committee of Union and Progress (CUP; Ottoman Turkish: İttihad ve Terakki Cemiyeti) was a revolutionary organization and later political party in the Ottoman Empire. Founded by medical students in Constantinople in 1889 and closely associated with the Young Turk movement, it led the Young Turk Revolution of 1908, which restored the Ottoman constitution and parliament. From 1913, following a coup d'état, the CUP ruled the empire as an increasingly authoritarian single-party regime, dominated by the "Three Pashas": Enver, Talaat, and Djemal.

Rise to power

Opposition to the absolutist rule of Sultan Abdul Hamid II coalesced in exile and military circles, especially among officers of the Third Army in Macedonia. In July 1908, CUP-backed officers revolted, forcing the Sultan to restore the 1876 constitution. After the counter-coup of 1909 (the 31 March Incident), the CUP consolidated its position, and in January 1913 its members stormed the Sublime Porte and seized power.

Rule and the First World War

The CUP government pursued centralization and Turkish nationalism, abrogated the capitulations, and aligned the empire with Germany, entering the First World War in October 1914. During the war, the CUP regime organized and carried out the Armenian genocide of 1915–1917, as well as persecutions of Assyrian and Greek Christians, through the Special Organization directed by the party's central committee. Following defeat in 1918, the CUP dissolved itself and its leaders fled abroad; Talaat, Enver, and Djemal were all assassinated or killed within a few years.

Legacy

The CUP's cadres and policies — including secularizing reform, nationalism, and etatism — strongly influenced the subsequent Republic of Turkey founded by Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, himself a former CUP member.
