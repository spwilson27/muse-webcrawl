Taxus baccata is a species of evergreen tree in the family Taxaceae, native to Europe, north-west Africa, Iran, and south-west Asia. It is the tree originally known as yew, though that name is now also applied to other species of Taxus. It is a slow-growing, exceptionally long-lived conifer famous for its association with churchyards and graveyards across Britain and Ireland.

Description

The yew is a small to medium-sized evergreen tree, typically 10–20 m tall, with reddish-brown bark, dark green needle-like leaves arranged in two flat rows, and a highly branched crown. Yews are mostly dioecious: male trees shed cones of pollen in spring, while female trees produce modified cones consisting of a single seed partly enclosed in a soft, bright red, berry-like cup called an aril — the source of the specific name baccata ("berry-bearing"). With age the trunk often becomes hollow and massively fluted, making age hard to measure; some churchyard yews are claimed to be several thousand years old, among them the Fortingall Yew in Scotland.

Toxicity and uses

Almost all parts of the yew are poisonous, containing taxine alkaloids; the red aril flesh is the only non-toxic part, though the seed within it is toxic. Historically, yew wood — hard, elastic, and durable — was prized for English longbows, a demand that depleted many European populations. In modern medicine, compounds from yews yielded the chemotherapy drug paclitaxel (Taxol), first isolated from the Pacific yew and now produced semi-synthetically and by cell culture. The yew is also ubiquitous in gardens as hedging and topiary, especially the upright Irish yew cultivar 'Fastigiata'.

Cultural significance

Long before Christianity, the yew's longevity and evergreen foliage made it a symbol of death and rebirth, and ancient yews survive in many churchyards, some thought to predate the churches beside them.
