Searches for Noah's Ark

Searches for Noah's Ark have been made almost continuously since the fourth century, as explorers, pilgrims, and modern "ark hunters" have sought physical remains of the biblical vessel said to have come to rest on the "mountains of Ararat" after the Genesis flood. Despite numerous expeditions and reported sightings, no verified remains of the Ark have ever been found, and the searches are generally regarded as pseudoscience and pseudoarchaeology by scholars.

Early accounts

Jewish and Christian writers of antiquity, including Flavius Josephus, reported that remains of the Ark were shown to visitors in Armenia. Medieval travellers such as Marco Polo mentioned the mountain of the Ark, and reports of timbers or structures seen high on Mount Ararat recur throughout the centuries.

Modern expeditions

From the nineteenth century onward, expeditions climbed Ararat in search of the vessel, and aerial photographs in the twentieth century fuelled interest in anomalies on the mountain. The Durupınar site, a boat-shaped geological formation near Ararat, was promoted from the 1970s as the Ark's remains, but geologists identify it as a natural rock formation. Other claims, including expeditions announcing discoveries in 2010, have not been substantiated.

Scholarly assessment

Mainstream archaeologists and geologists regard the search as a faith-driven pursuit without scientific support: the geological record shows no evidence of a global flood, and no expedition has produced independently verified artefacts.
