Travesty

Travesty is a literary or theatrical genre that mocks a serious work by treating its subject in a grotesquely inferior or inappropriate style. The word derives from the Italian travestire, "to disguise", through French travesti. In common usage, "a travesty" also denotes any grotesque or debased imitation — as in the phrase "a travesty of justice".

Literary travesty

Unlike parody, which typically imitates the style of a work to satirise other subjects, travesty degrades the subject matter itself: lofty heroes and events are rendered in low, vulgar, or comic language. Famous examples include Paul Scarron's Virgile travesti (1648–1653), which retold Virgil's Aeneid in burlesque verse, and Charles Cotton's English imitation Scarronides. The form flourished in 17th-century France and Restoration England.

Theatre and performance

In theatre, travesty also refers to the performance convention of an actor playing a role of another sex — the travesti tradition — seen in breeches roles in opera, pantomime principal boys, and drag performance.
