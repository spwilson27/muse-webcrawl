The Arecibo message is an interstellar radio message carrying basic information about humanity and Earth that was sent to the globular star cluster Messier 13 on 16 November 1974. The message was broadcast into space a single time via frequency-modulated radio waves during a ceremony to mark the remodelling of the Arecibo radio telescope in Puerto Rico. The message was directed at the Great Hercules Cluster, some 25,000 light-years away, in the hope that extraterrestrial intelligence might receive and decipher it.

Content

The message, drafted primarily by Frank Drake with the assistance of Carl Sagan and others, consists of 1,679 binary digits — a semiprime number (23 × 73) chosen so the bits can be arranged into a rectangle of 73 rows by 23 columns, forming a pictogram. Read in that arrangement, the image contains seven parts:

  1. The numbers one to ten in binary.
  2. The atomic numbers of hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and phosphorus — the elements of DNA.
  3. The formulas for the sugars and bases of DNA nucleotides.
  4. The number of nucleotides in DNA and a graphic of the double helix.
  5. A stick figure of a human, its height expressed in units of the message's wavelength, and the human population of Earth (about 4.3 billion in 1974).
  6. A diagram of the Solar System, with Earth highlighted.
  7. A diagram of the Arecibo telescope and the diameter of its dish.

Transmission and reception

The message was transmitted at 2380 MHz, shifted in frequency by 10 Hz, at a rate of one bit per second, taking just under three minutes. Because Messier 13 will have moved over the roughly 25,000 years the signal needs to arrive, the message will in fact miss the cluster's centre as it exists at arrival — a limitation acknowledged at the time. The broadcast was principally a demonstration of the upgraded telescope's power and a symbolic gesture; Drake and Sagan did not expect a reply. A hoax "reply" in the form of a crop circle appeared near the Chilbolton Observatory in England in 2001, rearranging the pictogram in the style of the original.

Legacy

The Arecibo message remains the most famous exercise in METI (messaging to extraterrestrial intelligence) and a staple of SETI discussions, including debates over whether broadcasting humanity's presence is prudent. The telescope itself collapsed in December 2020 after cable failures; a student competition, the Arecibo Wow! project, has worked to design a successor message.
