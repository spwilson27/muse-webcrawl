Ideal gas

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An ideal gas is a theoretical gas composed of many randomly moving point particles that do not interact except through perfectly elastic collisions. The ideal gas model obeys the ideal gas law and provides a good approximation to the behaviour of many real gases under ordinary conditions, though it fails at low temperatures and high pressures where molecular volume and intermolecular forces matter.

Ideal gas law

pV = nRT

where p is pressure, V is volume, n is the amount of substance in moles, R is the ideal gas constant (8.314 J·mol−1·K−1), and T is absolute temperature. The law combines Boyle's law, Charles's law, Avogadro's law, and Gay-Lussac's law, and was first stated in this form by Dmitri Mendeleev in 1874, building on the work of Émile Clapeyron.

Assumptions

The classical ideal gas model assumes that molecules occupy negligible volume, that collisions are elastic, that there are no intermolecular forces, and that molecular motion is random. In the quantum treatments, the Maxwell–Boltzmann, Bose–Einstein, and Fermi–Dirac ideal gases describe free particles obeying the respective statistics.

Thermodynamic properties

For an ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature (Joule's law), and enthalpy likewise. Heat capacities at constant volume and constant pressure differ by nR. Ideal-gas behaviour underlies the analysis of the Carnot cycle, adiabatic processes, and the kinetic theory derivation of pressure from molecular collisions.

Deviations

Real gases deviate from ideality as density rises or temperature falls; corrections are described by equations of state such as the van der Waals equation and by the compressibility factor.
