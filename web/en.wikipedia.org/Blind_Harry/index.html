Blind Harry

Blind Harry, the 15th-century Scottish makar.

Blind Harry (c. 1440 – after 1492), also known as Henry the Minstrel, was a Scottish poet or makar of the 15th century. He is famous as the author of The Wallace (full title The Actes and Deidis of the Illustre and Vallyeant Campioun Schir William Wallace), a long epic poem in Scots, composed around 1477, recounting the life of the Scottish hero William Wallace.

Life

Little is known of Harry's life. Records in the Lord High Treasurer's accounts show payments to "Blind Hary" for performances at the court of James IV in the early 1490s, and the poet William Dunbar mentions him in his Lament for the Makaris. Tradition holds that he was blind from birth and earned his living as a travelling minstrel reciting tales of Scottish heroes, though scholars doubt both the literal blindness and some details of his biography.

The Wallace

The poem, written in heroic couplets across twelve books and some 11,800 lines, presents Wallace as a near-superhuman patriot fighting the English under Edward I. It mixes documented events with legend — Harry claimed to draw on a Latin account by Wallace's chaplain John Blair, which has never been found, and many episodes are considered inventions. The sole surviving manuscript dates to 1488.

Despite its historical inaccuracies, The Wallace became one of the most popular books in Scotland, second only to the Bible in many households, and it profoundly shaped the Wallace legend — influencing, among later works, Jane Porter's novel The Scottish Chiefs and, at a remove, the 1995 film Braveheart. An 18th-century modernization by William Hamilton of Gilbertfield made the poem accessible to later readers including Robert Burns, who credited it with inspiring his own patriotism.
