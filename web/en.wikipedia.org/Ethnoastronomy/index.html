Ethnoastronomy is the anthropological study of how living (or historically documented) cultures understand, use, and embed celestial phenomena in their social lives — in calendars, navigation, ritual, myth, agriculture, and law. Together with archaeoastronomy, which studies the astronomy of past societies from material remains, it forms the field of cultural astronomy. Whereas archaeoastronomy often focuses on alignments of monuments, ethnoastronomy relies on ethnographic methods: interviews, participant observation, language, and oral tradition.

Scope

Ethnoastronomers document, for example, the star lore of Aboriginal Australian peoples, in which the dark clouds of the Milky Way form an "Emu in the Sky" used as a seasonal calendar; Polynesian star compasses and wave navigation that enabled settlement of the Pacific; the lunar-stellar calendars of East African pastoralists; and the Venus cycle in Mesoamerican codices such as the Dresden Codex. The field also studies folk astronomy in Europe and Asia, including weather lore, saint's-day star risings, and peasant calendars.

History and debates

The systematic study of non-Western astronomies grew in the twentieth century as anthropologists recorded Indigenous knowledge that colonial histories had ignored, and accelerated from the 1970s–80s with journals and societies devoted to cultural astronomy. Scholars debate how far modern ideas should be read into traditional sources, the ethics of recording sacred or restricted knowledge, and the contribution of Indigenous astronomies to heritage recognition — such as dark-sky protection and the naming of exoplanets and stars through the IAU's NameExoWorlds campaigns.
