Stile concitato

Monteverdi's Il combattimento di Tancredi e Clorinda (1624), the showcase work of the stile concitato.

Stile concitato (Italian for "agitated style") is a Baroque musical style developed by Claudio Monteverdi in the early 17th century to depict anger, war and agitation, characterised by rapid repeated notes, quick tremolandi, and driving rhythms. Monteverdi described it in the preface to his Eighth Book of Madrigals (1638).

Origins

Monteverdi claimed to have invented the style to complete music's expressive range: alongside the soft (molle) and moderate (temperato) styles, the agitated (concitato) style was, he argued, described by Plato and ancient writers but missing from the music of his predecessors. Its first full demonstration was Il combattimento di Tancredi e Clorinda (1624), a dramatic setting of an episode from Tasso's Gerusalemme liberata, in which string players were instructed to repeat a single note sixteen times to a measure and to pluck strings to imitate the clash of swords — an early written use of tremolo and pizzicato.

Characteristics and influence

The style relies on reiterated semiquavers, fanfare-like figures in the Dorian or "warlike" vein, and heightened declamation to embody violent emotion. It became a foundation of the Baroque doctrine of the affections and influenced depictions of battle and fury in composers from Heinrich Schütz to later opera composers.
