Mixing (mathematics)

In mathematics, mixing is an abstract concept originating in physics — the mixing of gases or fluids — which is used in ergodic theory, probability theory, and statistical mechanics to describe systems that evolve so that any two regions of the state space become asymptotically intertwined, in such a way that events become independent over time.

In ergodic theory

A measure-preserving transformation is called (strongly) mixing if, for any two measurable sets, the measure of the intersection of one set with the image of the other under iteration approaches the product of their measures as the number of iterations grows. Mixing implies ergodicity, but not conversely; the hierarchy of chaotic properties runs from ergodicity through weak mixing to strong mixing and to Kolmogorov (K-) systems. Examples of mixing systems include the Bernoulli shift and the Arnold cat map.

In probability theory

In the theory of stochastic processes, mixing conditions (such as alpha-mixing or strong mixing, introduced by Rosenblatt) quantify how quickly dependence between observations decays with their separation in time. These conditions underpin central limit theorems and other limit results for dependent random variables and time series.

Fluid mixing

In fluid dynamics, mixing describes the deformation and diffusion of material elements in a flow, studied with tools from dynamical systems theory; chaotic advection can produce efficient mixing even in laminar flows such as the blinking vortex and the tendril-whorl map.
