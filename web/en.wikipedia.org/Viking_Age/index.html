The Viking Age was the period during the Middle Ages, roughly 793 to 1066 CE, when Norsemen known as Vikings undertook large-scale raiding, colonising, conquest, and trading throughout Europe and beyond. It is conventionally dated from the raid on the monastery of Lindisfarne in northern England in 793 to the death of Harald Hardrada at the Battle of Stamford Bridge in 1066, weeks before the Norman Conquest.

Overview

Scandinavian seafarers, enabled by advanced longships capable of crossing oceans and penetrating rivers, ranged from Newfoundland (L'Anse aux Meadows) in the west to Baghdad-connected trade routes in the east. They founded settlements in Iceland and Greenland, the Danelaw in England, Normandy in France, and the Kievan Rus' along the Volga and Dnieper; they served as Varangian Guards in Constantinople and raided as far as Iberia and Italy. Debate continues over the causes — population pressure, political centralisation, trade opportunities, and new sailing technology are all cited.

Society and legacy

Viking society combined farming, craftsmanship, and seafaring within a culture rich in oral poetry, runic writing, and Norse mythology. The Viking Age diaspora left lasting linguistic, genetic, and place-name legacies across the North Atlantic and northern Europe, and the romanticised image of the Viking was revived in the 19th century.
