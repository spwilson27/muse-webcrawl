Nuclear-powered icebreaker

A nuclear-powered icebreaker is an icebreaking ship propelled by nuclear reactors. Such vessels have been built and operated only by the Soviet Union and Russia, whose fleet — based at Murmansk and managed by Atomflot — escorts merchant shipping along the Northern Sea Route through the Arctic Ocean.

Advantages

Nuclear propulsion offers enormous power for breaking thick ice and very long endurance: a nuclear icebreaker can operate for years without refuelling, an important asset in the remote Arctic where conventional fuel resupply is difficult. The reactors typically drive steam turbines generating electricity for powerful electric motors on the propeller shafts.

History and classes

The first nuclear icebreaker, and the world's first nuclear-powered surface ship, was the Soviet Lenin, launched in 1957 and commissioned in 1959. She was followed by the larger Arktika class, whose lead ship Arktika became the first surface vessel to reach the North Pole, in 1977. The shallow-draught Taymyr class was built for river estuaries. Russia's newer Project 22220 vessels — Arktika, Sibir, Ural, and successors — are the largest and most powerful icebreakers in the world, designed to break ice up to about 3 metres thick, and the even larger Project 10510 Leader class is planned.
