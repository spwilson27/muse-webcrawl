Map projection

Map projection
FieldCartography, geodesy, mathematics
ProblemRepresenting a curved surface on a plane without distortion of every property
ClassificationsBy surface (cylindrical, conic, azimuthal); by property (conformal, equal-area, equidistant, compromise)
Famous examplesMercator (1569), Robinson (1963), Gall–Peters

A map projection is any of a broad set of transformations employed to represent the curved two-dimensional surface of a globe on a plane. In a map projection, coordinates on a sphere or ellipsoid are transformed into coordinates on a plane. It had been realized since antiquity that a sphere cannot be flattened onto a plane without distortion: by Gauss's Theorema Egregium, no projection can preserve all properties at once, so every flat map is a compromise among area, shape, distance, and direction.

Properties and distortion

Projections are classified by what they preserve. Conformal projections (such as Mercator and stereographic) preserve local angles and shapes of small features, at the cost of inflating areas toward the edges. Equal-area projections (such as Mollweide, Albers, and Gall–Peters) preserve relative sizes while distorting shapes. Equidistant projections preserve distances along certain lines, and compromise projections such as Robinson and Winkel Tripel balance several distortions without preserving any metric exactly. Tissot's indicatrix — the pattern of ellipses formed by projecting small circles — is the standard tool for visualising a projection's distortion.

Common projections

The Mercator projection (1569), designed for navigation because rhumb lines appear straight, became the default wall map despite grossly enlarging high-latitude lands — the controversy that surrounded Arno Peters's promotion of the equal-area Gall–Peters map in the 1970s publicised this defect. Web mapping services standardised on "Web Mercator". National topographic mapping commonly uses transverse Mercator (UTM) and conic projections suited to mid-latitude countries.

Construction surfaces

Classically, projections are described as wrapping the globe with a developable surface: a cylinder (cylindrical projections), a cone (conic), or a plane (azimuthal/planar). Modern projections are defined mathematically rather than by any physical light-and-paper model, and hundreds have been catalogued.
