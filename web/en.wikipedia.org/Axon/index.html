An axon (from Greek ἄξων, "axis") or nerve fibre is a long, slender projection of a nerve cell, or neuron, in vertebrates, that typically conducts electrical impulses known as action potentials away from the nerve cell body. The function of the axon is to transmit information to different neurons, muscles, and glands. In certain sensory neurons (pseudounipolar neurons), such as those for touch and warmth, the axons are called afferent nerve fibres and the electrical impulse travels along these from the periphery to the cell body and then to the spinal cord along another branch of the same axon.

Structure

The axon arises from the cell body at the axon hillock and may extend for enormous distances relative to the soma — the longest human axons, in the sciatic nerve, exceed a metre. Many axons are wrapped in a myelin sheath produced by Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system or oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system; gaps between segments, the nodes of Ranvier, allow rapid saltatory conduction, reaching speeds of about 120 m/s. At its end the axon branches into telodendria terminating in synaptic boutons, which release neurotransmitters onto target cells.

History

Axons were distinguished from dendrites in the late 19th century through the work of Camillo Golgi and Santiago Ramón y Cajal, whose neuron doctrine established that the axon is the output structure of the neuron. Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley's experiments on the squid giant axon (1952) revealed the ionic mechanism of the action potential.
