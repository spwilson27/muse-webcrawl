Endurance riding

Endurance riding is an equestrian sport based on controlled long-distance races. In most endurance rides, the winner is the first horse and rider team to cross the finish line while stopping periodically to pass veterinary inspections that assess the horse's fitness to continue — the sport's guiding motto is "to finish is to win". Rides are commonly held over distances of 50 to 100 miles (80–160 km), with championship events over 160 km in a single day, and shorter introductory and multi-day formats also popular.

History

The sport developed from cavalry and postal-route tests of horses. The modern benchmark is the Tevis Cup, a 100-mile ride over the Sierra Nevada from Truckee to Auburn, California, founded in 1955 along the route of the Western States Trail. Endurance became an FEI discipline in the 1980s and grew rapidly in the Middle East as well as in Europe, Australia, and the Americas.

Competition format

Courses loop through checkpoints ("vet gates") where horses must recover to a set heart rate and pass checks of hydration, gut sounds, gait, and metabolic condition before continuing. Riders manage pace, feeding, and cooling carefully; crews assist at holds. Arabian horses dominate the sport for their stamina, efficient gaits, and heat tolerance.

Welfare

Veterinary control is central: horses judged unfit are eliminated regardless of placing. Nonetheless the sport has faced welfare controversies, particularly over high speeds and injuries in some regions, prompting rule reforms on speeds, rest periods, and supervision.
