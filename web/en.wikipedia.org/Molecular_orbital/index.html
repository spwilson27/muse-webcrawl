Molecular orbital

In chemistry, a molecular orbital is a mathematical function describing the location and wave-like behavior of an electron in a molecule. This function can be used to calculate chemical and physical properties such as the probability of finding an electron in any specific region. The terms atomic orbital and molecular orbital were introduced by Robert S. Mulliken in 1932 to mean one-electron orbital wave functions.

At an introductory level, a molecular orbital is often constructed as a linear combination of atomic orbitals (the LCAO approximation), first proposed in 1929 by Sir John Lennard-Jones. Molecular orbitals are delocalized over the whole molecule, in contrast to the localized bonds of valence bond theory.

Overview

A molecular orbital can be used to represent the regions in a molecule where an electron occupying that orbital is likely to be found. Molecular orbitals are obtained from the combination of atomic orbitals, which predict the location of an electron within an atom. A molecular orbital can specify the electron configuration of a molecule: the spatial distribution and energy of one (or one pair of) electron(s). Most commonly a molecular orbital is represented as a linear combination of atomic orbitals.

When two atomic orbitals combine, they produce a lower-energy bonding orbital and a higher-energy antibonding orbital, the latter conventionally marked with an asterisk (σ*). Filling the bonding orbital stabilizes the molecule, whereas filling the antibonding orbital destabilizes it. The bond order is calculated as one-half the difference between the numbers of bonding and antibonding electrons.

History

The theory of molecular orbitals was developed in the years just after the establishment of quantum mechanics in 1925. It was first propounded by Friedrich Hund and Robert Mulliken in 1927 and 1928. The linear combination of atomic orbitals or "LCAO" approximation for molecular orbitals was introduced in 1929 by Sir John Lennard-Jones. Quantum calculations were furthered by contributions from Erich Hückel, who proposed a simple method for π-electron systems in 1930.

Types

Molecular orbitals are classified by symmetry and the atomic orbitals from which they derive:

  • σ (sigma) orbitals are symmetric with respect to rotation about the bond axis.
  • π (pi) orbitals have a nodal plane passing through the nuclei and are formed from parallel p orbitals.
  • δ (delta) orbitals have two nodal planes containing the internuclear axis and arise in some transition-metal complexes.

The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) are central to understanding reactivity, excitation, and spectroscopic properties.

Applications

Molecular orbital theory explains phenomena that valence bond theory cannot, notably the paramagnetism of oxygen: the MO diagram of O2 places two unpaired electrons in degenerate π* antibonding orbitals. MO theory is also the basis of Woodward–Hoffmann rules for pericyclic reactions, frontier orbital theory, and computational quantum chemistry methods such as Hartree–Fock and density functional theory.
