Serfdom in Russia

Serfdom in Russia (Russian: крепостное право, krepostnoye pravo) was the system of feudal bondage under which Russian peasants were tied to the land and subordinated to landowners, nobles, the church, or the state. It developed between the 15th and 17th centuries and was not abolished until the Emancipation Reform of 1861 under Tsar Alexander II — far later than serfdom disappeared in most of Western Europe.

Rise of serfdom

Early restrictions on peasant movement appeared in the Sudebnik of 1497, which limited departure from a landlord to the week around St. George's Day in autumn. Under Ivan the Terrible "forbidden years" suspended even this right, and decrees of the 1590s–1600s set time limits for recovering fugitives. The Law Code (Ulozhenie) of 1649 under Tsar Alexis completed the enserfment: peasants were bound permanently and hereditarily, landlords gained wide police and judicial powers, and recovery of runaways became unlimited in time.

The serf economy

Serfs owed their masters labour dues (barshchina) or payments in cash or kind (obrok). By the late 18th century serfs could be bought and sold — increasingly without land and even separated from their families — mortgaged, gambled away, or exiled to Siberia by their owners. At the same time, state peasants and other categories remained personally free but tied to state land. On the eve of emancipation, roughly a third of Russia's population, some 23 million people, were privately owned serfs. Serfdom underpinned the service nobility and the fiscal-military state but was widely blamed, after Russia's defeat in the Crimean War, for economic and military backwardness.

Resistance and abolition

Serfs resisted through flight, petitions, work refusal, and periodic great revolts such as those of Stenka Razin (1670–71) and Yemelyan Pugachev (1773–75). Enlightened criticism from writers such as Alexander Radishchev and, later, the intelligentsia eroded the system's legitimacy. Alexander II, declaring it better to abolish serfdom from above than wait for it to be abolished from below, issued the Emancipation Manifesto on 19 February (3 March) 1861: serfs gained personal freedom and allotments of land, but redemption payments for that land, channelled through the peasant commune (mir), burdened them until the payments were cancelled in 1907. The reform is generally counted among the Great Reforms and a milestone — incomplete but transformative — in Russian history.
