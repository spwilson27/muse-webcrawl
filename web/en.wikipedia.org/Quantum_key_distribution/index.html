Quantum key distribution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Quantum key distribution (QKD) is a secure communication method that implements a cryptographic protocol involving components of quantum mechanics. It enables two parties to produce a shared random secret key known only to them, which can then be used to encrypt and decrypt messages, typically with the one-time pad or AES. Its security rests on the laws of physics rather than assumptions about computational difficulty.

How it works

In the best-known protocol, BB84, proposed by Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard in 1984, the sender (Alice) transmits photons prepared randomly in one of two conjugate bases. The receiver (Bob) measures in randomly chosen bases; afterward they compare bases over a public channel and keep only the matching results as the raw key. Because measuring a quantum state disturbs it, an eavesdropper's intervention introduces detectable errors, and the parties can bound the eavesdropper's information and distill a shorter, secure key by error correction and privacy amplification.

Implementations and limits

Commercial QKD systems operate over optical fibre at metropolitan distances, and satellite-based QKD was demonstrated by China's Micius satellite in 2017. Practical systems face rate and distance limits, side-channel attacks on real detectors, and the need for authenticated classical channels; QKD is therefore often compared and combined with post-quantum cryptography.
