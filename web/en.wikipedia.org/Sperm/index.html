Sperm

Sperm (from Ancient Greek σπέρμα, spérma, "seed") is the male reproductive cell, or gamete, in anisogamous forms of sexual reproduction (forms in which there is a larger, female reproductive cell and a smaller, male one). Animals produce motile sperm with a tail known as a flagellum, which are known as spermatozoa, while some red algae and fungi produce non-motile sperm cells, known as spermatia.

Structure

A spermatozoon consists of a head containing the haploid nucleus and the acrosome, a midpiece packed with mitochondria that supply energy, and a long flagellum that propels the cell. In humans the cell is about 50–60 micrometres long, and the head is roughly 5 micrometres across.

Production

Sperm are produced in the testes through spermatogenesis, a process that in humans takes about 74 days and continues throughout adult life, producing several hundred million sperm per day. Formation is optimal at temperatures slightly below core body temperature.

Function

The sperm's role is to reach and fuse with the ovum, delivering its haploid set of chromosomes to form a diploid zygote. Only a tiny fraction of ejaculated sperm reach the site of fertilisation in the fallopian tube, and normally only one fuses with the egg.

Discovery

Spermatozoa were first observed in 1677 by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, who described the "animalcules" he saw under his microscope in semen.
