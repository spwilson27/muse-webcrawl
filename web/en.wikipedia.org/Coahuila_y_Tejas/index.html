Coahuila y Tejas

CountryMexico
Existed1824–1835
CapitalSaltillo (later Monclova)
Today part ofCoahuila (Mexico); Texas (United States)

Coahuila y Tejas was one of the constituent states of the newly independent United Mexican States under the Constitution of 1824. It united the former Spanish provinces of Coahuila and Texas into a single state, a pairing driven more by the small population of Texas than by any natural affinity between the two regions.

History

The state legislature sat first at Saltillo, far to the south of Texas, a distance that Texan settlers resented. Under Mexico's colonization laws, the state administered the empresario system, granting contracts to men such as Stephen F. Austin to settle families in Texas; land policy was set by the state government, which at times sold vast tracts to speculators. In 1834–35 political conflict between Saltillo and Monclova over the capital, amid the centralizing drive of President Santa Anna, helped push the state toward disintegration.

Separation

The arrangement ended with the Texas Revolution: Texas declared its independence in 1836 and became a separate republic, while Coahuila continued as a Mexican state. The boundary and the legal legacy of the state period—including land titles granted under its authority—remained significant in Texas law long afterward.
