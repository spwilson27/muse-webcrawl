Mishneh Torah

The Mishneh Torah (Hebrew: מִש־נֵה תוּרָה, "Repetition of the Torah"), also known as the Yad ha-Hazakah ("The Strong Hand"), is a code of Jewish religious law (halakha) authored by Maimonides (Rabbi Moses ben Maimon, the Rambam). Completed between 1170 and 1180 in Egypt, it is one of the most influential works in Jewish law and a monument of medieval Hebrew literature: a comprehensive, systematically organised restatement of the entire Oral Torah, written in clear Mishnaic Hebrew.

Structure

The work is divided into fourteen books — hence "Yad" (hand), whose letters in Hebrew numerals equal fourteen — including the Book of Knowledge (Sefer ha-Madda), which opens with philosophical foundations (the existence and unity of God, ethics, and the laws of Torah study); the Book of Love (prayer and blessings); the Book of Seasons (Sabbath and festivals); and books on women, holiness, agriculture, temple service, sacrifices, purity, damages, acquisition, civil law, judges, and kings and wars. Maimonides prefaced the code with an introduction listing the chain of tradition and the 613 commandments.

Method and reception

Unlike the Talmud, the Mishneh Torah presents rulings without debate, sources, or attribution — an innovation that made the law accessible but also drew sharp criticism, notably from Abraham ben David of Posquières (the Raavad), whose glosses (hassagot) accompany the text in standard editions. Maimonides intended the code to be so complete that, with the Torah itself, no other book would be needed to decide law; this ambition provoked controversy, including bans in parts of 13th-century Europe. Nevertheless it became foundational, serving as a principal source for Joseph Karo's Shulchan Aruch, and is studied daily under study cycles such as the Rambam study programme.
