Guelphs and Ghibellines

The Guelphs and Ghibellines (Italian: guelfi e ghibellini) were factions supporting the Pope and the Holy Roman Emperor, respectively, in the Italian city-states of Central and Northern Italy during the 12th and 13th centuries. Their rivalry grew out of the Investiture Controversy. In general, Guelphs tended to come from wealthy mercantile families, whereas Ghibellines were predominantly landowners or the imperial party within the cities.

Origins of the names

The names derive from the German battle cries of the rival houses of Welf and Hohenstaufen: "Guelph" from the House of Welf, and "Ghibelline" from Waiblingen, a castle of the Hohenstaufen dynasty. According to tradition, at the Battle of Weinsberg in 1140 the opposing armies shouted "Welf!" and "Waiblingen!" The terms were imported into Italy during the reign of Frederick II in the 13th century.

History

The struggle divided great cities, most famously Florence, where after the Battle of Benevento (1266) and the fall of the Hohenstaufen, Guelph ascendancy was established. Florence's victorious Guelphs soon split into Black and White factions; the poet Dante Alighieri, a White Guelph, was exiled in 1302 after the Blacks seized power with papal support. The Battle of Montaperti (1260), a Sienese Ghibelline victory over Florence, remains one of the bloodiest episodes of the conflict.

Legacy

The faction names long outlived the imperial-papal struggle, becoming labels for local rivalries and family feuds across Italy. Heraldic motifs — the imperial eagle of the Ghibellines and the lilies or keys associated with the Guelphs — still appear in Italian civic heraldry. Charles I of Anjou's intervention in Italy in the 1260s decisively tipped the balance in favour of the Guelph cause.
