The Prime Minister of France (French: Premier ministre de la République française) is the head of government of the French Republic and the leader of the Council of Ministers. The office is appointed by the President of the Republic, who cannot dismiss a prime minister directly but can request a resignation; in practice the prime minister must command the confidence of the National Assembly, which can force the government out through a motion of censure. The prime minister's official residence and office is the Hôtel Matignon in Paris.

Role under the Fifth Republic

The Constitution of 1958 defines the division of power: the President determines the broad direction of policy, while, under Article 20, "the Government shall determine and conduct the policy of the Nation", and under Article 21 the prime minister "shall direct the actions of the Government", is responsible for national defence and ensures the implementation of laws. The prime minister proposes the other ministers for appointment, countersigns most presidential acts, and can engage the government's responsibility on a bill — most controversially through Article 49.3, which allows a bill to pass without a vote unless a censure motion succeeds.

When the president's party lacks a parliamentary majority, France has experienced "cohabitation" (1986–88, 1993–95, 1997–2002), in which the prime minister, drawn from the opposing majority, exercises most domestic authority. Michel Rocard, Édith Cresson (the first woman to hold the office, in 1991), Lionel Jospin and Jean Castex are among recent holders; the position's occupants have frequently gone on to contest the presidency.

History

The office developed from the "President of the Council of Ministers" (président du Conseil), created in 1815 and the effective head of government under the Third and Fourth Republics, though the title "prime minister" only became official with the Fifth Republic in 1958–59, when Michel Debré took office. Under the Fourth Republic the premiership was dominant but unstable, with governments averaging about six months; the Fifth Republic was designed to strengthen the executive through the presidency, leaving the prime minister in a position sometimes described as the president's "fuse" — absorbing unpopularity on the government's behalf.
