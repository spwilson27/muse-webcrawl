World-systems theory

World-systems theory (or world-systems analysis) is an approach to world history and social change, developed principally by the sociologist Immanuel Wallerstein from the 1970s, which treats the modern world-system — a single capitalist world-economy that emerged in Europe around the 16th century — rather than individual nation-states, as the proper unit of analysis.

Core concepts

The system is structured by an unequal division of labour between core regions, which concentrate high-profit, capital-intensive production and strong states; peripheral regions, supplying raw materials and cheap labour through coercive mechanisms; and semi-peripheral regions, which combine features of both and stabilise the system politically. Surplus flows from periphery to core through unequal exchange, reproducing global inequality. Wallerstein also described cyclical rhythms of expansion and stagnation and successive hegemonies — Dutch, British and American — within the system.

Influences and criticism

The approach builds on Marxism, the French Annales school (especially Fernand Braudel), and Latin American dependency theory. It influenced global history, international relations and development studies. Critics charge it with economic determinism, underplaying culture and internal class relations, and with difficulty explaining the rise of East Asian economies except by reclassifying them as semi-peripheral. Later work by Wallerstein and collaborators extended the framework to a predicted long-term crisis and transition of the capitalist world-system.
