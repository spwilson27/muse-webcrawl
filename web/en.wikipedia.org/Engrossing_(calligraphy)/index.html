Engrossing is the practice of producing formal documents — such as diplomas, certificates, resolutions, deeds and memorials — in fine, decorated calligraphy. In legal usage, to engross a document is to write out its final, official copy in a large, clear hand (from the French en gros, "in large"); in the nineteenth-century United States, engrossing developed into a distinct penmanship profession.

Practice

Engrossers worked by hand with flexible pointed pens, producing shaded scripts derived from English round hand and copperplate, often called Engrosser's script or Engraver's script in its printed form. Documents were embellished with flourished capitals, ornamental headings in Old English or blackletter styles, drawn borders, and sometimes painted heraldic devices, seals and illuminated initials. Skilled practitioners, known as engrossing penmen, staffed studios in cities such as New York and worked for universities, fraternal organisations, government bodies and businesses.

Decline and survival

The typewriter, photolithography and computer typesetting displaced commercial engrossing during the twentieth century, but the tradition survives in the hand-engrossed official documents still produced for ceremonial purposes — for example, the work of the calligraphers who prepare documents for the White House and for institutions such as the United States Congress, where enrolled bills and commemorative resolutions may still be engrossed by hand.
