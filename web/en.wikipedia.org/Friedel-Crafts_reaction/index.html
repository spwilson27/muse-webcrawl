Friedel–Crafts reaction
Named afterCharles Friedel, James Crafts
Reaction typeElectrophilic aromatic substitution
Discovered1877

The Friedel–Crafts reactions are a set of reactions developed by Charles Friedel and James Crafts in 1877 to attach substituents to an aromatic ring. The two principal reaction types are Friedel–Crafts alkylation and Friedel–Crafts acylation, both proceeding by electrophilic aromatic substitution and typically catalysed by a strong Lewis acid such as aluminium chloride.

Alkylation

In Friedel–Crafts alkylation, an alkyl halide reacts with an aromatic ring in the presence of a Lewis acid, attaching an alkyl group. The reaction suffers from drawbacks: the product is more reactive than the starting material, so polyalkylation is common, and carbocation intermediates can rearrange. It also fails on strongly deactivated rings.

Acylation

In Friedel–Crafts acylation, an acyl chloride or acid anhydride reacts to install an acyl group, forming an aryl ketone. The ketone product deactivates the ring, so the reaction stops after one substitution, and no rearrangement occurs. The resulting ketone can be reduced to an alkyl group by Clemmensen or Wolff–Kishner reduction, making acylation a reliable indirect route to alkylbenes.

Applications

Friedel–Crafts chemistry underpins large-scale industrial processes, including the production of ethylbenzene and cumene, and remains a staple of synthetic organic chemistry for constructing substituted aromatic compounds.
