Tessellation

A tessellation is the covering of a surface, often a plane, using one or more geometric shapes called tiles, with no overlaps and no gaps. In mathematics, tessellations can be generalized to higher dimensions and a variety of geometries. The word derives from tessella, the small square tiles used in Roman mosaics.

Regular tessellations

Only three regular tessellations of the plane use a single regular polygon: equilateral triangles, squares, and regular hexagons. Eight further semi-regular (Archimedean) tessellations combine two or more kinds of regular polygons with the same arrangement at every vertex.

In art and architecture

Tessellations appear in Islamic geometric art, in the mosaics of the Alhambra, and in the work of M. C. Escher, whose interlocking animals and figures remain the best-known artistic examples. In nature, honeycombs form a hexagonal tessellation, and columnar basalt such as the Giant's Causeway approximates one.

Aperiodic tilings

Some tile sets can cover the plane only non-periodically. Roger Penrose's tilings of the 1970s, and the single "hat" einstein tile announced in 2023, are famous examples of aperiodic tessellations.
