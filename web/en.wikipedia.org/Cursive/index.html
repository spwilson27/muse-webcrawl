Cursive

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


English cursive handwriting

Cursive (also known as joined-up writing, script, or longhand) is any style of penmanship in which characters are written joined in a flowing manner, generally to make writing faster, in contrast to block letters. The word derives from the Latin cursivus, "running".

History and styles

Rapid, ligatured hands existed in Roman and medieval writing. Early modern Europe developed distinct national hands — English round hand (copperplate), Spencerian script in 19th-century America, and the Palmer Method for business writing. German writers used Kurrent and later Sütterlin until the 1940s. Standardised school models include D'Nealian and Zaner-Bloser in the United States; Russian and other Cyrillic cursives differ markedly from print forms, as do cursive traditions in Arabic (an inherently joined script), Chinese (grass script), and many other writing systems.

Education debate

With the spread of keyboards, the place of cursive in schools became contested. The US Common Core standards (2010) did not require cursive, and many districts dropped it; from the mid-2010s a number of states reinstated cursive instruction, citing benefits for fine motor skills, reading development and access to historical documents. Cursive remains required for personal signatures in many contexts.
