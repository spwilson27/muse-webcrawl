Jewish resistance in German-occupied Europe


Jewish resistance in German-occupied Europe took forms from armed revolt and partisan warfare to rescue, documentation, and spiritual defiance.

Jewish resistance in German-occupied Europe refers to the organised and individual efforts by Jews to oppose Nazi persecution during the Holocaust. It encompassed armed uprisings in ghettos and camps, partisan warfare, escapes and rescue networks, smuggling of food and information, clandestine education and religious observance, and the documentation of Nazi crimes.

Ghetto uprisings

The largest armed revolt was the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of April–May 1943, led by the Jewish Combat Organization (ŻOB) and the Jewish Military Union (ŻZW), which held off German forces for nearly a month. Uprisings also occurred in the Białystok, Częstochowa, Będzin, and Vilna ghettos, and resistance organisations existed in Kraków, Kovno, and Minsk, where Jews escaped to join Soviet partisans in the forests.

Camp revolts

Prisoners rose in revolt at three extermination camps: at Treblinka (August 1943) and Sobibór (October 1943), hundreds escaped and Sobibór was closed by the Germans afterwards; at Auschwitz-Birkenau, members of the Sonderkommando revolted in October 1944 and destroyed one crematorium.

Partisans and rescue

Tens of thousands of Jews fought in partisan units, including the all-Jewish Bielski partisans in Belarus, who saved some 1,200 Jews in a forest community. Others joined national resistance movements in France, Yugoslavia, Greece, and elsewhere. Rescue efforts included the clandestine networks of Żegota in Poland, children's rescue by the Œuvre de secours aux enfants in France, and couriers such as those who smuggled reports of the extermination to the West. Historians also recognise unarmed "spiritual resistance" — secret schools, archives such as Emanuel Ringelblum's Oyneg Shabes, and the maintenance of religious and cultural life — as an essential part of the story.
