Shaoxing wine (Chinese: 紹興酒; pinyin: Shàoxīng jiǔ) is a variety of huangjiu — Chinese fermented rice wine — produced in and around the city of Shaoxing in Zhejiang province. Regarded as the most famous and prestigious style of huangjiu, it is made from glutinous rice, wheat-based starter (qu), and water from Jianhu (Mirror Lake), and is traditionally aged in sealed earthenware jars, sometimes for decades. Shaoxing wine is consumed warm as a beverage and is indispensable as a cooking ingredient in Chinese cuisine, used to deodorize seafood and meat, in marinades, red-cooked dishes, and drunken preparations.

History

Rice wine production in the Shaoxing area is traced back more than 2,500 years to the Spring and Autumn period; legend holds that King Goujian of Yue rewarded his soldiers with wine before the conquest of Wu. The wine's reputation grew through the Song and Ming dynasties, and by the Qing dynasty "Shaoxing" had become almost synonymous with fine rice wine throughout China. It was traditionally sent to the imperial court as tribute, and jars were buried at the birth of a daughter and dug up at her wedding — the origin of the names of the nü'er hong ("daughter red") style.

Production

Brewing begins in winter, when cold temperatures favour a slow, clean fermentation. Polished glutinous rice is soaked in Jianhu lake water, steamed, cooled, and mixed with wheat qu (containing Aspergillus and yeast) and a portion of aged wine as a starter. After primary fermentation in open vats — with regular stirring ("kai ba") to aerate the mash — the young wine is pressed, clarified, heated to pasteurize it, and sealed in earthenware jars coated with mud and lotus leaves for ageing. Fine grades may age for ten years or more, developing an amber colour and a complex, slightly nutty, umami-rich flavour with alcohol typically 15–20%.

Styles

  • Yuanhong (狀元紅, "champion red") — the standard dry style, named for jars painted red for successful examination candidates.
  • Jiafan (加飯, "added rice") — made with extra rice in the mash, richer and slightly sweeter, about 18% alcohol.
  • Huadiao (花雕, "flower carving") — aged wine sold in ornately carved or painted jars, traditionally the buried wedding wine of a daughter.
  • Shanniang (善釀) — a sweeter style in which aged wine replaces water in brewing.
  • Xiangxue (香雪, "fragrant snow") — fortified with distilled spirit, sweetest and strongest.

Culinary use

Shaoxing wine is a cornerstone of Chinese cooking: it is splashed into stir-fries, used in red-cooked (hongshao) pork and fish, in the marinade for drunken chicken and drunken shrimp, in dumpling fillings, and to neutralize the gaminess of meats and the muddiness of freshwater fish. Outside China, dry sherry is the usual recommended substitute where the wine is unavailable; bottles marked "cooking wine" in export markets are often salted and of lower quality than drinking grades.
