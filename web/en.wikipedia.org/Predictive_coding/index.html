Predictive coding

Predictive coding (also known as predictive processing) is a theory of brain function in which the brain is constantly generating and updating a model of the environment, used to predict incoming sensory signals. Only the mismatch between predictions and actual input — prediction error — needs to be passed up the cortical hierarchy for further processing, making the scheme an efficient form of coding.

Framework

The theory draws on ideas from signal processing, where predictive coding removes redundancy by transmitting only deviations from a predicted signal, and from Bayesian inference: higher cortical levels send predictions downward, lower levels return precision-weighted prediction errors, and perception is the resulting best explanation of the input. Karl Friston's free-energy principle generalises the scheme, proposing that organisms act to minimise surprise.

Evidence and applications

Predictive coding has been invoked to explain phenomena such as repetition suppression, mismatch negativity, binocular rivalry and certain accounts of autism and schizophrenia in terms of aberrant precision weighting. Hierarchical predictive models proposed by Rajesh Rao and Dana Ballard (1999) for visual cortex were an influential early formulation. Related ideas appear in machine learning, including predictive coding networks proposed as biologically plausible alternatives to backpropagation.
