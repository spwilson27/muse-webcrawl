Adaptive Coloration in Animals

Book
Author:Hugh B. Cott
Published:1940 (Methuen)
Subject:Camouflage, mimicry, animal coloration

Adaptive Coloration in Animals is a 1940 book by the English zoologist Hugh B. Cott. It is a comprehensive treatment of camouflage, warning coloration (aposematism), and mimicry in animals, written in the tradition of Edward Bagnall Poulton's The Colours of Animals (1890) and drawing in part on the ideas of the American artist-naturalist Abbott Handerson Thayer. The book remains a classic of evolutionary biology and of the scientific study of military camouflage.

Content

Cott organised animal coloration by function: concealment (crypsis), advertisement, and disguise. He explained principles of cryptic coloration including background matching, disruptive coloration breaking up an animal's outline, countershading (which he, following Thayer, emphasised as a means of flattening the appearance of a three-dimensional body), and the reduction of shadow. For warning coloration, he surveyed aposematic patterns and both Batesian and Müllerian mimicry. He also described "special protective resemblance" — masquerade as leaves, twigs, or droppings — and coloration used in courtship and threat displays.

Context and influence

Published during the Second World War, the book informed British camouflage work, in which Cott himself was involved as a camouflage expert in the Middle East. Its copious illustrations, from Cott's own photographs and drawings, set a standard for natural history writing. Modern textbooks on animal coloration treat it as the foundational mid-century synthesis, preceding later experimental work that tested its hypotheses on predation and survival.
