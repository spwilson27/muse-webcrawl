Bonsai

Origin: Japan (from Chinese penjing)

Meaning: "Tray planting" (盆栽)

Techniques: Pruning, wiring, root reduction

Bonsai (Japanese: 盆栽, literally "tray planting") is the Japanese art of growing and shaping miniature trees in containers. A bonsai is not a genetically dwarfed plant: any tree species can be used and is kept small through pot confinement, crown and root pruning, and shaping with wire. Specimens may live for centuries — some celebrated trees are estimated to be over a thousand years old.

History

The practice descends from the Chinese art of penjing, in which miniature landscapes and trees were cultivated as early as the Tang dynasty. Japanese Zen Buddhist monks brought the art to Japan, where by the Edo period it had become a refined pastime of the aristocracy and samurai class. Bonsai reached the West through exhibitions in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and spread worldwide after the Second World War.

Cultivation and aesthetics

Growers aim to reproduce the character of a mature tree in miniature, following style categories such as formal upright (chokkan), informal upright (moyogi), slanting (shakan), cascade (kengai), and forest plantings (yose-ue). Trees are displayed in shallow ceramic pots chosen to complement the plant, often on a stand with an accent element. Regular repotting, careful watering, and seasonal feeding are essential, since the restricted root volume dries quickly.

Popular species

Japanese maple, Japanese black pine, Chinese elm, juniper, and ficus are among the most widely used species. Indoor bonsai are usually tropical species such as ficus, while temperate trees require an outdoor winter dormancy period.
