London equations

The Meissner effect, explained phenomenologically by the London equations

The London equations, developed by brothers Fritz and Heinz London in 1935, are phenomenological relations that describe the electromagnetic behaviour of a superconductor. They were the first successful theoretical account of superconductivity, explaining both perfect conductivity and the Meissner effect — the expulsion of magnetic fields from a superconductor's interior.

The equations

The first London equation describes the acceleration of superconducting electrons by an electric field, implying that a steady current flows without resistance:

E = ∂/∂t (Λ J), where Λ = m / (ns e²)

The second equation describes the supercurrent's response to a magnetic vector potential:

∇ × (Λ J) = −B

Combined with Maxwell's equations, the second equation implies that a magnetic field decays exponentially inside a superconductor over a characteristic distance, the London penetration depth:

∇²B = B / λL², λL = √( m / (μ₀ ns e²) )

Significance and limitations

The equations correctly predict the Meissner effect and the exponential screening of magnetic fields, and they introduce the superfluid density ns as a fundamental quantity. They are, however, purely phenomenological: they assume the existence of a dissipationless carrier fluid rather than deriving it. A microscopic explanation came only with BCS theory (1957), and the London equations can be derived as a limiting case of the more general Ginzburg–Landau theory near surfaces and for slowly varying fields. The London equations also lead to the prediction of flux quantization in superconducting rings, confirmed experimentally in 1961.
