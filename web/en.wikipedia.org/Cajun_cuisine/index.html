Cajun cuisine is a style of cooking developed by the Cajun–Acadians who were deported from Acadia and settled in South Louisiana in the 18th century. Rooted in French country cooking and adapted to local ingredients — rice, crawfish, wild game, pork and peppers — it is characterised by robust, rustic one-pot dishes, in contrast to the more refined urban cooking of Louisiana Creole cuisine.

Foundations

Many Cajun dishes begin with a dark roux of flour and fat, cooked slowly to a rich brown, and with the "holy trinity" of chopped onion, celery and bell pepper, the local analogue of French mirepoix. Seasoning relies on cayenne pepper, black pepper, garlic and herbs. Meals are often cooked communally, exemplified by the crawfish boil and the boucherie, a traditional communal hog butchering.

Signature dishes

Gumbo combines meat or shellfish with okra or filé powder in a thick, dark stew served over rice. Jambalaya cooks rice directly with meat, sausage and vegetables in a single pot. Étouffée "smothers" crawfish or shrimp in a roux-based sauce. Other staples include boudin sausage, cracklins, sauce piquante, maque choux and rice dressing ("dirty rice"). Desserts include pecan pralines and beignets, the latter shared with Creole cooking.

History and popularisation

The cuisine developed in the isolated bayou and prairie parishes of Acadiana. It achieved national fame in the 1980s through chefs such as Paul Prudhomme, whose "blackened" redfish craze popularised Cajun seasoning — and briefly threatened the redfish fishery. The popular style of that era was often spicier and more heavily seasoned than traditional home cooking, leading to an enduring distinction between authentic and commercialised "Cajun" food.
