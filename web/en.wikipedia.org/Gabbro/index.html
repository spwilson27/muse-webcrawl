TypeIgneous rock, mafic, intrusive (plutonic)
CompositionPlagioclase feldspar (labradorite–bytownite), clinopyroxene (augite), olivine, ± orthopyroxene

Gabbro is a phaneritic (coarse-grained), mafic intrusive igneous rock formed from the slow cooling of magnesium-rich and iron-rich magma into a holocrystalline mass deep beneath the Earth's surface. Slow-cooling, deep crustal gabbro is the plutonic equivalent of the volcanic basalt, which is its fine-grained, rapidly cooled equivalent. Much of the Earth's oceanic crust is made of gabbro, formed at mid-ocean ridges.

Mineralogy and classification

Gabbro consists mostly of the calcium-rich plagioclase feldspar (usually labradorite to bytownite) and clinopyroxene (augite). Minor amounts of olivine and orthopyroxene may also be present, with accessory chromite, magnetite and ilmenite. On the QAPF classification gabbro contains less than 10% quartz and is distinguished from diorite by the An (anorthite) content of its plagioclase — gabbro's plagioclase is more calcic. Related rocks include norite (orthopyroxene-rich) and troctolite (olivine-rich), while a gabbro with very large plagioclase crystals may be called gabbro pegmatite.

Occurrence

Gabbro is found in layered intrusions produced by crystal settling in large magma chambers, famously in the Bushveld Complex of South Africa, the Stillwater Complex of Montana, the Skaergaard intrusion of Greenland and the Rum intrusion of Scotland. It also forms the lower part of ophiolite sequences, representing ancient oceanic crust thrust onto land, as in the Troodos Mountains of Cyprus and in Oman.

Uses

Because of its hardness and ability to take a polish, gabbro is sold commercially as "black granite" for dimension stone, gravestones, kitchen worktops and paving. Crushed gabbro is used as aggregate. Layered gabbroic intrusions host important deposits of chromium, nickel, platinum-group elements and vanadium.
