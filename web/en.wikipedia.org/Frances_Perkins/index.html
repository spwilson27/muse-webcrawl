Frances Perkins (born Fannie Coralie Perkins; April 10, 1880 – May 14, 1965) was an American social reformer and politician who served as the United States Secretary of Labor from 1933 to 1945, the longest-serving person in that post. A lifelong Democrat, she was the first woman to serve in a presidential cabinet — and the first woman to serve as a cabinet secretary in American history.

As a loyal supporter of her friend, President Franklin D. Roosevelt, Perkins helped make labor issues important in the emerging New Deal coalition. She was one of two Roosevelt cabinet members to remain in office for his entire presidency (the other being Interior Secretary Harold L. Ickes). Her most important work came as chair of the President's Committee on Economic Security, in which she spearheaded the adoption of the Social Security Act of 1935.

Early life and reform work

Perkins was born in Boston and raised in Worcester, Massachusetts. She graduated from Mount Holyoke College in 1902 and later earned a master's degree in political science from Columbia University. She worked in settlement houses in Chicago (including Hull House) and Philadelphia, taught sociology, and became executive secretary of the New York City Consumers League, lobbying for better working hours and conditions for women and children.

Perkins was an eyewitness to the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire of 25 March 1911, in which 146 garment workers died — an event that profoundly shaped her career. She subsequently served on New York's Factory Investigating Commission and worked closely with Al Smith and Robert F. Wagner to enact pioneering workplace safety and labor legislation. As New York State Industrial Commissioner under Governor Franklin D. Roosevelt (1929–1933), she oversaw the state's labor department during the onset of the Great Depression.

Secretary of Labor

Roosevelt appointed Perkins Secretary of Labor in 1933; she served until 1945. Before accepting, she presented Roosevelt with a list of policies she intended to pursue — including a 40-hour work week, a minimum wage, unemployment insurance, old-age insurance, and the abolition of child labor — and he agreed. Her tenure saw the creation of the Civilian Conservation Corps, the Public Works Administration's labor standards, the National Labor Relations Act's labour protections, the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 (establishing a minimum wage, overtime pay, and restrictions on child labour), and, above all, the Social Security system.

After leaving the cabinet in 1945, Perkins served on the United States Civil Service Commission until 1952 and taught at Cornell University until her death in 1965. The Frances Perkins Building in Washington, D.C., headquarters of the Department of Labor, is named for her.
