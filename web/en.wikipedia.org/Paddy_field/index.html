Paddy field

A paddy field (or paddy) is a flooded field of arable land used for growing semiaquatic crops, most notably rice and taro. It originates in the Neolithic rice-farming cultures of the Yangtze River basin in southern China and spread throughout East, Southeast and South Asia. Paddy cultivation requires abundant water and labour, and the fields are typically bounded by low earthen bunds that hold the water at a controlled depth.

Cultivation

Seedlings are usually raised in a nursery and transplanted by hand into the flooded, levelled field. The standing water suppresses most weeds, provides nutrients as it decomposes algae and organic matter, and maintains a stable temperature around the roots. Fields are drained before harvest so the crop can be cut, traditionally by hand with a sickle. On hillsides, paddies are constructed as elaborate terraces, such as the rice terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras and of Yuanyang in China.

Ecology

Flooded paddies are significant sources of atmospheric methane produced by anaerobic bacteria in the waterlogged soil, contributing a notable share of anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions. At the same time they form important artificial wetlands supporting fish, frogs, snails and water birds; traditional systems often raise fish or ducks within the paddies. Alternate wetting and drying irrigation has been promoted to reduce both water use and methane emissions.

History

Archaeological evidence from sites such as Hemudu and Tianluoshan indicates rice paddy cultivation in the lower Yangtze valley by about 5000 BCE. From China the technique spread to Korea, Japan, mainland and island Southeast Asia, and eventually to the Americas and southern Europe, where it persists in regions such as the Po Valley of Italy and the Camargue of France.
