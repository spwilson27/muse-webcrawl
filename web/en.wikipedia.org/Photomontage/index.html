Photomontage

Photomontage
MediumCut-and-pasted photographic prints, or digital compositing
Related formsCollage, combination printing, photocollage
Key movementsDada, Surrealism, Soviet avant-garde, Pop art
Notable artistsHannah Höch, John Heartfield, Alexander Rodchenko, Raoul Hausmann

Photomontage is the process and result of making a composite photograph by cutting, gluing, rearranging, and overlapping two or more photographs into a new image. Sometimes the finished composite is itself photographed so that it appears seamless. The technique ranges from whimsical Victorian combination prints to sharply political works of the 20th-century avant-garde, and survives today in digital compositing.

History

Combination printing was practised almost as soon as photography existed. In the 1850s, Oscar Rejlander assembled The Two Ways of Life (1857) from more than 30 negatives, and Henry Peach Robinson built narrative scenes from multiple negatives. Victorian amateurs created playful photocollages in albums, and trick photography — headless portraits, spirit photographs — entertained the public.

Dada and political montage

Berlin Dada artists — Hannah Höch, Raoul Hausmann, John Heartfield, and George Grosz — seized on photomontage around 1918 as an anti-art weapon, splicing mass-media images into fractured, satirical compositions. Heartfield's covers for the magazine AIZ in the 1930s became famous attacks on fascism. In the Soviet Union, Alexander Rodchenko and El Lissitzky used bold montage in posters and book design, while in Weimar and later exile, montage fed into advertising and film title design.

Later developments

Surrealists such as Man Ray and Max Ernst used montage to conjure dreamlike juxtapositions. After 1945 the technique permeated Pop art (Richard Hamilton's 1956 collage is a landmark), punk graphics (Jamie Reid's Sex Pistols sleeves), and hip-hop sampling aesthetics. Feminist artists such as Martha Rosler used montage to expose connections between domestic imagery and war reporting.

Digital era

Software such as Adobe Photoshop (released 1990) made seamless compositing routine, blurring photomontage into everyday image editing and raising the same questions about photographic truth that 19th-century combination printers first provoked.
