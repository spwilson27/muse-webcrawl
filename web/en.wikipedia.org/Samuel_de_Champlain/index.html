Samuel de Champlain (c. 1567 – 25 December 1635) was a French navigator, cartographer, explorer and administrator who founded Quebec City on 3 July 1608 and is widely regarded as the "Father of New France".

Explorations

Born at Hiers-Brouage on France's Atlantic coast, Champlain first sailed to Canada in 1603, exploring the St. Lawrence River. He helped found the ill-fated settlement at Port-Royal in Acadia (1605) before establishing a permanent habitation at Quebec in 1608. In 1609 he explored and named Lake Champlain and joined his Huron and Algonquin allies in battle against the Iroquois near Ticonderoga — an alliance that shaped French–Indigenous relations for generations.

New France

Over more than two dozen Atlantic crossings, Champlain mapped the Atlantic coast and the Great Lakes region, promoted the fur trade, and worked to build a lasting French colony. Quebec fell to English privateers in 1629 and Champlain was taken to England, but Canada was restored to France by treaty in 1632 and he returned as the colony's effective governor. He died at Quebec on Christmas Day 1635.

Legacy

Lake Champlain, which straddles the modern Canada–US border, bears his name, as do numerous towns, streets and institutions in Quebec and the northeastern United States. His published accounts and maps are foundational sources for the early history of Canada.
