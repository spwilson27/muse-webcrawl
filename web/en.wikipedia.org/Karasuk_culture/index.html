Karasuk culture

[Image: Karasuk bronze knives and artefacts from the Minusinsk Basin]

The Karasuk culture (Russian: Карасукская культура) was a Late Bronze Age society that flourished from about 1500 to 800 BCE in South Siberia and Central Asia, in the area of the upper Yenisei River (the Minusinsk Basin) and extending toward western Mongolia and the Altai. It is named after the Karasuk River, where characteristic burials were first excavated.

Characteristics

Karasuk society was based on mixed farming and stockbreeding, with sheep, cattle and horses, and supplemented by hunting and fishing. Settlements consisted of semi-subterranean houses. The culture is noted for its sophisticated bronze metallurgy: curved knives with sculpted animal-head pommels, daggers, celts and mirrors, some showing affinities with Shang and early Zhou dynasty bronzes of northern China, suggesting long-distance contacts across the steppe.

Burials and origins

The dead were buried in stone-box graves within square stone enclosures, accompanied by pottery, bronze ornaments and animal offerings. The Karasuk culture developed out of the preceding Okunev and Andronovo traditions with additional influences from the east; anthropologists have long debated the population's composition, as skeletal material shows a mixture of Europoid and Mongoloid features. It was succeeded in the region by the Iron Age Tagar culture.
