Studio album

A studio album is an album comprising tracks recorded in a recording studio, as contrasted with a live album, compilation, or remix. Studio albums are the primary format through which popular musicians present new original material, and the term became standard with the rise of the long-playing record in the mid-20th century.

History

The earliest studio albums were collections of 78 rpm discs bound in book-like albums. The introduction of the vinyl LP by Columbia Records in 1948 allowed a continuous sequence of songs to be conceived as a single artistic statement. By the 1960s, artists such as the Beatles and Bob Dylan treated the studio album as a unified work, a development often called the album era.

Recording process

Studio albums are typically produced over weeks or months of sessions involving recording, overdubbing, mixing, and mastering. A record producer oversees the process, shaping arrangements and performances. Advances such as multitrack recording, synthesizers, and digital audio workstations have greatly expanded what can be achieved in the studio.

Cultural significance

Critics and listeners often regard the studio album as the definitive measure of a recording artist's work. Lists of the greatest albums of all time focus almost exclusively on studio albums, and landmark releases have influenced fashion, politics, and other art forms.
