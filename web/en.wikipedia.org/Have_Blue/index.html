Lockheed Have Blue

Manufacturer: Lockheed Skunk Works
First flight: 1 December 1977
Number built: 2
Role: Stealth technology demonstrator

Have Blue was the code name for Lockheed's proof-of-concept demonstrator for a stealth fighter. Developed by the Skunk Works and flown in secret at Groom Lake (Area 51) from 1977, it validated the faceted design approach to drastically reducing radar cross-section. Its success led directly to the F-117 Nighthawk, the first operational stealth aircraft.

Development

The design drew on the 1964 paper by Soviet physicist Pyotr Ufimtsev on the diffraction of electromagnetic waves, which the Skunk Works team under Denys Overholser and Ben Rich realised could predict the radar signature of faceted shapes. Because 1970s computers could not model curved surfaces, the aircraft was shaped entirely from flat panels angled to deflect radar energy away from its source. The project was funded by DARPA, initially under the code name "Hopeless Diamond" for its improbable shape.

Design and testing

Two aircraft (serials 1001 and 1002) were built at about 60 percent of the F-117's size, using existing components such as General Electric J85 engines from the T-2 Buckeye. The Have Blue aircraft were inherently unstable and relied on a fly-by-wire system adapted from the F-16. Test flights began on 1 December 1977 with pilot Bill Park. Both prototypes were lost in accidents—the first in 1980 and the second in 1985—though both pilots survived.

Legacy

Have Blue proved that a practical aircraft could have a radar cross-section small enough to be nearly invisible to contemporary air defences. The programme remained classified until the F-117 was publicly revealed in 1988. The surviving wreckage of the first prototype is buried at Groom Lake; the second was reportedly later displayed.
