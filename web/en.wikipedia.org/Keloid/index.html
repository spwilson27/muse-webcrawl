Keloid

A keloid is a type of raised scar resulting from an overgrowth of dense fibrous tissue after a skin injury has healed. Unlike an ordinary (hypertrophic) scar, a keloid grows beyond the boundaries of the original wound, does not regress spontaneously, and tends to recur after removal. Keloids are benign but can be itchy, painful and cosmetically disfiguring.

Causes and risk factors

Keloids arise when fibroblasts produce excessive collagen — particularly type III collagen — during and after wound healing, a process influenced by transforming growth factor beta and genetic predisposition. They may follow surgery, ear piercing, acne, cuts, burns or even minor insect bites. Keloids are markedly more common in people with darker skin, including those of African, Asian and Hispanic ancestry, and often run in families. Common sites include the earlobes, shoulders, upper back, chest and jawline.

Signs and course

A keloid appears as a smooth, shiny, firm lump ranging from skin-coloured to pink, red or dark brown. It grows slowly over months or years, extending into surrounding normal skin with claw-like margins — the feature from which its name derives (Greek khēlē, crab's claw). Keloids do not shrink with time and frequently itch or become tender.

Treatment

Treatment is difficult and recurrence rates are high. Options include silicone gel sheeting, pressure therapy, intralesional corticosteroid injections (often combined with 5-fluorouracil), cryotherapy, laser treatment, and surgical excision — usually combined with injections or radiotherapy to reduce recurrence. Prevention in susceptible people includes avoiding elective piercings and tattoos and prompt silicone treatment of new scars.
