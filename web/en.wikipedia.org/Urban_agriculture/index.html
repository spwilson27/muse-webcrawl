Urban agriculture

Urban agriculture is the practice of cultivating, processing, and distributing food in or around urban areas. Urban agriculture also involves animal husbandry, aquaculture, beekeeping, and horticulture. These activities also occur in peri-urban areas, but urban agriculture generally means production within cities themselves.

Forms

Urban agriculture takes many forms: community gardens and allotments; rooftop gardens and greenhouses; backyard gardens; vertical farms and indoor hydroponic or aquaponic facilities; school and institutional gardens; and guerrilla gardening on vacant land. Commercial urban farms sell fresh produce to restaurants and local markets, while community projects emphasize food security, education, and neighbourhood cohesion.

History

City farming is ancient — Aztec chinampas and the market gardens surrounding pre-industrial cities are early examples. Wartime "victory gardens" in the 1940s produced a large share of vegetables in Allied countries. In the Global South, urban and peri-urban agriculture has long supplied a significant fraction of city food needs; Havana's organopónicos, developed after the collapse of Soviet imports in the 1990s, became a celebrated model. Interest surged again after the 2007–08 food price crisis and during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Benefits and challenges

Advocates cite shorter supply chains, fresher produce, reduced food miles, stormwater management, urban greening and cooling, and improved food access in underserved neighbourhoods. Challenges include soil contamination with heavy metals, competition for land, water costs, zoning restrictions, and the limited scale of production relative to city consumption. Policy responses include urban-agriculture zoning ordinances, composting programmes, and support for farmers' markets.
