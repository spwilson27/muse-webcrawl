Downcycling

Downcycling is the process of converting materials and products into new materials or products of lower quality and reduced functionality than the original. It is the most common form of recycling: because processes such as re-melting, shredding, or repulping tend to degrade or contaminate materials, the output can usually serve only less demanding applications, and after one or more cycles the material is eventually lost to landfill or incineration. The term contrasts with upcycling, in which waste is converted into products of higher value.

Examples

Most plastic recycling is downcycling: mixed or degraded polymers from bottles are turned into fleece jackets, plastic lumber, or park benches that cannot themselves be recycled into bottles. Paper fibres shorten with each repulping, so fine office paper cascades down into cardboard and tissue before the fibres are unusable. Glass and metals come closer to true closed-loop recycling, though contamination still causes losses; construction concrete is commonly crushed into aggregate for roadbeds, a classic downcycle. Even in metals, alloy mixing can downgrade material — for example, high-grade aluminium alloys contaminated with other metals can only be reused in lower-specification castings.

Discussion

The concept was introduced by engineer Reiner Pilz in 1994, who complained that European demolition rules "recycle" buildings by crushing them, and was popularised by William McDonough and Michael Braungart in their book Cradle to Cradle (2002), which argues that downcycled products may actually spread contaminants (for example, toxins in recycled plastics) and delays rather than prevents disposal. Advocates of circular-economy design respond with design for disassembly, mono-materials, and material passports intended to preserve quality so that genuine closed loops become possible. Defenders of conventional recycling note that even downcycling usually saves energy and resources compared with virgin production.
