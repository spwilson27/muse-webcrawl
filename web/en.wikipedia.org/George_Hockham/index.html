George Alfred Hockham (7 December 1938 – 16 September 2013) was a British engineer who, with Charles K. Kao, first proposed the use of optical fibres for long-distance telecommunications.

Career

Hockham worked at Standard Telecommunication Laboratories (STL) in Harlow, Essex. As a young engineer in the mid-1960s he was assigned to work with Kao on finding a new transmission medium to replace copper wire for telephone traffic.

The 1966 paper

In 1966 Kao and Hockham published their landmark paper, "Dielectric-fibre surface waveguides for optical frequencies", arguing that glass fibres could carry light over long distances if impurities could be reduced so that attenuation fell below 20 decibels per kilometre. The proposal laid the theoretical foundation for fibre-optic communication, which now underpins global telecommunications networks. Kao received the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work; Hockham, who died in 2013, was recognised by colleagues as its co-originator.

Later work

Hockham later worked on antenna theory and electromagnetic propagation, and spent part of his later career as a technical director and professor associated with Queen Mary University of London.
