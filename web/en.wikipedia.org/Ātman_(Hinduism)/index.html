Ātman (Hinduism)

Ātman, the innermost Self, is a central concept of the Upanishads

Ātman (Sanskrit: आत्मन्) is a Sanskrit word that refers to the (temporal) Self or the (true) Self, the essence of an individual and the primary subject in some schools of Indian philosophy. In Hindu philosophy, especially in the Vedānta school, Ātman is the first principle: the true self of an individual beyond identification with phenomena, the essence of an individual. To attain liberation (moksha), a human being must acquire self-knowledge (atma jnana), which is to realise that one's true self (Ātman) is identical with the transcendent self Brahman according to some schools.

Ātman is a central concept in all major schools of Hindu philosophy — Samkhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Mimamsa, and Vedanta — though each interprets it differently. In Advaita Vedanta, Ātman is held to be pure consciousness, identical with Brahman and untouched by the empirical world. In Dvaita Vedanta, by contrast, Ātman is an eternal individual soul distinct from Brahman (God).

Etymology

Ātman (आत्मन्) is a Sanskrit word meaning "inner self", "soul", "spirit", or "breath". It is derived from the Proto-Indo-European root *h₁eh₁tmṓ (a root meaning "breath"), and is thus cognate with Old English æthm ("breath") and German Atem. In early Vedic texts, the word appears in the sense of breath or vital essence, and its meaning gradually deepened to denote the innermost essence of a person.

Development of the concept

The earliest use of the word Ātman in Indian texts is found in the Rigveda, where it denotes the vital breath or essence. In the Brahmanas and Aranyakas, Ātman comes to mean the soul or the essence of a being. It reaches philosophical maturity in the early Upanishads, where it becomes one of the great themes of Indian thought. The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad and Chandogya Upanishad teach that Ātman is the self within all beings, smaller than a grain yet greater than the sky, and that realising it brings liberation from the cycle of rebirth (saṃsāra).

"That which is the finest essence — this whole world has that as its soul. That is Reality. That is Ātman. That thou art (Tat tvam asi)." — Chandogya Upanishad

In the schools of Hindu philosophy

Vedanta

The Upanishads form the basis of Vedanta, whose various schools dispute the relation between Ātman and Brahman. Shankara's Advaita (non-dualism) holds that Ātman and Brahman are one and the same reality, and that plurality is the product of ignorance (avidya). Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism) holds that individual selves are real and eternally distinct modes or attributes of Brahman, sharing its nature but not exhausting it. Madhva's Dvaita (dualism) teaches an eternal distinction between the individual soul (jivatman) and God (paramatman).

Yoga and Samkhya

The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali discuss the self in terms of the witness-consciousness (purusha), entangled with material nature (prakriti) through ignorance; liberation consists in discerning the difference between them. Samkhya likewise regards each Ātman (purusha) as eternal, unchanging, pure consciousness, distinct from the evolutes of prakriti.

Nyaya and Vaisheshika

The Nyaya school regards Ātman as the substratum of cognition and desire, arguing for its existence through inference: qualities such as knowledge, pleasure, and pain must inhere in a substance, and that substance is the soul. Vaisheshika similarly classifies Ātman as an eternal substance (dravya).

Relation to Buddhism and Jainism

Buddhism famously rejects the existence of a permanent, unchanging self in the doctrine of anatta (no-self), one of the most significant points of divergence between Buddhism and Hinduism. Jainism, by contrast, affirms the existence of individual souls (jiva), though it conceives them differently from the Ātman of the Upanishads. These debates shaped Indian philosophy for over two millennia.
