Natural hazard

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A natural hazard is a natural phenomenon that might have a negative effect on humans and other animals, or the environment. Natural hazard events can be grouped into two broad categories: geophysical hazards, originating in the solid earth, and biological hazards, involving living organisms. A hazard only becomes a disaster when it intersects with human exposure and vulnerability.

Geological hazards

Geological hazards include earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, tsunamis, landslides, avalanches, subsidence and sinkholes. Earthquakes result from sudden release of energy in the Earth's crust, while volcanic hazards include lava flows, ash fall, pyroclastic flows and lahars. Tsunamis are long ocean waves generated by undersea earthquakes, landslides or eruptions.

Hydrological and meteorological hazards

Water-related hazards include floods, coastal erosion, droughts, and limnic eruptions. Atmospheric hazards include tropical cyclones (hurricanes and typhoons), tornadoes, hailstorms, heat waves, cold waves, wildfires driven by weather, and blizzards. Climate change is altering the frequency and intensity of several of these hazards.

Biological hazards

Biological hazards include disease epidemics and pandemics, insect infestations such as locust swarms, and the spread of invasive species.

Risk assessment and mitigation

Hazard assessment combines the probability of an event with its potential impact. Mitigation strategies include land-use planning, building codes, early-warning systems, insurance, emergency preparedness, and protective infrastructure such as levees and seismic retrofitting. International frameworks such as the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction guide national policy.
