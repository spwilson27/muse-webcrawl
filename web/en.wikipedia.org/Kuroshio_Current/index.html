Kuroshio Current

The Kuroshio (Japanese: 黒潮, "Black Tide" or "Black Current"), also known as the Japan Current, is a strong, warm western boundary current in the western North Pacific Ocean. It is the Pacific analogue of the Gulf Stream, transporting warm tropical water poleward along the coasts of Taiwan and Japan and strongly influencing the climate and marine life of East Asia.

Path

The Kuroshio begins where the North Equatorial Current bifurcates east of the Philippines. It flows north past Taiwan, through the East China Sea along the continental shelf edge, exits through the Tokara Strait, and follows the Pacific coast of Kyushu, Shikoku, and Honshu. Near 35°N, off the Boso Peninsula, it turns east as the Kuroshio Extension, eventually merging into the North Pacific Current.

Physical properties

The current is narrow (about 100 km wide) and fast, with surface speeds reaching 2 m/s. Its clear, nutrient-poor water appears dark blue, hence "black current." South of Japan it alternates between a straight path and a "large meander" south of Honshu — an oscillation with major effects on fisheries and coastal sea level — and it sheds warm-core eddies into the Sea of Japan (via the Tsushima branch) and along its path.

Ecological and climatic importance

The Kuroshio carries tropical species northward, creates rich fishing grounds where it meets the cold Oyashio Current off northern Japan, and keeps southern Japan mild and humid in winter. Historically, castaways and drift voyages on the Kuroshio linked Japan with the wider Pacific, and the current is studied for ocean energy generation, including pilot Kuroshio power projects off Taiwan and Japan.
