Verner's law

Proposed byKarl Verner (1875)
Language familyGermanic (from Proto-Indo-European)
EffectVoicing of fricatives when the PIE accent did not precede

Verner's law is a sound law in historical linguistics describing a pattern of consonant voicing in the Germanic languages. Announced by the Danish linguist Karl Verner in 1875, it explained apparent exceptions to Grimm's law by showing that Proto-Germanic voiceless fricatives became voiced when the immediately preceding syllable did not carry the Proto-Indo-European accent.

The law

Under Grimm's law, PIE voiceless stops became Germanic fricatives (*p, *t, *k, *kʷ → *f, *þ, *x, *xʷ, plus *s). Verner showed that these fricatives instead voiced to *β, *ð, *ɣ, *ɣʷ, and *z when the PIE accent fell on a following syllable. This explains pairs such as English father with a voiced fricative (accent originally on the second syllable) versus brother with a voiceless one.

Consequences

Verner's law created alternations within verb paradigms, known as grammatischer Wechsel, visible in Old English and partly preserved in modern forms such as was/were (from *s → *z → r by rhotacization) and German ziehen/gezogen. Its discovery, grounded in accent, was a landmark in establishing that sound change is conditioned and regular, a principle central to the Neogrammarian school.
