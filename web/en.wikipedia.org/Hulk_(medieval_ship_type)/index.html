The hulk (also holk) was a type of medieval cargo ship of northern Europe, used from about the 10th to the 16th century. Alongside the cog it was one of the principal freight carriers of the Hanseatic trade, and it is depicted on town seals and in manuscript art throughout the Baltic and North Sea world.

Description

The hulk appears to have originated as a river and estuary vessel, possibly in the Frisian and Low Countries region, with a crescent-shaped hull whose planks swept up toward bow and stern in a "banana" profile. Early hulks were clinker-built with a single mast and square sail; later examples grew larger, acquired multiple masts, castles at bow and stern, and a sternpost rudder — replacing earlier side rudders — and came to rival or exceed the cog in size. The name derives from a Greek word for a towed cargo vessel (holkas).

Use and decline

Hulks carried bulk goods of the Hanseatic League and were also requisitioned as warships and transports, including for English expeditions. From the later 15th century the type was progressively displaced by carrack- and carvel-influenced designs with transom sterns, and "hulk" drifted in meaning to denote a worn-out vessel used for storage — the origin of the word's modern sense of a dismasted hull.
