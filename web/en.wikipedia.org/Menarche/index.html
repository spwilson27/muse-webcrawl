Menarche

Menarche (from Ancient Greek mēn, "month", and arkhē, "beginning") is the first occurrence of menstruation in a human female — the first menstrual period. It is a central event of female puberty, signalling that the reproductive system has matured to the point where ovulation and pregnancy become possible, though cycles in the first years are often anovulatory and irregular.

Timing

The average age at menarche in developed countries is around 12 to 13 years, with a normal range roughly between ages 9 and 16. Menarche typically occurs about two years after the start of breast development (thelarche) and near the end of the pubertal growth spurt. Over the last 150 years the average age has fallen by several years in industrialised societies — a secular trend attributed mainly to improved childhood nutrition and health — though it has levelled off in many countries.

Factors influencing timing

Timing is influenced by genetics (mother's age at menarche is a strong predictor), body composition — a critical level of body fat and the hormone leptin are thought to be permissive signals — nutrition, chronic illness and intense athletic training, which can delay it. Menarche before age 8 is one sign of precocious puberty; absence by age 15 warrants medical evaluation.

Cultural significance

Many cultures mark menarche with rites of passage and ceremonies — such as the Navajo Kinaaldá or the Japanese celebration with sekihan (red rice) — reflecting its social meaning as the transition from girlhood to womanhood. In some societies it has also been surrounded by menstrual taboos and restrictions.
