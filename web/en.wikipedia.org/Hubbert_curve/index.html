Hubbert curve

Hubbert curve
Type: Resource production model
Proposed by: M. King Hubbert (1956)
Form: Bell-shaped (logistic derivative)
Application: Oil, gas and mineral depletion

The Hubbert curve is an approximation of the production rate of a resource over time. It is a bell-shaped curve, mathematically the derivative of a logistic function, named after geophysicist M. King Hubbert, who introduced it in 1956 to model the depletion of fossil fuels — most famously predicting that crude oil production in the contiguous United States would peak between 1965 and 1971.

The model

Hubbert reasoned that production of a finite resource starts near zero, rises as discovery and infrastructure expand, and must eventually fall as the resource is depleted, so that cumulative production approaches a fixed total — the area under the curve. Production in the United States did peak in 1970 and declined for decades thereafter, broadly matching Hubbert's prediction, before the shale revolution reversed the trend in the 2000s.

Applications and criticism

Hubbert curves have been applied to global oil ("peak oil"), natural gas, coal, individual metals and even whaling and fisheries. Critics note that the model ignores price, technology, demand changes and new discoveries, which can produce multiple peaks or long plateaus; supporters argue it remains a useful first approximation for mature producing regions. The related technique of Hubbert linearisation estimates total recoverable resource from a plot of production-to-cumulative-production ratios.
