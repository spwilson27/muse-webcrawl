Shikoku (Japanese: 四国, "four provinces") is the smallest and least populous of Japan's four main islands, lying south of Honshu across the Seto Inland Sea and east of Kyushu. The island is divided into the prefectures of Ehime, Kagawa, Kōchi, and Tokushima — successors to the four historical provinces of Iyo, Sanuki, Tosa, and Awa that gave the island its name. Shikoku is best known internationally for the Shikoku Pilgrimage, a 1,200-kilometre circuit of 88 temples associated with the Buddhist monk Kūkai.

Geography

Rugged mountains run east–west through the island's centre, so most of the population lives on the narrower northern coastal plains facing the Inland Sea. The Pacific-facing south (Kōchi) has a warmer, wetter climate. Rivers such as the Yoshino and Shimanto — the latter famous as one of Japan's clearest large rivers — drain steep valleys, and the coasts feature the Naruto whirlpools in the strait between Tokushima and Awaji Island. Mount Ishizuchi (1,982 m), the highest peak in western Japan, is a sacred mountain.

History and culture

In the feudal period the four provinces were ruled by daimyo of the Chōsokabe, Tosa, and other domains; Tosa produced leading figures of the Meiji Restoration such as Sakamoto Ryōma. Tokushima's Awa Odori, one of Japan's largest dance festivals, draws over a million spectators each August. Shikoku is also home to the shikoku ken dog breed and distinctive regional foods — sanuki udon noodles from Kagawa, bonito (katsuo no tataki) in Kōchi, and citrus fruits in Ehime.

The Shikoku Pilgrimage

The 88-temple pilgrimage (Shikoku Hachijūhakkasho) circles the island over some 1,200 km, visiting temples linked to Kūkai (774–835), the founder of Shingon Buddhism who was born in Kagawa. Pilgrims (o-henro-san), traditionally dressed in white with a sedge hat and staff, walk the route over six weeks or more; others travel by bus or car. Local custom of o-settai — giving free food and gifts to pilgrims — is a cherished tradition.

Economy and transport

Until 1988 Shikoku was accessible only by ferry; it is now linked to Honshu by three expressway-bridge systems: the Seto-Ōhashi Bridge (1988), the Akashi Kaikyō–Naruto route (1998), and the Shimanami Kaidō (1999), popular with cyclists. Agriculture (citrus, vegetables), forestry in the mountainous south, paper, chemicals, and shipbuilding anchor the economy, while rural depopulation and ageing are pressing concerns.
