The Pyrenees (Spanish: Pirineos; French: Pyrénées; Catalan: Pirineus) are a mountain range in southwestern Europe forming a natural boundary between France to the north and Spain to the south, with the small country of Andorra entirely enclosed within them. The range stretches about 490 kilometres from the Bay of Biscay on the Atlantic coast to the Mediterranean Sea at Cap de Creus. Its highest peak is Aneto (3,404 m) in the Spanish province of Huesca.

Geography and geology

The Pyrenees were raised during the Alpine orogeny, mostly in the Eocene, as the Iberian microplate collided with Europe; their core is an axial zone of ancient Paleozoic granite and metamorphic rock, flanked by folded limestone and sandstone ridges. Passes are high and few compared with the Alps, which historically made the range a formidable frontier — famously described in the saying attributed to Louis XIV's era that "there are no longer any Pyrenees" after a Bourbon took the Spanish throne. Glaciers, once extensive, have shrunk to a few remnants on the highest massifs and are expected to disappear within decades. Notable features include the cirque of Gavarnie, the Ordesa canyon, and the pilgrimage town of Lourdes at the northern foothills.

People and culture

The western Pyrenees are the homeland of the Basque people, whose language predates the arrival of Indo-European tongues; Catalan is spoken in the east, with Occitan and Aragonese also present. Traditional livelihoods — transhumant pastoralism, forestry and smuggling across the frontier — have given way largely to tourism: ski resorts, the Tour de France's legendary climbs (such as the Col du Tourmalet), and national parks on both sides (Pyrenees National Park in France, Ordesa y Monte Perdido in Spain) draw millions of visitors. The range's wildlife includes the Pyrenean chamois (isard), bearded vulture, and a small, reintroduced population of brown bears, as well as the desman, an endemic semi-aquatic mammal.
