Fly fishing

Fly fishing is an angling method that uses an artificial "fly" — a lure made to imitate insects or other food organisms — cast with a specialised fly rod, reel and weighted line. Unlike most other forms of fishing, in which the weight of the lure carries the line, in fly fishing the weight of the line itself carries the nearly weightless fly to the target.

Techniques

The angler casts by moving the rod back and forth, forming a loop in the line that unrolls in the air and lays the fly gently on the water. Flies may float on the surface (dry flies), sink below it (nymphs and wet flies), or imitate larger prey such as baitfish (streamers). Fly fishing is most associated with trout and salmon in rivers and streams, but it is also practised for grayling, panfish, bass, pike, carp and many saltwater species such as bonefish and tarpon.

Equipment

Essential tackle includes a fly rod (typically 7 to 10 feet long), a fly reel, a tapered fly line, a leader and tippet of monofilament, and a selection of flies. Anglers often wear waders to stand in the water and use nets with soft mesh to release fish unharmed; catch and release is a strong tradition in the sport.

History

Descriptions of fishing with artificial flies date to Roman times; the Macedonian method described by Aelian in the 2nd century AD used red wool and feathers on a hook. The English treatise of 1496 attributed to Dame Juliana Berners described fly patterns and rods, and Izaak Walton's The Compleat Angler (1653) helped popularise the pastime. Modern fly fishing developed in the chalk streams of southern England and spread to North America, Australasia and worldwide during the 19th and 20th centuries.
