Denialism

Denialism is the rejection of well-established propositions — supported by strong scientific or historical evidence — through the use of rhetorical tactics that give the appearance of legitimate debate where none exists. The term is applied to movements such as Holocaust denial, HIV/AIDS denialism, the denial of the link between smoking and cancer promoted historically by the tobacco industry, and the rejection of the scientific consensus on climate change or vaccine safety. Scholars are careful to distinguish denialism from genuine scepticism and from scientific controversy, in which claims are tested against evidence; denialism is characterised by a conclusion fixed in advance and evidence selected or invented to defend it.

Rhetorical tactics

Taxonomies of denialism commonly identify five tactics: alleging conspiracies among scientists or historians; relying on fake experts while portraying the relevant expert community as corrupt or divided; cherry-picking data and quotations; demanding impossible standards of proof, so that any residual uncertainty is treated as refutation; and misrepresenting opponents' positions or using logical fallacies. Organised denial campaigns have often been funded by interested industries: the tobacco industry's decades-long strategy of manufacturing doubt about smoking's harms is a documented template later echoed in other debates.

Psychology and debate over the term

Research in psychology links denialist beliefs to motivated reasoning, identity-protective cognition, and distrust of institutions. Commentators also debate the term itself: some caution that labelling opponents "denialists" can foreclose discussion and that the label has sometimes been extended to contested policy or historical questions. Historians and scientists who use the term generally restrict it to cases where a strong evidential consensus exists and rejection depends on the tactics described above.
