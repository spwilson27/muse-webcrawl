Disjunctive normal form

In Boolean logic, a formula is in disjunctive normal form (DNF) if it is a disjunction of one or more conjunctions of literals, where a literal is a variable or the negation of a variable. A DNF formula is also known as a sum of products.

Definition

A formula in DNF has the form (A1 ∧ A2 ∧ … ) ∨ (B1 ∧ B2 ∧ …) ∨ …, where each Ai and Bi is a literal. For example, (a ∧ ¬b) ∨ (¬c ∧ d) is in disjunctive normal form. A DNF formula is in full disjunctive normal form if each variable appears exactly once in every conjunction; the conjunctions are then called minterms.

Conversion

Every propositional formula can be converted into an equivalent DNF by using laws of Boolean algebra: eliminating implications and biconditionals, pushing negations inward with De Morgan's laws and double negation, and distributing conjunction over disjunction. Conversion can cause an exponential blow-up in formula size. An alternative is to read the DNF directly from a truth table by forming one minterm for each row where the formula is true.

Applications

DNF is used in automated theorem proving, in the design of digital circuits (where it corresponds to sum-of-products logic implemented with AND-OR gates), and in logic programming. The dual form, in which the roles of conjunction and disjunction are swapped, is conjunctive normal form (CNF), which is standard for SAT solvers.
