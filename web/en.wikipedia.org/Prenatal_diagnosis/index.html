Prenatal diagnosis (or prenatal screening, when referring to risk assessment) is the testing of a fetus before birth to determine whether it has particular abnormalities, including genetic disorders, chromosomal conditions such as Down syndrome, and structural malformations. It is distinguished from prenatal screening: screening tests estimate risk, while diagnostic tests aim to confirm or exclude a condition.

Purposes

Prenatal diagnosis allows parents and clinicians to prepare for the birth of a child with a medical condition, plan delivery at an appropriate facility, consider in-utero treatment where available, and make informed decisions about continuing a pregnancy. Some conditions, such as certain heart defects and spina bifida, benefit from planned delivery care or fetal surgery.

Noninvasive methods

Ultrasound assesses fetal growth and anatomy; the first-trimester nuchal translucency scan screens for chromosomal abnormalities (see nuchal translucency). Maternal serum screening measures markers such as PAPP-A, hCG, and alpha-fetoprotein. Noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT), available since 2011, analyzes cell-free fetal DNA in maternal blood and detects trisomies 21, 18, and 13 with high sensitivity, though positive results require diagnostic confirmation.

Invasive methods

Chorionic villus sampling (CVS), usually at 10–13 weeks, samples placental tissue for karyotyping and genetic analysis. Amniocentesis, usually from 15 weeks, samples amniotic fluid. Both carry a small procedure-related miscarriage risk, now generally estimated below 0.5%. Modern analysis uses chromosomal microarray and, increasingly, exome sequencing.

Conditions detected

Testing can identify aneuploidies (Down, Edwards, and Patau syndromes), single-gene disorders (cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, Huntington's disease, fragile X), neural tube defects, and many structural anomalies of the heart, brain, kidneys, and skeleton.

Ethical issues

Prenatal diagnosis raises ethical questions about reproductive choice, disability rights, informed consent, and the possibility of sex selection or selection for non-medical traits. Guidelines emphasize non-directive genetic counseling so that decisions reflect the parents' values.
