Publicity stunt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Publicity stunt
Field: public relations, advertising, marketing
Purpose: generating news coverage
Related concepts: guerrilla marketing, viral marketing, media event

In marketing, a publicity stunt is a planned event designed to attract the public's attention to the event's organizers or their cause. Publicity stunts can be professionally organized, or set up by amateurs. Such events are frequently utilized by advertisers and celebrities, especially athletes and politicians, and by organisations promoting a product, film, book or political position.

Methods

Stunts trade on spectacle, novelty or controversy: record attempts, flash mobs, staged rescues, unusual product launches and deliberately provocative gestures are all common forms. The organiser aims for coverage in news media — "earned media" — that would cost far more to buy as advertising. The rise of social media has made images and short video of a stunt as important as press coverage itself.

History

The showman P. T. Barnum was a nineteenth-century pioneer of the form, manufacturing press interest in his exhibitions and performers. In the early twentieth century, publicist Edward Bernays staged events such as the 1929 "Torches of Freedom" march, in which debutantes smoked cigarettes in the Easter parade to associate smoking with women's emancipation. Film studios of the studio era regularly invented romances, feuds and record attempts to publicise releases.

Criticism

Critics note that stunts can mislead the public when presented as spontaneous news, can endanger participants or bystanders, and can backfire when the public perceives manipulation. A failed stunt may attract attention of entirely the wrong kind, damaging the brand it was meant to promote.
