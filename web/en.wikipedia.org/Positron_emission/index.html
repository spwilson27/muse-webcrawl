Positron emission

Positron emission (β⁺ decay)
TypeRadioactive decay
Particle emittedPositron (e⁺) and electron neutrino (νe)
Change in nucleusProton → neutron; atomic number decreases by 1
Example¹⁸F → ¹⁸O + e⁺ + νe

Positron emission, or beta plus decay (β⁺ decay), is a subtype of radioactive decay in which a proton inside a nucleus is converted into a neutron while releasing a positron and an electron neutrino. The atomic number of the atom therefore decreases by one, transforming it into a different element, while the mass number remains unchanged.

Mechanism

In positron emission a proton is transformed according to the reaction p → n + e⁺ + νe, mediated by the weak interaction. The process can occur only when the mass of the parent atom exceeds that of the daughter atom by at least twice the electron mass (1.022 MeV/c²), because the emitted positron must be created and an orbital electron is effectively lost from the atom. Positron emission competes with electron capture, which achieves the same nuclear conversion; lighter proton-rich nuclei favour positron emission, and heavier ones electron capture.

Discovery

The positron was predicted by Paul Dirac in 1928 and observed by Carl Anderson in cosmic rays in 1932. In 1934 Irène and Frédéric Joliot-Curie produced the first artificially induced positron emitter, phosphorus-30, by bombarding aluminium with alpha particles — the discovery of artificial radioactivity.

Applications

Positron-emitting radionuclides such as fluorine-18, carbon-11, nitrogen-13, and oxygen-15 are the basis of positron emission tomography (PET), a major medical imaging technique. After emission, the positron travels a short distance in tissue before annihilating with an electron, producing two 511 keV gamma rays emitted in nearly opposite directions; detecting these pairs allows precise localisation of the tracer. Fluorine-18, incorporated into fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), is widely used to image metabolic activity in oncology, cardiology, and neurology.
