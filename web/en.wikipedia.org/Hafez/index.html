Khwaja Shams-ud-Din Muhammad Hafez-e Shirazi (Persian: خواجه شمس‌الدین محمد حافظ شیرازی), known by his pen name Hafez (حافظ, "the memorizer", one who has memorised the Quran), was a Persian lyric poet whose collected works are regarded as a pinnacle of Persian literature. His works are found in almost every Iranian home, are learned by heart, and are still used as proverbs and sayings.

Life

Hafez was born in Shiraz around 1315. Orphaned young, he worked as a baker's assistant and later as a copyist while pursuing religious studies; tradition holds that he memorised the Quran at an early age, earning his pen name. He became a court poet under the Muzaffarid rulers of Shiraz, including Abu Ishaq Inju and Shah Shuja, and also served as a teacher of Quranic studies. Invited to India by Mahmud Shah I of the Deccan, he famously set out but turned back after a storm at sea, commemorating the episode in verse. He died in Shiraz in 1390 and was buried in the Musalla Gardens; his tomb, the Hafezieh, remains a major site of pilgrimage and tourism.

Works and themes

Hafez's collected poems, his Divan, contain several hundred ghazals along with qasidas, elegies and short pieces. His ghazals celebrate love, wine, the tavern and the beloved, while simultaneously carrying Sufi mystical readings in which wine signifies divine ecstasy and the beloved the divine. This ambiguity — earthly and spiritual at once — is central to his artistry. His style is noted for its musicality, wordplay, and seamless movement between images within a single poem.

Influence and legacy

Hafez profoundly influenced subsequent Persian, Turkish and Urdu poetry. In the West he inspired Goethe's West–Eastern Divan and was admired by Emerson, who translated some of his poems. In Iran, his Divan is used in fal-e Hafez, a popular form of bibliomancy in which a randomly opened page is consulted for guidance, especially on Yalda Night and Nowruz. His collected works were first published in Calcutta in 1791 and have been translated into many languages.
