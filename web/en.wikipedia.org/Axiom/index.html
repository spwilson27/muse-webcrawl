An axiom, postulate, or assumption is a statement that is taken to be true, to serve as a premise or starting point for further reasoning and arguments. The word comes from the Ancient Greek word axíōma (ἀξίωμα), meaning "that which is thought worthy or fit". In classical philosophy, an axiom was supposed to be a statement so evident as to be accepted without controversy; in modern logic and mathematics, axioms are simply the formal starting points of a deductive system, whose usefulness rather than obviousness justifies them.

Historical development

The method of axioms was first systematically used in Euclid's Elements, where five postulates and five "common notions" underpin the whole of plane geometry. For two thousand years the parallel postulate was suspected of being provable from the others; its independence was demonstrated in the 19th century by the creation of consistent non-Euclidean geometries (Lobachevsky, Bolyai, Riemann). David Hilbert's Foundations of Geometry (1899) recast axiomatics in fully formal terms, launching the formalist programme that culminated in set-theoretic foundations such as Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory (ZFC).

Incompleteness

Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) showed that any consistent formal system strong enough to contain arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency and contains true statements it cannot prove, placing fundamental limits on the Hilbert programme. The axiom of choice and the continuum hypothesis are celebrated examples of statements independent of ZFC.
