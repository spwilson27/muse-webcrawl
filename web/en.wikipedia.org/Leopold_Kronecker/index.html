Leopold Kronecker (7 December 1823 – 29 December 1891) was a German mathematician who worked on number theory, algebra, and logic. He is as famous for his philosophy of mathematics — his insistence that mathematics be grounded in the integers and in finite, constructive procedures — as for his theorems, and his dictum "God made the integers, all else is the work of man" has become proverbial.

Life

Kronecker was born in Liegnitz (now Legnica, Poland) into a wealthy Jewish family and studied at the University of Berlin, where Ernst Kummer was his teacher and lifelong friend. After earning his doctorate in 1845 he managed his family's banking and estate business for nearly a decade, doing mathematics privately, before returning to Berlin in 1855. Financially independent, he lectured at the university without a chair until 1883, when he succeeded Kummer as professor.

Mathematics

Kronecker made fundamental contributions to the theory of algebraic number fields, elliptic functions, and the theory of equations. The Kronecker–Weber theorem states that every abelian extension of the rational numbers is contained in a cyclotomic field. His name survives in the Kronecker delta, the Kronecker product, the Kronecker symbol, and Kronecker's approximation theorem in Diophantine approximation.

Philosophy and controversies

A leading finitist, Kronecker opposed the set theory of his younger Berlin colleague Georg Cantor and the non-constructive existence proofs of the Weierstrass school, demanding that every mathematical object be constructible in finitely many steps from the natural numbers. His institutional influence in Berlin made him a formidable opponent, and his disputes with Cantor remain a celebrated episode in the foundations of mathematics; his programme anticipated later constructivism and intuitionism.
