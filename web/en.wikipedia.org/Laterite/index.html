Laterite

Laterite is a soil type rich in iron and aluminium, formed in hot and wet tropical areas by intense and prolonged weathering of the underlying parent rock. Nearly all laterites are rusty-red in color because of their high iron oxide content. The name derives from the Latin word later, meaning "brick": in India and elsewhere, soft laterite can be cut into blocks that harden on exposure to air and have been used as building material for centuries, including in the temples of Angkor.

Formation

Laterites develop where high rainfall and temperature leach silica and bases from the soil profile while iron and aluminium oxides accumulate near the surface, a process called laterization. The resulting material ranges from soft earthy masses to hard, cemented crusts (duricrusts) that resist erosion and form protective caps on plateaus and mesas.

Distribution and uses

Laterites cover about one third of the Earth's continental land area, principally in the tropics of South America, Africa, India, Southeast Asia, and northern Australia. Economically important lateritic deposits include bauxite (the principal ore of aluminium), nickel laterites, and some iron ores. As a soil, laterite is typically nutrient-poor and acidic, presenting challenges for agriculture, though it supports forests and, once cut, provides a durable low-cost road and building material.
