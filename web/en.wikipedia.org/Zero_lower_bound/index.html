Zero lower bound

The zero lower bound (ZLB) is a macroeconomic problem that occurs when the short-term nominal interest rate is at or near zero, causing a liquidity trap and limiting the capacity of the central bank to stimulate economic growth through conventional monetary policy.

The problem

Central banks normally stimulate a weak economy by cutting nominal interest rates. But because people can hold cash, which pays a zero nominal return, rates cannot easily be pushed far below zero: nominal rates cannot fall below the return on holding currency, minus the costs of storing and insuring it. When inflation is very low or negative, the inability to lower real interest rates sufficiently can prolong recessions and deflation.

Historical episodes

The problem was first widely discussed in Japan in the 1990s, when the Bank of Japan cut its policy rate to near zero during the "Lost Decade". It became central after the Great Recession, when the US Federal Reserve, the Bank of England and the European Central Bank all reached the effective lower bound.

Responses

Central banks responded with unconventional policies, including quantitative easing, forward guidance and, in the euro area, Switzerland, Denmark, Sweden and Japan, mildly negative interest rates — showing that the bound is not exactly zero but an "effective lower bound" somewhat below it.
