The International Olympic Committee (IOC) is a non-governmental sports organisation based in Lausanne, Switzerland. Created by Pierre de Coubertin on 23 June 1894 at the Sorbonne in Paris, it is the authority responsible for organising the modern Summer and Winter Olympic Games and is the governing body of the National Olympic Committees (NOCs).

History

Coubertin, inspired by the ancient Olympic Games and by English public-school sport, convened the 1894 Olympic Congress, which founded the IOC and awarded the first modern Olympics to Athens in 1896. The IOC was originally a small self-electing body of Coubertin's associates; Demetrios Vikelas was its first president, with Coubertin himself presiding from 1896 to 1925. The Winter Games were added in 1924, the Youth Olympic Games in 2010, and the Olympic Movement now encompasses 206 NOCs and dozens of international federations.

Role and governance

The IOC owns the Olympic symbols and the Games themselves, selects host cities, negotiates the enormously valuable broadcast and sponsorship rights (the TOP programme), and coordinates the Olympic Movement through the Olympic Charter. Its members — around one hundred individuals, including athletes' representatives — elect a president for an eight-year term, renewable once for four years. Revenue, measured in billions of US dollars per four-year cycle, is redistributed largely to organising committees, NOCs and federations.

Criticism

The IOC has faced recurrent criticism over corruption in host-city bidding (notably the Salt Lake City scandal of 1998, which led to reforms), the spiralling cost of hosting, doping crises including the Russian state-sponsored doping programme, and questions over human rights in host countries. In response it has adopted Agenda 2020 reforms and a new host-selection process based on dialogue rather than competitive bidding.
