Shakespearean tragedy

Shakespearean tragedy is the designation given to the plays of William Shakespeare that fall into the genre of tragedy. In the First Folio (1623), Shakespeare's plays were divided into comedies, histories and tragedies; about ten plays are classed as tragedies, including the four great tragedies written in a remarkable burst between about 1600 and 1606: Hamlet, Othello, King Lear and Macbeth.

Characteristics

A Shakespearean tragedy typically centres on a single figure of high status whose fall and death structure the play. Following the model of Aristotle's Poetics as filtered through Seneca and English revenge tragedy, the hero is brought down by a combination of character — the much-discussed "tragic flaw" or hamartia — chance, and hostile or indifferent fortune; A. C. Bradley's influential Shakespearean Tragedy (1904) argued that the catastrophe flows from character in action rather than from a simple moral defect. Common features include a five-act structure, supernatural elements (the Ghost in Hamlet, the witches in Macbeth), soliloquies that open the hero's mind to the audience, madness real or feigned, multiple deaths culminating in the hero's, and a partial restoration of order in the closing scene. Tragedy is frequently interwoven with comic subplots or scenes, as with the gravediggers in Hamlet and the porter in Macbeth.

The tragedies

The tragedies of the Folio are: Titus Andronicus, Romeo and Juliet, Julius Caesar, Hamlet, Troilus and Cressida (its generic status is disputed), Othello, King Lear, Macbeth, Antony and Cleopatra, Coriolanus and Timon of Athens. Of these, Romeo and Juliet differs in ending in the deaths of young lovers rather than of a single protagonist, and several plays — Julius Caesar above all — sit at the boundary with the histories.

Influences and criticism

Shakespeare drew on classical tragedy, medieval de casibus stories of the fall of princes, chronicle history, and the contemporary revenge play. Criticism of the tragedies is a vast field, from Samuel Johnson and the Romantics (Coleridge, Hazlitt, Keats) through Bradley to 20th-century approaches — psychoanalytic, feminist, new historicist — and Jan Kott's Shakespeare Our Contemporary, which read the plays in the light of modern political violence. The four great tragedies remain among the most performed and studied works in world literature.
