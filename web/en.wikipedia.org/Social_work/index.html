Social work

Social work is an academic discipline and practice-based profession that promotes social change and development, social cohesion, and the empowerment and liberation of people. Its practitioners work with individuals, families, groups, and communities facing poverty, disability, mental illness, addiction, abuse, and the effects of discrimination, guided by principles of social justice, human rights, collective responsibility, and respect for diversity.

History

Modern social work emerged from nineteenth-century philanthropy: the Charity Organization Societies' "scientific charity" and home visiting, the settlement house movement led by figures such as Jane Addams at Chicago's Hull House, and Mary Richmond's systematization of casework in Social Diagnosis (1917). The profession expanded with the twentieth-century welfare state, and today social workers are employed in child protection, hospitals and mental health services, schools, criminal justice, aging services, and policy advocacy. The 2014 global definition adopted by the International Federation of Social Workers frames the profession around both person and environment — helping people navigate the systems around them and working to change unjust systems.

Practice

Practice is conventionally divided into micro (work with individuals and families: assessment, counseling, case management, crisis intervention), mezzo (groups and organizations), and macro levels (community organizing, policy analysis, and advocacy). In many jurisdictions, social work is a licensed profession: in the United States, clinical social workers with the LCSW credential can diagnose and treat mental disorders, and social workers form the largest group of mental health service providers. Common methods include strengths-based and person-in-environment assessment, motivational interviewing, and trauma-informed care.

Education and ethics

Professional education is delivered at bachelor's (BSW), master's (MSW), and doctoral levels with supervised field placements, accredited in the U.S. by the Council on Social Work Education. Codes of ethics, such as that of the National Association of Social Workers, emphasize service, dignity of the person, confidentiality with defined limits, and competence.
