The Cuban Missile Crisis, also known as the October Crisis, was a 13-day confrontation between the United States and the Soviet Union in October 1962 over Soviet ballistic missiles deployed in Cuba. It is generally regarded as the moment the Cold War came closest to escalating into a full-scale nuclear war.

Background

After the failed U.S.-backed Bay of Pigs invasion of Cuba in 1961 and with American Jupiter nuclear missiles stationed in Turkey and Italy, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev agreed to Fidel Castro's request to place Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba — both to deter a future U.S. invasion and to redress the strategic imbalance. The secret deployment, Operation Anadyr, began in mid-1962.

The thirteen days

On 14 October a U-2 spy plane photographed missile sites under construction. President John F. Kennedy convened the Executive Committee (ExComm) and on 22 October announced a naval "quarantine" of Cuba to block further offensive weapons, demanding the missiles' removal. Tensions peaked on 27 October — "Black Saturday" — when a U-2 was shot down over Cuba, killing Major Rudolf Anderson, and another strayed into Soviet airspace; unknown to Washington, Soviet submarines and tactical nuclear weapons on the island were near authorised use.

Resolution and aftermath

On 28 October Khrushchev announced the missiles would be dismantled and returned to the USSR, in exchange for a public U.S. non-invasion pledge and a secret assurance to withdraw the Jupiters from Turkey. The crisis prompted creation of the Moscow–Washington hotline and the 1963 Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty.
