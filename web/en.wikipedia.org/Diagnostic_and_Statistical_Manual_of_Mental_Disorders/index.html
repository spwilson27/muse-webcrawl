Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), published by the American Psychiatric Association (APA), is the standard classification of mental disorders used by clinicians, researchers, insurers, and policymakers in the United States and widely influential elsewhere.

History

The first edition appeared in 1952, listing 106 disorders in a framework influenced by psychodynamic theory. DSM-II followed in 1968. DSM-III (1980) marked a revolution: it introduced explicit diagnostic criteria, a multiaxial system, and an ostensibly atheoretical, descriptive approach, greatly increasing diagnostic reliability. DSM-IV was published in 1994, DSM-5 in 2013, and a text revision, DSM-5-TR, in 2022.

Structure

The DSM organises disorders into categories such as neurodevelopmental disorders, schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders, mood disorders, anxiety disorders, trauma- and stressor-related disorders, dissociative disorders, somatic symptom disorders, eating disorders, personality disorders, and neurocognitive disorders, each with specified criteria and codes aligned with the ICD.

Criticism

The manual has been criticised for medicalising normal behaviour, for the validity of its categories, for cultural bias, and for the pharmaceutical industry ties of some contributors. Alternative frameworks such as the ICD-11 and the research-oriented NIMH Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) reflect differing approaches to classification.
