Indictment

An indictment (pronounced "in-DYTE-ment") is a formal accusation that a person has committed a crime. In jurisdictions that use the concept, a grand jury issues an indictment after considering evidence presented by a prosecutor; the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution requires indictment by a federal grand jury for capital or otherwise infamous crimes.

United States

In the federal system and many states, serious felony charges must be brought by grand jury indictment, though many states permit prosecutors to proceed by an "information" instead. An indictment returned by the required majority of grand jurors is called a "true bill"; a refusal is a "no bill." Indictments may be sealed until the accused is arrested. Following indictment, the defendant is arraigned and enters a plea.

England and Wales

Grand juries were abolished in England and Wales in the twentieth century, but "trial on indictment" remains the term for Crown Court trials, with the indictment being the formal document setting out the counts the defendant faces, prepared by the prosecution.

Contents

An indictment typically names the accused, cites the statute violated, and states the essential facts constituting the offense with sufficient particularity to inform the defendant of the charge and to protect against double jeopardy.
