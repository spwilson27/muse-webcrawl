Almond

The almond (Prunus amygdalus, syn. Prunus dulcis) is a species of tree in the rose family native to Iran and surrounding countries, cultivated worldwide for its edible seed. Although commonly called a nut, the almond seed is botanically the kernel of a drupe, whose leathery hull splits at maturity to reveal the pitted shell within.

Cultivation

Almonds were domesticated in the eastern Mediterranean in antiquity and spread along trade routes to southern Europe and North Africa, reaching California in the nineteenth century. Today California's Central Valley produces roughly 80 percent of the world's commercial crop. The trees blossom very early — in February in California — and most cultivars are self-incompatible, requiring cross-pollination by bees; more than a million commercial beehives are trucked into the orchards each spring, the largest managed pollination event in the world. The crop's heavy irrigation demand in a drought-prone state has made it a focus of debate about water use in agriculture.

Uses and nutrition

Sweet almonds are eaten raw or roasted, ground into flour and marzipan, pressed for oil, and processed into almond milk and butter. They are rich in monounsaturated fat, vitamin E, magnesium, and fibre. Bitter almonds, a distinct variety, contain amygdalin, which releases cyanide, and are used in small quantities for flavouring extracts and oils after processing.
