Ghat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A ghat is a flight of steps leading down to a body of water, particularly a holy river, in India and other parts of South Asia. The word comes from Hindi ghāṭ (घाट), meaning a landing place or a mountain pass, and in English usage it has two principal senses: the steps and embankments along sacred rivers, and the mountain ranges of peninsular India, the Eastern Ghats and Western Ghats, which the word describes as stepped or terraced escarpments.

Riverfront ghats

The most famous ghats line the western bank of the Ganges at Varanasi, where some 84 ghats stretch for several kilometres along the river. They serve ritual bathing, prayer, laundry, boat landings, and cremation; the burning ghats at Manikarnika and Harishchandra are used for Hindu funerals, whose ashes are immersed in the river. Notable Varanasi ghats include Dashashwamedh Ghat, where the evening Ganga aarti fire ceremony is performed daily, Man Mandir Ghat, and Assi Ghat at the confluence with the Assi River.

Other important ghat complexes stand at Haridwar and Rishikesh on the upper Ganges, at Prayagraj (Allahabad) at the Triveni Sangam confluence, at Ujjain on the Shipra, at Nashik on the Godavari, and along the Yamuna at Mathura and Vrindavan. In Kolkata, ghats along the Hooghly River range from bathing steps to the great cremation ground at Nimtala. Ghats are also characteristic of temple tanks and reservoirs in South India.

Mountain ghats

In Indian geography, the Western Ghats and Eastern Ghats are the mountain ranges running along the western and eastern edges of the Deccan Plateau. The term is also used for individual passes and the steep roads crossing them — as in the Bhor Ghat between Mumbai and Pune — many of which were engineered in the 19th century for road and rail links through the escarpment.

Cultural significance

Ghats are stages for the daily and festival life of Hindu India: dawn bathing, yoga, offerings of flowers and lamps floated on the water, mass rituals during Kumbh Mela and Chhath, and the immersion of idols during Ganesh Chaturthi and Durga Puja. Many were endowed by royal patrons — the ghats of Varanasi were largely rebuilt in the 18th century under Maratha patronage — and they remain among the oldest continuously inhabited urban landscapes in the world.
