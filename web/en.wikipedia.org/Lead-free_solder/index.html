Lead-free solder

Lead-free solder is solder that contains little or no lead, developed to replace traditional tin-lead alloys (such as 60/40 or 63/37 Sn-Pb) because of the toxicity of lead and environmental regulations. The European Union's Restriction of Hazardous Substances (RoHS) directive, effective 2006, drove a worldwide transition in consumer electronics.

The most common lead-free alloys are based on tin with silver and copper — the SAC family, for example SAC305 (96.5% tin, 3.0% silver, 0.5% copper) — with a melting point around 217–220 °C, higher than the 183 °C of tin-lead solder. Other alloys add bismuth, indium, zinc or antimony for specialised applications.

Lead-free solders generally require higher reflow temperatures, wet surfaces less readily, and can be prone to tin whisker growth, which drove changes in component finishes and assembly processes; some sectors, including aerospace and medical devices, long retained exemptions for leaded solder.
