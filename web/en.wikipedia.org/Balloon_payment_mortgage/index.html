Balloon payment mortgage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A balloon payment mortgage is a mortgage that does not fully amortize over its term, leaving a balance due at maturity — a large final "balloon payment." Monthly payments during the term are often calculated as if the loan would amortize over a longer period (for example, 30 years), but the loan matures after a shorter period, commonly five to seven years for residential loans, at which point the remaining principal must be paid in full, typically by refinancing or by selling the property.

Structure

In a typical balloon loan the borrower enjoys lower monthly payments (and often a lower interest rate) than a fully amortizing loan of the same term, because lenders face less interest-rate risk over the short term. At maturity the borrower generally intends to refinance into a new loan; some balloon mortgages include a reset option or a conditional right to renew at prevailing rates if the borrower has paid on time and still owns and occupies the property. Two-step mortgages combine a balloon structure with such a one-time reset.

Risks

The central risk is refinancing risk: if credit conditions tighten, property values fall, or the borrower's finances deteriorate, the borrower may be unable to refinance or sell for enough to cover the balance, risking default and foreclosure when the balloon comes due. Balloon structures were implicated in waves of defaults during the Great Depression — when most American mortgages were short-term balloon notes routinely rolled over, until credit markets seized — and again featured among risky loan products before the 2008 financial crisis. Post-crisis U.S. regulation (the ability-to-repay and qualified mortgage rules) restricts balloon payments in most consumer mortgages, though they remain common in commercial real estate lending.
