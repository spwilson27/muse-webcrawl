Capture of Rome

Italian troops entering Rome through the breach at Porta Pia, 1870 (placeholder)

The capture of Rome (Italian: Presa di Roma) on 20 September 1870 was the final event of the long process of Italian unification known as the Risorgimento. Royal Italian Army troops under General Raffaele Cadorna breached the Aurelian Walls near Porta Pia and occupied the city, ending the temporal power of the popes over the Papal States, which had lasted for more than a millennium. Rome was declared the capital of the Kingdom of Italy in 1871.

Background

Rome had been proclaimed the intended capital of Italy since 1861, but it remained under Pope Pius IX, protected by a French garrison. Attempts by Giuseppe Garibaldi to seize the city were stopped at Aspromonte (1862) and by French troops at Mentana (1867). The outbreak of the Franco-Prussian War in July 1870 forced Napoleon III to withdraw the French garrison; after the French collapse at Sedan, the Italian government of Giovanni Lanza moved to annex the city, first attempting negotiation, which the Pope rejected.

The assault

Cadorna's force of about 50,000 men crossed the Papal frontier on 11 September 1870. On the morning of 20 September, Italian artillery opened a breach in the walls near Porta Pia; after a few hours of fighting, papal Zouaves and other defenders, under orders from Pius IX to offer only token resistance once the breach was made, surrendered. Total casualties were modest — roughly 49 Italian soldiers and 19 papal troops killed. A plebiscite on 2 October voted overwhelmingly for annexation to Italy.

Aftermath

Pius IX declared himself a "prisoner in the Vatican" and refused to recognise the annexation, beginning the "Roman Question", which festered until the Lateran Treaty of 1929 created Vatican City. Rome became Italy's capital on 1 July 1871, and 20 September was long celebrated as a national holiday in Italy.
