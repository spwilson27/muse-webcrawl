Nunavut (Inuktitut: ᓄᓇᕗᑦ, "our land") is the largest and northernmost territory of Canada. It was separated officially from the Northwest Territories on April 1, 1999, via the Nunavut Act and the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement Act, which had been the largest Indigenous land claims settlement in Canadian history. Nunavut comprises a major portion of Northern Canada and most of the Arctic Archipelago. Its vast size makes it the fifth-largest country subdivision in the world, as well as North America's second-largest (after Greenland). The capital Iqaluit (formerly Frobisher Bay), on Baffin Island, was chosen by plebiscite in 1995.

Geography

Nunavut includes Ellesmere Island in the far north (Canada's northernmost point, Cape Columbia, is 769 km from the North Pole), the eastern and southern portions of Victoria Island, and all islands in Hudson, James and Ungava bays. The terrain ranges from mainland tundra to the ice caps and mountains of the Arctic Cordillera; Mount Barbeau on Ellesmere Island (2,616 m) is the territory's highest point. There are no roads connecting Nunavut's 25 communities to one another or to southern Canada; travel is by air year-round and by sea during the brief ice-free season.

History

The region has been inhabited for about 4,500 years, first by Paleo-Eskimo cultures (Pre-Dorset and Dorset) and then by the Thule people, ancestors of today's Inuit, who arrived around 1000 CE. European contact began with Martin Frobisher's voyages in the 1570s and intensified with the Hudson's Bay Company fur trade, whaling, and 20th-century missions, trading posts, and Cold War Distant Early Warning (DEW) Line stations. The Nunavut Land Claims Agreement, signed in 1993, gave Inuit title to some 350,000 km² of land, cash payments, and resource rights, and provided for the creation of the new territory in 1999.

Demographics and government

About 85% of residents are Inuit, making Nunavut the only Canadian jurisdiction with an Indigenous majority. Inuktitut is the mother tongue of a majority and, with Inuinnaqtun, English and French, an official language. The Legislative Assembly operates by consensus government without political parties. Nunavut faces significant challenges: the highest cost of living in Canada, housing shortages and overcrowding, food insecurity (with country food — caribou, char, seal, whale — remaining nutritionally and culturally central), and elevated rates of tuberculosis and suicide. Mining (gold, iron ore), fishing, and tourism are the main industries beyond public administration.
