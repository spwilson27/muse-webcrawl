Capsicum annuum is a fruiting plant from the family Solanaceae (nightshades), within the genus Capsicum, native to the Americas. Like most chilli and sweet peppers, it is a relatively small, bushy plant, grown widely for its edible fruit. It is the most widely cultivated of the five domesticated Capsicum species.


Cultivars of C. annuum range from sweet bell peppers to hot cayenne.

Domesticated in Mexico and northern South America thousands of years ago, C. annuum spread worldwide after the Columbian exchange and became integral to cuisines across Europe, Africa, and Asia.

Cultivars

The species encompasses an enormous range of pod shapes, colours, and pungency, measured in Scoville heat units (SHU). Well-known cultivar groups include:

  • Bell peppers — large, blocky, sweet (0 SHU), eaten green or ripe in red, yellow, and orange.
  • Jalapeño — medium-hot (c. 2,500–8,000 SHU), often pickled or smoked as chipotle.
  • Cayenne — long, thin, hot peppers (c. 30,000–50,000 SHU), usually dried and ground.
  • Poblano — mild, dark green; dried form is the ancho chile.
  • New Mexico chile, serrano, bird's eye, peperoncino, and pimiento — regional types used fresh, dried, or as paprika spice.

Characteristics

Plants typically grow 0.5–1.5 m tall, with white (occasionally purple-tinged) flowers and fruit that ripens from green to red, yellow, orange, or brown. Heat is produced by capsaicinoids concentrated in the placental tissue. The species is normally grown as an annual in temperate climates though it is a short-lived perennial in the tropics.

Cultivation and uses

C. annuum is grown commercially on every continent; China, Mexico, Indonesia, and Spain are among the largest producers. Peppers are eaten fresh, cooked, dried, pickled, and processed into sauces, spice blends, and oleoresin extracts used in food and pepper spray. Capsaicin also has pharmaceutical uses in topical analgesics.
