Fair Youth

The Fair Youth is the unnamed young man addressed in the majority of William Shakespeare's sonnets. Sonnets 1–126 of the 1609 sequence are directed to or concern this beautiful young man, urging him in the opening "procreation sonnets" to marry and have children who will carry on his beauty. The relationship portrayed evolves from admiration and exhortation to intense affection, betrayal — notably in the triangle involving the Dark Lady — and eventual reconciliation.

Identity

The youth's identity has been debated for centuries. The two leading candidates are Henry Wriothesley, 3rd Earl of Southampton, Shakespeare's patron, to whom the narrative poems Venus and Adonis and The Rape of Lucrece were dedicated, and William Herbert, 3rd Earl of Pembroke, whose initials "W.H." match the dedication of the sonnets to "Mr. W.H." as their "only begetter". Other proposed candidates include a literal reading in which the youth is fictional or an idealised figure, and the theory that the sonnets are partly autobiographical and partly conventional exercises in a fashionable genre.

Themes

The Fair Youth sonnets explore beauty, time, mortality, procreation, love and poetic immortality. Famous examples include Sonnet 18 ("Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?"), Sonnet 29, Sonnet 71 and Sonnet 116. Interpretations of the speaker's love for the youth — ranging from platonic admiration to erotic desire — have been central to Shakespeare scholarship and to the cultural reception of the sonnets.
