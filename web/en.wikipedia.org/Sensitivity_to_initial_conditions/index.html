Sensitivity to initial conditions

Sensitive dependence on initial conditions is a property of a dynamical system that exhibits significant sensitivity to slight changes in the initial conditions. Systems that are highly sensitive to initial conditions are said to display the butterfly effect: initially nearly identical states diverge exponentially over time, so that long-term prediction becomes practically impossible even though the system is fully deterministic. It is one of the defining features of chaos theory.

Definition

A system displays sensitive dependence if arbitrarily small errors in measuring its current state grow, on average, exponentially fast. The rate of divergence is characterized by the Lyapunov exponent: a positive largest Lyapunov exponent is generally taken as an indication that the system is chaotic. In a chaotic attractor, nearby trajectories separate exponentially until they are as far apart as the size of the attractor permits.

The butterfly effect

Meteorologist Edward Lorenz discovered the phenomenon in the early 1960s when rerunning a weather simulation with rounded initial values produced a completely different forecast. In a 1972 talk he popularized the idea with the question: "Does the flap of a butterfly's wings in Brazil set off a tornado in Texas?" Lorenz's simplified convection model, the Lorenz system, became the iconic example of a strange attractor.

Implications

Sensitive dependence places a fundamental limit on prediction in systems such as weather, fluid turbulence, planetary orbits, and some biological and economic models: beyond a prediction horizon, forecast skill decays regardless of model accuracy, because measurements can never be infinitely precise.
