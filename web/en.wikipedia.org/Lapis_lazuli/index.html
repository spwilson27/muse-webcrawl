Lapis lazuli (UK: /ˈlæpɪs ˈlæz(j)ʊli, ˈlæʒʊ-, -ˌlaɪ/; US: /ˈlæz(j)əli, ˈlæʒə-, -ˌlaɪ/), or lapis for short, is a deep-blue metamorphic rock used as a semi-precious stone that has been prized since antiquity for its intense colour. As early as the 7th millennium BC, lapis lazuli was mined in the Sar-e Sang mines, in Shortugai, and in other mines in Badakhshan province in modern northeast Afghanistan.

Lapis lazuli artifacts, dated to 7570 BC, have been found at Bhirrana, the oldest site of the Indus Valley civilisation. Lapis was highly valued by the Indus Valley Civilisation (3300–1900 BC), and pieces have been found in the burials of the Neolithic settlement of Mehrgarh, near the Bolan Pass in western Pakistan. Lapis jewellery is also known from ancient Egypt — most famously the funerary mask of Tutankhamun — and from Mesopotamia, Greece, Rome, China and pre-Columbian America.

Composition

Lapis lazuli is a rock composed chiefly of the mineral lazurite, a feldspathoid silicate whose sulphur content produces the intense blue colour, together with varying amounts of white calcite and golden metallic flecks of pyrite. The finest material is an even, intense violet-blue with small, evenly distributed pyrite; excessive calcite reduces its value. The name derives from the Latin lapis, "stone", and the Persian lāzhuward, "blue".

Ultramarine

From the late Middle Ages, ground lapis lazuli was processed into the pigment ultramarine, the most expensive and finest blue pigment of the Renaissance — costlier, weight for weight, than gold. It was used by painters such as Masaccio, Perugino, Titian and Vermeer, often reserved for the robes of the Virgin Mary, until a synthetic equivalent was invented in 1826.

Sources

The historic mines in Badakhshan, Afghanistan, remain the principal source of the finest lapis. Other deposits occur near Lake Baikal in Siberia, in the Chilean Andes (the source of Inca-era material), in Myanmar, Pakistan, Canada and the United States.
