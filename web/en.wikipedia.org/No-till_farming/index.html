No-till farming

No-till farming
Also calledZero tillage, direct drilling
Key ideaGrowing crops without disturbing the soil through tillage
Global extentOver 200 million hectares worldwide (early 2020s estimates)
Leading regionsSouth America (Brazil, Argentina), United States, Canada, Australia

No-till farming (also called zero tillage or direct drilling) is an agricultural technique for growing crops or pasture without disturbing the soil through tillage (ploughing or other mechanical agitation). Seeds are placed directly into the residue of the previous crop using specialised drills and planters that cut a narrow slot, drop in seed and fertiliser, and close the furrow. Although ancient farmers practised forms of no-till, the modern system became widespread only after the mid-20th century with the advent of effective herbicides and seeding machinery.

Benefits

Undisturbed soil retains its structure, fungal networks and organic matter, which improves water infiltration and retention and sharply reduces erosion by wind and water — a lesson driven home by the Dust Bowl of the 1930s. Leaving residue on the surface protects the soil and provides habitat. No-till also sequesters carbon in the soil, reduces fuel and labour costs, and requires fewer field passes. Adoption grew fastest in the Americas and Australia, driven by erosion control, moisture conservation and economics.

Drawbacks and criticisms

Without tillage to bury weeds, many no-till systems rely more heavily on herbicides, contributing to herbicide-resistant weed populations. Nutrients and acidity can stratify near the surface, some soils warm more slowly in spring, and yields can dip during the transition years as soil biology adjusts. Researchers debate how much of the carbon benefit persists at depth and how dependent the system is on chemical weed control; variants using cover crops and rotations aim to reduce that dependence.

Practices

Modern no-till is usually combined with crop rotation, cover crops, and keeping the soil covered at all times — the trio of principles known as conservation agriculture. Strip-till, in which only narrow bands are tilled where seed is placed, is a related compromise technique.
