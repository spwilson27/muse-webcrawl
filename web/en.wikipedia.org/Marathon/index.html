Marathon

The marathon is a long-distance foot race with a distance of 42.195 kilometres (about 26 miles 385 yards), usually run as a road race but also on trails. The event was instituted for the first modern Olympic Games in 1896 in Athens, inspired by the legend of the Greek messenger Pheidippides, who was said to have run from the battlefield of Marathon to Athens to announce victory, then collapsed and died.

History

The 1896 Olympic marathon, won by Greece's Spyridon Louis, covered roughly 40 kilometres. Distances varied until the 1908 London Olympics, where the course was extended so the race would finish in front of the royal box at White City Stadium, giving 26 miles 385 yards; this was standardised as the official distance in 1921. The Boston Marathon, first run in 1897, is the world's oldest annual marathon. Women's marathon running developed later: women were long excluded from official races, but pioneers such as Kathrine Switzer (Boston, 1967) pressed for inclusion, and a women's Olympic marathon was added in 1984, won by Joan Benoit.

The modern event

More than 800 marathons are held worldwide each year. The largest, in New York City, regularly has over 50,000 finishers. Elite racing is organised around the World Marathon Majors series (Tokyo, Boston, London, Berlin, Chicago and New York). Berlin's fast, flat course has been the site of many world records. In 2019, Eliud Kipchoge ran 1:59:40 in a special exhibition event in Vienna — the first sub-two-hour marathon — though the assisted conditions meant it did not count as a world record; the ratified men's record was set by Kelvin Kiptum in Chicago in 2023.

Training and physiology

Recreational runners typically train for several months, building to long runs of 30 km or more. The marathon stresses glycogen stores, which typically deplete around 30–35 km — the point runners call "hitting the wall" — and pacing, hydration and carbohydrate intake are central to performance. Health risks include hyponatraemia from over-drinking and, rarely, sudden cardiac events.
