Kingdom: Animalia

Class: Insecta
Order: Coleoptera
Family: Lampyridae

A firefly emitting its bioluminescent flash.

The Lampyridae are a family of elateroid beetles with more than 2,000 described species, many of which are light-emitting. They are soft-bodied beetles commonly called fireflies, lightning bugs, or glowworms for their conspicuous production of light, mainly during twilight, to attract mates. Light production in Lampyridae originated as an aposematic warning signal that the beetles are distasteful, and later evolved as a signal for sexual selection.

Bioluminescence

Fireflies produce light in specialised organs in the abdomen through a chemical reaction involving the enzyme luciferase acting on the substrate luciferin, in the presence of oxygen and ATP. The reaction is remarkably efficient, producing very little heat — a "cold light". Flash patterns, colour, and timing are species-specific and form a courtship dialogue: flying males emit a pattern and perched females respond after a characteristic delay. Some predatory females of the genus Photuris mimic the responses of other species to lure and eat their males. In parts of Southeast Asia and the southeastern United States, some species synchronise their flashes across large groups.

Life cycle and habitat

Firefly larvae live in moist soil and leaf litter, feeding on snails, slugs, and worms; many larvae also glow, apparently as a warning to predators. Adults of many species feed little or not at all. Fireflies favour warm, humid environments — meadows, marshes, and forest edges. Many populations are declining due to habitat loss, pesticide use, and light pollution, which interferes with their flash communication.
