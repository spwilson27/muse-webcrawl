Immunology

Immunology studies the immune system in health and disease.

Immunology is a branch of biology and medicine that covers the study of immune systems in all organisms. Immunologists study the physiological functioning of the immune system in states of both health and disease, malfunctions of the immune system in disorders such as autoimmune diseases, hypersensitivities, immune deficiency, and transplant rejection, as well as the physical, chemical, and physiological characteristics of the components of the immune system in vitro, in situ, and in vivo.

History

The discipline grew from nineteenth-century microbiology: Elie Metchnikoff discovered phagocytosis and championed cellular immunity, while Paul Ehrlich developed the side-chain theory of antibody formation that underpinned humoral immunity. Twentieth-century milestones included the elucidation of antibody structure, the clonal selection theory of Frank Macfarlane Burnet, the discovery of the major histocompatibility complex, and the development of monoclonal antibody technology by Köhler and Milstein in 1975.

Subfields and applications

Clinical immunology deals with disorders of the immune system, from allergies and asthma to primary immunodeficiencies and HIV/AIDS. Immunology underpins vaccination, organ transplantation, blood transfusion, cancer immunotherapy such as checkpoint inhibitors and CAR-T cells, and diagnostic techniques including ELISA and flow cytometry. Related branches include immunogenetics, neuroimmunology, reproductive immunology, and ecoimmunology, which studies immune investment in wild animals.
