Conformity

Conformity is the act of matching attitudes, beliefs, and behaviours to group norms, politics or being like-minded. Norms are implicit, specific rules that a group of individuals share and use to guide their interactions with others. People often choose to conform to society rather than to pursue personal desires — because it is often easier to follow the path others have made already, rather than forging a new one.

Types

Herbert Kelman distinguished three processes of social influence: compliance (public conformity without private acceptance), identification (conforming to maintain a valued relationship or role), and internalisation (genuine acceptance of the group's beliefs). Morton Deutsch and Harold Gerard distinguished normative influence (conforming to be liked or accepted) from informational influence (conforming because one believes the group is correct).

Classic experiments

Muzafer Sherif (1935)

Using the autokinetic effect, Sherif showed that individuals' estimates of a stationary light's movement converged toward a group norm.

Solomon Asch (1951)

In Asch's line-judgment experiments, participants gave obviously wrong answers about one-third of the time when confederates unanimously chose an incorrect line; about 75% conformed at least once. A single dissenting ally sharply reduced conformity.

Stanley Milgram

Milgram's obedience experiments demonstrated a related phenomenon — compliance with authority figures — while his "crashing" studies examined how people respond to norm violations.

Factors affecting conformity

Conformity increases with group size up to a point, unanimity, group cohesion and the perceived status of the group, and tends to be higher in collectivist cultures. Unanimity is especially important: one dissenter greatly reduces conformity. Age, self-esteem and the public nature of responses also matter.

Minority influence

Consistent minorities can shift majority views, as shown by Serge Moscovici's experiments; minority influence tends to produce private, lasting conversion rather than public compliance.
