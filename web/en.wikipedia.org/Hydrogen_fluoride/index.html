Hydrogen fluoride is a chemical compound with the formula HF. This colourless gas or liquid is the principal industrial source of fluorine and is the precursor to most fluorine compounds, including pharmaceuticals, fluoropolymers such as Teflon, and refrigerants. It is an unusually powerful contact poison: it readily penetrates tissue and binds calcium and magnesium, so exposure can cause deep burns and dangerous systemic hypocalcaemia.

Structure and properties

Hydrogen fluoride boils at 19.5 °C, far higher than the other hydrogen halides, because its molecules associate into zigzag chains through strong hydrogen bonds; in the gas phase it forms oligomers such as (HF)6. Dissolved in water it forms hydrofluoric acid. Although classified as a weak acid (pKa ≈ 3.2) because of strong internal hydrogen bonding and homoconjugation in solution, aqueous HF is aggressively corrosive and uniquely able to dissolve silica and glass, forming hexafluorosilicic acid — the property used for etching glass and in semiconductor fabrication.

Production and uses

HF is produced by treating the mineral fluorspar (calcium fluoride) with concentrated sulfuric acid in rotary kilns. It is converted industrially to elemental fluorine by electrolysis, to aluminium fluoride and cryolite for aluminium smelting, to uranium hexafluoride for enrichment, and to a wide range of organofluorine compounds. It also serves as a catalyst in petroleum alkylation units for producing high-octane petrol.

Safety

Contact with hydrogen fluoride is a medical emergency: fluoride ions penetrate tissues deeply and sequester calcium, potentially causing cardiac arrhythmia and death even from burns covering a small skin area. First aid includes copious washing and application of calcium gluconate gel.
