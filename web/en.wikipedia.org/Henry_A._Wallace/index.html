Henry Agard Wallace (7 October 1888 – 18 November 1965) was an American politician, journalist and agricultural innovator who served as the 33rd vice president of the United States from 1941 to 1945 under President Franklin D. Roosevelt. Before that he was Secretary of Agriculture (1933–1940), presiding over New Deal farm programs, and afterwards Secretary of Commerce (1945–1946). The great liberal standard-bearer of his era, he ran for president in 1948 on the Progressive Party ticket.

Agriculture

Wallace came from a distinguished Iowa farm family: his grandfather edited Wallaces' Farmer and his father, Henry C. Wallace, was Secretary of Agriculture under Presidents Harding and Coolidge. A plant geneticist by training, Wallace founded the Hi-Bred Corn Company in 1926 — later Pioneer Hi-Bred — which pioneered commercial hybrid seed corn and transformed American agriculture. As Roosevelt's agriculture secretary he administered the Agricultural Adjustment Act, soil conservation programs and rural relief, and championed the "ever-normal granary" concept of managed farm surpluses.

Vice presidency and the Commerce Department

Chosen as Roosevelt's running mate in 1940, Wallace as vice president chaired the Board of Economic Warfare, toured Latin America and Asia, and articulated a vision of the postwar world in his famous 1942 "Century of the Common Man" speech. Party conservatives, distrusting his liberalism and mysticism, forced him off the ticket in 1944 in favour of Harry S. Truman — who thus succeeded Roosevelt in 1945. Roosevelt appointed Wallace Secretary of Commerce, and Truman kept him until September 1946, when he fired him over a speech advocating a softer policy toward the Soviet Union.

1948 campaign and later life

As editor of The New Republic and then as the Progressive Party's presidential nominee in 1948, Wallace campaigned against the emerging Cold War, for civil rights and desegregation in the South, and for expanded welfare programs. His campaign was damaged by Communist Party support, which he declined to repudiate, and he won only 2.4 percent of the vote. He later broke with the Progressives over the Korean War, recanted his earlier views of the USSR in the 1952 article "Where I Was Wrong", and retired to plant breeding at his farm in South Salem, New York, developing strawberries, gladioli and chickens. He died of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis in 1965.
