Flavia Julia Helena (c. 246/248 – c. 330), often known as Saint Helena, was an empress of the Roman Empire and mother of the emperor Constantine the Great. She is traditionally credited with finding the relics of the True Cross on a pilgrimage to the Holy Land late in her life, and she is venerated as a saint in the Catholic and Orthodox churches.

Life

Helena was probably born in Drepanon in Bithynia, a town Constantine later renamed Helenopolis in her honour. Of humble origin according to later sources, she became the wife or partner of Constantius Chlorus, to whom she bore Constantine. Constantius set her aside for a politically advantageous marriage, but when Constantine became emperor in 306 he recalled his mother to court, bestowed on her the title of Augusta and placed her image on coins.

Pilgrimage and the True Cross

A devout Christian, Helena undertook a pilgrimage to Syria Palaestina and Jerusalem in 326–328, founding and endowing churches including, by tradition, the Church of the Nativity in Bethlehem and the Church of Eleona on the Mount of Olives. Later tradition — reported by Ambrose and elaborated by subsequent writers — held that she supervised excavations at Golgotha and discovered the True Cross and other relics of the Passion. Her feast is celebrated on 18 August in the West and on 21 May in the East, together with her son.

Legacy

Numerous places are named for her, including the island of Saint Helena. Her relics are claimed by several churches, and she remains a popular subject of medieval legend, including the Welsh tradition that made her a daughter of the British king Coel of Colchester.
