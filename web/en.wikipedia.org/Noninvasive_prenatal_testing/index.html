Noninvasive prenatal testing

Noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT), also known as cell-free DNA (cfDNA) screening, is a method of determining the risk that a foetus will be born with certain genetic abnormalities. This testing analyses small fragments of DNA that are circulating in a pregnant woman's blood; unlike most DNA in cell nuclei, these fragments are free-floating rather than within cells, and a fraction originates from the placenta.

Method and scope

Introduced commercially in 2011, NIPT counts DNA fragments from each chromosome in a maternal blood sample drawn from about 10 weeks of gestation. Excess fragments from chromosome 21, 18 or 13 indicate increased risk of Down syndrome, Edwards syndrome or Patau syndrome respectively. Panels may also screen sex-chromosome conditions and selected microdeletions, with lower accuracy for the rarer conditions.

Advantages and limits

Detection rates for Down syndrome exceed 99% with very low false-positive rates, far better than older serum screening, and the test carries no miscarriage risk, unlike amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling. NIPT is nonetheless a screening test, not a diagnosis: positive results require invasive confirmation, and results can be confounded by low foetal fraction, confined placental mosaicism or maternal conditions.
