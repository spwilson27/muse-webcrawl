Quartic function

In algebra, a quartic function is a function of the form f(x) = ax4 + bx3 + cx2 + dx + e, where a is nonzero. It is defined by a polynomial of degree four, called a quartic polynomial. A quartic equation sets such a polynomial equal to zero. Quartic functions are the highest-degree polynomial functions that are always solvable by radicals: the general quartic can be solved with a formula involving arithmetic operations and root extractions, whereas no such general formula exists for degree five and above, by the Abel–Ruffini theorem.

History

The solution of the quartic equation is credited to Lodovico Ferrari, a student of Gerolamo Cardano, around 1540. It was published in Cardano's Ars Magna (1545), alongside the solution of the cubic. Ferrari's method reduces the quartic to a cubic auxiliary equation, known as the resolvent cubic.

Properties

The graph of a quartic with positive leading coefficient rises to positive infinity on both ends; with negative leading coefficient it falls on both ends. A quartic can have zero, two, or four real roots (counted without multiplicity), and it may have up to three local extrema and two inflection points. A biquadratic function, of the form f(x) = ax4 + cx2 + e, can be solved by substituting y = x2, reducing it to a quadratic.

Solving the quartic

Ferrari's solution

Ferrari's method begins by depressing the quartic — removing the cubic term by a linear substitution — and then completing the square on both sides after introducing an auxiliary parameter. Choosing the parameter so that one side becomes a perfect square requires solving a cubic equation. The result expresses the four roots in terms of square and cube roots of the coefficients.

Other methods

Descartes' method factors the quartic into two quadratics; Euler's method uses a different resolvent. In numerical practice, roots are usually found with iterative methods such as Newton's method or dedicated polynomial root finders.

Applications

Quartic functions arise in geometry (for example, the intersection of conic sections), in optics, in computer graphics where quartic surfaces and toroidal patches are used, and in problems of minimizing or interpolating quantities governed by fourth-degree polynomials.
