Gin

Gin is a distilled alcoholic drink flavoured predominantly with juniper berries (Juniperus communis) and other botanicals. It originated as a medicinal liquor made by monks and alchemists across Europe and later developed in the Low Countries as jenever, from which modern gin evolved after becoming popular in Britain.

Gin is produced by redistilling a neutral spirit with botanicals — typically juniper, coriander seed, angelica root, orris root, liquorice, citrus peel, and others — or by passing the spirit vapours through a basket of botanicals. The European Union and other jurisdictions require a minimum alcoholic strength (37.5% ABV in the EU; 40% in the United States) and that the predominant flavour be juniper.

History

Dutch jenever was popularised in England after the Glorious Revolution of 1688 brought William of Orange to the throne. Cheap, unregulated gin production in early 18th-century London fuelled the so-called "Gin Craze", depicted in Hogarth's engraving Gin Lane (1751), and prompted a series of Gin Acts restricting its sale. The invention of the column still in the 19th century enabled the cleaner "London dry" style.

Styles

  • London dry gin — a dry, juniper-forward style made by redistilling neutral spirit with botanicals, with nothing but water added after distillation;
  • Plymouth gin — a style historically associated with Plymouth, England;
  • Old Tom gin — a lightly sweetened style popular in the 18th and 19th centuries;
  • Jenever — the Dutch and Belgian original, made with a malt wine base;
  • Sloe gin — a liqueur made by infusing gin with sloe berries and sugar.

Gin is the base of many classic cocktails, including the martini, the gin and tonic, the Negroni, and the Tom Collins. The gin and tonic originated among British officers in India, who combined quinine tonic water with gin.
