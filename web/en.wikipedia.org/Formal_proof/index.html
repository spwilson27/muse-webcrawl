Formal proof

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In logic and mathematics, a formal proof or derivation is a finite sequence of sentences (known as well-formed formulas in the case of a formal language), each of which is an axiom or follows from the preceding sentences in the sequence by a rule of inference. A formal proof differs from an informal mathematical proof, written in natural language, in that every step is justified by explicit syntactic rules, so that the correctness of the proof can in principle be checked mechanically.

Formal systems

A formal proof takes place within a formal system, which consists of a formal language, a set of axioms, and rules of inference. The last sentence of the sequence is a theorem of the system. Examples of proof systems include natural deduction, sequent calculus, and Hilbert-style axiomatic systems. The notion of formal proof was central to the foundational work of Frege, Russell, Hilbert, and Gödel; Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that sufficiently strong consistent formal systems cannot prove their own consistency.

Computer-assisted formalisation

Because fully formal proofs are extremely long, they are usually produced and checked with software: interactive theorem provers and proof assistants such as Coq, Lean, Isabelle, and Agda. Notable formalised results include the four colour theorem, the Kepler conjecture (the Flyspeck project), and the Feit–Thompson odd order theorem. Formal verification applies the same techniques to prove the correctness of computer hardware and software.
