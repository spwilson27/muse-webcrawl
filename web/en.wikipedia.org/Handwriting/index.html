Handwriting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Handwriting is the personal and enduring style of writing done by hand with a writing instrument such as a pen or pencil. It includes both printing (manuscript) and cursive styles, and is distinguished from formal calligraphy or typeface. Because every person's handwriting is unique and largely consistent, it is studied in forensic document examination, while the pseudoscientific practice of inferring personality from handwriting is known as graphology.

History and education

Handwriting styles evolved with writing materials — reed pens, quills, steel nibs, fountain pens and ballpoints each reshaping letterforms. School systems traditionally taught model scripts such as the Palmer Method and D'Nealian in the United States or copperplate-derived hands in Europe. With the spread of keyboards, the teaching of cursive has declined in many countries, prompting debate about handwriting's cognitive and educational benefits; research associates writing by hand with improved letter recognition and memory in children.

Health and legibility

Poor handwriting is sometimes called dysgraphia when it reflects a specific learning difficulty rather than lack of instruction. Handwriting speed and legibility remain professionally important in fields such as medicine, where illegible prescriptions have prompted a shift to electronic records.
