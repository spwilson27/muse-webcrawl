Myometrium

Part ofUterus
Tissue typeSmooth muscle
LayersLongitudinal, circular (stratum vasculare), and inner longitudinal
Adjacent layersEndometrium (inner), perimetrium (outer)
FunctionUterine contraction in labour and menstruation

The myometrium is the middle layer of the wall of the uterus, consisting largely of smooth muscle cells (uterine myocytes) embedded in connective tissue rich in blood vessels, lymphatics, and nerves. It lies between the inner lining, the endometrium, and the outer serosa, the perimetrium. The myometrium is what allows the uterus to expand enormously during pregnancy and to contract powerfully during labour to expel the baby — among the strongest contractions produced by any muscle in the body.

Cross-section of the uterine wall: the myometrium is the thick muscular middle layer.

Structure

The myometrium is organised into layers of smooth muscle running longitudinally (outer), circularly (middle, the vascular stratum vasculare), and longitudinally again (inner, adjacent to the endometrium); the inner portion, the junctional zone, is visible on MRI and plays a role in sperm transport and implantation. Muscle bundles interlace in a criss-cross pattern that, on contraction, also compresses the uterine blood vessels — a "living ligature" mechanism that limits bleeding after delivery of the placenta.

Physiology

During pregnancy the myometrium grows by hypertrophy and stretches while remaining relatively quiescent under the influence of progesterone. At term, rising oestrogen, prostaglandins, and expression of oxytocin receptors convert it to a synchronously contracting organ; oxytocin from the pituitary drives labour contractions, and synthetic oxytocin is used to induce labour or to treat postpartum haemorrhage. Outside pregnancy, gentler myometrial contractions occur across the menstrual cycle and contribute to menstrual cramps (dysmenorrhoea) when prostaglandin levels are high.

Disorders

Benign tumours of the myometrium — leiomyomas, commonly called fibroids — are extremely common and can cause heavy bleeding, pain, and infertility. In adenomyosis, endometrial tissue grows into the myometrium, producing an enlarged, tender uterus. Malignant tumours (leiomyosarcomas) are rare. After childbirth, failure of the myometrium to contract (uterine atony) is the leading cause of postpartum haemorrhage.
