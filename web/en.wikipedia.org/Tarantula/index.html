Tarantula

Tarantulas comprise a group of large and often hairy spiders of the family Theraphosidae. As of recent classifications, roughly 1,100 species have been identified in about 170 genera, found on every continent except Antarctica, with the greatest diversity in the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Australia. The name originally referred to the European wolf spider Lycosa tarantula of southern Italy, associated in folklore with "tarantism" and the tarantella dance, before being applied to the large mygalomorph spiders of the New World.

Description

Tarantulas range in size from a few centimetres to the Goliath birdeater (Theraphosa blondi) of South America, which can have a leg span of about 30 cm (12 in) and a mass over 170 g (6 oz) — the largest spider by mass. Like all spiders they have eight legs, two body segments (cephalothorax and abdomen), and chelicerae bearing fangs that fold parallel to the body axis, striking downward. Most species are covered in fine hairs; many New World tarantulas possess urticating hairs on the abdomen, which they kick at threats and which can irritate skin and eyes.

Behaviour and diet

Tarantulas are nocturnal ambush predators that do not spin prey-catching webs, though they line burrows, tree hollows, or ground retreats with silk. They feed mainly on insects and other arthropods, with large species also taking small vertebrates such as frogs, lizards, and occasionally birds. Their venom is adapted to subdue invertebrate prey; bites to humans are generally comparable to a bee sting in severity, though painful, and no reliably documented human deaths are attributed to tarantula bites.

Life cycle

Females are long-lived — often 20 to 30 years in captivity — while males typically live only a few years after maturity. Females lay egg sacs containing hundreds to thousands of eggs and may guard them until hatching. Tarantulas grow by moulting, shedding the exoskeleton; females continue to moult throughout life and can regenerate lost legs over successive moults.

Keeping and conservation

Docile species such as the Chilean rose and Mexican redknee are popular in the pet trade. Habitat loss and over-collection threaten some species; several Brachypelma and Poecilotheria species are listed under CITES Appendix II.
