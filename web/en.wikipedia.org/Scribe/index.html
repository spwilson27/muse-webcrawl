Scribe

Statue of an ancient Egyptian scribe, seated with a papyrus scroll on his lap.

A scribe is a person who serves as a professional copyist, especially one who made copies of manuscripts before the invention of automatic printing. The profession of scribe first developed in ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt, where writing was essential for recording laws, taxes, trade, religious texts and royal decrees, and where literacy was confined to a small, trained elite.

Ancient Egypt

In ancient Egypt, scribes formed a respected class of administrators. They were trained in schools (per-ankh, "houses of life") in the two scripts, hieratic and later demotic, as well as hieroglyphs. Scribes recorded accounts, tax assessments, legal proceedings and literature, and their patron deities were Thoth and the goddess Seshat. The famous "Seated Scribe" statue in the Louvre is among the best-known depictions of the profession.

Mesopotamia

In Sumer, Akkad, Babylon and Assyria, scribes wrote cuneiform on clay tablets in temple and palace schools (edubba, "tablet houses"). Their copies preserved epics such as the Epic of Gilgamesh, law codes such as that of Hammurabi, and vast administrative archives.

Jewish scribes

In Judaism, the sofer is a scribe trained to transcribe Torah scrolls, tefillin and mezuzot according to strict rules. After the Babylonian exile, figures such as Ezra "the scribe" became religious authorities, and in the Second Temple period "scribes" (Greek: grammateis) were a class of scholars and interpreters of the Law frequently mentioned in the New Testament.

Medieval Europe

During the Middle Ages, monastic scribes in scriptoria copied biblical, liturgical and classical texts by hand, preserving much of ancient literature. Later, lay professional scribes and stationers served universities, courts and merchants. The invention of the printing press in the 15th century gradually displaced the copyist's trade, though scribes continued in legal and administrative roles for centuries.
