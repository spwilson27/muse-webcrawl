The University of Paris (French: Université de Paris) was a medieval university in Paris, France, one of the world's first universities. Established in the mid-twelfth century, it grew from the cathedral school of Notre-Dame and became a leading centre of theology and philosophy, organised into four faculties: Arts, Medicine, Law and Theology. It is often called the Sorbonne after the college founded by Robert de Sorbon in 1257.

History

The university received a charter from King Philip II in 1200 and recognition from Pope Innocent III; statutes were issued by the papal legate Robert of Courçon in 1215. Masters such as Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas taught there, and the university's theology faculty was an arbiter of doctrine for the Catholic Church. A great dispersion in 1229 after conflict with the town won the university further privileges.

Suppression and revival

The university was suppressed during the French Revolution in 1793, and higher education in France was reorganised under Napoleon as the Université de France. Faculties were re-established in the nineteenth century, and the Sorbonne was rebuilt under the Third Republic.

Division

Following the student unrest of May 1968, the Faure law split the University of Paris into thirteen autonomous universities in 1970, numbered Paris I through Paris XIII. Successors include Sorbonne University and Panthéon-Sorbonne (Paris I). The name "Sorbonne" remains associated with the historic building in the Latin Quarter shared by several of these institutions.
