Heat shock protein

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) are a family of proteins produced by cells in response to exposure to stressful conditions. They were first described in relation to heat shock, but they are now known to be induced by other stresses including cold, UV light and environmental toxins, and to be expressed during wound healing and tissue remodelling. Many HSPs act as molecular chaperones, helping other proteins fold correctly, preventing aggregation, and assisting in the refolding or degradation of damaged proteins.

Discovery

The heat shock response was first observed in 1962 by the Italian geneticist Ferruccio Ritossa, who noticed a pattern of chromosome puffing in the salivary glands of fruit flies (Drosophila) that had been accidentally exposed to elevated temperature. The proteins responsible were characterised in the 1970s and proved to be highly conserved across virtually all organisms, from bacteria to humans.

Classification

Heat shock proteins are grouped into families according to their molecular weight, including Hsp60, Hsp70 and Hsp90 — among the most abundant and best-studied chaperones — as well as the small heat shock proteins (sHSPs). Hsp70 proteins assist in folding and in transporting proteins across membranes; Hsp90 is important in the maturation of signalling proteins such as steroid hormone receptors and kinases, and is a target of interest in cancer research.

Functions and medical relevance

Beyond stress protection, HSPs participate in protein transport, immune responses and antigen presentation. Extracellular heat shock proteins can stimulate the immune system, and HSP-based approaches have been investigated in vaccines and cancer immunotherapy. Because many tumours overexpress Hsp90, inhibitors of this chaperone have been developed as potential anticancer drugs.
