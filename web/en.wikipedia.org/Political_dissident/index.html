Political dissident

A political dissident is a person who actively challenges an established political system, doctrine or policy, in opposition to the authority of the state or ruling party. The term came into particularly wide use for critics of authoritarian governments, especially dissidents in the Soviet Union and Eastern Bloc during the Cold War, but it applies generally to individuals who dissent from an established political order, often at personal risk.

Soviet and Eastern Bloc dissidents

In the Soviet Union, dissidents (dissidenty) emerged openly after the Khrushchev Thaw and the 1966 Sinyavsky–Daniel trial. They included human-rights activists associated with the Moscow Helsinki Group (founded 1976 to monitor Soviet compliance with the Helsinki Accords), religious believers, national minorities and advocates of emigration. Notable figures included Andrei Sakharov, Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, Vladimir Bukovsky, Natan Sharansky and Yelena Bonner. The Soviet authorities responded with imprisonment, exile, expulsion, and the punitive use of psychiatry.

Methods

Dissidents characteristically relied on legalistic and nonviolent methods: circulating samizdat (self-published) texts, signing open letters and petitions, documenting human-rights abuses, and appealing to constitutional and international legal commitments. In Czechoslovakia, Charter 77 (1977), associated with Václav Havel and Jan Patočka, applied similar methods.

Wider usage

The term has since been applied to dissenters under many regimes, including China (for example Wei Jingsheng and Liu Xiaobo), Cuba, Iran, Myanmar (Aung San Suu Kyi) and others. Related concepts include prisoner of conscience, a term promoted by Amnesty International for persons imprisoned for the nonviolent expression of their beliefs.
