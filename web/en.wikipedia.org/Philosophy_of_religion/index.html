Philosophy of religion

Main topicsExistence of God, problem of evil, faith and reason, religious language, life after death
Key figuresAnselm, Aquinas, Hume, Kant, Kierkegaard, Plantinga, Swinburne
Related fieldsTheology, metaphysics, epistemology, ethics

Philosophy of religion is the branch of philosophy concerned with questions about religion, including the nature and existence of God or gods, the examination of religious experience, analysis of religious language and texts, and the relationship of religion to science, ethics, and other areas of philosophy. Unlike theology, it approaches these questions from a standpoint not presupposing the truth of any particular faith tradition.

Arguments about God's existence

Classical arguments for the existence of God include the ontological argument of Anselm (and later Descartes and Gödel), which reasons from the concept of a greatest conceivable being; cosmological arguments (Aquinas's "Five Ways," the kalām argument), which reason from contingency or the beginning of the universe; and teleological or design arguments, from the apparent order and fine-tuning of nature. Critics from Hume and Kant onward have challenged each of these, and contemporary debates continue in analytical form.

The problem of evil

The problem of evil asks how an omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good God can coexist with the world's suffering. The logical version (associated in modern form with J. L. Mackie) claims an outright inconsistency; responses include free-will defenses (notably Alvin Plantinga's) and soul-making theodicies (John Hick). The evidential version, pressed by William Rowe, argues that apparently gratuitous evils make God's existence improbable.

Faith, reason, and language

Philosophers debate whether religious belief requires evidence (evidentialism), may be "properly basic" (Reformed epistemology), or should be taken on faith (fideism). In the 20th century, logical positivists such as A. J. Ayer argued religious utterances are cognitively meaningless; later discussion turned to Wittgensteinian views of religious language as a distinctive "form of life," and to analyses of religious experience, miracles (Hume's famous critique), religious diversity, and the possibility of life after death.

Scope

Historically focused on Western monotheism, the field increasingly engages Hindu, Buddhist, Islamic, Jewish, and other traditions, and topics such as pantheism, polytheism, and non-theistic conceptions of the divine.
