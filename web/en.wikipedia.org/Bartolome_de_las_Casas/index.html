Bartolomé de las Casas (1484–1566) was a Spanish Dominican friar, historian, and social reformer who became the most famous early advocate for the rights of the Indigenous peoples of the Americas. Known as the "Defender of the Indians," he documented the atrocities committed by Spanish colonizers and spent decades lobbying the Spanish Crown for more humane laws.

Early life in the Indies

Las Casas first sailed to Hispaniola in 1502, where he received an encomienda — a grant of Indigenous labor. He participated in expeditions to Cuba and witnessed brutal massacres of native people. In 1514, after a period of spiritual reflection prompted by a sermon denouncing the treatment of the Indians, he renounced his encomienda and dedicated his life to their defense.

Advocacy and writings

Las Casas returned repeatedly to Spain to plead the Indigenous cause at court. His most famous work, A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies (1552), catalogued Spanish cruelty and was translated across Europe, contributing to the "Black Legend" of Spanish colonization. He also wrote the monumental Historia de las Indias. His advocacy helped bring about the New Laws of 1542, which restricted the encomienda system. In 1550–51 he debated the philosopher Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda at Valladolid over whether Indigenous peoples were natural slaves.

Legacy

Las Casas served briefly as bishop of Chiapas in New Spain. Though he once suggested African labor as a substitute for Indigenous labor — a position he later repented — he is remembered as a foundational figure in the development of universal human rights and international law.
