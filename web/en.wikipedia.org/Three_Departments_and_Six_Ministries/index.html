Structure of the central government in imperial China

The Three Departments and Six Ministries system (三省六部) was the primary structure of the central government in imperial China from the Sui dynasty (581–618) until the end of the Qing dynasty in 1912. It divided imperial administration between three high policy organs and six executive ministries, creating a durable framework that influenced government in Korea, Vietnam and Japan.

The Three Departments

The Central Secretariat (中書省) drafted imperial edicts and policy. The Chancellery (門下省) reviewed and could return edicts for reconsideration, acting as a check on both emperor and Secretariat. The Department of State Affairs (尚書省) implemented policy through the Six Ministries beneath it. Under the early Tang this division separated drafting, review and execution; over time the offices merged in practice, and under the Song and Ming the system was progressively hollowed out.

The Six Ministries

  • Ministry of Personnel (吏部) — appointments, evaluations and promotions of civil officials.
  • Ministry of Revenue (戶部) — census, taxation and the treasury.
  • Ministry of Rites (禮部) — court ritual, state ceremonies, foreign envoys and the imperial examinations.
  • Ministry of War (兵部) — military administration, officers and logistics.
  • Ministry of Justice (刑部) — laws, prisons and judicial review.
  • Ministry of Works (工部) — public construction, irrigation and state manufactures.

History

The system was formalised under the Sui and perfected under the Tang dynasty, whose administrative law in turn influenced Japan's ritsuryō government. The Ming founder, the Hongwu Emperor, abolished the Secretariat in 1380 after the Hu Weiyong case and ruled through the Six Ministries directly. Under the Qing the ministries remained, increasingly overseen by the Grand Council.
