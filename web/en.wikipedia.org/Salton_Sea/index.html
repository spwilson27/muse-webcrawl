Salton Sea

The shrinking shoreline of the Salton Sea, California.

The Salton Sea is a shallow, landlocked, highly saline body of water in Southern California's Imperial and Coachella valleys. Lying about 236 feet (72 m) below sea level, it is the largest lake in California by surface area. The modern sea was created accidentally in 1905, when Colorado River floodwater breached an irrigation canal and poured into the Salton Sink for two years.

History

The basin had held a succession of freshwater lakes, most recently Lake Cahuilla, in earlier centuries. After the 1905 flood formed the sea, it became a popular resort in the 1950s and 1960s, with yacht clubs, marinas and celebrity visitors at communities such as Salton City and Bombay Beach.

Environmental crisis

With no outlet, the sea has grown steadily saltier — now saltier than the Pacific Ocean — fed by agricultural runoff from the Imperial Valley. Tilapia and other introduced fish once supported huge bird populations on the Pacific Flyway, but rising salinity and pollution have caused mass die-offs. Since the early 2000s the sea has been shrinking, exposing toxic dust-laden playa that contributes to high asthma rates in surrounding communities. California has committed to habitat and dust-suppression projects, including the Salton Sea Management Program.

In popular culture

The decaying resorts and surreal landscapes of Bombay Beach and the nearby art community of East Jesus have made the area a magnet for photographers and filmmakers.
