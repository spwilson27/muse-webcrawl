Carpel

A carpel is the female reproductive organ of a flower, consisting of an ovary, a stigma, and usually a style. Carpels are thought to be evolutionarily modified leaves (megasporophylls) bearing ovules along their margins. One or more carpels make up the pistil, and together all carpels of a flower constitute the gynoecium.

Structure

The ovary at the base contains one or more ovules attached by placentation; after fertilisation it develops into the fruit wall (pericarp). The style is a stalk raising the stigma, the receptive surface on which pollen germinates. Carpels may be free (apocarpous gynoecium) or fused together (syncarpous), as in the tomato or tulip.

Evolution

The carpel is a defining feature of the flowering plants (angiosperms): by enclosing the ovules, it protects them and, after fertilisation, aids seed dispersal through fruit formation. Fossil and developmental evidence suggests carpels arose by the infolding of a spore-bearing leaf around its ovules.

Placentation

The arrangement of ovules within the ovary — axile, parietal, basal, apical, or free-central — is an important character in plant taxonomy.
