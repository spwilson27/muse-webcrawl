Myrmecology


Labelled image: ant colony behavior, a central topic of myrmecology

Myrmecology (from Greek: μύρμηξ, myrmex, "ant" and λόγος, logos, "study") is a branch of entomology focusing on the scientific study of ants. Some early myrmecologists considered ant society as the ideal form of society and sought in it the solution to human problems of organization. Ants continue to serve as model systems for studying questions in evolutionary biology, chemical communication, and complex self-organizing behavior.

History

The word myrmecology was coined by William Morton Wheeler (1865–1937), though human interest in ants dates back much further, with references in ancient religious texts. The first scientist to attempt a scientific study of ants was Auguste Forel (1848–1931). Twentieth-century figures included Edward O. Wilson, whose work on colony organization, caste systems and pheromone communication transformed the field, and Bert Hölldobler, co-author with Wilson of The Ants (1990), which won the Pulitzer Prize.

Scope

Myrmecology encompasses ant taxonomy and systematics, colony structure and demography, division of labor, chemical ecology (including trail and alarm pheromones), symbiotic relationships with other organisms (myrmecophiles, fungus-growing ants, and ant–plant mutualisms), and the ecological impact of ants as predators, seed dispersers, and soil engineers. Modern methods include DNA barcoding, transcriptomics, and automated behavioral tracking of individually marked workers.
