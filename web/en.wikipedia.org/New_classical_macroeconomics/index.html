New classical macroeconomics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

New classical macroeconomics, sometimes simply called the new classical school, is a school of thought in macroeconomics that builds its analysis entirely on a neoclassical framework. It emphasizes the importance of rigorous microfoundations, in particular rational expectations, and holds that markets generally clear and that prices and wages adjust flexibly.

The school emerged in the 1970s around economists such as Robert Lucas Jr., Thomas Sargent, Neil Wallace, and Robert Barro, initially as a challenge to mainstream Keynesian economics. Its central propositions included the policy ineffectiveness proposition — the claim that anticipated monetary policy cannot systematically affect real output — and the Lucas critique, which argued that econometric policy evaluation is invalid when it ignores how policy changes alter agents' expectations and behavior.

Key ideas

New classical economists assumed agents are rational, make optimal decisions subject to constraints, and use all available information when forming expectations, so that forecast errors are random rather than systematic. In models with continuous market clearing, involuntary unemployment has no natural place; observed fluctuations in output were attributed to real shocks, especially technology shocks, giving rise to real business cycle theory, a direct descendant of the new classical approach.

Influence and criticism

The new classical approach transformed macroeconomic methodology, making dynamic general equilibrium models with explicit microfoundations the standard for research. Critics objected to its conclusions as empirically implausible and argued that nominal rigidities, imperfect information, and coordination failures matter for real-world business cycles. New Keynesian economics arose partly in response, accepting the methodological demands of the new classicals while reintroducing sticky prices and wages. The eventual "new neoclassical synthesis" combined elements of both schools.
