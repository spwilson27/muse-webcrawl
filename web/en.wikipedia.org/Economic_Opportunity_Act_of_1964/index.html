The Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 (Pub. L. 88–452) authorized the formation of local Community Action Agencies as part of the War on Poverty. These agencies are directly regulated by the federal government. In March 1964, President Lyndon B. Johnson introduced the bill to Congress; he had declared an "unconditional war on poverty" in his State of the Union address in January 1964. The legislation was signed into law on August 20, 1964.

President Johnson signing the Act, 1964

The act created the Office of Economic Opportunity (OEO), headed by Sargent Shriver, to administer a range of anti-poverty programs. It authorized nearly $1 billion for programs including:

  • Job Corps — residential education and job training for disadvantaged young people.
  • Neighborhood Youth Corps — work experience for urban and rural youth.
  • Work-Study programs — part-time employment for college students.
  • VISTA (Volunteers in Service to America) — a domestic volunteer service corps.
  • Community Action Programs — grants to local Community Action Agencies, which administered programs such as Head Start, legal services, and community health centers.
  • Adult Basic Education and work experience programs, and loans for low-income rural families and migrant workers.

Legacy

Although the Office of Economic Opportunity was dismantled in the early 1970s under President Richard Nixon, many of its programs survived in other agencies: Head Start moved to the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare, Job Corps to the Department of Labor, and Community Action Agencies continued with federal support. The act remains a landmark of Johnson's Great Society domestic agenda.
