Private international law

Private international law, also known as conflict of laws, is the set of rules or principles that govern the choice of law in disputes that contain a foreign element, and which determine whether a court has jurisdiction and whether a foreign judgment will be recognised and enforced. Despite its name, it is primarily domestic law in each country, supplemented by international conventions.

Scope

A court hearing a case with foreign connections must answer three questions: (1) Does it have jurisdiction to hear the case? (2) If so, which jurisdiction's substantive law should it apply? (3) If a foreign judgment is involved, should it be recognised and enforced? Typical cases involve international contracts, cross-border torts, marriages and divorces between nationals of different states, and international child abduction.

Key concepts

Connecting factors such as domicile, nationality, the place where a contract was made (lex loci contractus), or where a tort occurred (lex loci delicti) point to the applicable law. The doctrine of renvoi — where a forum's choice-of-law rule refers to a foreign law that in turn refers the matter back or onward — remains controversial and is rejected for many commercial matters.

Harmonisation

The Hague Conference on Private International Law has produced many conventions, including the Hague Adoption Convention and the Hague Child Abduction Convention. Within the European Union, regulations such as Rome I (contracts) and Rome II (non-contractual obligations) unify choice-of-law rules across member states.
