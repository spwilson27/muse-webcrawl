Plasmapheresis

Automated plasmapheresis at a plasma donation centre

Plasmapheresis (from the Greek plasma, something moulded, and aphairesis, taking away) is the removal, treatment, and return or exchange of blood plasma or components thereof from and to the blood circulation. It is thus an extracorporeal therapy, a medical procedure performed outside the body. The method is also used to collect plasma for transfusion and for the manufacture of plasma-derived medicines from donors.

Technique

Blood is drawn from the patient or donor and passed through a device that separates plasma from the cellular components — either by centrifugation or by filtration through a membrane. The red cells, white cells and platelets are returned to the circulation, while the plasma is discarded, retained for donation, or replaced with albumin or fresh frozen plasma, depending on the purpose. A typical therapeutic plasma exchange removes and replaces one to one-and-a-half plasma volumes per session.

Uses

Therapeutic plasma exchange is used to remove harmful antibodies, immune complexes, toxins or abnormal proteins from the blood. Established indications include thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, Guillain–Barré syndrome, myasthenia gravis in crisis, anti-GBM disease and certain other autoimmune and neurological disorders, as well as removal of paraproteins in hyperviscosity syndrome. In donation, plasmapheresis allows plasma to be collected more frequently than whole blood because the cells are returned; plasma-derived products made from such donations include clotting factors, immunoglobulins and albumin.

History

The procedure was first described in experiments in the early twentieth century and developed clinically from the 1950s–60s with continuous-flow centrifuge machines. The word itself was coined by John Abel and colleagues at Johns Hopkins in 1914. Automated apheresis machines are now standard in blood centres worldwide.
