Rasa (aesthetics)

Rasa (Sanskrit: रस, literally "juice, essence, taste") is a fundamental concept in Indian aesthetics, denoting the emotional flavour or aesthetic relish experienced by the audience of a work of art—particularly drama, dance, music, and poetry. The theory of rasa originates in the Natya Shastra, the ancient treatise on dramaturgy attributed to Bharata Muni (roughly 200 BCE–200 CE), and was extensively developed by later commentators, most notably the Kashmiri philosopher Abhinavagupta (c. 950–1016).

Theory

According to the Natya Shastra, rasa arises from the combination of determinants (vibhava), consequents (anubhava), and transitory emotional states (vyabhicaribhava) which awaken a durable emotional state (sthayibhava) in the spectator. Bharata's famous rasa-sutra states: "Rasa is produced from a combination of determinants, consequents, and transitory states." The spectator who savours rasa is called a rasika.

The nine rasas

Bharata enumerated eight rasas, to which a ninth was later added:

  • Shringara — love, romance, beauty
  • Hasya — laughter, comedy
  • Karuna — sorrow, compassion, pathos
  • Raudra — anger, fury
  • Vira — heroism, courage
  • Bhayanaka — terror, fear
  • Bibhatsa — disgust, aversion
  • Adbhuta — wonder, amazement
  • Shanta — peace, tranquillity (added later)

Influence

Rasa theory remains central to classical Indian dance forms such as Bharatanatyam and Kathakali, to Indian classical music, and to Indian cinema, where popular films are often structured to evoke a sequence of rasas. In devotional (bhakti) traditions, rasa was adapted to describe the devotee's emotional relationship with the divine, notably in Gaudiya Vaishnavism.
