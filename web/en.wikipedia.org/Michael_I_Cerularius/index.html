Michael I Cerularius (Greek: Μιχαὴλ Κηρουλάριος; c. 1000 – 21 January 1059) was the Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople from 1043 to 1058. Ambitious and politically powerful, he is remembered chiefly for his part in the events of 1054 that produced the Great Schism between the Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic churches.

Rise

Born into a well-connected Constantinopolitan family, Cerularius was implicated in a plot against Emperor Michael IV, tonsured and exiled; on his return he became a monk and adviser, and in 1043 Emperor Constantine IX appointed him patriarch. He wielded great influence at court and over the city's populace, at times overshadowing the emperors themselves.

The schism of 1054

Tensions between Greek East and Latin West — over the Filioque clause, unleavened bread in the Eucharist, clerical celibacy and papal claims of universal jurisdiction — came to a head when Pope Leo IX sent legates under Cardinal Humbert to Constantinople. After a public confrontation, on 16 July 1054 Humbert laid a bull of excommunication against Cerularius on the altar of Hagia Sophia; Cerularius responded by excommunicating the legates. The mutual anathemas (lifted only in 1965) symbolise the permanent split between Orthodoxy and Catholicism.

Downfall

Cerularius's political dominance provoked Emperor Isaac I Komnenos, who had him arrested in 1058; the patriarch died in exile at Madytus in January 1059 before his trial could be completed. His contemporary Michael Psellos wrote an account of his career and an accusation prepared for the trial.
