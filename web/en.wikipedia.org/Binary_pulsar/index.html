Binary pulsar

A binary pulsar is a pulsar with a binary companion, often a white dwarf or neutron star (but in rare cases, another pulsar). Binary pulsars are one of the most useful tools astronomers have, because studying their orbital motion allows the very precise measurement of relativistic effects.

The Hulse–Taylor pulsar

The first binary pulsar, PSR B1913+16, was discovered in 1974 by Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor using the Arecibo Observatory. Its orbital period was observed to shrink over time at exactly the rate predicted by general relativity for energy loss through gravitational waves — the first indirect evidence for gravitational radiation. Hulse and Taylor received the 1993 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery.

The double pulsar

In 2003 astronomers discovered PSR J0737−3039, the only known double pulsar, in which both neutron stars are observable as pulsars. The system provides the most stringent tests of general relativity to date, including measurements of Shapiro delay, periastron advance, and orbital decay. Other binary pulsars, in which the companion is a white dwarf, are used to test alternative theories of gravity and to measure neutron star masses, some approaching two solar masses.
