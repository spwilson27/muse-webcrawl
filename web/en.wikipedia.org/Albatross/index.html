Albatross

Albatrosses are large seabirds of the family Diomedeidae, ranging widely in the Southern Ocean and the North Pacific. They are among the largest flying birds: the wandering albatross has the greatest known wingspan of any living bird, up to about 3.5 metres. Albatrosses are masters of dynamic soaring, travelling enormous distances over the open ocean with minimal flapping, and come ashore only to breed on remote islands.

Biology and behaviour

Albatrosses feed mainly on squid, fish and krill, taken at the surface or in shallow dives. They have a highly developed sense of smell, unusual among birds. Most species are monogamous, forming long-term pair bonds renewed through elaborate courtship dances. Breeding is slow: a single egg is laid, and some species breed only every two years. Albatrosses can live for over 60 years; the Laysan albatross "Wisdom" is the oldest known wild bird.

Species and conservation

The family comprises 22 generally recognised species in four genera. Many are threatened by longline fishing bycatch, plastic ingestion and introduced predators at breeding colonies; at least 15 species are considered at risk of extinction. International protection is coordinated under the Agreement on the Conservation of Albatrosses and Petrels (ACAP).

Cultural significance

The albatross became a famous literary symbol through Samuel Taylor Coleridge's The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (1798), in which killing the bird brings a curse, giving rise to the metaphor of an "albatross around one's neck".
