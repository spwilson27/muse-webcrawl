Battlegroup (army)

A battlegroup (British/Commonwealth term) or task force (United States term) in modern military theory is the basic building block of an army's fighting force. A battlegroup is formed around a combat unit — typically an infantry battalion or an armoured regiment — and is reinforced with sub-units drawn from other arms and services, such as artillery, engineers, air defence and signals, to create a self-contained combined-arms formation tailored to its mission.

A battlegroup combines armour, infantry, artillery and engineers under one commander.

Concept

The battlegroup is usually commanded by the commanding officer of its core unit, a lieutenant colonel. Its composition is flexible: an infantry battalion may receive a squadron of tanks, while an armoured regiment may gain infantry companies, depending on the task. This allows commanders to balance their forces at short notice in accordance with the principle of combined arms — the idea that arms used together achieve more than the sum of their parts.

Origins

The practice grew out of the Second World War, notably the German Kampfgruppe — an ad hoc combined-arms formation named after its commander — and various British and American improvised groupings. In the post-war British and Commonwealth armies, "battlegroup" became the standard term for the battalion-level combined arms grouping, and the concept underpins NATO land doctrine.

Modern usage

Battlegroups form the basis of deployments such as the European Union Battlegroups, on-call multinational formations of about 1,500 troops, and NATO's forward battlegroups in Eastern Europe. Larger formations — brigades and divisions — are themselves built by grouping battlegroups.
