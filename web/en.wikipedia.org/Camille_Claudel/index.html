Camille Rosalie Claudel (French pronunciation: [kamij klodɛl]; 8 December 1864 – 19 October 1943) was a French sculptor known for her figurative works in bronze and marble. She died in relative obscurity, but later gained recognition for the originality and quality of her work. The subject of several biographies and films, Claudel is well known for her sculptures including The Waltz and The Age of Maturity.

She was the elder sister of the poet and diplomat Paul Claudel and the protégée and lover of sculptor Auguste Rodin.

Early life

Camille Claudel was born in Fère-en-Tardenois, in the Aisne department in northern France, the first child of Louis Prosper Claudel and Louise Athanaïse Cécile Claudel (née Cerveaux). The family moved to Villeneuve-sur-Fère while she was still a baby. Her younger brother Paul Claudel was born in 1868. During her childhood, the family moved several times — to Bar-le-Duc (1870), Nogent-sur-Seine (1876) and Wassy-sur-Blaise (1879) — though they retained the house in Villeneuve and spent their summers there.

As a child Claudel was already passionate about modelling, working in clay and creating portraits of family members. Although her mother disapproved of her artistic ambitions, her father was supportive. In 1881 the family moved to Paris, where Louis Prosper arranged for his daughter to study sculpture. Because women were not then admitted to the École des Beaux-Arts, Claudel enrolled at the Académie Colarossi and studied under the sculptor Alfred Boucher, who recognized her talent.

Rodin and the Paris years

In 1883, when Boucher left for Italy, he asked his friend Auguste Rodin to take over the supervision of Claudel and her fellow students. Rodin was deeply impressed by her work, particularly the bust La Vieille Hélène, and by 1884 Claudel was working in his studio, assisting with major commissions such as The Burghers of Calais and The Gates of Hell. The two became lovers as well as artistic collaborators, a relationship that lasted roughly a decade.

Claudel's own work of this period shows both Rodin's influence and her own distinct sensibility. Her Sakuntala (1888), a tall figure of a kneeling woman inspired by the Sanskrit drama of Kalidasa, was acclaimed at the Salon. Her marble group The Waltz (La Valse, 1893), depicting two dancers swept into a whirling movement, was initially censured for its nudity; Claudel revised the work with swirling drapery and it became one of her best-known pieces. Clotho (1893) portrays one of the Fates as an emaciated old woman entangled in her own threads.

The Age of Maturity and later work

Claudel separated from Rodin in the early 1890s amid his refusal to leave his long-term partner Rose Beuret, and by about 1898 the relationship had ended. Her large bronze group The Age of Maturity (L'Âge mûr, c. 1899) is often read autobiographically: a kneeling young woman reaches imploringly toward a middle-aged man who is led away by an old woman. A version cast by Eugène Blot was exhibited in 1905.

Working in near isolation in her studio on the boulevard d'Italie, Claudel produced the small onyx-marble group The Wave (1897–1903), inspired by Hokusai, and the intimate genre sculptures known as Les Causeuses (The Gossips). Despite critical respect, commissions and sales were scarce, and financial support from Rodin and her family dwindled. She grew increasingly reclusive and convinced that Rodin was plotting against her and plagiarising her ideas.

Confinement and death

After the death of her father in March 1913 — her principal protector — Claudel was committed by her brother Paul and mother to the asylum at Ville-Évrard, and in 1914 transferred to the asylum of Montdevergues near Avignon, where she remained for the rest of her life. She repeatedly wrote letters protesting her confinement and asking to return home, but she was rarely visited and never released. She died on 19 October 1943 and was buried in an unmarked communal grave at the asylum cemetery.

Legacy

Claudel's reputation was revived in the 1980s through exhibitions of her work and the biography by her great-niece Reine-Marie Paris. The 1988 film Camille Claudel, starring Isabelle Adjani, brought her story to a wide audience, and Bruno Nuytten's earlier and Juliette Binoche's later portrayal in Camille Claudel 1915 (2013) renewed interest. In 2017 the Musée Camille Claudel opened in Nogent-sur-Seine, housing the largest collection of her works. In 2023 France marked the 80th anniversary of her death with commemorative exhibitions.
