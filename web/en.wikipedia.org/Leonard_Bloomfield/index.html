Leonard Bloomfield

Leonard Bloomfield (April 1, 1887 – April 18, 1949) was an American linguist who led the development of structural linguistics in the United States during the 1930s and 1940s. He is considered to be the father of American distributionalism.

Born: April 1, 1887, Chicago, Illinois
Died: April 18, 1949, New Haven, Connecticut
Field: Linguistics
Known for: Structural linguistics

Career

Bloomfield graduated from Harvard in 1906 and studied under German philologists at Leipzig and Göttingen. He taught at the University of Illinois, Ohio State University, the University of Chicago, and finally Yale, where he was Sterling Professor from 1940 until his death. He was a founding member of the Linguistic Society of America in 1924.

Work

His textbook Language (1933) became the standard introduction to linguistics in America, advocating a rigorous behaviorist and mechanistic approach: language study should rest on observable speech, with meaning deferred until it could be defined in physical terms. Bloomfield also did outstanding descriptive work on Algonquian languages, including Menominee, and on Tagalog, and contributed to the comparative reconstruction of Proto-Algonquian.

Legacy

The "Bloomfieldian" school dominated American linguistics until the rise of generative grammar under Noam Chomsky, one of whose teachers, Zellig Harris, had carried Bloomfield's distributional methods forward.
