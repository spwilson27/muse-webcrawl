Xeriscaping

Origin of termDenver Water, 1981
EtymologyGreek xeros (dry) + landscaping
GoalReduce or eliminate supplemental irrigation

Xeriscaping is the process of landscaping, or gardening, that reduces or eliminates the need for supplemental water from irrigation. The term was coined by the Denver Water department in 1981, combining the Greek xeros ("dry") with "landscaping". Xeriscapes are promoted in arid and drought-prone regions — and increasingly anywhere water conservation matters — as an alternative to thirsty lawns and high-water plantings.

Principles

Denver Water's classic formulation lists seven principles: planning and design; soil analysis and improvement; efficient irrigation; zoning plants into hydrozones of similar water needs; use of mulch; limited turf areas; and appropriate maintenance. Contrary to the "zeroscaping" stereotype — gravel and a few cacti — a xeriscape can be lush, using regionally appropriate plants, including native grasses, perennials, and shade trees.

Advantages and criticism

Advocates cite large water savings (often 50–75 percent), lower maintenance, and habitat benefits from native plants. Critics note that badly executed conversions can increase heat-island effects when vegetation is replaced by rock, and some homeowners associations and municipalities have clashed over lawn-replacement rules, prompting laws that protect water-wise landscaping.
