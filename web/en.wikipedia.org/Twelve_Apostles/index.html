In Christian theology and ecclesiology, the apostles, particularly the Twelve Apostles (also known as the Twelve Disciples or simply the Twelve), were the primary disciples of Jesus, the central figure in Christianity. During the life and ministry of Jesus in the 1st century AD, the apostles were his closest followers and became the primary teachers of the gospel message of Jesus.

The Twelve

The lists of the Twelve vary slightly among the Gospels. The Synoptic lists agree broadly: Simon Peter, James son of Zebedee ("the Greater"), John son of Zebedee, Andrew, Philip, Bartholomew (Nathanael), Matthew (Levi), Thomas ("Doubting Thomas"), James son of Alphaeus ("the Less"), Thaddeus (Jude son of James), Simon the Zealot, and Judas Iscariot. After Judas's betrayal and death, Matthias was chosen by lot to restore the Twelve (Acts 1).

Role in the early church

According to the New Testament, the Twelve were commissioned by the risen Jesus to "make disciples of all nations" (the Great Commission). Peter became the leading figure in Jerusalem, while the others traditionally spread the gospel across the known world — Andrew to Greece and the Black Sea, Thomas to India (the Malankara tradition), Matthew to Ethiopia, and others across Asia Minor, Persia, and Rome. Their writings and attributed writings became much of the New Testament.

Legacy

The number twelve echoed the twelve tribes of Israel, symbolizing the renewal of God's people. Bishops in many traditions trace their authority to the Twelve through apostolic succession, and their names appear in countless churches, artworks, and place names.
