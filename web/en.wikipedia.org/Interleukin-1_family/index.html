The interleukin-1 family is a group of 11 cytokines that play a central role in the regulation of immune and inflammatory responses. The family includes the classical agonists interleukin-1 alpha (IL-1α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), their natural antagonist the interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra), and the later-discovered members IL-18, IL-33, IL-36α, IL-36β, IL-36γ, IL-36Ra, IL-37 and IL-38. Most of the genes cluster on human chromosome 2.

History

IL-1 was first described in the 1940s as "endogenous pyrogen", a factor from activated white blood cells that caused fever. It was cloned in the 1980s and found to be a potent mediator of inflammation. The discovery of IL-1Ra, a naturally occurring receptor antagonist, led to the drug anakinra, used in rheumatoid arthritis and autoinflammatory diseases.

Biology

IL-1β is produced as an inactive precursor by macrophages and other cells and is activated by caspase-1 within inflammasome complexes, then secreted. IL-1α acts largely as an "alarmin" released from damaged cells. Signalling occurs through the IL-1 receptor (IL-1R1) and the adaptor MyD88, activating NF-κB and MAP kinase pathways. Family members such as IL-18 (interferon-gamma inducing factor) and IL-33 (an alarmin involved in type-2 immunity) share the family's β-trefoil fold and receptor architecture.

Clinical significance

Excess IL-1 activity drives autoinflammatory syndromes such as cryopyrin-associated periodic syndromes and contributes to gout, type 2 diabetes and atherosclerosis. Blocking IL-1 with anakinra, canakinumab or rilonacept is therapeutically effective in several of these conditions; the CANTOS trial (2017) showed that canakinumab reduced cardiovascular events, supporting the inflammatory hypothesis of atherosclerosis.
