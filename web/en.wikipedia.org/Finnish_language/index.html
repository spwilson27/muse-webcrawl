Finnish (suomi) is a Uralic language of the Finnic branch, spoken by the majority of the population of Finland and by ethnic Finns outside of Finland. It is one of the two official languages of Finland, alongside Swedish, and an official minority language in Sweden. Finnish is a member of the Finnic group of the Uralic family of languages, which also includes Estonian and a small number of minority languages spoken throughout the Baltic Sea region and Russia.

Grammar

Finnish is an agglutinative language with a rich system of inflection. Nouns and adjectives are declined in about fifteen cases, including the nominative, genitive, partitive, and a series of locative cases. Finnish has no grammatical gender and no articles. Vowel harmony governs the choice of vowel variants in suffixes, and consonant gradation produces systematic alternations in word stems. Word stress always falls on the first syllable, and vowel and consonant length are phonemic — a feature captured in the largely phonemic orthography.

History

Like the other Finnic languages, Finnish descends from Proto-Finnic, spoken around the Gulf of Finland perhaps two thousand years ago. Written Finnish was created by the bishop Mikael Agricola, whose translation of the New Testament (1548) founded the literary language. The publication of the national epic Kalevala by Elias Lönnrot in 1835 and 1849 greatly raised the status of Finnish, which gained full equality with Swedish in the late nineteenth century.

Vocabulary

The core vocabulary is Uralic in origin, supplemented by layers of loanwords from Baltic, Germanic, Scandinavian and — in modern times — English. Famous long compound words, such as lentokonesuihkuturbiinimoottoriapumekaanikkoaliupseerioppilas, illustrate the language's productive compounding.
