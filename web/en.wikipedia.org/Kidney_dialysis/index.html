Kidney dialysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Kidney dialysis (from Greek διάλυσις, dialysis, 'dissolution'; from διά, dia, 'through' and λύσις, lysis, 'loosening or splitting') is the process of removing excess water, solutes, and toxins from the blood in people whose kidneys can no longer perform these functions naturally. Along with kidney transplantation, it is a type of renal replacement therapy.

Dialysis may need to be initiated when there is a sudden rapid loss of kidney function (acute kidney injury) or when kidney function has gradually declined to kidney failure (stage 5 chronic kidney disease, reached when the glomerular filtration rate is below 15% of normal). The main types are haemodialysis, in which blood is filtered by an external machine, and peritoneal dialysis, in which the patient's own peritoneum serves as the filter membrane.

Principles

Dialysis works on the principles of the diffusion of solutes and ultrafiltration of fluid across a semi-permeable membrane. In haemodialysis, blood flows on one side of the membrane and a specially formulated dialysis fluid (dialysate) flows in the opposite direction, so waste products such as urea and potassium diffuse down their concentration gradients into the dialysate while desirable substances (such as bicarbonate) diffuse into the blood. Excess water is removed by applying a pressure gradient (ultrafiltration). Dialysis corrects the fluid, electrolyte, and acid–base disturbances of kidney failure, but it does not replace the kidneys' endocrine functions (such as erythropoietin production and vitamin D activation), which are managed with medication.

Haemodialysis

In haemodialysis, the patient's blood is pumped through an extracorporeal circuit containing a dialyser ("artificial kidney") of hollow synthetic fibres. Treatment typically lasts about four hours and is performed three times per week, usually in a dialysis centre, though home haemodialysis (including longer nocturnal schedules) is used by a minority. Reliable vascular access is essential: an arteriovenous fistula surgically created in the arm is preferred; grafts and central venous catheters are alternatives with higher infection and clotting risks. Anticoagulation (usually heparin) prevents clotting in the circuit. Treatment adequacy is commonly measured by the Kt/V index or urea reduction ratio.

Peritoneal dialysis

Peritoneal dialysis uses the patient's peritoneal membrane as the filter. Dialysate is instilled into the peritoneal cavity through a permanent catheter and exchanged several times a day (continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis, CAPD) or overnight by a cycler machine (automated peritoneal dialysis, APD). Wastes diffuse from peritoneal capillaries into the fluid, which is drained and replaced. Ultrafiltration is driven by the osmotic effect of glucose (dextrose) or icodextrin in the dialysate. Peritoneal dialysis is performed at home and offers more continuous, gentler fluid removal, but carries risks of peritonitis and gradual membrane failure.

Indications and timing

Urgent dialysis is indicated for life-threatening complications refractory to medical management, summarized by the mnemonic AEIOU: severe metabolic Acidosis, Electrolyte abnormalities (especially hyperkalaemia), dialysable Intoxications (such as lithium, methanol, or salicylates), fluid Overload (pulmonary oedema), and Uraemic complications (pericarditis, encephalopathy, bleeding). For chronic kidney failure, initiation is guided by symptoms and declining function rather than a single laboratory threshold; trials have not shown benefit from starting very early based on filtration rate alone. In many health systems dialysis is provided under large public programmes — in the United States, Medicare has covered kidney failure treatment since 1972 regardless of age.

Complications

Complications include intradialytic hypotension and cramps, access infection and thrombosis, dialysis disequilibrium syndrome (cerebral oedema from rapid solute shifts, now uncommon), peritonitis in peritoneal dialysis, and long-term problems such as dialysis-related amyloidosis, cardiovascular disease (the leading cause of death in dialysis patients), mineral and bone disorder, and anaemia. Dialysis patients have substantially reduced life expectancy compared with the general population, though outcomes vary widely with age and comorbidity; kidney transplantation generally offers better survival and quality of life for eligible patients.

History

The Scottish chemist Thomas Graham described dialysis of colloids in 1861. Georg Haas performed early human haemodialysis in Germany in the 1920s. Willem Kolff built the first practical rotating-drum dialyser in the occupied Netherlands in 1943–45 and treated the first patient to recover from acute renal failure. Chronic dialysis became feasible after Belding Scribner developed the Teflon shunt in Seattle in 1960, and the arteriovenous fistula (Cimino–Brescia, 1966) provided durable access. Peritoneal dialysis was developed clinically from the 1960s onward, with Tenckhoff's catheter (1968) making long-term treatment practical.
