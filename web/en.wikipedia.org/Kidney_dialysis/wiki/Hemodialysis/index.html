Hemodialysis, also spelled haemodialysis or simply dialysis, is a process of purifying the blood of a person whose kidneys are not working normally. It is one of two main forms of kidney dialysis, the other being peritoneal dialysis. Hemodialysis removes excess water, solutes, and toxins from the blood across a semipermeable membrane in an external machine (dialyzer), substituting for the filtration function of a healthy kidney.

Principle

Blood is drawn from the patient, anticoagulated (usually with heparin), and pumped through the dialyzer, where it flows on one side of a semipermeable membrane while dialysate flows countercurrently on the other side. Small solutes such as urea, potassium, and creatinine diffuse down their concentration gradients into the dialysate, while excess fluid is removed by ultrafiltration driven by a transmembrane pressure gradient. The cleansed blood is then returned to the patient.

Vascular access

Effective hemodialysis requires high blood flow rates, obtained through one of three access types: an arteriovenous (AV) fistula (the preferred long-term access, created surgically by joining an artery to a vein), an AV graft using synthetic tubing, or a central venous catheter, generally reserved for temporary or urgent use because of higher infection risk.

Regimens

Conventional in-center hemodialysis is performed three times weekly for about four hours per session. Alternatives include home hemodialysis, short daily dialysis, and nocturnal dialysis, which provide more total dialysis time and gentler fluid removal. Adequacy is commonly measured by the single-pool Kt/V urea index and standardised weekly Kt/V.

Complications

Common complications include intradialytic hypotension, muscle cramps, infection or thrombosis of the access, and, long term, dialysis-related amyloidosis and cardiovascular disease. Historically, contaminated water systems led to aluminium toxicity; modern water purification standards have greatly reduced such hazards.

History

The first successful human hemodialysis was performed by Willem Kolff in the Netherlands in 1943–1945 using a rotating-drum artificial kidney. Chronic maintenance dialysis became practical after Belding Scribner developed the Teflon shunt in 1960, and the creation of the Brescia–Cimino fistula in 1966 provided durable access.
