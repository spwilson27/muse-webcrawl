Kidney transplantation or renal transplantation is the organ transplant of a kidney into a patient with end-stage kidney disease (ESRD). It is generally regarded as the treatment of choice for eligible patients, offering better survival and quality of life than long-term dialysis. Kidney transplantation is classified as deceased-donor or living-donor transplantation depending on the source of the donor organ.

History

The first successful kidney transplant was performed in 1954 at the Peter Bent Brigham Hospital in Boston by Joseph Murray and colleagues, between identical twins Ronald and Richard Herrick, avoiding rejection without immunosuppression. Murray later received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1990) for this work. The introduction of azathioprine, and later ciclosporin in the late 1970s and 1980s, made transplantation between unrelated people routinely successful.

Procedure

In most cases the patient's failed kidneys are left in place; the donor kidney is implanted in the iliac fossa of the lower abdomen, with its artery and vein anastomosed to the external iliac vessels and its ureter implanted into the bladder. Living-donor kidneys are often removed laparoscopically or robotically. A functioning graft usually produces urine immediately or within days (delayed graft function being more common with deceased-donor organs).

Compatibility and allocation

Candidates are evaluated for ABO blood group compatibility and screened for donor-specific antibodies via crossmatching; tissue typing of HLA antigens informs matching and risk. Deceased-donor organs are allocated by national systems (such as UNOS in the United States) based on waiting time, sensitisation, and match quality. Kidney paired donation allows incompatible living donor–recipient pairs to exchange donors.

Immunosuppression and complications

Recipients take lifelong immunosuppressive drugs — typically a calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus or ciclosporin), an antimetabolite (mycophenolate), and corticosteroids, often with antibody induction therapy — to prevent acute and chronic rejection. Side effects include infection, malignancy, diabetes, and drug nephrotoxicity. Graft survival averages roughly 10–15 years for deceased-donor kidneys and longer for living-donor kidneys.
