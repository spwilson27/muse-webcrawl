Chronic kidney disease is staged by glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and albuminuria.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a type of long-term kidney disease in which a gradual decline in kidney function occurs over a period of months to years. It is defined as abnormalities of kidney structure or function present for more than three months, with implications for health. Initially there are generally no symptoms; later symptoms may include leg swelling, feeling tired, vomiting, loss of appetite, and confusion.

Classification

CKD is classified into five stages (G1–G5) based on the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), from stage 1 (kidney damage with normal or high GFR, ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2) to stage 5 (kidney failure, GFR <15), and further characterised by the degree of albuminuria (A1–A3). Stage 5 CKD, also called end-stage kidney disease, generally requires treatment with dialysis or a kidney transplant for survival.

Causes

The most common causes are diabetes mellitus and high blood pressure, which together account for the majority of cases in developed countries. Other causes include glomerulonephritis, interstitial nephritis, polycystic kidney disease, prolonged obstruction of the urinary tract, and recurrent kidney infections. In some regions, CKD of unknown cause (CKDu), possibly related to heat stress and agrochemical exposure, affects agricultural workers.

Complications

Complications of CKD include anaemia, mineral and bone disorders, metabolic acidosis, hyperkalaemia, fluid overload, and a greatly increased risk of cardiovascular disease, which is the leading cause of death in people with CKD. As function declines, accumulation of uraemic toxins produces the syndrome of uraemia.

Management

Treatment focuses on slowing progression: control of blood pressure (often with ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers), blood sugar management in diabetes, dietary salt and protein moderation, and avoidance of nephrotoxic drugs. SGLT2 inhibitors have been shown to slow progression in many patients. When kidney failure develops, options are haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, kidney transplantation, or conservative care.

Epidemiology

CKD affects roughly 10% of the world's population — more than 800 million people — and caused over a million deaths annually in recent estimates, with a burden rising due to ageing populations and increasing rates of diabetes and hypertension.
