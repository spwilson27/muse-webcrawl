Superalloy

A superalloy, or high-performance alloy, is an alloy that exhibits excellent mechanical strength, resistance to thermal creep deformation, good surface stability, and resistance to corrosion and oxidation at high temperatures — typically at 0.7 or more of its absolute melting temperature. Superalloys are usually based on nickel, cobalt, or iron, with substantial amounts of chromium and other elements such as aluminum, titanium, tungsten, molybdenum, and rhenium.

Development and structure

Superalloys were developed from the 1940s onward, driven by the demands of jet engines. Nickel-based superalloys derive their strength from a two-phase microstructure: a face-centered cubic gamma (γ) matrix strengthened by coherent precipitates of gamma prime (γ′), an ordered Ni3(Al,Ti) phase. Grain-boundary strengtheners such as carbon, boron, and zirconium, and solid-solution strengtheners like tungsten and molybdenum, further improve creep life.

Advances in processing — vacuum melting, directional solidification, and single-crystal casting — eliminated grain boundaries in turbine blades, allowing them to operate at temperatures above the initial melting point of early alloys, aided by internal cooling passages and thermal barrier coatings.

Applications

Familiar families include Inconel, Hastelloy, Waspaloy, and René alloys. Superalloys are used in gas turbine hot sections, rocket engines, nuclear and chemical processing, and other environments combining extreme heat, stress, and corrosive media.
