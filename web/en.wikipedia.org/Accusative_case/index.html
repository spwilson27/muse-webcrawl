Accusative case

Accusative case
Grammatical functionMarks the direct object of a transitive verb
AbbreviationACC
Contrasts withNominative case, dative case, ergative case
ExamplesLatin puellam, German den Mann, Russian knigu

The accusative case (abbreviated ACC) of a noun is the grammatical case used to receive the direct object of a transitive verb. In the syntax of a language, the accusative is the case of the object on which the action is carried out. The same case is used in many languages for the objects of (some or all) prepositions.

The name derives from Latin casus accusativus, itself a translation of Greek αἰτιατικὴ πτῶσις, originally meaning the case of the effect produced — a mistranslation that has been retained in grammatical tradition.

In Indo-European languages

Proto-Indo-European had an accusative case, preserved in Latin, Greek, Sanskrit, German, Russian and many other languages. In Latin, masculine and feminine nouns of the first and second declensions end in -m in the singular accusative (e.g. puella → puellam, "girl"). In German, the accusative is visibly distinguished from the nominative only in masculine singular articles and pronouns (der → den).

Other functions

Beyond the direct object, the accusative commonly marks extent of time or space (Latin multos annos, "for many years"), direction of motion, and serves as the object of prepositions governing motion (German in die Stadt, "into the city"). In Greek, the cognate accusative specifies respect ("suffering in his eyes").

Alignment

Languages with nominative–accusative alignment mark the subject of both intransitive and transitive clauses with the nominative and the object with the accusative. This contrasts with ergative–absolutive languages, which group the intransitive subject with the transitive object. Some languages, such as Hindi and Basque in certain constructions, show split systems.
