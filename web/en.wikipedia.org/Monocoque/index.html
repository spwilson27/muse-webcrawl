Monocoque

Monocoque (/ˈmɒnəkɒk, -koʊk/), also called structural skin, is a structural system in which loads are supported by an object's external skin, in a manner similar to an egg shell. The term is also used to indicate a structure in which the skin and internal framework are a single unit.

The word monocoque is a French term for "single shell" or (of boats) "single hull". First used for boats, a true monocoque carries both tensile and compressive forces within the skin and can be recognised by the absence of a load-carrying internal frame.

Aircraft

Early aircraft were constructed using an internal framework of wood or steel tubing covered with fabric. Designers soon realised the advantages of a shell that carried the loads itself: the 1912 Deperdussin Monocoque racer used a moulded plywood shell. By the 1930s, stressed-skin aluminium semi-monocoque construction — in which the skin shares loads with internal frames and stringers — had become standard for airliners and military aircraft, and remains so in nearly all modern aeroplanes.

A semi-monocoque fuselage: the skin, frames and stringers share structural loads.

Automobiles

In motor racing, the monocoque "tub" forms the survival cell around the driver. Carbon-fibre composite monocoques were introduced to Formula One by McLaren in 1981 with the MP4/1, dramatically improving driver safety and chassis stiffness while reducing weight. Most modern road cars use a unitary ("unibody") steel construction that applies the same principle, replacing the older body-on-frame design.

Bicycles

Monocoque carbon-fibre bicycle frames are moulded in one or a few pieces, allowing tube shapes to be optimised for aerodynamics and stiffness without the joints required by lugged or welded construction.

Rockets

Balloon tanks, as used in the early Atlas and Centaur rockets, are an extreme form of monocoque: the propellant tank walls are so thin they rely on internal pressure to maintain their shape and carry flight loads.
