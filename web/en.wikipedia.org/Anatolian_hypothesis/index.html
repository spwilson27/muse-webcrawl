Anatolian hypothesis

The Anatolian hypothesis, proposed by British archaeologist Colin Renfrew in his 1987 book Archaeology and Language, holds that the Proto-Indo-European language was spoken in Anatolia (modern Turkey) around 7000–6000 BC and spread into Europe and Asia principally through the peaceful, gradual expansion of early farming populations — the Neolithic "wave of advance" — rather than by later migrations of mounted pastoralists. It contrasts with the mainstream Kurgan (steppe) hypothesis, associated with Marija Gimbutas, which places the homeland in the Pontic-Caspian steppe around 4000–3500 BC. Note that the topic is unrelated to the medieval Anatolian beyliks.

Argument

Renfrew argued that the largest known demographic process in prehistoric Europe — the spread of agriculture from the Near East beginning around 7000 BC — was the most plausible vector for a language family of Indo-European scale. On this view, farming communities carrying early Indo-European speech advanced through the Balkans and along the Danube and Mediterranean coasts; Greek would reflect an early arrival in the Aegean, and the Anatolian languages (Hittite, Luwian) would represent the homeland's remnant rather than an intrusion. Later versions by Renfrew and others allowed secondary spreads (for example steppe movements for some branches) within a broadly farming-first framework.

Evidence and criticism

Support was claimed from glottochronological and later Bayesian phylogenetic studies (notably Gray & Atkinson 2003, and Bouckaert et al. 2012) that dated Indo-European divergence to roughly 8000–9500 years ago, consistent with an Anatolian origin. However, most historical linguists objected that such dates conflict with reconstructed vocabulary for wheeled vehicles, wool, and other late-Neolithic/Chalcolithic technologies, which imply Proto-Indo-European was still unified after c. 4000 BC. From 2015 onward, ancient DNA studies transformed the debate: they documented a massive migration of Pontic steppe pastoralists (Yamnaya-related ancestry) into Europe around 3000 BC and a separate steppe-related movement into South Asia, strongly supporting the steppe model for most branches. Genetic evidence does suggest the Anatolian branch split early and that farming populations from Anatolia spread into Europe — so elements of Renfrew's framework persist in hybrid models — but the heartland and chronology of late Proto-Indo-European are now generally placed on or near the steppe.
