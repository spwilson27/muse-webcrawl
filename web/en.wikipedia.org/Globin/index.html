Globin

The globin fold: eight alpha-helices (the "globin fold") surrounding a heme group.

The globins are a superfamily of heme-containing globular proteins, involved in binding and/or transporting oxygen. These proteins all incorporate the globin fold, a series of eight alpha-helical segments. Two prominent members of the family are myoglobin and hemoglobin. Both contain a heme prosthetic group whose iron atom binds one oxygen molecule reversibly.

Structure

Members of the globin superfamily share a common three-dimensional fold, even where their amino-acid sequences differ substantially. The classic globin fold consists of eight alpha helices, conventionally labelled A through H, arranged around a hydrophobic pocket that cradles the heme group. A conserved histidine residue coordinates the heme iron. The fold is among the most ancient and widely distributed protein architectures, occurring in all kingdoms of life.

Types

Several globin subfamilies are recognized in vertebrates: hemoglobin, the tetrameric oxygen carrier in red blood cells (composed in adults of two alpha and two beta chains); myoglobin, the monomeric oxygen store in muscle; neuroglobin, found in the brain and retina; and cytoglobin, expressed in many tissues. Plants have leghemoglobins in nitrogen-fixing root nodules, and many bacteria and archaea possess truncated globins and flavohemoglobins involved in oxygen sensing and nitric oxide detoxification.

Evolution

Globins are thought to have evolved from a common ancestral oxygen-binding protein over a billion years ago. Gene duplication and divergence produced the alpha- and beta-globin gene clusters in vertebrates, whose developmental regulation switches expression from embryonic to fetal to adult forms. Mutations in globin genes cause the hemoglobinopathies, including sickle cell disease and the thalassemias.
