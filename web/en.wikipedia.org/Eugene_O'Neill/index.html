Eugene O'Neill

Born: Eugene Gladstone O'Neill, 16 October 1888, New York City, US
Died: 27 November 1953 (aged 65), Boston, Massachusetts, US
Notable awards: Nobel Prize in Literature (1936); Pulitzer Prize for Drama (four times)

Eugene Gladstone O'Neill (October 16, 1888 – November 27, 1953) was an American playwright. His poetically titled plays were among the first to introduce into the U.S. the drama techniques of realism earlier associated with Russian playwright Anton Chekhov, Norwegian playwright Henrik Ibsen, and Swedish playwright August Strindberg. The tragedy Long Day's Journey into Night is often numbered on the short list of the finest U.S. plays in the 20th century, alongside Tennessee Williams's A Streetcar Named Desire and Arthur Miller's Death of a Salesman.

Career

The son of actor James O'Neill, Eugene spent time as a sailor, prospector, and newspaper reporter before turning to playwriting. With the Provincetown Players he developed his early one-act sea plays; Beyond the Horizon (1920) won the Pulitzer Prize, as did Anna Christie (1922), Strange Interlude (1928), and, posthumously, Long Day's Journey into Night (1957). Experimental works such as The Emperor Jones and The Hairy Ape explored expressionism and psychological breakdown.

Later years

O'Neill was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1936. A degenerative neurological condition ended his writing career in his last decade. His final works, including The Iceman Cometh, A Moon for the Misbegotten, and the autobiographical Long Day's Journey into Night, secured his reputation as the foremost American dramatist of his era.
