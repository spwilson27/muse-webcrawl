The Ayyubid dynasty was a Muslim dynasty of Kurdish origin founded by Saladin (Ṣalāḥ ad-Dīn Yūsuf ibn Ayyūb). Having abolished the Fatimid Caliphate in Egypt in 1171, Saladin united Egypt, Syria, Upper Mesopotamia, the Hejaz and much of Yemen under his rule, and founded the dynasty that bore his father's name, Ayyub. At its height, the Ayyubid confederation dominated the Muslim Near East and confronted the Crusader states of the Levant.

Saladin and the Crusades

Saladin, originally a commander in Zengid service, took power in Egypt in 1169 as vizier, ended two centuries of Fatimid rule, and after Nur al-Din's death in 1174 extended his authority over Damascus and Aleppo. In 1187 he destroyed the Crusader field army at the Battle of Hattin and retook Jerusalem, provoking the Third Crusade under Richard I of England. The war ended with the Treaty of Jaffa (1192), leaving the coast to the Crusaders but Jerusalem in Muslim hands.

The Ayyubid confederation

After Saladin's death in 1193 his empire was divided among his sons, brothers and nephews as a family confederation of principalities — Egypt, Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, Hama and others — whose rulers frequently quarrelled but also cooperated against common threats. His brother al-Adil and nephew al-Kamil ruled Egypt effectively; al-Kamil famously negotiated with Frederick II, ceding Jerusalem by treaty in 1229, and met St Francis of Assisi during the Fifth Crusade.

Decline and fall

In 1250, amid the Seventh Crusade of Louis IX, the last Ayyubid sultan of Egypt, Turanshah, was murdered by his own Mamluk guard, inaugurating the Mamluk Sultanate. Ayyubid princes lingered in Syria until the Mongol invasions of 1260, and a minor line survived at Hisn Kayfa into the sixteenth century. The Ayyubid era is remembered for military architecture — citadels at Cairo, Damascus and Aleppo — and a revival of Sunni learning through the madrasa system.
