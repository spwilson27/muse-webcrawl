Modern paganism


Image placeholder: a modern pagan ceremony at Avebury

Modern paganism, also known as contemporary paganism and neopaganism, is a collective term for new religious movements which are influenced by or claim to be derived from the pre-Christian beliefs of various European, North African, and Near Eastern peoples. Though they share similarities, contemporary pagan religious movements are diverse, with no single set of beliefs, practices, or texts common to all.

Overview

Scholars divide modern paganism broadly into eclectic movements, which syncretise material from many sources, and reconstructionist movements, which attempt to revive a specific historical tradition using academic research. Prominent traditions include Wicca, founded in England in the mid-20th century by Gerald Gardner and others; Heathenry, reviving Germanic and Norse religion; modern Druidry; Hellenic and Roman reconstructionism; and Slavic, Baltic, and Finnic native faiths. Common emphases include reverence for nature, seasonal festivals organised as a Wheel of the Year, polytheism or pantheism, and ritual magic in some traditions.

History

The movement's roots lie in Romanticism and 19th-century folklore scholarship, esoteric orders such as the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, and early 20th-century nature and woodcraft movements. Wicca's public emergence in 1950s Britain was followed by rapid growth in the United States from the 1960s, where modern paganism intertwined with feminism, environmentalism, and the counterculture. From the 1990s, the internet accelerated networking and the spread of solitary practice.

Demographics

Estimates vary widely and adherents often hold no formal affiliation; surveys have suggested several hundred thousand to over a million practitioners in the United States, with significant communities in the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and — for reconstructionist faiths — Eastern and Northern Europe. Modern paganism is a recognised religion for purposes such as military chaplaincy and census reporting in several countries.
