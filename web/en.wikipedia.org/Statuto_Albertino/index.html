Statuto Albertino

The Statuto Albertino (Albertine Statute) was the constitution granted by King Charles Albert of Sardinia on 4 March 1848. It became the founding charter of the Kingdom of Italy on unification in 1861 and remained formally in force, albeit suspended in practice under Fascism, until the republican Constitution of 1948 replaced it — making it one of the longest-lived constitutions in European history and the basis of Italy's first century as a modern state.

Provisions

Modelled on the French Charter of 1830 and the Belgian constitution of 1831, the Statute established a constitutional monarchy. The king retained executive power, command of the armed forces and the right to appoint senators and ministers; legislative power was shared between the Crown and a bicameral parliament — a Senate of royal appointees and an elected Chamber of Deputies. Eighty-four articles guaranteed equality before the law, personal liberty, freedom of the press and of assembly, and the inviolability of property, while declaring Roman Catholicism the religion of the state with other cults tolerated. Suffrage was initially restricted by tax and literacy to about 2 per cent of the population.

Evolution and end

Because the Statute was a flexible constitution, amendable by ordinary legislation, it evolved over decades toward parliamentary government, universal male suffrage (1912) and female suffrage (1946). Its flexibility also allowed Benito Mussolini to hollow out liberal institutions after 1922 without formally abolishing it. The institutional referendum of 2 June 1946 chose a republic, and the new Constitution entered into force on 1 January 1948.
