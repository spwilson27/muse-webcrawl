Second messenger

Second messengers relay and amplify signals inside the cell.

Second messengers are intracellular signalling molecules released by the cell in response to exposure to extracellular signalling molecules — the first messengers. Second messengers trigger physiological changes at the cellular level such as proliferation, differentiation, migration, survival, apoptosis and depolarisation.

Mechanism

When a hormone, neurotransmitter or growth factor (first messenger) binds a receptor on the cell surface, the activated receptor switches on intracellular enzymes or channels that produce second messengers. Because each activated enzyme can generate many messenger molecules, the signal is amplified in a cascade. Second messengers in turn activate protein kinases and other targets, and are rapidly degraded or sequestered to terminate the signal.

Common second messengers

  • Cyclic AMP (cAMP) — produced by adenylyl cyclase downstream of G protein-coupled receptors; activates protein kinase A. Its discovery by Earl Sutherland in the 1950s founded the field.
  • Cyclic GMP (cGMP) — produced by guanylyl cyclase; mediates, among other things, the action of nitric oxide on smooth muscle.
  • Inositol trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG) — split from membrane phospholipids by phospholipase C; IP3 releases Ca2+ from the endoplasmic reticulum while DAG activates protein kinase C.
  • Calcium ions (Ca2+) — released from internal stores or entering through channels, they activate calmodulin and many enzymes.
  • Nitric oxide (NO) — a gaseous messenger that diffuses into neighbouring cells.

Signalling pathways

Second messengers are central to major signal transduction systems including the GPCR pathways (Gs, Gi, Gq), receptor tyrosine kinase cascades that feed into them, and phototransduction in the retina, where light-driven cGMP hydrolysis closes ion channels.
