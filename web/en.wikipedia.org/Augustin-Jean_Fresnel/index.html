Augustin-Jean Fresnel (10 May 1788 – 14 July 1827) was a French civil engineer and physicist whose research in optics led to the almost unanimous acceptance of the wave theory of light, excluding any remnant of Newton's corpuscular theory, from the late 1830s until the end of the 19th century. He is perhaps better known for inventing the catadioptric (reflective/refractive) Fresnel lens and for pioneering the use of "stepped" lenses to extend the visibility of lighthouses, saving countless lives at sea.

Wave theory of light

Working largely in isolation while employed as an engineer on road-building projects, Fresnel revived and extended the wave theory of light of Christiaan Huygens and Thomas Young. His memoir on diffraction, submitted to the Académie des Sciences in 1818, brilliantly explained diffraction in terms of the interference of secondary wavelets. Siméon Denis Poisson, a sceptic, pointed out that Fresnel's theory predicted a bright spot at the centre of a circular obstacle's shadow; when François Arago confirmed the prediction experimentally — the so-called Poisson spot — the theory was triumphantly vindicated. Fresnel went on to explain polarisation by proposing that light waves are transverse, and derived the Fresnel equations governing reflection and refraction at interfaces.

The Fresnel lens

Appointed to the French lighthouse commission, Fresnel designed a lens of concentric annular prisms that concentrated a lamp's light into a powerful beam while remaining far lighter than an equivalent solid lens or mirror. The first Fresnel lens was installed at the Cordouan lighthouse in 1823, and the design spread to lighthouses worldwide.

Legacy

Fresnel died of tuberculosis aged only 39. His name is one of the 72 names inscribed on the Eiffel Tower, and numerous optical concepts — Fresnel diffraction, the Fresnel integrals, Fresnel zones, and Fresnel drag — bear his name.
