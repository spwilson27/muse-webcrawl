Literary canon

The literary canon is the body of books, narratives, and other texts considered to be the most important and influential of a particular time period or culture — the works most often preserved, studied, and taught. The term adapts the ecclesiastical "canon" of scripture to secular literature. In modern debate "the Western canon" denotes the tradition of influential books from Homer and the Bible through Shakespeare, Dante, Cervantes, and later authors.

Formation

Canons form through the accumulated judgement of critics, scholars, teachers, publishers, and readers, and are institutionalized in school and university curricula, anthologies (such as the Norton Anthology), library lists, and series like the Great Books of the Western World (1952) associated with Mortimer Adler and the University of Chicago. Canon formation mixes aesthetic judgement with social and institutional forces: availability of texts, national identity, and educational needs.

The canon wars

In the 1980s and 1990s, especially in American universities, the canon became the centre of the "canon wars" or culture wars. Critics argued the traditional canon over-represented dead white European males and excluded women, minority, and non-Western writers; defenders such as Allan Bloom (The Closing of the American Mind, 1987) argued for the enduring value of great books, while Harold Bloom's The Western Canon (1994) defended an aesthetic canon centred on Shakespeare against what he called the "School of Resentment". Curricula consequently broadened, and scholarship increasingly studies canon formation itself.

National and other canons

Most literary traditions maintain canons — the Chinese Four Great Classical Novels, the Japanese classics from The Tale of Genji onward, the Persian Shahnameh, and Sanskrit epics — and sub-canons flourish for genres such as science fiction and children's literature. Digital-era lists and prizes (Booker, Nobel) continue to reshape what counts as canonical.
