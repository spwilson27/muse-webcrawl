Williams syndrome (WS), also Williams–Beuren syndrome, is a genetic disorder caused by the spontaneous deletion of genetic material from a specific region of chromosome 7. It is characterised by mild to moderate intellectual disability, distinctive facial features, cardiovascular disease and a strikingly friendly, outgoing personality.

Signs and symptoms

People with Williams syndrome often have an "elfin" facial appearance with a broad forehead, short nose and wide mouth. The most significant medical problem is cardiovascular disease, notably supravalvular aortic stenosis, caused by deletion of the ELN gene, which encodes the elastic protein elastin. Infants may have high blood calcium (hypercalcemia) and feeding difficulties. Cognitively, the syndrome typically combines overall intellectual disability with relative strengths in language and music and severe difficulty with visuospatial tasks. Individuals are often described as exceptionally sociable and empathetic, with little social fear, alongside high rates of anxiety and attention deficit.

Cause and diagnosis

The deletion at 7q11.23 removes roughly 26 to 28 genes and usually arises as a new mutation, though an affected parent has a 50% chance of passing it on. Diagnosis is confirmed by genetic testing, commonly fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) or chromosomal microarray. The syndrome was first described by J. C. P. Williams in 1961 and independently by Alois Beuren in 1962.

Management

There is no cure; management involves monitoring of the heart and blood vessels, treatment of hypercalcemia and hypertension, and educational and therapeutic support tailored to the individual's profile.
