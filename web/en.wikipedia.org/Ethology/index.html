Ethology is the scientific study of animal behaviour, usually with a focus on behaviour under natural conditions and viewing behaviour as an evolutionarily adaptive trait. Ethologists study behaviour in the field and laboratory, asking both how a behaviour works and why it evolved.

History

Modern ethology emerged in the 1930s with the work of Konrad Lorenz, Nikolaas Tinbergen, and Karl von Frisch. Lorenz described imprinting in greylag geese; von Frisch decoded the waggle dance of honeybees; Tinbergen's experiments on gulls and sticklebacks established rigorous field methods. The three shared the 1973 Nobel Prize. Ethology contrasted with behaviourism, which emphasised laboratory learning in a few model species.

Key concepts

A fixed action pattern is an innate, stereotyped sequence triggered by a sign stimulus. Tinbergen's four questions frame any behaviour by its mechanism, development (ontogeny), function (adaptive value), and evolutionary history (phylogeny). Later developments include behavioural ecology, sociobiology, and cognitive ethology, which apply optimal foraging theory, game theory, and studies of animal cognition.

Applications

Ethology informs animal welfare assessment, conservation (e.g., reintroduction training), pest management, and the design of enrichment for captive animals.
