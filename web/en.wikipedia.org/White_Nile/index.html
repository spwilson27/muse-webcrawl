The White Nile (Arabic: النيل الأبيض, an-Nīl al-ʾAbyaḍ) is one of the two main tributaries of the Nile, the other being the Blue Nile. It flows north from Lake Victoria through Uganda and South Sudan to Khartoum, where it meets the Blue Nile. Its name comes from the pale, clay-coloured sediment that lightens its waters. Although longer than the Blue Nile, it contributes a steadier but smaller share of the Nile's total flow.

Course

Leaving Lake Victoria at Jinja as the Victoria Nile, the river passes through Lake Kyoga and over Murchison Falls to Lake Albert. From there, as the Albert Nile and then the Bahr al Jabal, it enters South Sudan and spreads into the Sudd, one of the world's largest wetlands, where roughly half its water is lost to evaporation. Joined by the Bahr el Ghazal and the Sobat, it continues north as the White Nile proper through Kosti to Khartoum.

Exploration

The source of the White Nile was one of the great geographical puzzles of the 19th century. John Hanning Speke reached Lake Victoria in 1858 and identified it as the Nile's source, a claim disputed by Richard Burton; expeditions by Samuel Baker and Henry Morton Stanley later confirmed the lake's role.

Importance

The river is vital for irrigation, fisheries, transport and hydroelectricity in the countries it crosses, and its waters are governed by contested colonial-era and modern Nile basin agreements among the riparian states.
