Compound interest

Compound interest is the addition of interest to the principal sum of a loan or deposit—in other words, interest on interest. It is the result of reinvesting interest so that interest in the next period is earned on the principal plus previously accumulated interest. Compound interest contrasts with simple interest, where interest is calculated only on the original principal.

Calculation

The total accumulated value, including principal P, after t years at an annual rate r compounded n times per year is A = P(1 + r/n)nt. As the compounding frequency increases, growth approaches continuous compounding, A = Pert, involving Euler's number e. The "rule of 72" approximates the doubling time of an investment as 72 divided by the percentage rate.

History

Compound interest was known to ancient Babylonian mathematicians and appears in Old Babylonian problem tablets. It was condemned as usury by Roman law and by medieval Christian and Islamic teaching, which restricted interest-taking. Richard Witt's Arithmetical Questions (1613) was an early book devoted to the subject, and Jacob Bernoulli's work on continuous compounding led to the constant e.
