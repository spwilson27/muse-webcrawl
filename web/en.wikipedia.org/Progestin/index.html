Progestin

A progestin is a synthetic progestogen — a medication that produces effects similar to those of the natural hormone progesterone. Progestins are widely used in hormonal contraception, hormone replacement therapy, and the treatment of various gynecological disorders.

Uses

The most common use of progestins is in hormonal birth control, either alone (the "mini-pill", implants, injections such as depot medroxyprogesterone acetate, and hormonal intrauterine devices) or combined with an estrogen in combined oral contraceptives. In menopausal hormone therapy, progestins are added to estrogen to protect the endometrium from unopposed estrogen stimulation, which raises the risk of endometrial cancer. Progestins are also used to treat endometriosis, abnormal uterine bleeding, amenorrhea, and, at high doses, certain hormone-sensitive cancers.

Classification

Progestins are classically grouped by generation and by chemical structure. First-generation compounds include norethindrone (norethisterone) and its relatives, derived from 19-nortestosterone. Later generations include levonorgestrel and gestodene (gonanes), and the spironolactone-derived drospirenone, which has antimineralocorticoid and antiandrogenic activity. Other progestins, such as medroxyprogesterone acetate, are pregnane derivatives of progesterone itself.

Pharmacology

Progestins act as agonists of the progesterone receptor. Depending on their structure they may also bind androgen, glucocorticoid, and mineralocorticoid receptors, producing varying side-effect profiles, including androgenic effects such as acne or, conversely, antiandrogenic effects. In contraception they work chiefly by suppressing the luteinizing hormone surge, thickening cervical mucus, and altering the endometrium.

History

The first orally active progestin, ethisterone, was synthesized in 1938; norethindrone followed in 1951 and became the progestin component of the first combined oral contraceptive, Enovid, approved in 1960.
