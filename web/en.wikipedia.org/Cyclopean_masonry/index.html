Cyclopean masonry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cyclopean masonry is a type of stonework found in Mycenaean architecture, built with massive limestone boulders, roughly fitted together with minimal clearing between adjacent stones and no use of mortar. The term derives from the belief of classical Greeks that only the Cyclopes, mythical giants, could have moved stones so large.

Characteristics

The fortification walls of Mycenae, Tiryns, Argos and Athens built in this technique during the Late Bronze Age (c. 1400–1200 BC) use boulders a metre or more across, packed with smaller stones in the interstices. Later, more regular polygonal masonry and ashlar work developed out of the same tradition. Roman-era writers such as Pausanias marvelled at the walls of Tiryns, claiming a pair of mules could not dislodge the smallest stone.

Other traditions

Similar megalithic masonry appears independently in many cultures — in the Inca walls of Sacsayhuamán in Peru, Nuragic Sardinia, Malta's temples and Japanese castle foundations — though the term "Cyclopean" is properly tied to the Bronze Age Aegean. In modern usage it is also applied to very large mass-concrete structures ("cyclopean concrete").
