Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) is a class of anticoagulant medications. They are used in the prevention of blood clots and treatment of venous thromboembolism (deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism) and in the treatment of myocardial infarction.

Medical uses

LMWHs are used for prophylaxis against thrombosis in hospitalised and surgical patients, for the treatment of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, for unstable angina and non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction, and for bridging anticoagulation in selected patients. Because of their predictable pharmacokinetics, they can be given by fixed weight-adjusted subcutaneous dose without routine laboratory monitoring in most patients.

Mechanism of action

Heparin is a naturally occurring polysaccharide that inhibits coagulation. LMWHs are obtained by fractionation or depolymerisation of polymeric (unfractionated) heparin, yielding shorter chains with an average molecular weight below about 8,000 Da. Like unfractionated heparin, LMWH binds to and potentiates antithrombin, but the shorter chains inhibit factor Xa more than thrombin (factor IIa), giving a higher anti-Xa to anti-IIa activity ratio.

Side effects

The main adverse effect is bleeding. Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) occurs less frequently with LMWH than with unfractionated heparin. Long-term use may contribute to reduced bone density. The anticoagulant effect of LMWH is only partially reversed by protamine sulfate.
