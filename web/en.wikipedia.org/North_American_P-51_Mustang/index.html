The North American Aviation P-51 Mustang is an American long-range, single-seat fighter and fighter-bomber used during World War II and the Korean War, among other conflicts. It was designed in 1940 by a team led by lead designer Edgar Schmued at North American Aviation (NAA) to meet a requirement of the British Purchasing Commission. The prototype NA-73X airframe was rolled out just 102 days after the order was placed and first flew on 26 October 1940.

Design and development

The Mustang was designed around the Allison V-1710 engine, whose performance at high altitude was limited. When fitted with the Rolls-Royce Merlin engine — built under licence in the United States by Packard — the aircraft was transformed: the P-51B/C and the definitive bubble-canopy P-51D combined speed, manoeuvrability and, with drop tanks, the range to escort heavy bombers all the way to Berlin and back.

Operational history

Flying with the USAAF's Eighth and Fifteenth Air Forces and the RAF, Mustangs claimed roughly 4,950 enemy aircraft in the European theatre and played a decisive role in winning air superiority over the Luftwaffe in 1944, enabling sustained daylight bombing. In the Pacific, P-51s flew very-long-range escort missions from Iwo Jima. The type served again in Korea as a ground-attack aircraft (redesignated F-51) and remained in military service with some air forces into the 1980s; many survive as warbirds and air racers.
