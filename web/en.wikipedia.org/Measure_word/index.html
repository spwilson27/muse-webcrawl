Measure word

Also calledClassifier, counter word
Typical inChinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Thai, Malay, Bengali, many indigenous American and Australian languages
FunctionUnit used with numerals or demonstratives to count or refer to nouns

A measure word, or classifier, is a word or morpheme used in combination with a numeral or demonstrative to indicate the quantity of a noun. Measure words are obligatory in many languages — including the Chinese languages, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese and Thai — where a numeral cannot directly modify a noun: Chinese speakers say sān běn shū ("three [volume] book"), using the measure word běn appropriate to bound volumes.

Types

Linguists distinguish sortal classifiers, which categorize the noun itself by shape, animacy, function or social status (for example, a classifier for long thin objects, for animals, or for honoured persons), from mensural classifiers or true measure words, which denote units or quantities — containers, handfuls, groups — comparable to English "three head of cattle," "two sheets of paper" or "a cup of tea." English preserves traces of the system in such partitive constructions, but does not require classifiers for ordinary counting.

Distribution

Classifier systems are characteristic of East and Southeast Asian languages and are also widespread in languages of the Americas, Australia and parts of Africa. Japanese has hundreds of counters of Chinese and native origin, such as -hon for long cylindrical objects and -hiki for small animals. Grammars usually list measure words as a distinct part of speech in Chinese linguistics, where they number in the dozens of common items, including the all-purpose gè.
