Galaxy merger

A galaxy merger occurs when two or more galaxies collide and gravitationally combine into a single, larger galaxy. Although the stars within merging galaxies rarely collide directly — the space between them being vast — the gravitational interactions distort the galaxies' shapes, drive gas inward, and can trigger intense bursts of star formation. Mergers are a fundamental mechanism of galaxy evolution and a key element of hierarchical structure formation in the universe.

Process

During an encounter, tidal forces draw out long tails and bridges of stars and gas. Friction between gas clouds and dynamical friction between dark matter halos cause the galaxies to lose orbital energy and spiral together over hundreds of millions to billions of years. As gas funnels to the center, it may ignite a starburst and feed the central supermassive black holes, producing an active galactic nucleus or quasar phase. The merged remnant often relaxes into an elliptical galaxy.

Classification

Mergers are described as major when the galaxies are of comparable mass (mass ratios up to about 3:1) and minor when a small galaxy is absorbed by a much larger one. Gas-rich "wet" mergers produce starbursts, while gas-poor "dry" mergers do not. Famous examples include the Antennae Galaxies (NGC 4038/4039) and the Mice Galaxies.

The Milky Way's future

The Milky Way is currently absorbing dwarf galaxies such as the Sagittarius Dwarf Spheroidal, and in roughly 4–5 billion years it is expected to merge with the Andromeda Galaxy, forming a large elliptical remnant sometimes nicknamed "Milkomeda".
