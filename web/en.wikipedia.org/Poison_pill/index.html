A poison pill deters hostile takeovers by threatening massive dilution of the acquirer.

A poison pill, formally a shareholder rights plan, is a defensive tactic used by a company's board of directors against a hostile takeover. The plan issues rights to existing shareholders that activate ("flip in") if an unwanted acquirer buys a threshold stake — commonly 10–20 percent — allowing all other shareholders to buy new shares at a deep discount. This massively dilutes the acquirer's holding and makes the takeover prohibitively expensive unless the board redeems the pill.

History and legality

The poison pill was devised in 1982 by mergers-and-acquisitions lawyer Martin Lipton during the era of the corporate raiders. Its validity was upheld by the Delaware Supreme Court in Moran v. Household International (1985), and Delaware courts have since permitted boards to "just say no" to hostile bids while a pill is in place, subject to duties articulated in cases such as Unocal and Airgas (2011), where a pill helped defeat a year-long hostile campaign. Pills typically last up to ten years but are now often adopted for shorter, purpose-specific periods with shareholder pressure for limits.

Variations and modern use

"Flip-over" pills grant target shareholders discounted shares in the acquiring company after a merger. Poison pills are also used for non-takeover purposes: companies adopted them to protect tax assets (net operating loss pills, with low ~5 percent triggers), and in 2022 Twitter's board adopted a one-year pill in response to Elon Musk's unsolicited bid before ultimately agreeing to his acquisition. Critics argue pills entrench management; defenders respond that they force bidders to negotiate with the board, extracting higher prices for shareholders.
