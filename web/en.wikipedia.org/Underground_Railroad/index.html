Underground Railroad

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Underground Railroad was a network of secret routes and safe houses established in the United States during the early to mid-19th century. It was used by enslaved African Americans primarily to escape into free states and from there to Canada. The network, primarily the work of free African Americans, was also assisted by abolitionist sympathizers and others. Enslaved people who risked escape and those who aided them are collectively referred to as the passengers and conductors of the railroad respectively.

Various other routes led to Mexico, where slavery had been abolished, and to islands in the Caribbean that were not under slaveholders' power. An earlier escape route running south toward Florida, then under Spanish rule, existed in the late 17th and early 18th centuries. However, the generally accepted estimate is that by 1850, 100,000 enslaved people had escaped via the Underground Railroad network.

Terminology

Members of the network used railway metaphors: routes were "lines", stopping places were "stations", helpers who provided shelter were "stationmasters", guides were "conductors", and the enslaved people travelling were "passengers" or "cargo". The term "Underground Railroad" is said to have arisen after 1831, when an enslaved man named Tice Davids escaped across the Ohio River and his pursuer, finding no trace of him, remarked that he must have gone off on "an underground road".

Notable participants

Harriet Tubman, herself formerly enslaved, made some 13 missions to rescue approximately 70 enslaved people, using the network of antislavery activists and safe houses. Other notable figures included Levi Coffin, a Quaker who helped more than 3,000 fugitives in Indiana and Ohio; Thomas Garrett, who aided some 2,700 escapes from Delaware; William Still, who kept meticulous records of the people he helped in Philadelphia; and Frederick Douglass, who sheltered fugitives at his home in Rochester, New York.

Routes and destinations

Canada was a major destination because its long border offered many points of entry, slavery had been abolished there by the British Empire, and Canadian law protected fugitives from extradition. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, which compelled citizens in free states to assist in capturing escapees and denied the accused a jury trial, made Canada still more important as a destination. British North America — where slavery was abolished in 1834 — received an estimated 30,000 to 40,000 freedom seekers.

Legacy

The Underground Railroad occupies a central place in American memory of resistance to slavery. The United States National Park Service administers the National Underground Railroad Network to Freedom programme, which documents and preserves associated sites, and the Harriet Tubman Underground Railroad National Historical Park was established in Maryland in 2013.
