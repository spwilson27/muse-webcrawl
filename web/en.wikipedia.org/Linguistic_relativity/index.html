Linguistic relativity
The hypothesis is named for Sapir and Whorf

The principle of linguistic relativity, commonly called the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, holds that the structure of a person's language influences their worldview and cognition, so that speakers of different languages perceive and think about the world differently. In its strongest form — linguistic determinism, that language determines thought — the hypothesis is widely rejected; in weaker forms, that language influences habitual thought, it remains an active research topic in linguistics, psychology and anthropology.

Origins

The idea has roots in the work of Wilhelm von Humboldt and Franz Boas. Edward Sapir argued that "the real world is to a large extent unconsciously built up on the language habits of the group." His student Benjamin Lee Whorf developed the idea through studies of Hopi, claiming its grammar encoded a different concept of time from European languages — a reading later scholars have contested.

Research areas

  • Colour terms: Berlin and Kay's work on basic colour terms suggested universal constraints, countering strong relativism; later studies show language-specific effects on colour discrimination near category boundaries.
  • Space: speakers of languages using absolute directions (e.g. Guugu Yimithirr, with cardinal points instead of "left/right") show exceptional dead-reckoning orientation.
  • Number: languages with restricted number words, such as Pirahã, are associated with poorer exact counting of larger quantities.
  • Time and grammar: studies examine how tense systems, grammatical gender and evidentiality marking affect memory and categorisation, with mixed and often small effects.

Criticism

Critics, including Steven Pinker, argue that thought is independent of natural language (a "language of thought"), citing Whorf's contested Hopi analysis and cases where effects fail to replicate. Contemporary "neo-Whorfian" research accepts modest, domain-specific influences of language on attention and memory.
