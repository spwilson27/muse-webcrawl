Mfecane

The Mfecane (isiZulu, Zulu pronunciation), also known by the Sesotho name Difaqane or Lifaqane ("forced migration" / "crushing"), was a period of widespread chaos and warfare among indigenous communities in southern Africa between roughly 1815 and 1840. Traditionally associated with the rise of the Zulu kingdom under Shaka, the upheaval is said to have caused the death of one to two million people and the depopulation of large areas, transforming the political map of the region.

Traditional account

In the classic narrative, population pressure, drought, and competition for trade routes led to intensifying conflict among the Nguni peoples of the eastern seaboard. Shaka's military innovations — short stabbing spear (iklwa), large shields, and disciplined regimental (amabutho) tactics — enabled the Zulu to conquer and absorb neighbouring chiefdoms. Displaced groups fled and in turn displaced others, creating a chain reaction: the Ndebele under Mzilikazi moved to the Transvaal and then to present-day Zimbabwe; the Kololo under Sebetwane crossed to Barotseland; the Gaza kingdom arose in Mozambique; and Moshoeshoe I forged the Sotho kingdom in the mountains of Lesotho.

Historical debate

Since the late 20th century, historians have debated the scale and causes of the Mfecane. Julian Cobbing controversially argued in 1988 that the Mfecane was largely an "alibi" constructed by settler historiography to obscure the role of European and colonial slave trading and raiding at Delagoa Bay and the Cape. Most historians accept substantial warfare and state formation while questioning the traditional death tolls (estimates ranged from one to two million) and monocausal explanations.

Consequences

Whatever its causes, the era produced new states across the interior — the Zulu, Swazi, Gaza, Ndebele and Sotho kingdoms — and left parts of the highveld thinly populated when Voortrekker settlers arrived in the 1830s, a fact later used to justify settler land claims.
