Alms

Definition: Charitable giving to the poor
Religions: Christianity, Islam (zakat, sadaqah), Judaism (tzedakah), Buddhism (dāna), Hinduism
Related: Almshouse, tithe

Alms are money, food, or other material goods donated to people living in poverty. Almsgiving is a religious practice in many traditions, regarded variously as a duty of justice, an act of charity, or a means of spiritual merit. The English word derives from the Greek eleēmosynē, meaning mercy or pity.

In world religions

In Christianity, almsgiving is classically grouped with prayer and fasting as the three pillars of penitential practice, emphasised during Lent. In Islam, the obligatory zakat is one of the Five Pillars, while voluntary giving is called sadaqah. Jewish law obliges tzedakah (charitable giving) as a matter of righteousness; Maimonides codified a ladder of giving in which enabling a person to support themselves ranks highest. In Buddhism and Hinduism, dāna (generosity) is a foundational virtue, and giving food to monks is a daily practice for laypeople in Theravada countries.

History

Organised almsgiving shaped medieval institutions such as monasteries, hospitals, and almshouses. Modern welfare states and charities have largely institutionalised poor relief, though individual almsgiving to beggars remains common worldwide and is periodically debated in law and ethics.
