Municipality

A municipality is usually a single administrative division having corporate status and powers of self-government or jurisdiction as granted by national and regional laws to which it is subordinate. The term typically denotes the territory and governing body of a city, town, or village, or sometimes a small group of such settlements.

Powers and functions

Municipalities generally have authority over local services: water supply and sanitation, local roads and public transport, waste collection, zoning and building codes, public libraries, parks, and primary policing and fire services. They finance these through local taxes — commonly property taxes — fees, and transfers from higher levels of government. Their powers vary enormously: in some federal systems, such as that of the United States, municipal authority is limited by principles such as Dillon's Rule, under which local governments possess only powers expressly granted by the state; in other countries municipalities enjoy constitutional protection and broad autonomy.

Government

Municipal government is commonly headed by a mayor, either directly elected or chosen by a municipal council. Structures include the mayor–council system, the council–manager system with a professional administrator, and, historically, the town meeting in New England. The word derives from the Latin municipium, a town whose citizens held Roman citizenship while retaining local self-government.

Around the world

The concept takes different national forms: communes in France, Gemeinden in Germany, municipalities in India governed by the 74th Constitutional Amendment, and local government areas in Australia. Municipalities range in population from a few dozen residents to megacities of tens of millions, and in several countries — notably in Scandinavia — they are the primary providers of welfare services including schools and elder care.
