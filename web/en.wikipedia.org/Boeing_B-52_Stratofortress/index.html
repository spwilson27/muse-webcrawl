The Boeing B-52 Stratofortress is an American long-range, subsonic, jet-powered strategic bomber. Designed and built by Boeing, it has been operated by the United States Air Force since 1955. Capable of carrying up to 70,000 pounds (32,000 kg) of weapons, it has a typical combat radius of about 4,480 miles (7,210 km) without aerial refueling. Affectionately nicknamed the "BUFF" (Big Ugly Fat Fellow), it is expected to remain in service into the 2050s—potentially a century after its first flight.

Development

The B-52 originated in a 1946 requirement for a new intercontinental bomber. Boeing's design evolved from a straight-wing turboprop concept into an eight-jet swept-wing aircraft; the YB-52 prototype first flew on 15 April 1952. A total of 744 aircraft were built across eight variants (A through H), the last delivered in 1962. Only the B-52H, powered by eight Pratt & Whitney TF33 turbofans, remains in service.

Operational history

During the Cold War, B-52s flew airborne alert patrols under Strategic Air Command as part of the nuclear triad. In the Vietnam War they carried out massive conventional bombing in Operations Arc Light and Linebacker II. B-52s launched the majority of cruise missiles in the 1991 Gulf War and have since served in Kosovo, Afghanistan, Iraq, and against ISIS, increasingly as precision-strike and close-air-support platforms. A re-engining program (Commercial Engine Replacement Program) with Rolls-Royce F130 engines and radar upgrades is intended to keep the fleet viable for decades.
