IEEE 802.3 is a working group of the IEEE and the collection of standards it produces, which define the physical layer and data link layer's media access control (MAC) sublayer for wired Ethernet. First published in 1983, the standard has been expanded by dozens of amendments covering speeds from 10 Mbit/s to hundreds of gigabits per second, and media from coaxial cable and twisted pair to optical fibre.

History

Ethernet was developed at Xerox PARC by Robert Metcalfe and colleagues in the 1970s and commercialised by the DEC–Intel–Xerox (DIX) consortium in 1980. The IEEE 802.3 standard of 1983 closely followed the DIX specification, defining 10BASE5 "thick Ethernet" using the carrier-sense multiple access with collision detection (CSMA/CD) protocol. Subsequent milestones included 10BASE-T over telephone-grade twisted pair (1990), Fast Ethernet at 100 Mbit/s (802.3u, 1995), Gigabit Ethernet (1998), 10 Gigabit Ethernet (2002), Power over Ethernet (802.3af, 2003), and Terabit Ethernet amendments in the 2020s.

Frame format and operation

An 802.3 frame carries a preamble and start-frame delimiter, destination and source MAC addresses, an optional 802.1Q VLAN tag, a length/EtherType field, a payload of 46–1500 bytes, and a frame check sequence. Modern networks use switches in full-duplex point-to-point links, so the original CSMA/CD collision mechanism is rarely active, but the frame format and addressing remain essentially those of the 1983 standard—a longevity almost unmatched in computing.
