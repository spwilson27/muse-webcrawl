Corinthian order

TypeClassical architectural order
OriginAncient Greece, late 5th century BC
Defining featureCapital carved with acanthus leaves and volutes
Other ordersDoric, Ionic, Tuscan, Composite

The Corinthian order (Greek: Κορινθιακὸς ῥυθμός, Korinthiakós rythmós; Latin: Ordo Corinthius) is the last developed of the three principal classical orders of Ancient Greek architecture and Roman architecture. It is characterised by slender fluted columns and elaborate capitals decorated with acanthus leaves and scrolls (volutes). The Corinthian order is named for the Greek city-state of Corinth, to which its invention was attributed in legend.


A Corinthian capital, with two rows of acanthus leaves and corner volutes.

History

According to Vitruvius, the sculptor Callimachus invented the capital after seeing acanthus leaves growing around a basket placed on a young girl's grave. The earliest known Corinthian capital was found at the Temple of Apollo Epicurius at Bassae (c. 420 BC), and the order was used sparingly in Greece — at the Tholos of Epidaurus and the Choragic Monument of Lysicrates in Athens (335/334 BC). The Romans adopted it enthusiastically: it became the dominant order of imperial architecture, used on the Pantheon portico and countless temples and basilicas.

Characteristics

The capital consists of an inverted bell (kalathos) wrapped in two rows of acanthus leaves, with small volutes springing from cauliculi (stalks) at the corners, supporting a concave-sided abacus. The shaft is usually fluted with fillets, like the Ionic, and stands on an Attic base. The entablature is richly ornamented, often with modillions or dentils. Renaissance theorists codified proportions of roughly ten shaft-diameters in height, making it the most slender and decorative of the orders.

Later use

Revived in the Renaissance by theorists such as Alberti and Serlio, the Corinthian order became the standard for grand churches, palaces, and civic buildings, and reached perhaps its widest use in the Beaux-Arts and Neoclassical architecture of the 18th to early 20th centuries — from St Paul's Cathedral to the United States Capitol, whose columns use a distinctive American variant with corn and tobacco leaves carved in place of acanthus.
