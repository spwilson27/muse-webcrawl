Trade union

A trade union (or labor union in American English) is an organisation of workers formed to protect and advance their common interests — wages, hours, working conditions, job security and benefits — principally through collective bargaining with employers. Unions may also engage in political campaigning, provide legal and welfare services to members, and, as a last resort, organise strikes and other industrial action.

History

Modern unions emerged with the Industrial Revolution, when factory workers in Britain, continental Europe and North America began combining to resist long hours, low pay and dangerous conditions. Early combinations were often illegal: Britain's Combination Acts (1799–1800) criminalised them, and the Tolpuddle Martyrs were transported to Australia in 1834 for swearing a union oath. Legal recognition expanded through the 19th and 20th centuries — Britain's Trade Union Act 1871, the US National Labor Relations Act 1935 (Wagner Act), and post-1945 ILO conventions on freedom of association.

Structure and types

Unions are organised as craft unions (one trade), industrial unions (one industry), or general unions (many sectors). Local branches federate into national unions, which in turn join national centres such as the British TUC, the American AFL–CIO, or Germany's DGB. In many European countries, sector-level bargaining and works councils give unions influence beyond their membership; union density exceeds 50 percent in the Nordic countries but is around 10 percent in the United States.

Decline and renewal

From the late 20th century, union membership declined in most advanced economies due to deindustrialisation, anti-union legislation, globalisation and the growth of service and gig work. Debates continue over the effects of unions on wages (a persistent union wage premium), inequality, productivity and inflation, and recent organising drives in logistics, technology and coffee retail have revived interest in unions among younger workers.
