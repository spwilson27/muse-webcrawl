Antiochus III the Great (c. 241 – 3 July 187 BC) was the sixth ruler of the Seleucid Empire, reigning from 222 to 187 BC. He restored much of the empire's lost territory, earning the epithet "the Great", but his expansion westward brought him into conflict with the Roman Republic, which defeated him decisively.

Early reign and eastern campaigns

Succeeding his murdered brother Seleucus III at about eighteen, Antiochus faced rebellions in Media and Persia (under the satrap Molon) and in Asia Minor (under his cousin Achaeus), both of which he suppressed. His first war against Ptolemaic Egypt (the Fourth Syrian War) ended in defeat at Raphia in 217 BC. Between 209 and 204 BC he undertook a great eastern expedition (anabasis) through Armenia, Parthia, Bactria, and to the borders of India, renewing friendship with the Indian king Sophagasenus and restoring Seleucid supremacy in the Upper Satrapies.

Western expansion and war with Rome

In the Fifth Syrian War (202–195 BC) Antiochus conquered Coele-Syria from Egypt, defeating the Ptolemaic army at Panium in 200 BC. He then campaigned in Thrace and intervened in Greece at the invitation of the Aetolian League, landing in 192 BC. Rome declared war; Antiochus was defeated at Thermopylae (191 BC) and, decisively, at the Battle of Magnesia (190 BC) by the Scipios. By the Treaty of Apamea (188 BC) he surrendered his territory in Asia Minor west of the Taurus, paid a huge indemnity of 15,000 talents, and gave up his elephants and most of his fleet. He died in 187 BC while plundering a temple in Elymais to raise money for the indemnity.

Legacy

Antiochus refounded and renamed many cities, supported Greek civic institutions, and is remembered in Jewish tradition for the settlement that allowed the Jews to live "according to their ancestral laws"; the persecutions described in the books of the Maccabees belong to the reign of his son, Antiochus IV Epiphanes.
