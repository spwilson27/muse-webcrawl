Sindh (Sindhi: سنڌ) is one of the four provinces of Pakistan. Located in the southeastern region of the country, it is the third-largest province by land area and the second-largest by population, after Punjab. Its capital and largest city is Karachi, Pakistan's main seaport and financial centre. Sindh is the historic homeland of the Sindhi people and the site of the ancient Indus Valley cities.

History

Sindh was home to the Indus Valley Civilisation, whose best-known city, Mohenjo-daro, flourished around 2500 BCE. The province takes its name from the Indus River (Sindhu in Sanskrit). It was successively ruled by Persian, Greek (under Alexander the Great), Mauryan, Gupta and local dynasties.

In 711 CE the Umayyad general Muhammad bin Qasim conquered Sindh, making it one of the earliest regions of South Asia to come under Muslim rule. Later rulers included the Soomra, Samma, Arghun, Tarkhan and Talpur dynasties. The British annexed Sindh in 1843 after the Battle of Miani, administering it as part of the Bombay Presidency before it became a separate province in 1936. In 1947 Sindh became part of the new state of Pakistan, and Karachi served as the national capital until 1959.

Geography

Sindh stretches from the Kirthar mountains in the west to the Thar Desert in the east, with the fertile Indus plain at its centre. The province has a coastline on the Arabian Sea and the Indus delta near Thatta. Its climate is predominantly arid and very hot in summer.

Economy and culture

Karachi contributes a large share of Pakistan's industrial output, trade and revenue. Sindh is a major producer of cotton, rice, wheat and sugarcane, aided by irrigation from barrages on the Indus such as Sukkur and Kotri. The province is renowned for its Sufi shrines, including those of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar at Sehwan and Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai at Bhit Shah, and for crafts such as ajrak textiles and Sindhi caps.
