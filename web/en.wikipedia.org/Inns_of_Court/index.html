Inns of Court

The Inns of Court in London are the professional associations for barristers in England and Wales. There are four Inns of Court — Lincoln's Inn, Inner Temple, Middle Temple, and Gray's Inn. All barristers must belong to one of them. They have supervisory and disciplinary functions over their members, and historically provided legal education and training; the Inns are also the only bodies with the power to call a student to the Bar.

History

The Inns originated in the fourteenth century, developing around the area between the City of London and Westminster where the royal courts sat. By the sixteenth century they had become, in effect, England's third university, providing a legal and general education to sons of the gentry as well as aspiring lawyers. Their numbers declined during the Civil War period, but they retained their monopoly on calling barristers. The smaller Inns of Chancery, once preparatory institutions, were dissolved in the nineteenth century.

The four Inns

  • Lincoln's Inn — the largest of the Inns, in Holborn, famed for its library and Great Hall.
  • Inner Temple and Middle Temple — occupying the former Knights Templar precinct between Fleet Street and the Thames, sharing the Temple Church.
  • Gray's Inn — north of Holborn, historically associated with many leading common lawyers and judges.

Role today

Modern bar training is delivered by approved providers, but the Inns still administer scholarships, provide continuing professional development, and "call" qualified students to the Bar in formal ceremonies. Each Inn is governed by its benchers (senior members), and the four Inns jointly participate in the Council of the Inns of Court.
