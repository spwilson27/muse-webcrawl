Limnology

Limnology is the study of inland aquatic ecosystems — lakes, ponds, reservoirs, rivers, streams, wetlands, and groundwater. It integrates the physical, chemical, geological, and biological characteristics of inland waters, in the same way that oceanography treats the oceans. The name derives from the Greek limnē, "lake".

Scope and history

The Swiss naturalist François-Alphonse Forel founded the discipline with his monographs on Lake Geneva (1892–1904). Later pioneers included August Thienemann and Einar Naumann in Europe, and Edward Birge and Chancey Juday at the University of Wisconsin, who established lake typologies based on productivity (oligotrophic, mesotrophic, eutrophic). G. Evelyn Hutchinson's Treatise on Limnology (from 1957) synthesised the field.

Key topics

Limnologists study thermal stratification and mixing of lakes, light and nutrient budgets, oxygen dynamics, plankton communities, food webs, sediment processes, and the effects of acidification and eutrophication from nutrient pollution. The field underpins water-resource management, fisheries, drinking-water treatment, and understanding of the global carbon cycle, since inland waters bury and emit significant carbon.
