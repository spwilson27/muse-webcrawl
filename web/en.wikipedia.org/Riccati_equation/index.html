Riccati equation

In mathematics, a Riccati equation in the narrowest sense is any first-order ordinary differential equation that is quadratic in the unknown function. In other words, it is an equation of the form

y′(x) = q0(x) + q1(x) y(x) + q2(x) y(x)2

where q0, q1 and q2 are given functions. The name is given in honour of the Italian mathematician Count Jacopo Francesco Riccati (1676–1754), who discussed special cases of such equations in the early 18th century; the equation was also studied by Daniel Bernoulli and by members of the Bernoulli family.

Properties

Unlike linear differential equations, Riccati equations do not in general possess closed-form solutions. However, if one particular solution y1 is known, the general solution can be obtained: the substitution y = y1 + 1/v converts the equation into a first-order linear equation for v, which can be solved by standard methods. A further classical transformation converts a Riccati equation into a second-order linear differential equation, linking it to the theory of special functions.

Special cases

If q2 = 0, the equation reduces to a first-order linear equation; if q0 = 0, it reduces to a Bernoulli equation. The special equation y′ = xm + y2, studied by Riccati himself, is solvable in terms of Bessel functions for particular values of the exponent m; Liouville showed in the 1840s that for most values no solution exists in terms of elementary functions — one of the earliest results of differential Galois theory.

Applications

Riccati equations arise in many areas of applied mathematics and engineering. The algebraic Riccati equation is central to modern control theory, where it appears in the linear–quadratic regulator (LQR) problem and in Kalman filtering; its solutions determine optimal feedback gains. Matrix Riccati equations also appear in differential games, network theory, and the study of stochastic processes. In quantum mechanics, Riccati-type equations occur in supersymmetric and factorisation methods.
