Web tracking

TechniquesCookies, web beacons, fingerprinting, tracking pixels
PurposesAnalytics, advertising, personalisation
CountermeasuresAd blockers, tracker blocking, Do Not Track

Web tracking is the practice by which operators of websites and third parties collect, store and share information about a user's activities on the World Wide Web. Analysis of an individual user's browsing behaviour can be used to provide content that enables the operator to infer their preferences, and may be of interest to various parties, including advertisers and data brokers. Web tracking can be part of visitor management and behavioural advertising.

Techniques

The classic mechanism is the third-party cookie: an advertising or analytics element embedded in many sites sets a cookie that lets its operator recognise the same browser across all of them. Other techniques include web beacons (invisible tracking pixels), ETags and local storage, URL parameters passed between sites, and browser fingerprinting, which identifies users from combinations of device characteristics such as fonts, screen size and graphics rendering. Logged-in accounts allow cross-device tracking tied to a person's identity.

Debate and regulation

Tracking underpins the targeted-advertising economy that funds much of the free web, but privacy advocates criticise its opacity and scale, and researchers have documented data collection by thousands of third parties on typical sites. Laws such as the GDPR and the California Consumer Privacy Act impose consent and disclosure requirements. Browser vendors have responded with tracking protection (Firefox's Enhanced Tracking Protection, Safari's Intelligent Tracking Prevention), Google's long-planned deprecation of third-party cookies in Chrome, and signals such as "Do Not Track" and Global Privacy Control. Users counter tracking with ad blockers, privacy extensions and VPNs.
