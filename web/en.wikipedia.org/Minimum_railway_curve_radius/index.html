The minimum railway curve radius is the shortest allowable radius for the center line of railway track under a particular set of conditions, such as vehicle type, speed, and track gauge. The minimum radius determines how sharply a railway can turn, and so it constrains alignments through mountains, cities, and yards, as well as the design of the rolling stock that must negotiate the curves.

Unlike road vehicles, railway vehicles have flanged steel wheels and (usually) rigid axles in bogies that cannot steer freely, so very sharp curves cause excessive flange wear, squealing, resistance, and risk of derailment or rail rollover. The governing factors are the vehicle's wheelbase or bogie spacing, the permitted speed, cant (superelevation) and cant deficiency, and the gauge. Main-line standards often set absolute minima around 190 metres, while high-speed lines use radii of several kilometres to maintain passenger comfort. At the other extreme, street tramways negotiate curves of about 10–20 metres, and industrial and mine railways can be tighter still.

Design trade-offs

Engineers balance sharper, cheaper alignments against slower journey times and higher maintenance. Transition spirals ease vehicles into curves, and check rails or gauge widening may be added on tight radii. Vehicle designs such as radial (steering) bogies and shorter fixed wheelbases permit trains to operate over tighter curves without excessive wear.
