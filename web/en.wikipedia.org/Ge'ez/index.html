Ge'ez (ግእዝ, Gəʿəz) is an ancient South Semitic language that originated in the region of present-day Ethiopia and Eritrea. It was the language of the Kingdom of Aksum and of the Ethiopian imperial court. Although it ceased to be a spoken vernacular roughly a thousand years ago, it survives as the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church and the Beta Israel community, and as a language of scholarship and poetry.

History

Ge'ez emerged in the first millennium BC among Semitic-speaking peoples of the northern Horn of Africa. Inscriptions in an early consonantal form appear from the pre-Aksumite period; with the rise of Aksum and its conversion to Christianity in the 4th century AD, Ge'ez developed its distinctive vocalised script and a rich written literature, including Bible translations from Greek. By the 10th–13th centuries, spoken Ge'ez had been displaced by languages such as Amharic and Tigrinya, but it remained the sole written language of Ethiopia until the 19th century.

Literature

The Ge'ez corpus includes royal chronicles, saints' lives (gadlat), hymns, legal codes such as the Fetha Nagast, and the Kebra Nagast, the national epic recounting the Queen of Sheba's visit to Solomon and the transfer of the Ark of the Covenant to Ethiopia. The earliest surviving illuminated manuscripts in the world that are still in their original location — the Garima Gospels, dated by radiocarbon to between the 4th and 7th centuries — are written in Ge'ez.

Linguistic features

Ge'ez is written in its own script, an abugida in which each character represents a consonant–vowel syllable, written left to right. The language preserves archaic Semitic features, including a set of guttural consonants and a verb system built on triconsonantal roots with templatic derivations. Its vocabulary and script underlie the modern Ethiopic languages Amharic, Tigrinya and Tigre.
