Internet censorship in China

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Internet censorship in China is among the most extensive and sophisticated in the world. The Chinese government, under the Chinese Communist Party, regulates internet access through a combination of legislation, administrative controls by agencies such as the Cyberspace Administration of China, and technical filtering systems popularly known as the Great Firewall of China. More than sixty internet regulations exist, and content is monitored and filtered at the levels of internet service providers, companies, and individuals.

The Great Firewall

The Great Firewall — a pun on the Great Wall of China — emerged from the Golden Shield Project, initiated in the late 1990s by the Ministry of Public Security. It blocks access to selected foreign websites and slows cross-border traffic using techniques including IP address blocking, DNS spoofing and hijacking, URL and keyword filtering, deep packet inspection, and active probing and blocking of circumvention protocols such as VPNs and Tor.

Blocked services

Many major foreign platforms are inaccessible from mainland China, including Google Search and most Google services, Facebook, Instagram, X (Twitter), YouTube, WhatsApp, Wikipedia (all languages, blocked since 2019), and many international news outlets. Domestic alternatives — Baidu for search, WeChat and Weibo for social media, Youku and Bilibili for video — operate under Chinese law, requiring real-name registration and obliging companies to monitor and remove prohibited content.

Legal framework

Key laws include the 2017 Cybersecurity Law, the 2021 Data Security Law, and the Personal Information Protection Law. Regulations prohibit content that endangers national security, subverts state power, damages national honour, or spreads rumours disturbing social order. Internet companies employ large teams of human censors; automated systems scan posts for sensitive keywords, and accounts may be suspended or users detained for posts deemed unlawful.

Circumvention and effects

Many users employ virtual private networks (VPNs) to bypass filtering, though unauthorised VPN services are restricted and periodically cracked down upon. Scholars debate the system's goals: research suggests censorship targets especially content with collective-action potential, and that the regime combines blocking with "flooding" — distraction and pro-regime cheerleading, sometimes attributed to paid commentators known as the "50 Cent Party".
