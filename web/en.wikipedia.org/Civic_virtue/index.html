Civic virtue

FieldPolitical philosophy
Related ideasRepublicanism, public spirit, common good

Civic virtue is the dedication of citizens to the common welfare of their community or country, even at the cost of their individual interests. It is the disposition to subordinate private advantage to public good, and it is a central concept in republican political thought from classical antiquity onward.

Historical development

In ancient Greece and Rome, virtue (aretē, virtus) was bound up with the life of the citizen in the polis: Aristotle held that the good citizen participates in ruling and being ruled, and Roman exempla such as Cincinnatus celebrated selfless public service. Civic humanists of the Renaissance, notably Machiavelli, revived the ideal of virtù as the quality that preserves free republics against corruption and fortuna.

Enlightenment thinkers such as Montesquieu made virtue the animating principle of republics in particular. The American Founders, influenced by this tradition, argued that constitutional structures alone could not sustain liberty without a measure of public-spiritedness, and promoted public education partly to cultivate it.

Modern discussion

Contemporary theorists debate whether liberal societies can sustain civic virtue — manifested in voting, jury service, military service, volunteering, and respect for shared institutions — without illiberal coercion. Communitarians and civic republicans argue for thicker civic education and participation, while liberals stress rights-protecting neutrality.
