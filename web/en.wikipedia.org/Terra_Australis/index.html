Terra Australis

Terra Australis (Latin for "southern land") was a hypothetical continent postulated in antiquity and depicted on European maps from the 15th to the 18th centuries. Geographers reasoned that the landmasses of the northern hemisphere must be balanced by a great southern continent.

History

The idea appears in the works of Aristotle and Ptolemy, and on maps by Mercator and Ortelius as an enormous landmass spanning the south polar regions. Sightings of Tierra del Fuego, Australia and other southern lands were at first incorporated into the supposed continent. Voyages by Abel Tasman and James Cook progressively reduced it: Cook's second voyage (1772–1775) crossed the Antarctic Circle and showed that any southern continent must lie far south, largely beneath the ice.

Legacy

The name survives in "Australia", derived from Terra Australis Incognita ("unknown southern land"). The real continent of Antarctica, first sighted in 1820, lies close to where the hypothetical continent was imagined, but far smaller and colder than the temperate land of early maps.
