Fleet Prison

The Fleet Prison was a notorious London prison by the side of the Fleet River in the City of London, operating from the 12th century until 1846. From the 17th century it was used mainly as a debtors' prison and was notorious for its corruption, cruelty and the system of fees and rackets by which wardens and turnkeys enriched themselves.

History

A prison stood on the site from 1197. It was destroyed during the Peasants' Revolt of 1381 and again in the Great Fire of London in 1666, and was rebuilt after the Gordon Riots of 1780. Although formally under the Marshal of the King's Bench, the wardenship was effectively bought and sold; inmates paid for food, lodging, bedding and even for having their irons removed. Wealthier prisoners could pay for the "liberty of the Rules", living outside the prison walls in the surrounding streets.

In literature and closure

Charles Dickens, whose father John Dickens was imprisoned for debt in the related Marshalsea, depicted the Fleet in The Pickwick Papers. Parliamentary inquiries in 1729 exposed abuses after prisoners were tortured and murdered. The Fleet was closed in 1842 after legislation consolidated debtors' prisons, and demolished in 1846.
