Coercive persuasion

Coercive persuasion is a social influence process in which a person or group uses coercive pressure — including isolation, control of information, threats, exhaustion and manipulation of rewards and punishments — to induce changes in another person's beliefs, attitudes or behaviour. The term is closely related to the popular concept of "brainwashing" and to Robert Jay Lifton's model of thought reform.

Thought reform

Psychiatrist Robert Jay Lifton studied the "re-education" of prisoners in Maoist China and identified eight criteria of thought reform, including milieu control, mystical manipulation, the demand for purity, the cult of confession, sacred science, loaded language, doctrine over person, and the dispensing of existence. Edgar Schein developed a related model from the study of Korean War prisoners of war.

Applications and debate

The concept has been applied to prisoner-of-war camps, cults, abusive relationships and some interrogation programmes. Critics, including many social scientists and courts, have questioned whether coercive persuasion can override free will to the extent sometimes claimed, and professional bodies such as the American Psychological Association have declined to endorse the strongest versions of the theory. The term remains influential in discussions of undue influence and high-control groups.
