The High Priest of Israel (Hebrew: Kohen Gadol, "Great Priest") was the chief religious official of ancient Israel, the head of the priesthood descended from Aaron, the brother of Moses. The office stood at the centre of Israelite worship from the wilderness period through the era of the First and Second Temples in Jerusalem.

Duties

The high priest supervised the sacrificial cult, blessed the people with the priestly blessing, and wore distinctive vestments including a breastplate set with twelve stones, the ephod, and a golden plate (tzitz) inscribed "Holy to the LORD". His most solemn duty came on Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, when he alone entered the Holy of Holies to offer incense and sprinkle the blood of sacrifices for the sins of the nation.

Succession and history

Tradition traces the line from Aaron through his son Eleazar. During the Second Temple period the office became politically powerful and was at times appointed by foreign rulers, including the Seleucids and later Herod and the Roman prefects. High priests such as Annas and Caiaphas figure prominently in accounts of the trial of Jesus. The office effectively ended with the Roman destruction of the Temple in 70 CE.

Legacy

The memory of the high priesthood persisted in synagogue liturgy, in the priestly blessing still pronounced by kohanim, and in hopes expressed in some prayers for the restoration of the Temple service.
