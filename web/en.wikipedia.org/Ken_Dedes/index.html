Ken Dedes

Ken Dedes (13th century) was the first queen of Singhasari, wife of Ken Arok, the founder of the Rajasa dynasty of Singhasari and Majapahit, East Java. She is remembered in Javanese tradition as a woman of extraordinary beauty whose destiny — to be the mother of kings and of murderers of kings — frames the tragic cycle of the Rajasa house told in the Pararaton chronicle.

Legend

According to the Pararaton (Book of Kings), Ken Dedes was the daughter of the Buddhist priest Mpu Purwa of Panawijen. The akuwwu (district chief) of Tumapel, Tunggul Ametung, abducted and married her. The young outlaw Ken Arok, then in Tunggul Ametung's service, saw her as she descended from a carriage: her sarong slipped, and a supernatural radiance — the nareswari, the mark of a woman destined to bear kings — shone from her. A Brahmin, Lohgawe, told Ken Arok that whoever possessed Ken Dedes would become a universal monarch.

Ken Arok commissioned the smith Mpu Gandring to forge a kris, murdered Tunggul Ametung with it while he slept beside Ken Dedes, and framed the retainer Kebo Ijo, who was executed with the same blade. Ken Arok then married the widowed Ken Dedes, who was pregnant with Tunggul Ametung's son, Anusapati. Arok went on to defeat Kediri in 1222 and found Singhasari, with Ken Dedes as his queen.

The curse of Mpu Gandring

The unfinished kris whose forging Ken Arok had cut short became the instrument of a famous curse: Mpu Gandring, killed by Arok for his impatience, prophesied that the dagger would kill seven generations of the Rajasa line. The curse is said to have claimed Tunggul Ametung, Kebo Ijo, Ken Arok himself (murdered by Anusapati with the same kris), Anusapati (killed by Tohjaya) and others. Ken Dedes's son Anusapati thus avenged his true father, and the queen stood at the origin of the dynasty's bloody succession.

Legacy

A celebrated statue of Prajnaparamita, the Buddhist goddess of transcendent wisdom, discovered near Singhasari and now in the National Museum of Indonesia, is traditionally identified as a deified portrait of Ken Dedes. Her name is borne by streets, schools and cultural institutions across East Java, and she remains a byword in Indonesian culture for fateful beauty.
