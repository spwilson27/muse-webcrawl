Ney

The ney is an end-blown flute that figures prominently in many traditions of Middle Eastern and Central Asian music. Made from a hollow cane or reed stem, it is one of the oldest instruments still in use, depicted in Egyptian tomb paintings more than 4,500 years old.

Description

The Persian ney is a knotty reed tube with five or six finger holes; the Turkish ney adds a mouthpiece (baspare) of horn or plastic that rests on the lips. The player blows across the sharpened rim at an angle, producing a breathy, expressive tone. Different lengths correspond to different pitches, and master players (neyzen in Turkish tradition) command a wide range with multiple octaves.

Cultural role

The ney is central to Persian and Arabic art music, Turkish classical and Ottoman court music, and Sufi ritual: it accompanies the whirling of the Mevlevi dervishes, and Rumi's Masnavi famously opens with the lament of the reed cut from its bed, a metaphor for the soul's longing. Noted performers include Niyazi Sayin and Kudsi Erguner.
