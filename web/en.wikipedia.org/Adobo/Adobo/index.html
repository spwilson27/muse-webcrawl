Adobo

Adobo
TypeMarinade / cooking method
OriginSpain; adopted in the Philippines and Latin America
Key ingredientsVinegar, soy sauce (Philippines), garlic, bay leaves, peppercorns

Adobo (from Spanish adobar, "to marinate") refers both to a marinade or sauce and to dishes prepared with it. The term covers several distinct traditions: Spanish and Latin American adobo is a marinade of vinegar, paprika, oregano and garlic; Filipino adobo is a cooking method in which meat, seafood or vegetables are braised in vinegar, soy sauce, garlic, bay leaves and black peppercorns, and is widely considered the unofficial national dish of the Philippines.

Filipino adobo

The Filipino method predates Spanish colonization: indigenous cooks preserved and flavored meat in vinegar and salt. Spanish colonizers applied the name adobo to the similar local practice. Chicken and pork adobo (adobong manok at baboy) is the best-known version; regional variants use coconut milk (adobo sa gata), turmeric, or no soy sauce (adobong puti).

Other traditions

In Mexico, adobo is a sauce of dried chiles used for pork and for chipotles en adobo. In Puerto Rico, adobo is a seasoned salt rub for meats. Portuguese carne em vinha d'alhos — pork in wine and garlic — is a related preparation that traveled as far as Goa (vindaloo) and Madeira.
