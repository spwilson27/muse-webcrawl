Adobo

This article is about the Filipino dish and cooking method. For the Spanish marinade, see Spanish adobo.
Filipino chicken adobo, served with rice.

Adobo refers to a popular dish and cooking process in Philippine cuisine in which meat, seafood, or vegetables are marinated and braised in a mixture of vinegar, soy sauce, garlic, bay leaves, and black peppercorns. It is frequently considered the unofficial national dish of the Philippines. The most common versions are chicken adobo (adobong manok) and pork adobo (adobong baboy), or a combination of the two.

History

The cooking method predates Spanish colonisation: Indigenous Filipinos preserved and cooked food in vinegar and salt, a technique well suited to the tropical climate. When Spanish colonisers arrived in the 16th century, they applied the word adobo — from the Spanish adobar, "to marinate" — to the native dish, although the Filipino preparation is distinct from Spanish adobo. Soy sauce was introduced through trade with China, and over time largely replaced salt in most recipes. In 2021, the Philippine Department of Trade and Industry began work on standardising a reference recipe for adobo as part of an effort to promote Filipino cuisine internationally.

Preparation

The meat is typically marinated in the vinegar-soy sauce mixture, then browned in oil with garlic, and finally simmered in the marinade with bay leaves and whole peppercorns until tender and the sauce reduces. The finished dish balances salty, sour, and garlicky flavours and is almost always served with steamed white rice.

Variations

There are countless regional and household variations across the Philippines:

  • Adobong puti ("white adobo") — made without soy sauce, using only vinegar and salt, regarded by some as the oldest form.
  • Adobo sa gata — cooked with coconut milk, common in the Bicol and southern Luzon regions.
  • Adobong dilaw ("yellow adobo") — coloured with turmeric.
  • Adobong pusit — squid adobo, darkened by squid ink.
  • Adobong kangkong — water spinach cooked adobo-style, a vegetarian version.
  • Adobo flakes — leftover adobo shredded and fried until crisp.

In other cuisines

The word adobo also names marinades and sauces elsewhere in the Spanish-influenced world: Spanish adobo, a paprika-and-vinegar marinade; Mexican adobo sauces, often made with dried chillies; and Puerto Rican adobo, a dry seasoning blend. A different dish, chipotles en adobo, consists of smoked jalapeños in a tomato-based sauce.
