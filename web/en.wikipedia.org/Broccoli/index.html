Broccoli

Broccoli (Brassica oleracea var. italica) is an edible green plant in the cabbage family (Brassicaceae) whose large flowering head, stalk, and small leaves are eaten as a vegetable. It is a cultivar of the same species as cabbage, kale, cauliflower, and Brussels sprouts, all descended from wild cabbage native to the Mediterranean coast. The word broccoli comes from the Italian plural of broccolo, "the flowering crest of a cabbage," itself a diminutive of brocco, "small nail."

Cultivation

Developed in the northern Mediterranean by Etruscan and Roman growers, broccoli was little known in northern Europe until the eighteenth century and reached North America with Italian immigrants in the nineteenth. It is a cool-season crop, thriving at temperatures between 18 and 23 °C (64–73 °F), and is harvested by hand while the flower buds are still closed; left too long, the head erupts into yellow flowers. The most familiar type is Calabrese broccoli, named for Calabria, though sprouting and purple varieties exist. China and India are the largest producers; the vegetable is naturally rich in vitamin C, vitamin K, fibre, and the sulfur compound sulforaphane, studied for potential health effects.

Culinary use

Broccoli is eaten raw in salads and with dips, or cooked by steaming, boiling, roasting, and stir-frying; prolonged boiling destroys much of its vitamin content and intensifies its sulfur flavours. Some people perceive its glucosinolate compounds as bitter owing to the TAS2R38 taste gene, the same variant that affects the tasting of phenylthiocarbamide.
