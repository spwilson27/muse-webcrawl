Autofiction

Autofiction blurs the boundary between autobiography and the novel.

Autofiction is a literary genre that combines autobiography and fiction: the author appears as a character, usually the narrator and protagonist, in a work that is presented or received as a novel while drawing on the author's real life. The term was coined by the French writer Serge Doubrovsky in 1977 to describe his novel Fils, written in response to Philippe Lejeune's theory of the "autobiographical pact".

Characteristics

In classic autobiography, Lejeune argued, author, narrator and protagonist are identical and the text promises truth. Autofiction keeps the identity of names while adopting the freedoms of fiction: scenes are invented or re-arranged, chronology is compressed, and the reader cannot finally determine what is factual and what is fabricated. Doubrovsky defined the genre as the "fiction of events and facts strictly real" — a definition later critics have loosened considerably.

History and practitioners

Writers cited as precursors include Colette, Louis-Ferdinand Céline, Jean Genet and, in Japan, the confessional I-novel (shishōsetsu) tradition. The genre flourished in France with authors such as Doubrovsky, Marie Darrieussecq, Catherine Cusset and Hervé Guibert, and in the English-speaking world with writers including Chris Kraus, Ben Lerner, Rachel Cusk and Sheila Heti. Karl Ove Knausgård's six-volume My Struggle and the work of Annie Ernaux — winner of the 2022 Nobel Prize in Literature — are frequently discussed in relation to autofiction, though both authors have complicated the label.

Debate

Critics dispute whether autofiction names a distinct genre, a marketing category, or simply a new name for the autobiographical novel. Others question its ethics: real people appear thinly disguised, and the author's claim of fiction can clash with the factual claims implied by using real names. The genre's popularity in the twenty-first century has been linked to memoir culture, the internet age of self-presentation, and a literary reaction against both traditional realism and postmodern detachment.
