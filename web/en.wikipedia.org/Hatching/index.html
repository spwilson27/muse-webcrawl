Hatching

Hatching (from Old English hæccan) is the process by which an animal emerges from an egg. It applies to oviparous animals — including birds, most reptiles, amphibians, fish, and many invertebrates — at the end of the incubation period, when the developing embryo, having completed its growth within the egg, breaks through the eggshell and enters the outside world. In art, the same word denotes hachure, a technique of shading with closely spaced parallel lines.

The process

As hatching approaches, the embryo typically absorbs the remaining yolk, which sustains it through its first hours or days of life. In birds and some reptiles, the chick uses a temporary horny structure on its beak called the egg tooth to pip — to make the first small hole in the shell — and then gradually rotates within the egg, chipping a circular fracture (unzipping) until the cap of the shell can be pushed off. A specialised hatching muscle at the back of the neck powers these movements. The effort may take from a few hours to more than a day.

Synchrony and cues

In many species, siblings synchronise their hatching through acoustic signals — clicking and vocalisations exchanged through the shells — so that the whole clutch emerges together, an advantage for precocial species that leave the nest immediately. Temperature, vibration, and sound can all act as hatching cues; some turtle and crocodilian embryos adjust their hatching time in response to conditions, and in several reptiles incubation temperature also determines sex.

Assisted and artificial hatching

In poultry production, eggs are incubated artificially in incubators and hatcheries, where temperature, humidity, and turning are controlled so that chicks hatch uniformly. Conservation programmes for endangered birds and reptiles likewise rely on artificial incubation and, occasionally, assisted hatching when an embryo fails to emerge on its own.
