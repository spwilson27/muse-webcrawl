Jugular venous pressure

The jugular venous pressure (JVP, sometimes referred to as jugular venous pulsation) is the indirectly observed pressure over the venous system via visualization of the internal jugular vein. It is a bedside clinical measurement that provides an estimate of central venous pressure and right atrial pressure.

Examination

The patient is positioned at about 45 degrees, and the clinician observes the pulsation of the internal jugular vein in the neck, measuring the vertical height of the pulsation above the sternal angle. Because the internal jugular vein connects directly to the right atrium without valves, the height of the column reflects right atrial pressure. The waveform has characteristic components (the a, c, and v waves and the x and y descents) corresponding to events in the cardiac cycle.

Clinical significance

An elevated JVP is a key sign of fluid overload and right-sided or biventricular heart failure, and also occurs in tricuspid stenosis, constrictive pericarditis, and cardiac tamponade. Abnormal waveforms aid diagnosis: cannon a waves suggest atrioventricular dissociation, and Kussmaul's sign — a paradoxical rise in JVP on inspiration — is seen in constrictive pericarditis. A low JVP suggests hypovolemia.
