Lynchet

A lynchet is a bank of earth that builds up on the downslope side of a field ploughed over many years on a slope, with a corresponding loss of soil (a "negative lynchet") on the upslope side. Lynchets are characteristic features of ancient — especially Iron Age and Romano-British "Celtic" — field systems in Britain and northwestern Europe, and survive as prominent terraces on chalk downland where the fields have long since reverted to pasture.

Lynchets on the downs of southern England

Formation

When a field on sloping ground is repeatedly ploughed, soil is moved predominantly downhill — by the action of the plough turning the furrow downslope and by soil creep and rainwash. Soil accumulates against the lower field boundary (often a hedge or fence line) as a positive lynchet, while the upper edge of the field is cut back into the slope as a negative lynchet, forming a scarp. Over centuries of cultivation the pair can create a terrace several metres high, even where the field was never deliberately terraced.

Archaeological significance

Because lynchets require long-continued ploughing, they are key evidence for the age and layout of prehistoric field systems, such as the coaxial "Celtic fields" of Salisbury Plain, the South Downs and the Yorkshire Wolds. Fossilised under grassland after abandonment, they are among the most visible earthworks of the British Iron Age and Romano-British landscape. Related features include the deliberate cultivation terraces of medieval strip lynchets, found notably in Devon and elsewhere, where slopes were intentionally benched to create level strips.
