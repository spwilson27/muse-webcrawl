Handmade paper sheets, formed from a pulp of plant fibres.

Paper is a thin sheet material formed by pressing together moist fibres of cellulose pulp derived from wood, rags, grasses, or other vegetable sources and drying them into flexible sheets. It is a versatile material with many uses, including printing, painting, packaging, cleaning, decoration, and construction; paper and the pulp-paper industry are among the largest industrial sectors in the world. The word derives from papyrus, the plant-based writing material of ancient Egypt, though the two are made by entirely different methods.

History

Archaeological fragments of true paper from China date to the 2nd century BCE. In 105 CE the Han dynasty court official Cai Lun presented an improved papermaking process using tree bark, hemp waste, rags, and fishing nets, and he is traditionally credited as paper's inventor. Paper spread along the Silk Road: to Korea and Japan, to the Islamic world after the Battle of Talas (751 CE), where mills were established at Samarkand and Baghdad, and onward to Moorish Spain and Christian Europe by the 12th–13th centuries. In Europe, the demand created by Gutenberg's printing press (c. 1450) drove production, but handmade rag paper remained scarce until 19th-century inventions — the Fourdrinier continuous paper machine (1803) and wood-pulp processes (groundwood, then the kraft chemical process) — made cheap paper abundant and transformed publishing, education, and commerce.

Manufacture

Modern papermaking begins with pulp: wood chips are reduced mechanically or chemically (the kraft process dissolves lignin in an alkaline liquor) into cellulose fibres. The pulp is washed, bleached, and refined, then diluted to a slurry of about 1% fibre and sprayed onto a moving wire screen. Water drains away, the wet web is pressed between rollers, dried on steam-heated cylinders, and calendered to the desired finish. Fillers such as calcium carbonate and kaolin improve opacity and printability, while sizing agents control ink absorption. A large modern machine can produce a web several metres wide at speeds above 100 km/h.

Uses and environment

Paper's largest single use today is packaging (corrugated board and cartonboard), followed by printing and writing papers, tissue, and specialty grades including banknotes, filter paper, and electrical insulation. Paper is widely recycled — recovery rates exceed 60% in Europe — and recycled fibre is a major feedstock. Environmental concerns include deforestation and habitat loss where pulpwood is not sustainably managed, high water and energy use, and mill effluent; certification schemes such as FSC and the shift to plantation fibre address some of these. Despite predictions of the "paperless office," global production remains around 400 million tonnes per year.
