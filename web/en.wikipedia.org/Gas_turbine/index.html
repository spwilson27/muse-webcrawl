Gas turbine

A gas turbine, also called a combustion turbine, is a type of continuous-flow internal combustion engine. Its three main upstream components are a rotating gas compressor, a combustor, and a turbine on the same shaft. Air is compressed, mixed with fuel and burned, and the hot gases expand through the turbine, producing shaft power or thrust.

Operating principle

The gas turbine operates on the Brayton cycle: isentropic compression, constant-pressure heat addition, isentropic expansion, and constant-pressure heat rejection. Real machines achieve thermal efficiencies of roughly 35–40% in simple cycle and up to about 60% in combined-cycle power plants, where exhaust heat raises steam for a second turbine.

Applications

Gas turbines power nearly all modern aircraft as turbojet, turbofan, and turboprop engines; they generate electricity in peaking and combined-cycle plants; and they drive warships, tanks such as the M1 Abrams, and industrial compressors and pumps. Their high power-to-weight ratio and rapid start-up are key advantages.

History

Early concepts date to John Barber's 1791 patent. Frank Whittle and Hans von Ohain independently developed the turbojet in the 1930s, leading to the first jet flights in 1939. Industrial gas turbines for power generation followed after the Second World War.
