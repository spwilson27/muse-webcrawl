Zeppelin

A Zeppelin is a type of rigid airship named after the German inventor Ferdinand von Zeppelin, who pioneered rigid airship development at the beginning of the 20th century. Zeppelin's notions were first formulated in 1874 and developed in detail in 1893. They were patented in Germany in 1895 and in the United States in 1899. After the outstanding success of the Zeppelin design, the word zeppelin came to be commonly used to refer to all rigid airships.

Zeppelins were first flown commercially in 1910 by Deutsche Luftschiffahrts-AG (DELAG), the world's first airline in revenue service, which by mid-1914 had carried over 34,000 passengers without a serious incident. During the First World War, German Zeppelins were used for reconnaissance and for bombing raids over Britain and France. In the interwar period, giant passenger airships such as the Graf Zeppelin and Hindenburg operated transatlantic services.

Design

A Zeppelin's hull contains a rigid framework of aluminium alloy girders forming rings and longitudinal beams, covered by fabric, with separate internal gas cells filled with hydrogen (and, in American ships, helium). Engines were mounted in external gondolas, and the control car and passenger accommodation were built into or suspended below the hull.

Decline

The destruction by fire of the Hindenburg at Lakehurst, New Jersey, on 6 May 1937, together with the speed and economy of aeroplanes, ended the era of large passenger airships. A modern revival, the semi-rigid Zeppelin NT, has flown sightseeing and scientific missions since the 1990s.
