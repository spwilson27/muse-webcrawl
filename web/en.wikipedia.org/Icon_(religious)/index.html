Icon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An icon (from Greek eikōn, "image" or "likeness") is a religious work of art, most commonly a painting, in the cultures of the Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, and certain Eastern Catholic churches. Icons typically depict Jesus Christ, Mary, saints, or angels, and are used in private devotion and public worship as aids to prayer and as objects of veneration.

Theology and use

Orthodox theology holds that the honour shown to an icon passes to its prototype—the person depicted—and that icons are a legitimate consequence of the Incarnation, in which God became visible in human form. The Seventh Ecumenical Council (Nicaea II, 787) affirmed the veneration of icons and distinguished it from the worship due to God alone. Icons are typically painted on wood panels in egg tempera, following traditional prototypes and iconographic conventions such as inverse perspective.

History

Devotional images are attested from the earliest Christian centuries; panel icons survive from the 6th and 7th centuries, notably at Saint Catherine's Monastery on Mount Sinai. The Byzantine Iconoclasm controversies of the 8th and 9th centuries saw imperial bans on religious images, ending with the restoration of icons in 843, celebrated as the Triumph of Orthodoxy. Icon painting spread from Byzantium to the Slavic world, where Russian schools—including the masters Theophanes the Greek and Andrei Rublev, whose Trinity is among the most famous icons—produced distinctive traditions.

Forms

Icons appear on church iconostases (icon screens), as processional and miracle-working images, in domestic "beautiful corners," and in miniature forms such as travelling triptychs. Metal covers called oklads or rizas often protect painted panels.
