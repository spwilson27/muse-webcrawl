The Shuttle–Mir program was a collaborative spaceflight programme between Russia and the United States, carried out between 1994 and 1998. Designated "Phase One" of the International Space Station programme, it joined the American Space Shuttle with the Russian space station Mir: eleven Space Shuttle missions flew to Mir (nine docking), seven American astronauts lived aboard Mir for long-duration stays totalling almost 1,000 days, and Russian cosmonauts flew on the Shuttle. The programme was born of post-Cold War diplomacy and an intergovernmental agreement of 1992–93, and Russia was paid some $400 million by NASA for the flights and experience.

Missions and incidents

The programme began with a close rendezvous (but no docking) by Discovery on STS-63 in February 1995, after cosmonaut Sergei Krikalev had flown on STS-60 in 1994 and astronaut Norman Thagard had launched to Mir aboard a Soyuz in March 1995 — the first American to fly on a Russian vehicle. The first docking, by Atlantis on STS-71 in June 1995, created for a time the largest spacecraft ever assembled, and a Shuttle later delivered a Russian-built docking module (STS-74). The long-duration stays aboard the ageing Mir exposed NASA to real station operations — and to real emergencies: in 1997 astronaut Michael Foale's stay saw a fire in an oxygen generator (February 1997) and a collision between the Progress M-34 cargo ship and the Spektr module (June 1997), which depressurised Spektr and nearly forced evacuation. Jerry Linenga's and Foale's accounts, and the final stay of Andy Thomas in 1998, fed debate in the U.S. Congress over the programme's safety and value.

Legacy

Despite the controversies, Shuttle–Mir taught the two space programmes to work together — joint mission control, docking hardware (the APAS-89/95 androgynous system), long-duration medical data, and crew-exchange experience — all directly applied to the International Space Station, whose first module was launched in November 1998, months after the final Shuttle–Mir docking.
