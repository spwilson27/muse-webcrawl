Death squad

A death squad is an armed group whose primary activity is carrying out extrajudicial killings or enforced disappearances as part of political repression, genocide, ethnic cleansing, or revolutionary terror. Death squads are often associated with the security forces of a state, with paramilitary organizations, or with organized crime, but they usually operate covertly so that their sponsors can deny responsibility.

History

The term gained prominence in Latin America during the Cold War. In El Salvador, death squads linked to the military and landowner elites murdered tens of thousands during the civil war of 1979–1992, including Archbishop Óscar Romero in 1980. Similar organizations operated in Guatemala, Honduras, Colombia, Brazil, and Argentina, where the "dirty war" of 1976–1983 saw thousands disappeared by state forces. Death squads have also been documented in settings as varied as Nazi Germany's Einsatzgruppen, Francoist Spain, Northern Ireland, Iraq, and the Philippines.

Characteristics

Death squads typically target perceived opponents — activists, journalists, trade unionists, clergy, suspected insurgents, or members of marginalized groups — using abduction, torture, murder, and clandestine burial to spread fear while preserving the authorities' plausible deniability. Their activity is considered a grave violation of human rights and international humanitarian law, and truth commissions in several countries have documented their crimes and links to state institutions.
