Bobcat

The bobcat (Lynx rufus), also known as the red lynx, is one of the four extant species within the medium-sized wild cat genus Lynx. Native to North America, it ranges from southern Canada through most of the contiguous United States to Oaxaca in Mexico. It is listed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List since 2002, due to its wide distribution and large population.

Description

The bobcat resembles other species of the midsize Lynx genus, but is on average the smallest of the four. Its coat is variable, though generally tan to grayish-brown, with black stripes on the body and dark bars on the forelegs and tail. Its spotted patterning acts as camouflage. The ears are black-tipped and pointed, with short, black tufts. The species is named for its short, "bobbed" tail, which averages about 10 cm in length and bears a white underside spot visible in flight.

Adults range from about 47.5 to 125 cm in length including the tail, stand 30 to 60 cm at the shoulder, and typically weigh between 6.4 and 18.3 kg.

Habitat and behavior

The bobcat is an adaptable predator inhabiting wooded areas, semidesert, urban edges, forest edges, and swampland. It is primarily nocturnal and solitary, marking its territory with claw marks and scent deposits. Its diet consists mainly of rabbits and hares, but it also hunts insects, chickens and other birds, geese, rodents, and deer.

Reproduction

Breeding generally occurs in late winter or early spring. After a gestation of about 60–70 days, the female gives birth to a litter of one to six kittens, which stay with their mother for nine to twelve months.
