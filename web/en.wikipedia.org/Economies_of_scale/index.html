Economies of scale

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The long-run average cost curve typically falls with output before diseconomies of scale set in.

In microeconomics, economies of scale are the cost advantages that enterprises obtain due to their scale of operation, with cost per unit of output decreasing with increasing scale. Economies of scale arise from the inverse relationship between the quantity produced and per-unit fixed costs — the greater the quantity of a good produced, the lower the per-unit fixed cost, because these costs are spread over a larger number of units.

Sources

Technical economies arise from the physical efficiency of larger plant and machinery. Managerial and organisational economies come from specialisation of labour and management. Purchasing and marketing economies reflect bulk-buying discounts and the spreading of advertising costs, while financial economies arise because larger firms typically borrow at lower interest rates.

Diseconomies of scale

Beyond an optimal size, average costs may begin to rise — diseconomies of scale — because of communication problems, slower decision-making, loss of managerial control and worker alienation in very large organisations.

External economies

External economies of scale occur when an entire industry grows: firms benefit from skilled local labour pools, specialised suppliers and knowledge spillovers, as in industrial clusters such as Silicon Valley. The related concept of economies of scope concerns cost savings from producing a variety of related products together.
