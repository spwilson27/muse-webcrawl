Maximilien Robespierre

Maximilien François Marie Isidore de Robespierre (6 May 1758 – 28 July 1794) was a French lawyer and statesman, widely regarded as one of the most influential and controversial figures of the French Revolution. A member of the Jacobin Club and of the Committee of Public Safety, he became the dominant political figure during the Reign of Terror (1793–94), in which thousands of alleged enemies of the Revolution were executed. Nicknamed "the Incorruptible" for his refusal of bribes and his austere devotion to principle, he was overthrown and guillotined in the Thermidorian Reaction of July 1794.

Early life and politics

Born in Arras, Robespierre was orphaned young and educated on a scholarship at the prestigious Lycée Louis-le-Grand in Paris, where he absorbed classical republicanism and the philosophy of Jean-Jacques Rousseau. Trained as a lawyer, he returned to Arras and won election to the Estates-General of 1789 for the Third Estate. In the National Constituent Assembly he argued for universal male suffrage, the abolition of the death penalty (an irony given his later career), freedom of the press and the rights of Jews and actors, and against the royal veto and colonial slavery.

Rise to power

As the Revolution radicalised, Robespierre defended the Parisian sans-culottes and opposed the war with Austria declared in 1792, fearing it would empower generals or the court. After the fall of the monarchy he sat in the National Convention, led the Montagnard faction against the Girondins, and in July 1793 joined the Committee of Public Safety, the executive body directing the war effort and internal security. Under the emergency regime of the Terror, revolutionary tribunals and the Law of Suspects sent some 17,000 people to official execution. Robespierre justified terror as "an emanation of virtue" in defence of the Republic of virtue he envisioned, while also supporting social measures such as price controls (the General Maximum), abolition of slavery in the colonies (1794), and a deistic state religion, the Cult of the Supreme Being, whose festival he presided over in June 1794.

Fall and death

The accelerating executions of the Law of 22 Prairial and Robespierre's threats against unnamed deputies convinced the Convention that no one was safe. On 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794) he was shouted down and arrested; released by supporters, he was retaken at the Hôtel de Ville with a shattered jaw (from a pistol shot, whether self-inflicted or fired by a gendarme remains disputed) and guillotined the next day with 21 associates, including Saint-Just and Couthon. Historians continue to debate his legacy: tyrant and architect of terror to some, sincere democratic idealist overtaken by civil war and invasion to others.
