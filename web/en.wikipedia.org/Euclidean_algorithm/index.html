Euclidean algorithm

In mathematics, the Euclidean algorithm is an efficient method for computing the greatest common divisor (GCD) of two integers, the largest number that divides them both without remainder. It is named after the ancient Greek mathematician Euclid, who first described it in Books VII and X of his Elements (c. 300 BC).

Principle

The algorithm is based on the principle that the greatest common divisor of two numbers does not change if the larger number is replaced by its difference with the smaller number, or more efficiently, by the remainder of their division. Repeatedly replacing the larger number by the remainder eventually yields a remainder of zero; the last non-zero remainder is the GCD.

For example, gcd(1071, 462): 1071 = 2 × 462 + 147; then 462 = 3 × 147 + 21; then 147 = 7 × 21 + 0. Hence gcd(1071, 462) = 21.

Procedure

function gcd(a, b)
    while b ≠ 0
        (a, b) := (b, a mod b)
    return a

Extended Euclidean algorithm

The extended Euclidean algorithm additionally computes the Bézout coefficients — integers x and y such that ax + by = gcd(a, b). This is essential in number theory and cryptography, for example for computing modular multiplicative inverses used in the RSA cryptosystem.

History and applications

The algorithm is one of the oldest algorithms still in common use. It appears in Euclid's Elements and in ancient Chinese and Indian mathematics in related forms. Gabriel Lamé proved in 1844 that the number of steps is at most five times the number of decimal digits of the smaller number. Today the algorithm is used in computer algebra systems, cryptography, and simplifying fractions.
