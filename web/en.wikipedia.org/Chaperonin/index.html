Chaperonin

The barrel-shaped GroEL chaperonin
Chaperonin
TypeMolecular chaperone
ExamplesGroEL/GroES, Hsp60, CCT/TRiC
FunctionATP-dependent protein folding

Chaperonins are a family of large, barrel-shaped protein complexes that assist the folding of other proteins inside the cell. They belong to the broader class of molecular chaperones. A chaperonin provides an isolated chamber in which an unfolded or misfolded polypeptide can fold without aggregating with other proteins.

Structure and mechanism

The best-studied chaperonin is the bacterial GroEL/GroES system. GroEL consists of two stacked rings of seven subunits each, forming a hollow cylinder. A substrate protein binds inside the cavity; the co-chaperonin GroES then caps the chamber, and hydrolysis of ATP drives conformational changes that allow the substrate to fold before being released. Eukaryotic cells possess the related group II chaperonin CCT (also called TRiC), which folds proteins such as actin and tubulin.

Classification

Group I chaperonins are found in bacteria and in organelles of endosymbiotic origin (mitochondria and chloroplasts) and require a detachable lid cofactor. Group II chaperonins occur in archaea and the eukaryotic cytosol and have a built-in lid formed by protrusions of the subunits.
