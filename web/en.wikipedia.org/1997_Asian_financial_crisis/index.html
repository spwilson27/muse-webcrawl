1997 Asian financial crisis

PeriodJuly 1997 – 1998 (with effects into 1999)
OriginThailand (collapse of the baht)
Most affectedThailand, Indonesia, South Korea, Malaysia, Philippines
Key eventsBaht floated 2 July 1997; IMF programmes for Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea; fall of Suharto (May 1998)

The 1997 Asian financial crisis was a period of financial turmoil that gripped much of East and Southeast Asia during the late 1990s. It began on 2 July 1997 when Thailand, after exhausting its foreign reserves defending a fixed exchange rate, floated the baht, which promptly collapsed. The crisis spread rapidly through the region, causing currency devaluations, stock market crashes, banking failures and sharp economic contractions in Indonesia, South Korea, Malaysia, the Philippines and elsewhere.

Causes

During the early 1990s the "Asian Tiger" economies attracted massive inflows of foreign capital, fuelled by high growth and pegged exchange rates that encouraged borrowing in U.S. dollars. The result was large current-account deficits, asset bubbles in property and equities, weak banking supervision, and heavy short-term foreign debt — much of it unhedged. When export growth slowed in 1996 and the dollar strengthened, pressure mounted on the pegs. Speculative attacks, including by hedge funds, forced Thailand to abandon its peg.

Spread and IMF response

Contagion hit the Philippines, Malaysia and Indonesia within weeks; by autumn 1997 the crisis had reached South Korea, whose conglomerates (chaebols) carried enormous debts. The International Monetary Fund assembled rescue packages of about $17 billion for Thailand, $40 billion for Indonesia and $58 billion for South Korea — then the largest in its history — conditioned on high interest rates, fiscal austerity and structural reforms. The programmes were controversial: critics such as Joseph Stiglitz argued the austerity deepened the recessions, while Malaysia under Mahathir Mohamad rejected IMF help, imposed capital controls in September 1998, and recovered comparably quickly.

Consequences

Indonesia suffered the worst collapse: the rupiah lost over 80 per cent of its value, GDP shrank by about 13 per cent in 1998, and rioting in May 1998 forced President Suharto to resign after 32 years in power, ushering in democratic reform. Regional GDP contracted sharply, millions fell into poverty, and the crisis contributed to financial turmoil in Russia (1998) and Brazil (1999). Longer-term legacies included Asian central banks' massive accumulation of foreign-exchange reserves, regional cooperation through the Chiang Mai Initiative, and lasting debate about capital controls and the IMF's crisis prescriptions.
