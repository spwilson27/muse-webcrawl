First Jewish–Roman War

The First Jewish–Roman War (66–74 CE), sometimes called the Great Jewish Revolt (Hebrew: המרד הגדול, ha-Mered Ha-Gadol) or The Jewish War, was the first of three major rebellions by the Jews against the Roman Empire fought in Roman-controlled Judaea, resulting in the destruction of Jewish towns, the displacement of its people and the appropriation of land for Roman military use, as well as the destruction of the Jewish Temple and polity.

Background

Following Pompey's conquest of the East in 63 BCE, Judaea came under Roman domination, first as a client kingdom under Herod the Great and later, from 6 CE, as a Roman province. Tensions grew over Roman taxation, the insensitive conduct of equestrian governors, and clashes between Jewish and Hellenised populations. The crucifixion of rebels, the procurator Gessius Florus's seizure of funds from the Temple treasury in Jerusalem in 66 CE, and a subsequent massacre of Jews in the city ignited open revolt.

Course of the war

The rebels destroyed the Roman garrison in Jerusalem, and an initial punitive expedition under Cestius Gallus, governor of Syria, was ambushed and routed at the Battle of Beth Horon in November 66, losing some 5,800 men and the legionary eagle of Legio XII Fulminata. A Jewish provisional government was formed in Jerusalem, which minted coins and appointed commanders for the districts.

The emperor Nero appointed the general Vespasian to suppress the revolt. With three legions and numerous auxiliaries — perhaps 60,000 men — Vespasian systematically reconquered Galilee in 67 CE, capturing the fortified towns of Jotapata (after a 47-day siege) and Gamla. The Jewish commander at Jotapata, Josephus, surrendered and became a Roman client and later the war's historian. Jewish resistance was weakened throughout by vicious internecine fighting between rival factions in Jerusalem: the Zealots, the forces of Simon Bar Giora, and the followers of John of Giscala.

Siege of Jerusalem

After the Year of the Four Emperors (69 CE), Vespasian returned to Rome as emperor, leaving his son Titus to conclude the war. Titus besieged Jerusalem in the spring of 70 CE with four legions. After months of fighting and starvation within the city, Roman forces breached the walls, burned the city, and destroyed the Second Temple. The treasures of the Temple — including the Menorah and silver trumpets — were carried to Rome and famously depicted on the Arch of Titus. Josephus claimed over a million people perished in the siege, a figure modern historians regard as exaggerated, though casualties were unquestionably enormous.

Masada and aftermath

The last stronghold, the mountain fortress of Masada overlooking the Dead Sea, held out under Eleazar ben Ya'ir and his Sicarii until 73 or 74 CE. After the Romans built a massive assault ramp, the defenders — according to Josephus — died by collective suicide rather than surrender. The war devastated Judaea: Jerusalem was razed, the Temple tax (the Fiscus Judaicus) was redirected to Rome, and a permanent legionary garrison (Legio X Fretensis) was stationed in the province. The revolt and its failure shaped Rabbinic Judaism, which reconstituted itself at Yavneh under Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai, and set the stage for later revolts, culminating in the Bar Kokhba revolt of 132–136 CE.

Sources

The principal surviving account is The Jewish War by Flavius Josephus, written in Greek under Flavian patronage; his reliability and pro-Roman bias remain subjects of scholarly debate. Tacitus also treats the war briefly in his Histories.
