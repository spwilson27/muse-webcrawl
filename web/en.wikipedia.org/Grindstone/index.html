Grindstone

A grindstone is a round sharpening stone used for grinding or sharpening ferrous tools, mounted in a frame (a trough) and rotated by hand, foot treadle, or motor. The wheel is typically made of a naturally occurring fine-grained sandstone or, in modern versions, an artificial abrasive such as silicon carbide or aluminium oxide bonded into a wheel. Water is commonly dripped onto the stone to cool the edge being sharpened, wash away debris, and prevent overheating that would soften the steel.

Use and construction

The tool to be sharpened — an axe, knife, scythe, chisel, or drill bit — is held against the slowly rotating face of the wheel so that the abrasive grains remove metal and restore a keen edge. Large treadle grindstones were a fixture of farms and workshops for centuries, and travelling knife-grinders carried smaller versions from village to village. The work demanded care: a wheel run too dry or too fast could draw the temper from a blade, and wheels with flaws could shatter dangerously at speed, which led to traditional tests of "ringing" a stone before mounting.

Grindstone grits are graded from coarse (for re-profiling damaged edges) to fine (for finishing). In industrial settings the term extends to the large grinding wheels used in machining, although those are governed by their own standards and safety codes.

In language and culture

The grindstone figures in the idiom "keep one's nose to the grindstone", meaning to work hard and steadily, an image drawn from the bent posture of the sharpener leaning over the wheel. Historically, grindstones were quarried in many regions, and exhausted or broken stones were often reused as building material or doorsteps.
