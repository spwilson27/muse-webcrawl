Auction theory

Auction theory is the branch of game theory and applied economics that studies how people act in auction markets and how auction rules affect outcomes — the allocation of goods and the prices paid. It analyses bidding strategies, revenue, efficiency and optimal auction design, and has guided the sale of assets from radio spectrum to treasury bonds and electricity.

Standard formats

Four classic formats dominate: the open ascending (English) auction; the descending (Dutch) auction; the first-price sealed-bid auction; and the second-price sealed-bid (Vickrey) auction, in which the winner pays the second-highest bid, making truthful bidding a dominant strategy. William Vickrey's foundational 1961 paper proved, under symmetric independent private values, the revenue equivalence theorem: all four formats yield the seller the same expected revenue.

Key concepts

Important ideas include the winner's curse in common-value auctions, where the highest bidder tends to overestimate the item's worth; the linkage principle, by which revealing information raises revenue; and optimal reserve prices (Myerson, 1981). Multi-object extensions include simultaneous ascending auctions and combinatorial (package) bidding.

Applications

Auction theory was famously applied to the 1994 US FCC spectrum auctions, designed by economists including Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson, generating billions of dollars while allocating licences efficiently. Spectrum auctions worldwide have since raised hundreds of billions. Online advertising auctions, electricity markets, emissions permits and procurement all draw on the field. Milgrom and Wilson received the 2020 Nobel Prize in Economics for their work on auction theory and new auction formats.
