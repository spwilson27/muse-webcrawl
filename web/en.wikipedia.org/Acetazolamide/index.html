Acetazolamide

Acetazolamide, sold under the trade name Diamox among others, is a medication used to treat glaucoma, epilepsy, acute mountain sickness, periodic paralysis, idiopathic intracranial hypertension (raised brain pressure of unclear cause), heart failure and altitude sickness. It may be used long term for the treatment of open angle glaucoma and short term for acute angle closure glaucoma until surgery can be carried out. It is taken by mouth or injection into a vein.

Acetazolamide is a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, and it is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Mechanism of action

By inhibiting the enzyme carbonic anhydrase, acetazolamide reduces the reabsorption of bicarbonate, sodium and water in the proximal tubule of the kidney, producing a mild diuresis and a metabolic acidosis. In the eye, it decreases the production of aqueous humour, lowering intraocular pressure. The induced metabolic acidosis stimulates ventilation, which is the basis of its use in preventing and treating acute mountain sickness.

Side effects

Common side effects include numbness and tingling in the fingers and toes (paraesthesia), loss of appetite, altered taste (especially for carbonated drinks), vomiting, diarrhoea, and increased urination. More serious effects can include electrolyte disturbances such as low blood potassium, metabolic acidosis, and kidney stones. It is a sulfonamide, and allergic reactions can occur in people with sulfa allergy. Use is generally not recommended during pregnancy or in significant kidney or liver disease.

History

Acetazolamide was introduced into medicine in 1952, arising from the observation that sulfonamide antibiotics caused an alkaline diuresis, which led to the development of dedicated carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.
