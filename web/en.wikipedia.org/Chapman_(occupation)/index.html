Chapman (occupation)

A chapman carrying his pack of wares
Chapman
OccupationItinerant trader, pedlar
EtymologyOld English cēapmann, "tradesman"
RelatedPedlar, hawker, chapbook

A chapman was an itinerant dealer or hawker in early modern Britain, a travelling merchant who sold goods from a pack carried on his back or by horse. The word derives from the Old English cēapmann, from cēap ("barter, business, a purchase") and mann ("man"), and survives in the modern surname Chapman.

Trade and chapbooks

Chapmen travelled between towns, fairs, and villages selling small wares such as ribbons, pins, laces, and printed material. They were the principal distributors of chapbooks — cheap pocket-sized pamphlets containing ballads, folktales, religious tracts, and almanacs — which took their name from the chapmen who sold them. In Scotland, chapmen formed incorporated societies in some counties and held their own fairs and courts.

Decline

The occupation declined during the 18th and 19th centuries as shops spread, transport improved, and cheap print was distributed through booksellers and newsagents. Licensing laws also increasingly regulated itinerant sellers, who were frequently viewed with suspicion by settled communities.
