Doctors' trial

United States of America v. Karl Brandt, et al.
Court: Military Tribunal I, Nuremberg
Dates: 9 December 1946 – 20 August 1947
Defendants: 23 (20 physicians, 3 officials)
Outcome: 7 death sentences, 9 prison terms, 7 acquittals

The Doctors' trial was the first of the twelve Nuremberg Military Tribunals for war crimes held by the United States in its occupation zone in Germany after the end of World War II. Officially United States of America v. Karl Brandt, et al., the trial prosecuted 23 defendants, most of them physicians, accused of involvement in Nazi human experimentation and the mass murder of people in the "euthanasia" programme (Aktion T4).

Indictment

The defendants were charged with war crimes and crimes against humanity, including performing medical experiments on concentration camp prisoners without their consent — high-altitude, freezing, malaria, seawater, sulfanilamide and bone-regeneration experiments among them — and with participating in the extermination of concentration camp inmates and the murder of disabled Germans under the T4 programme.

Verdicts

Seven defendants, including Karl Brandt (Hitler's personal physician) and Karl Gebhardt, were sentenced to death and hanged at Landsberg Prison on 2 June 1948. Nine received prison sentences ranging from ten years to life, and seven were acquitted. (The tribunal's presiding judge was Walter B. Beals.)

The Nuremberg Code

The trial's great legacy is the Nuremberg Code, a set of ten principles for ethical human experimentation set out in the judgment. The Code established voluntary, informed consent as absolutely essential and has profoundly influenced research ethics, medical law, and later frameworks such as the Declaration of Helsinki — central to modern protections against disease research abuses.
