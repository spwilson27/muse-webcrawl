Cardiopulmonary resuscitation

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is an emergency procedure performed when the heart stops beating (cardiac arrest). It combines chest compressions — which manually circulate blood to the brain and heart — with rescue breathing in trained rescuers. Immediate CPR can double or triple the chance of survival, especially when combined with early defibrillation.

Technique

For an adult, rescuers compress the centre of the chest at a rate of 100–120 per minute to a depth of 5–6 cm, allowing full recoil. Trained rescuers give cycles of 30 compressions to 2 breaths; for untrained bystanders, guidelines recommend compression-only ("hands-only") CPR. An automated external defibrillator (AED) should be attached as soon as available and its prompts followed.

Chain of survival

CPR is one link in the "chain of survival": recognition of arrest and calling emergency services, immediate CPR, rapid defibrillation, advanced resuscitation by professionals, and post-arrest care. Survival falls by roughly 10% for each minute without CPR and defibrillation.

History

Modern CPR was developed around 1960, when Kouwenhoven, Jude, and Knickerbocker demonstrated closed-chest cardiac massage, complementing the mouth-to-mouth ventilation described by Safar and Elam. Standardised training by organisations such as the American Heart Association and the European Resuscitation Council has since spread the technique worldwide.
