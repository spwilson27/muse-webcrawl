The aardvark (Orycteropus afer) is a medium-sized, burrowing, nocturnal mammal native to Africa. It is the only living species of the order Tubulidentata, named for its unique tube-shaped teeth, which lack enamel and pulp cavities and are worn down and regrown continuously. The name "aardvark" comes from Afrikaans, meaning "earth pig", after its pig-like snout and burrowing habits; it is also called the antbear or anteater, though it is not closely related to South American anteaters.

Description and behaviour

Aardvarks weigh typically 60 to 130 kilograms and measure up to about 130 cm in body length, with a long head, donkey-like ears, an arched back and sparse, yellowish-grey hair. Their sense of smell is exceptionally acute — they possess more turbinate bones than any other mammal — and they use their keen nose and hearing to find termites and ants. With powerful spade-like claws they can dig into concrete-hard termite mounds within minutes, sealing their nostrils against dust while feeding; a single animal may eat tens of thousands of insects in a night, extending a sticky tongue up to 30 cm long.

Ecology

Aardvarks live throughout sub-Saharan Africa in grassland and savanna, digging burrows that may be used by many other species, from warthogs to aardwolves, making them an important ecosystem engineer. They are mostly solitary, meeting only to mate; females give birth to a single cub after a gestation of about seven months. Though listed as Least Concern, populations are thought to be declining in places because of habitat loss and drought affecting their invertebrate prey.
