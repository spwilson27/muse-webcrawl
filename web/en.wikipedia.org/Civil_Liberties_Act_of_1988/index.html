Civil Liberties Act of 1988

Full title: An act to implement recommendations of the Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians
Enacted: August 10, 1988
Signed by: President Ronald Reagan
Public Law: 100-383

The Civil Liberties Act of 1988 is a United States federal law that granted reparations to Japanese Americans who had been interned by the United States government during World War II. The act was signed into law by President Ronald Reagan on August 10, 1988.

The legislation offered a formal apology on behalf of the United States government and provided a tax-free payment of $20,000 to each surviving internee who had been forcibly relocated and incarcerated under Executive Order 9066, issued by President Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1942. Roughly 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, about two-thirds of them American citizens, had been removed from their homes on the West Coast and confined in camps.

Background

The act grew out of the work of the Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians (CWRIC), established by Congress in 1980. The commission's 1983 report, Personal Justice Denied, concluded that the internment had been the product of "race prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership" rather than military necessity, and recommended an official apology and individual payments.

Provisions

The act acknowledged the fundamental injustice of the evacuation, relocation, and internment; apologized on behalf of the people of the United States; established a public education fund; and authorized restitution payments to eligible individuals. Payments began in 1990, accompanied by a signed letter of apology from President George H. W. Bush. In total, more than 82,000 former internees received redress payments.
