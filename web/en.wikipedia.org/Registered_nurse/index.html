Registered nurse

Registered nurses form the largest group of health professionals in many countries.

A registered nurse (RN) is a nurse who has graduated from a nursing programme and met the requirements for a nursing licence outlined by a government-authorised regulatory body. Registered nurses provide and coordinate patient care, educate patients and the public about health conditions, and provide advice and emotional support to patients and their families.

Education and licensing

Entry routes vary by country. In the United States, candidates complete an Associate Degree in Nursing, a hospital diploma, or a Bachelor of Science in Nursing, and must pass the NCLEX-RN examination before licensure by a state board of nursing. In the United Kingdom, registered nurses qualify through NMC-approved degree programmes and are entered on the Nursing and Midwifery Council register, typically in one of four fields: adult, child, mental health or learning disabilities nursing.

Scope of practice

RNs assess patients, record symptoms and medical histories, administer medicines and treatments, operate medical equipment, perform diagnostic tests and interpret results, and teach patients how to manage illness or injury after treatment. They work in hospitals, physicians' offices, schools, public health, home health care and the military, and may specialise in areas such as critical care, paediatrics, oncology or emergency nursing. Advanced practice registered nurses — including nurse practitioners, clinical nurse specialists, nurse anaesthetists and nurse midwives — hold graduate degrees and an expanded scope.

History

Modern professional nursing emerged in the nineteenth century under reformers such as Florence Nightingale. The first registration systems appeared in the early twentieth century — New Zealand passed the Nurses Registration Act in 1901 — and the title "registered nurse" has since become the standard professional designation in much of the English-speaking world.
