A developed country, or advanced economy, is a sovereign state that has a high quality of life, a developed economy and advanced technological infrastructure relative to other less industrialised nations. Common criteria include a high per-capita income, a "very high" score on the Human Development Index (HDI), an advanced post-industrial economy dominated by the service sector, and developed infrastructure and institutions. Countries not fitting these criteria are classified as developing countries or least developed countries.

Definitions and measures

There is no single agreed definition. The International Monetary Fund classifies a set of "advanced economies" (including the United States, Canada, most of Western Europe, Japan, Australia, New Zealand, South Korea and others) based on factors such as export diversification, integration into the global financial system and per-capita income. The World Bank labels economies with a gross national income per capita above a high threshold (about US$14,000 in the mid-2020s) as "high income." The United Nations Development Programme's HDI combines life expectancy, education and income; countries scoring 0.800 or above have "very high human development."

Characteristics

  • High GDP per capita and high standards of living
  • Service-sector-dominated, formerly industrial economies
  • Low infant mortality and long life expectancy
  • High literacy and widespread tertiary education
  • Developed physical infrastructure — transport, energy, telecommunications
  • Stable, accountable political institutions and rule of law
  • Low population growth, often below-replacement fertility

Terminology

Related terms include "First World" (a Cold War-era political designation), "the North" in the North–South divide, "industrialised country," and "more economically developed country" (MEDC). The developed/developing dichotomy has been criticised as simplistic: the World Bank announced in 2016 that it would phase out "developing country" as an analytical category owing to its lack of precision, and scholars note wide variation within each group. Some rapidly industrialising states — the "newly industrialised countries" or emerging markets such as China, Brazil, India and Mexico — sit between the categories.

Graduation and debate

Countries can "graduate" between categories: South Korea, Singapore, Taiwan and Israel, once considered developing, are now routinely classified as advanced economies. Conversely, economic crises have raised questions about whether some countries' status can slip. The classification has practical consequences, affecting trade preferences, development assistance eligibility and climate-negotiation obligations under the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities.
