Regulation and licensure in engineering

Regulation and licensure in engineering is established by various jurisdictions of the world to encourage public welfare, safety, well-being and other interests of the general public, and to define the licensure process through which an engineer becomes authorised to provide professional services to the public.

Licensure

In the United States, engineering licensure is regulated at the state level. The typical path involves earning an accredited engineering degree (usually through an ABET-accredited programme), passing the Fundamentals of Engineering (FE) examination to become an Engineer in Training (EIT) or Engineer Intern (EI), gaining several years of supervised professional experience, and passing the Principles and Practice of Engineering (PE) examination. Licensed engineers may use the post-nominal PE and are entitled to seal (stamp) engineering drawings and documents for public works.

In Canada, the title "Professional Engineer" (P.Eng) is protected by law and regulated by provincial and territorial associations such as Professional Engineers Ontario. In the United Kingdom, the Engineering Council grants the titles of Chartered Engineer (CEng), Incorporated Engineer (IEng) and Engineering Technician (EngTech), although the generic title "engineer" is not itself restricted. Similar chartered or registered statuses exist in Australia (CPEng), India, South Africa (Pr Eng) and many other countries; the Washington Accord provides international recognition of accredited engineering degrees.

Scope

Licensure is generally mandatory for engineers who offer services directly to the public, approve designs, or take responsibility for work affecting public safety — for example in civil, structural and much consulting practice. Many engineers working in industry under the so-called "industrial exemption" in the United States do not require licensure, because their employer's products are covered by other regulatory frameworks.

Professional ethics

Licensing bodies maintain codes of ethics obliging engineers to hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public, to practise only in areas of their competence, and to act as faithful agents of their clients and employers.
