Pointillism is a technique of painting in which small, distinct dots of colour are applied in patterns to form an image. Georges Seurat and Paul Signac developed the technique in 1886, branching from Impressionism. The term was coined by art critics in the late 1880s — initially mockingly — to describe their work, in which colours are juxtaposed rather than mixed on the palette, relying on the eye and mind of the viewer to blend the colour spots into a fuller range of tones. It is a founding technique of Neo-Impressionism, also known as Divisionism or Chromoluminarism.

Technique and theory

The Pointillists drew on nineteenth-century colour science, particularly Michel Eugène Chevreul's law of simultaneous contrast and Ogden Rood's Modern Chromatics, which held that pigments placed side by side are perceived as mixed at a distance, and that optical mixture produces greater luminosity than mixture on the palette. Seurat's masterpiece, A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte (1884–86), exhibited in 1886, announced the method: millions of tiny, even touches of complementary and contrasting colour build the scene without traditional chiaroscuro.

History

After Seurat's early death in 1891, Signac became the movement's theorist and organiser, codifying its principles in From Eugène Delacroix to Neo-Impressionism (1899). The style spread to Belgium (Théo van Rysselberghe), Italy (where it fed into Divisionism and Futurism) and the Netherlands. In the early 1900s the strict dots loosened into larger, mosaic-like strokes; the young Henri Matisse's experiments with Signac at Saint-Tropez in 1904 helped launch Fauvism. Although sometimes criticised for a mechanical quality, Pointillism is regarded as a pivotal attempt to give painting a rational, scientific basis.
