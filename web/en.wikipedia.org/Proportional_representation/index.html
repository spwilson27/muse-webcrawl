Proportional representation

Proportional representation (PR) characterises a class of electoral systems designed so that the composition of an elected body reflects, as closely as practicable, the overall distribution of votes cast for parties or groups. If a party wins 30 percent of the vote, it receives roughly 30 percent of the seats. PR stands in contrast to majoritarian and plurality systems, such as first-past-the-post, in which the leading candidate in each district wins and parties' national seat shares can diverge widely from their vote shares. Most democracies in the world today use some form of proportional representation for their national legislatures.

Main forms

Party-list proportional representation

In party-list PR, voters choose a party (or, in open-list systems, candidates within a party list), and seats are allocated to parties in proportion to their votes, then filled from ranked candidate lists. Seats are distributed within districts or nationally by an apportionment formula: the D'Hondt method, which tends to favour larger parties, or the Sainte-Laguë method, which is more neutral to party size. Many countries impose a legal threshold — commonly 4 or 5 percent of the national vote — that a party must cross to gain seats, in order to limit extreme fragmentation.

Single transferable vote

The single transferable vote (STV) uses multi-member districts and ranked ballots. Candidates reaching a quota — usually the Droop quota — are elected, and surplus or eliminated candidates' votes transfer to the voter's next preference. STV achieves proportionality while keeping the vote centred on individual candidates rather than parties; it is used in Ireland, Malta, and for the Australian Senate.

Mixed systems

Mixed-member proportional representation (MMP), used in Germany and New Zealand, combines district seats with compensatory list seats so that each party's total matches its share of the party vote. Parallel voting (MMM) is a related mixed system without the compensatory, proportional overall result.

History

The ideas behind PR were developed in the nineteenth century: Thomas Hare and John Stuart Mill championed STV in Britain, while list systems with proportional allocation were pioneered in Belgium (adopted 1899, associated with Victor D'Hondt) and spread through Europe after the First World War as suffrage expanded. The adoption of PR is often linked to the rise of mass parties and to efforts to give growing socialist movements parliamentary representation in proportion to their support.

Advantages and disadvantages

Supporters argue that PR wastes fewer votes, produces legislatures that mirror the electorate, encourages higher turnout, improves representation of women and minorities, and broadens policy debate. Critics point to coalition governments and possible instability, the weakening of the link between a representative and a single geographic constituency, the power of party organisations over closed candidate lists, and the possibility that small extremist parties gain a foothold — objections that thresholds and open lists are designed to mitigate. Empirical research generally finds PR systems associated with somewhat higher turnout and greater voter satisfaction, and with more frequent coalition government than plurality systems.
