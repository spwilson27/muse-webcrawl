Eisenmenger syndrome

Eisenmenger syndrome
SpecialtyCardiology
SymptomsCyanosis, breathlessness, fatigue, clubbing
CauseUnrepaired congenital heart defect with left-to-right shunt
Named afterVictor Eisenmenger

Eisenmenger syndrome is a condition in which a long-standing congenital heart defect with a left-to-right shunt — such as a large ventricular septal defect, atrial septal defect or patent ductus arteriosus — causes pulmonary vascular disease and pulmonary hypertension severe enough that the shunt reverses to right-to-left, producing cyanosis.

Pathophysiology

Chronic exposure of the pulmonary circulation to increased blood flow and pressure leads to irreversible remodelling and obstruction of the pulmonary vessels. As pulmonary vascular resistance rises above systemic resistance, deoxygenated blood bypasses the lungs, causing systemic arterial desaturation, secondary erythrocytosis and multi-organ complications. The syndrome was described by the Austrian physician Victor Eisenmenger in 1897 and characterised physiologically by Paul Wood in the 1950s.

Signs and symptoms

Patients develop central cyanosis, digital clubbing, exertional dyspnoea, fatigue and palpitations. Complications include haemoptysis, stroke from paradoxical embolism, brain abscess, gout, arrhythmias and sudden death. Pregnancy carries a very high maternal risk and is generally contraindicated.

Treatment

Once established, the underlying defect can no longer be safely closed. Management is supportive and includes pulmonary vasodilator therapies such as phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors, endothelin receptor antagonists and prostacyclins. Definitive treatment is heart–lung transplantation or lung transplantation with repair of the defect, reserved for selected patients.
