The Wakefield Mystery Plays, also known as the Towneley Cycle, are a cycle of thirty-two mystery plays performed in medieval and early Tudor England. The cycle, preserved in a mid-15th-century manuscript now held by the Huntington Library in California, dramatizes biblical history from the Creation to the Last Judgment, and is traditionally associated with the town of Wakefield in the West Riding of Yorkshire.

The Wakefield Master

Six of the plays — including Noah, the First Shepherds' Play, the Second Shepherds' Play, Herod the Great, the Buffeting and the Judgment — are distinguished by a distinctive nine-line stanza and a vigorous, comic and often satirical style, and are attributed to an anonymous author known as the "Wakefield Master". The Second Shepherds' Play (Secunda Pastorum), in which the shepherd Mak steals a sheep and disguises it as his newborn baby before the shepherds attend the Nativity, is the most famous work in the cycle and a masterpiece of early English drama.

Performance history

Like the other great English cycles (York, Chester and the N-Town plays), the Wakefield plays were performed outdoors, probably on pageant wagons or a fixed stage, around the feast of Corpus Christi. Records of performance in Wakefield run from the 14th century until the cycle was suppressed in the later 16th century during the Reformation. The "Towneley" name comes from the Towneley family of Burnley, in whose library the manuscript was kept for centuries.

The plays have been revived in modern times, notably in productions at Wakefield Cathedral and by the National Theatre (as part of The Mysteries, 1977–85, adapted by Tony Harrison).
