Richard III (2 October 1452 – 22 August 1485) was King of England from 26 June 1483 until his death in 1485. He was the last king of the Plantagenet dynasty and its cadet branch the House of York. His defeat and death at the Battle of Bosworth Field, the last decisive battle of the Wars of the Roses, marked the end of the Middle Ages in England.

Richard was the youngest surviving son of Richard, Duke of York, and Cecily Neville. After his father's death in 1460 and his brother Edward IV's seizure of the throne in 1461, Richard, as Duke of Gloucester, proved a loyal and capable commander and administrator, governing the north of England effectively. When Edward IV died in April 1483, Richard was named Lord Protector of the realm for Edward's eldest son, Edward V. Within weeks, the late king's marriage was declared invalid, making his children illegitimate (the "Princes in the Tower"), and Richard accepted the crown. The two princes disappeared during the summer of 1483 and are widely believed to have been murdered, on whose orders remains one of history's most debated questions.

Early life

Richard was born at Fotheringhay Castle, the fourth surviving son of the Duke of York. After York's death at Wakefield (1460), the boy was sent with his brother George to the Burgundian court, returning when Edward IV secured the throne. Created Duke of Gloucester, he was trained in arms at Middleham Castle under his cousin Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick ("the Kingmaker"), whose younger daughter Anne he later married. Richard fought with distinction at Barnet and Tewkesbury (1471), and as Constable and Admiral of England and Warden of the West Marches he built a power base centred on Middleham and York, where his administration was remembered fondly. In 1482 he commanded the invasion of Scotland that recovered Berwick-upon-Tweed.

Seizure of power

Edward IV died suddenly on 9 April 1483. As Protector, Richard intercepted the young Edward V's party at Stony Stratford and arrested his Woodville relatives, including Earl Rivers. In London, Richard had Lord Hastings summarily executed (13 June) after alleging a conspiracy. On 22 June a sermon at St Paul's Cross argued the illegitimacy of Edward's children on the grounds of Edward's alleged precontract of marriage to Lady Eleanor Butler; Parliament embodied this claim in the act Titulus Regius (1484), and Richard was proclaimed king on 26 June and crowned on 6 July 1483. The Princes in the Tower were seen less and less often and vanished from the record; a rebellion in October 1483 (Buckingham's revolt) was suppressed, and Buckingham executed.

Reign

Richard's only Parliament (January 1484) passed legislation that historians have often praised: it abolished benevolences (forced gifts), reformed bail and jury rules, protected buyers of land against secret trusts, and promoted English trade and printing. Richard founded the College of Arms (1484) and endowed religious foundations. His only legitimate son, Edward of Middleham, died in April 1484; Queen Anne died in March 1485 amid rumours — which Richard publicly denied — that he intended to marry his niece Elizabeth of York. Throughout, exiled Lancastrians and disaffected Yorkists rallied to Henry Tudor in Brittany.

Death at Bosworth

Henry Tudor landed in Wales on 7 August 1485. The armies met near Market Bosworth on 22 August. Though Richard's forces were probably larger, the Stanleys' intervention against him and the death of the Duke of Norfolk turned the battle; Richard led a mounted charge directly at Henry's position, killing his standard-bearer, but was cut down — the last English king to die in battle, and the last king of England to die on English soil in battle. Henry was proclaimed king as Henry VII, founding the Tudor dynasty.

Reputation and the Princes in the Tower

Tudor-era writers, notably Thomas More and, following them, Shakespeare in Richard III, fixed Richard's image as a deformed, murderous tyrant who killed his nephews; Shakespeare's hunchbacked villain with a withered arm became the popular picture. Revisionists, organized since the 1920s in the Richard III Society, argue that the case against Richard is propaganda and that his record in the north and his legislation show a conscientious ruler; the identity of the princes' murderer (if they were murdered) remains contested, with Henry VII and Buckingham among alternative suspects. Forensic analysis of the rediscovered skeleton confirmed scoliosis — curvature of the spine producing uneven shoulders — but no withered arm.

Discovery of remains

Richard's body was buried without ceremony at Greyfriars Church, Leicester, later demolished; legend wrongly held the bones had been thrown into the River Soar. In September 2012 archaeologists from the University of Leicester, working with the Richard III Society, excavated a Leicester car park on the Greyfriars site and found a skeleton with scoliosis and severe battle injuries. DNA comparison with matrilineal descendants of Richard's sister Anne of York, radiocarbon dating, and other evidence led the university to announce on 4 February 2013 that the remains were, beyond reasonable doubt, Richard III's. He was reinterred in Leicester Cathedral on 26 March 2015.
