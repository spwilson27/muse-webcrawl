Trans-Saharan trade

A camel caravan crossing the Sahara

Trans-Saharan trade is trade between sub-Saharan Africa and North Africa that requires travel across the Sahara. Though the practice began in prehistoric times, the peak of trade extended from the 8th century until the early 17th century. The Sahara once had a different climate and environment; in Roman times it supported trade and travel by horse-drawn vehicles, but the large-scale commerce of the medieval period depended on the camel, whose introduction to the region in late antiquity made regular crossing of the deep desert possible.

Routes and commodities

Caravans linked the Mediterranean coast and the oases of the Maghreb with the savanna kingdoms of West Africa. The western route ran from Sijilmasa in Morocco to Taghaza and on to Timbuktu and the Niger bend; central routes connected Tripoli with the Lake Chad basin through the Fezzan, and Tripoli and Tunis with Kano and the Hausa cities; an eastern route ran through Egypt toward Darfur. Southbound cargoes centred on salt from desert mines such as Taghaza and Taoudenni, along with cloth, beads, metalware and horses. Northbound came gold from the forest and savanna fields of Bambouk, Buré and Akan country, ivory, kola nuts and, in large numbers, enslaved people. Caravans could number thousands of camels and depended on wells, oasis towns and organised seasonal departures.

Effects and decline

The trade underpinned the wealth of the Ghana, Mali and Songhai empires and of the Kanem-Bornu state, spread Islam into West Africa along with literacy and scholarship — Timbuktu became a famous centre of learning — and integrated the region into the wider Afro-Eurasian economy. The Moroccan invasion of Songhai in 1591, the diversion of West African gold to European ships on the Guinea coast, and insecurity along the routes caused a long decline from the 17th century, though the trade continued on a reduced scale into the colonial era.
