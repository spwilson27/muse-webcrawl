Social stratification

Social stratification refers to the hierarchical arrangement of individuals and groups in societies, in which people are ranked — and rewarded unequally — according to wealth, income, occupation, education, status, and power. Sociologists distinguish relatively closed systems, such as slavery, estates, and caste, where position is ascribed at birth and movement is restricted, from relatively open class systems, where achievement and mobility are possible though still strongly shaped by birth.

Theoretical perspectives

Karl Marx located stratification in relations to the means of production: a bourgeoisie owning capital confronting a proletariat selling its labor. Max Weber offered a multidimensional account distinguishing class (market position), status (prestige and lifestyle), and party (organized power). Functionalists Kingsley Davis and Wilbert Moore argued that stratification is a universal necessity that motivates talented people into functionally important roles, a thesis criticized for justifying inequality and assuming rewards track social importance. Pierre Bourdieu added the concepts of cultural and social capital, showing how tastes and networks reproduce advantage across generations.

Dimensions and consequences

Stratification operates through income and wealth inequality, occupational prestige, educational credentials, and intersecting ascriptive categories — race, ethnicity, gender, caste — captured in the framework of intersectionality. Its consequences are visible in systematic differences in health and life expectancy, residential segregation, political influence, exposure to crime and environmental hazards, and children's opportunities. The degree of openness of a stratification system is measured by social mobility research.

Global stratification

At the world scale, sociologists analyze global inequality between and within nations, using world-systems and dependency perspectives as well as development economics, and examine how globalization, deindustrialization, and the growth of the precariat are reshaping class structures in wealthy and poor countries alike.
