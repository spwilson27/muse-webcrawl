The Erechtheion or Erechtheum is an ancient Greek temple on the north side of the Acropolis of Athens, built during the years 421–406 BC in the Ionic order. It is famous both for its elegant, asymmetrical design — dictated by the uneven ground and the multiple shrines it had to house — and for the Porch of the Maidens, whose roof is supported by six carved female figures known as the Caryatids.

History and function

The temple replaced an earlier Archaic temple destroyed by the Persians in 480 BC. It was built to house several of Athens' most ancient and sacred cults in a single structure: the olive-wood cult statue of Athena Polias, the altars of Poseidon-Erechtheus, the hero Erechtheus, and the shrine of Cecrops. According to myth, the building covered the marks of the contest between Athena and Poseidon for patronage of the city: the trident marks in the rock and the salt spring of Poseidon, and the sacred olive tree of Athena, which stood in the adjoining precinct.

In later centuries the building served as a Christian church, a Byzantine chapel, and, under Ottoman rule, a residence within the Acropolis garrison. One of the original Caryatids was removed by Lord Elgin in the early 19th century and is now in the British Museum; the remaining original figures were moved to the Acropolis Museum in 1979 and replaced on the building by replicas.

Architecture

The marble temple is built on sloping ground, with its eastern and western parts at markedly different levels. The eastern porch, fronted by six tall Ionic columns, gave access to the chamber of Athena Polias. On the north side is a large porch with Ionic columns sheltering the trident marks; on the south, the smaller Porch of the Maidens projects over the older precinct. The building's refined Ionic ornament — including its richly carved doorways and the guilloche mouldings of the north porch — made it one of the most influential monuments of the Classical Greek style.
