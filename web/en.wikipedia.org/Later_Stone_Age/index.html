Later Stone Age

The Later Stone Age (LSA) is a period in African prehistory following the Middle Stone Age. It is conventionally dated from about 50,000–40,000 years ago until the adoption of farming or metalworking in each region — in some hunter-gatherer contexts, effectively into the historical era. The term was coined by Astley John Goodwin and C. van Riet Lowe in 1929 as part of a three-age system for southern Africa (Earlier, Middle, and Later Stone Ages), which roughly parallels the Lower, Middle, and Upper Paleolithic sequence used elsewhere.

Characteristics

The LSA is characterized by smaller, more specialized stone tools than those of the Middle Stone Age, including microliths — small blades and segments hafted into composite tools such as arrows, spears, and sickles. Hunter-gatherers of the LSA made extensive use of the bow and arrow, snares, and fishing equipment, and exploited a broad spectrum of foods, including fish, shellfish, and small game, in what is sometimes interpreted as an intensification of subsistence.

Other hallmarks include an abundance of worked bone and ostrich eggshell — including beads and water flasks — grinding equipment, and rich traditions of rock art, notably the paintings and engravings of the San peoples of southern Africa. LSA peoples were anatomically modern humans; in much of Africa the makers of LSA industries were the ancestors or close relatives of historic hunter-gatherer groups such as the San and the pygmy peoples of the Congo Basin, while in North and East Africa later populations also included incoming food producers.

Regional sequences

Named LSA industries include the Robberg, Albany, and Wilton industries of southern Africa, and the Capsian of the Maghreb; in East Africa, microlithic industries appear at sites such as Lukenya Hill and Enkapune Ya Muto, where some of the earliest microlithic assemblages and ostrich eggshell beads (about 40,000 years old) have been found. The transition from the Middle to the Later Stone Age was gradual and regionally variable, and researchers debate whether it reflects a cognitive or demographic shift.

End of the period

In regions bordering farming peoples, LSA hunter-gatherer societies interacted with pastoralists and agriculturalists for millennia — in southern Africa from about 2,000 years ago with the arrival of sheep herding — and some communities, such as San groups in the Kalahari, maintained Stone Age toolkits into the 20th century.
