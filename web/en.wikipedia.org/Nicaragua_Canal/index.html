The Nicaragua Canal (Spanish: Canal de Nicaragua), formally the Nicaraguan Canal and Development Project, refers to a series of proposals, dating back to the colonial era, to build a shipping canal through Nicaragua connecting the Caribbean Sea (and thus the Atlantic Ocean) with the Pacific Ocean, using the San Juan River and Lake Nicaragua as the central reach. Although repeatedly surveyed—including by the United States, which ultimately built the Panama Canal instead—the canal was never constructed.

Early proposals

The Nicaraguan route was a leading candidate for an isthmian canal throughout the nineteenth century. Cornelius Vanderbilt's Accessory Transit Company moved California-bound passengers across Nicaragua in the 1850s, and the United States negotiated the Clayton–Bulwer Treaty (1850) with Britain over canal rights. US survey commissions repeatedly studied the route, and work was even briefly begun by the Maritime Canal Company in 1889 before bankruptcy intervened. In 1902 the US chose Panama, partly after lobbying that publicised Nicaragua's volcanic hazards—the famous postage stamp showing the erupting Momotombo volcano.

HKND project

In June 2013 Nicaragua's National Assembly granted the Hong Kong-based HKND Group, controlled by Chinese businessman Wang Jing, a 50-year renewable concession to build and operate a 259-kilometre canal estimated to cost US$40–50 billion, together with associated ports, an airport, and a free-trade zone. A ceremonial groundbreaking took place in December 2014, but little substantive construction followed. The project drew strong opposition from farmers and communities along the route, who faced expropriation, and from environmentalists concerned about dredging in Lake Nicaragua, the largest freshwater reserve in Central America. After Wang Jing's fortune collapsed in China's 2015 stock market turmoil, work stalled entirely; HKND's offices closed in 2018, and in May 2024 Nicaragua's legislature formally repealed the concession law.
