John Loudon McAdam

Born: 23 September 1756, Ayr, Scotland
Died: 26 November 1836 (aged 80), Moffat, Scotland
Occupation: Civil engineer, road builder
Known for: Inventing the "macadam" road surface

John Loudon McAdam (23 September 1756 – 26 November 1836) was a Scottish civil engineer and road builder. He invented a new process, "macadamisation", for building roads with a smooth, hard surface that would be more durable and less muddy than soil-based tracks.

Early life

Born in Ayr, McAdam moved to New York in 1770 to work in his uncle's counting house and made a fortune during the American Revolution. Returning to Scotland in 1783, he became a trustee of local turnpike roads and began experimenting with road construction at his own expense.

Macadam roads

As surveyor of roads for the Bristol turnpike trust from 1816, McAdam applied his method systematically: a raised, well-drained roadbed built up from layers of small, angular broken stones bound by their own dust, compacted by traffic. His treatises of 1816 and 1819 spread the technique across Britain and abroad. Later, tar-bound variants produced "tarmacadam", shortened to tarmac.

Later career

In 1827 McAdam was appointed the first Surveyor General of Metropolitan Roads in London. Parliament, which had earlier declined to purchase his process, ultimately granted him payments for his work. His sons and grandsons continued as prominent road engineers.
