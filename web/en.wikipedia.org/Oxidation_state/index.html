Oxidation state

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In chemistry, the oxidation state, or oxidation number, is the hypothetical charge that an atom would have if all bonds to atoms of different elements were treated as fully ionic. Oxidation states are typically integers — positive, negative or zero — and are used to describe the degree of oxidation (loss of electrons) or reduction (gain of electrons) of atoms in compounds and to balance redox reactions and name compounds systematically.

Rules for assignment

IUPAC defines the oxidation state through an ionic approximation of bonding, assigning electrons to the more electronegative partner in each bond. Practical rules follow: the oxidation state of a free element is 0; fluorine is −1 in its compounds; oxygen is usually −2 (except in peroxides, −1, and with fluorine); hydrogen is +1 with non-metals and −1 in metal hydrides; the sum of oxidation states equals the total charge of the species. In Stock notation, the oxidation state appears as a Roman numeral, as in iron(III) chloride.

Examples and uses

Transition metals characteristically show several oxidation states — manganese ranges from −3 to +7, appearing as purple permanganate, Mn(VII), and green manganate, Mn(VI). Fractional oxidation states arise in mixed-valence compounds such as magnetite, Fe3O4 (average +8/3). Oxidation states underpin the balancing of redox equations, electrochemistry, corrosion science and the geochemistry of ore deposits. They are a bookkeeping device rather than a direct measure of real atomic charge. See also valence, electronegativity and redox.
