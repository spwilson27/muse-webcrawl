Non-stick surface

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A non-stick surface is an engineered surface designed to reduce the ability of other materials to stick to it. Non-stick cookware, such as frying pans, is coated so that food does not adhere during cooking, allowing cooking with little or no oil and making cleaning easier.

PTFE coatings

The most common non-stick material is polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), discovered by Roy Plunkett at DuPont in 1938 and marketed under the Teflon trademark. Marc Grégoire applied it to cookware in France in the 1950s under the Tefal brand. PTFE has an extremely low coefficient of friction and low surface energy. Overheating PTFE above roughly 300 °C degrades the coating and can release fumes harmful to birds and irritating to humans, and the historical use of PFOA as a processing aid raised environmental and health concerns before it was phased out.

Other types

Alternatives include ceramic sol-gel ("ceramic non-stick") coatings, silicone bakeware, enamelled cast iron, and seasoned cast iron or carbon steel, whose polymerised oil layer provides moderate release properties. Superhydrophobic surfaces inspired by the lotus leaf are an active research area.
