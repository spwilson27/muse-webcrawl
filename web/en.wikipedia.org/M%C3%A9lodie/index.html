A mélodie is a French art song, a setting of a poem for solo voice and piano that flourished in France from the mid-19th century into the 20th century. Although related to the German Lied, the mélodie developed its own sensibility, emphasising subtlety of declamation, refined harmony and an intimate relationship between voice and accompaniment.

History

The mélodie grew out of the earlier French romance, a simpler strophic song. Hector Berlioz's Les nuits d'été (1841), settings of poems by Théophile Gautier, are often regarded as the first great cycle of mélodies. Charles Gounod composed nearly 200 songs and helped establish the genre's popularity in salons and concert halls.

The genre reached its height with Gabriel Fauré, whose settings of Verlaine, including the cycle La bonne chanson, are considered masterpieces. Henri Duparc wrote only a handful of songs before abandoning composition, but works such as "L'invitation au voyage" remain central to the repertoire. Claude Debussy and Maurice Ravel carried the mélodie into the modernist era, while Francis Poulenc became its leading exponent in the 20th century.

Characteristics

Mélodies typically favour a restrained, syllabic treatment of the text that follows the natural rhythms of French verse. The piano part is rarely mere accompaniment; instead it creates atmosphere, comments on the poem and shares the expressive burden with the singer.
