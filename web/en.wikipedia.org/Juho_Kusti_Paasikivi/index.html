Juho Kusti Paasikivi (1870 – 1956) was a Finnish politician who served as the seventh president of Finland from 1946 to 1956. He was the architect of Finland's post–Second World War foreign policy toward the Soviet Union, known as the Paasikivi Doctrine (later the Paasikivi–Kekkonen Line), which preserved Finnish independence and democracy through accommodation of Soviet security interests.

Early career

Born Johan Gustaf Hellsten in Koski (Hämeenkoski), he Fennicized his name to Juho Kusti Paasikivi. Trained as a lawyer, he became a leading figure in the Finnish Party and later the National Coalition Party, serving as a senator and as prime minister of the first government of independent Finland in 1918, when he favoured a constitutional monarchy. He negotiated the Treaty of Tartu with Soviet Russia in 1920 and later worked in banking, before returning as Finland's envoy to Stockholm and, in 1939–40, as negotiator in Moscow before the Winter War.

Wartime and presidency

Paasikivi led the negotiations that produced the Moscow Armistice of 1944 ending the Continuation War, and became prime minister again in 1944. When President C. G. E. Mannerheim resigned in 1946, Parliament elected Paasikivi to succeed him. As president he steered Finland through the Paris Peace Treaty of 1947 and the 1948 Finno-Soviet Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation and Mutual Assistance, securing tolerable terms while keeping Finland outside the Soviet bloc — the core of the Paasikivi Doctrine that facts of geography and great-power interests must govern small-state policy.

Legacy

Re-elected in 1950, Paasikivi oversaw the 1952 Helsinki Olympics and the return of the Porkkala naval base by the USSR shortly before his death in office in 1956. He was succeeded by Urho Kekkonen, who continued his line for a quarter-century.
