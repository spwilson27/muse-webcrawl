Surrogacy

Surrogacy is an arrangement, usually supported by a legal agreement, whereby a woman (the surrogate) agrees to carry and give birth to a child for another person or couple, who will become the child's parent(s). It is used when pregnancy is medically impossible or dangerous, or by single people and same-sex couples seeking to have children.

Types

In gestational surrogacy, the surrogate carries an embryo created by in vitro fertilisation from the egg and sperm of the intended parents or donors, and has no genetic relationship to the child; this is now the predominant form. In traditional surrogacy, the surrogate's own egg is fertilised, making her the genetic mother, which raises additional legal and emotional complexity. Arrangements may be commercial, in which the surrogate is paid beyond expenses, or altruistic, in which she is not.

Legal status

Surrogacy law varies widely. Commercial surrogacy is permitted in some US states and in a shrinking number of other countries; several European states permit only altruistic surrogacy; others prohibit all surrogacy, and some declare surrogacy agreements unenforceable. The landmark US case In re Baby M (1988) invalidated a traditional surrogacy contract in New Jersey. Because of conflicting laws, cross-border ("international") surrogacy has produced difficult disputes over parentage and the child's citizenship, and several former destination countries, including India and Thailand, have closed commercial surrogacy to foreigners.

Ethical debate

Supporters frame surrogacy as an exercise of reproductive freedom that creates families and can be a freely chosen, fairly compensated arrangement. Critics raise concerns about the exploitation of economically vulnerable women, the commodification of children and reproduction, and the welfare of surrogates in weakly regulated markets. Religious traditions differ widely in their judgements.
