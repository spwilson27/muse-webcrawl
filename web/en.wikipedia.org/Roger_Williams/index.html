Roger Williams

Born: c. 1603, London, England
Died: 1683, Providence, Rhode Island
Known for: Founding Rhode Island; advocacy of religious freedom and separation of church and state

Roger Williams (c. 1603 – 1683) was an English-born Puritan minister, theologian, and author who founded Providence Plantations in the Colony of Rhode Island and the Providence Plantations. Banished from the Massachusetts Bay Colony for his unorthodox religious and political views, he established a settlement founded on religious liberty, and became an early advocate of the separation of church and state and of fair dealings with Native Americans.

Early life and banishment

Williams was educated at Cambridge and took holy orders in the Church of England before adopting Puritan views. He arrived in Boston in 1631 and ministered at Salem and Plymouth. His challenges to the colonial charter's claim to Native land, his denial that magistrates could punish religious offences, and his separatist preaching led the Massachusetts General Court to banish him in 1635. Warned of arrest and deportation, he fled into the wilderness in January 1636.

Providence and Rhode Island

Aided by the Narragansett sachems Canonicus and Miantonomi, Williams founded Providence in 1636 on land he considered properly purchased from its Native owners. The colony welcomed dissenters, and Williams secured a parliamentary patent in 1643 and a royal charter in 1663 guaranteeing freedom of conscience. He wrote A Key into the Language of America (1643), an important study of the Narragansett language, and defended his principles in The Bloudy Tenent of Persecution (1644), addressed to John Cotton.

Legacy

Williams's ideas about soul liberty influenced later American thought and the First Amendment. He served as president of the colony and, during King Philip's War (1675–76), despite his friendship with the Narragansett, served Providence's defence. He died in 1683.
