Antiochus IV Epiphanes (Greek: Ἀντίοχος ὁ Ἐπιφανής, "God Manifest"; c. 215 – 164 BC) was a king of the Seleucid Empire from 175 BC until his death. A former hostage in Rome under the Treaty of Apamea, he seized the throne after his brother Seleucus IV was assassinated. He is notorious in Jewish and Christian tradition for his persecution of Judaism in Judea, the desecration of the Temple in Jerusalem, and the resulting Maccabean Revolt — events commemorated by the festival of Hanukkah and described in the Books of the Maccabees and Daniel.

Accession

Antiochus had been exchanged as a hostage in Rome and was living in Athens when his brother was murdered by the minister Heliodorus in 175 BC. With Pergamene support he reached Syria and took the throne, sidelining his nephew Demetrius, the rightful heir held hostage in Rome. He adopted the divine epithet Theos Epiphanes ("God Manifest"), which his detractors punned into Epimanes ("the Madman"), a nickname reflecting his eccentric generosity and unpredictable public behaviour described by Polybius.

War with Egypt and Roman humiliation

In the Sixth Syrian War (170–168 BC) Antiochus twice invaded Ptolemaic Egypt, captured Memphis, and besieged Alexandria. In 168 BC, as he stood before Alexandria at Eleusis, the Roman envoy Gaius Popillius Laenas famously drew a circle around him in the sand and demanded he decide on withdrawal before stepping out of it. Antiochus submitted, ending Seleucid ambitions in Egypt and demonstrating Rome's supremacy in the eastern Mediterranean.

Persecution and the Maccabean Revolt

In Judea, Antiochus intervened in factional struggles over the high priesthood, sacked Jerusalem in 169 BC, and in 167 BC issued decrees forbidding Jewish religious practice: Sabbath observance, circumcision, and dietary laws were banned, and an altar to Zeus was erected in the Temple — the "abomination of desolation" of the Book of Daniel. The priest Mattathias and his sons, the Maccabees, launched a guerrilla revolt; by 164 BC Judas Maccabeus had retaken Jerusalem and rededicated the Temple, the event celebrated at Hanukkah.

Death and succession

Antiochus spent his last years campaigning in the east against Parthia and Armenia and replenishing his treasury. He died in 164 BC at Tabae in Persia, possibly of disease. Biblical and later accounts framed his death as divine punishment. He was succeeded by his young son Antiochus V Eupator under the regency of Lysias.
