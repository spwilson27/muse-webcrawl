Gangrene is a type of tissue death caused by a lack of blood supply. Symptoms may include a change in skin colour to red or black, numbness, swelling, pain, skin breakdown and coolness. The feet and hands are most commonly affected. Gangrene is not itself a disease but a result of other conditions — infection, injury, or long-term diseases affecting the circulation such as diabetes and atherosclerosis.

Types

Dry gangrene results from gradually reduced blood flow, typically from peripheral artery disease; the tissue dries, darkens and shrivels without significant bacterial infection. Wet gangrene involves bacterial infection of the dead tissue, producing swelling, blistering and pus, and it spreads rapidly and can be fatal. Gas gangrene (clostridial myonecrosis) is caused by bacteria such as Clostridium perfringens growing in injured tissue and producing gas within it; it is a medical emergency. Internal gangrene can affect organs such as the bowel or gallbladder. Fournier gangrene involves the perineum and genitals.

Causes and risk factors

Anything that severely restricts blood supply or introduces deep bacterial contamination can cause gangrene: severe trauma and crush injuries, frostbite, burns, vasculitis, Raynaud's phenomenon, and poorly controlled diabetes. Tobacco smoking and immunosuppression further raise risk.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is based on appearance, symptoms, blood tests and imaging of blood flow. Treatment aims to restore circulation where possible (angioplasty or bypass surgery), remove dead tissue (debridement), and treat infection with antibiotics. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy is an adjunct for some types. Where tissue cannot be saved, amputation may be necessary to stop progression. Wet and gas gangrene carry substantial mortality even with treatment.
