Zhu Xi

Zhu Xi (Chinese: 朱熹; 18 October 1130 – 23 April 1200), also known by his courtesy name Yuanhui and art name Zhonghui, was a Chinese calligrapher, historian, philosopher, poet and politician of the Song dynasty. He is generally regarded as the most influential figure of Neo-Confucianism, whose synthesis dominated Chinese thought and the civil-service examination system for over six centuries, and spread to Korea, Japan and Vietnam.

Life

Born in Fujian, Zhu Xi passed the jinshi examination at the precocious age of 18 (in 1148). His official career was modest and often troubled — he served in several local posts and memorialised the throne with reform proposals — for he spent most of his life teaching, writing and gathering disciples, notably at the restored White Deer Grotto Academy in Jiangxi, one of China's famed academies. Briefly disgraced in the 1190s when his teaching was condemned as "false learning", he was rehabilitated at the end of his life.

Philosophy

Drawing on the Cheng brothers, Zhu Xi systematised Neo-Confucian metaphysics around li (理, principle or pattern) and qi (氣, material force): everything is a union of the two, and the Supreme Ultimate (taiji) is the sum of all principle. Self-cultivation proceeds through the "investigation of things" (gewu) and reverent attentiveness. He edited and commented on the Four Books — the Great Learning, Doctrine of the Mean, Analects and Mencius — whose study, with his commentaries, became the core curriculum of the imperial examinations from 1313 until their abolition in 1905.

Influence

Zhu Xi's commentaries became state orthodoxy across East Asia: Joseon Korea's scholars venerated him, and Tokugawa Japan adopted his school as official learning. His collected works run to dozens of volumes.
