Feminist philosophy

Feminist philosophy is an approach to philosophy that applies philosophical methods to feminist questions and, conversely, uses feminist insights to re-examine traditional philosophical concepts, arguments, and the canon. It is closely connected to the feminist movement and seeks both to understand the causes of women's oppression and to critique the ways philosophy itself may have encoded gender bias.

History

While feminist philosophy emerged as a recognized field in the 1970s, its roots are much older. Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792) argued philosophically for women's rational equality; John Stuart Mill's The Subjection of Women (1869) applied liberal principles to women's emancipation. In the twentieth century, Simone de Beauvoir's The Second Sex (1949), with its claim that "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman," supplied foundational analyses of gender as socially constructed. From the 1970s onward, feminist philosophers recovered forgotten women in the history of philosophy and began systematic critiques of mainstream ethics, epistemology, and metaphysics.

Main areas

  • Feminist epistemology — questions the ideal of the disembodied, purely objective knower; standpoint theory argues that marginalized social positions can confer distinctive epistemic advantages.
  • Feminist ethics — includes the ethics of care developed by Carol Gilligan and Nel Noddings, emphasizing relationships, responsiveness, and context over abstract universal rules.
  • Feminist metaphysics — analyzes the concepts of gender and sex, social construction, and personal identity; Judith Butler's theory of gender performativity is a prominent example.
  • Feminist political philosophy — examines the public/private distinction, the family, reproductive rights, and global justice.
  • Feminist philosophy of science — studies how social values and gender have shaped scientific practice and findings.

Approaches and debates

Feminist philosophy draws on the same broad traditions as the rest of the discipline — liberal, Marxist and socialist, radical, psychoanalytic, existentialist, poststructuralist, and analytic approaches — and it intersects with critical race theory and intersectionality, a framework developed by Kimberlé Crenshaw that analyzes how gender interacts with race, class, and other axes of identity. Ongoing debates concern the definition of "woman," the metaphysics of gender categories, and the proper goals of feminist theory.
