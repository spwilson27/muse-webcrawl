Computer Fraud and Abuse Act

The CFAA is codified at 18 U.S.C. § 1030

The Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) is a United States cybersecurity law enacted in 1986 as an amendment to the Counterfeit Access Device and Computer Fraud and Abuse Act of 1984. Codified at 18 U.S.C. § 1030, it criminalizes unauthorized access to computers — including accessing a computer "without authorization" or "exceeding authorized access" — and provides a civil cause of action for victims.

Provisions

The statute covers, among other things, obtaining national security information, compromising government or financial computers, computer fraud, causing damage to a protected computer, trafficking in passwords, and extortion involving computers. Penalties range from fines and short imprisonment for first-time misdemeanors to decades in prison for repeat or aggravated offenses.

Criticism and interpretation

The CFAA has been criticized as vague and overbroad, particularly the phrase "exceeds authorized access", which prosecutors and employers used against employees and researchers who violated terms of service or workplace computer policies. High-profile prosecutions, including that of internet activist Aaron Swartz, intensified calls for reform ("Aaron's Law"). In Van Buren v. United States (2021), the Supreme Court narrowed the statute, holding that a person "exceeds authorized access" only by obtaining information from areas of a system they are not entitled to access at all — not by misusing information they are entitled to see. The Court's 2024-related jurisprudence and the Department of Justice's 2022 charging policy further limited prosecutions of good-faith security researchers.
