Calculus of variations

The calculus of variations (or variational calculus) is a field of mathematical analysis that uses variations, small changes in functions and functionals, to find maxima and minima of functionals: mappings from a set of functions to the real numbers.

Overview

Whereas ordinary calculus deals with functions of finitely many variables, the calculus of variations deals with functionals, typically expressed as definite integrals involving functions and their derivatives. A classic problem is to find, among all smooth curves joining two points, the one minimizing an integral such as arc length or travel time. Functions that maximize or minimize functionals may be found using the Euler–Lagrange equation of the calculus of variations.

History

The field began with Johann Bernoulli's 1696 brachistochrone challenge, solved by Bernoulli, Newton, Leibniz, L'Hôpital, and Jacob Bernoulli; the solution curve is a cycloid. Leonhard Euler and Joseph-Louis Lagrange developed the systematic theory in the 18th century, and Lagrange's work recast mechanics as analytical mechanics. Later developments by Jacobi, Weierstrass, Hilbert — whose 20th problem spurred the direct method in the calculus of variations — and Emmy Noether, whose theorem connects symmetries of variational problems with conservation laws, made the subject central to physics and geometry.

The Euler–Lagrange equation

For a functional

J[y] = ∫₁x₂ L(x, y(x), y′(x)) dx,

a necessary condition for an extremum is that y satisfy the Euler–Lagrange equation:

∂L/∂y − d/dx (∂L/∂y′) = 0.

Applications

The calculus of variations underlies Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics, the principle of least action, general relativity's Einstein–Hilbert action, minimal surface theory, optimal control, image processing, and economics.
