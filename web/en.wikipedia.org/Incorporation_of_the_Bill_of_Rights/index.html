Incorporation of the Bill of Rights

In United States constitutional law, incorporation is the doctrine by which portions of the Bill of Rights — the first ten amendments to the U.S. Constitution — have been made applicable to the state governments. Originally, as the Supreme Court held in Barron v. Baltimore (1833), the Bill of Rights restricted only the federal government. After the ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment in 1868, the Court gradually held that most of the Bill of Rights' guarantees also bind the states through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause (and, in the view of many scholars, its Privileges or Immunities Clause).

History

The Fourteenth Amendment was adopted after the Civil War to secure citizenship and civil rights for formerly enslaved people. In the Slaughter-House Cases (1873), the Supreme Court read the Privileges or Immunities Clause narrowly, foreclosing one route to incorporation. Instead, beginning in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the Court pursued selective incorporation: it asked, right by right, whether each guarantee was "fundamental to the American scheme of justice" (the formulation in Duncan v. Louisiana, 1968) and therefore binding on the states through the Due Process Clause.

Early milestones included Gitlow v. New York (1925) for freedom of speech, Near v. Minnesota (1931) for freedom of the press, De Jonge v. Oregon (1937) for assembly, Cantwell v. Connecticut (1940) for free exercise of religion, and Everson v. Board of Education (1947) for the Establishment Clause. The criminal-procedure revolution of the Warren Court incorporated the Fourth, Fifth, Sixth and Eighth Amendment protections in cases such as Mapp v. Ohio (1961), Gideon v. Wainwright (1963) and Miranda v. Arizona (1966, applying incorporated rights).

Provisions that remain unincorporated include the Fifth Amendment's grand-jury indictment requirement (Hurtado v. California, 1884) and the Seventh Amendment right to a civil jury trial. The Ninth and Tenth Amendments, being structural in character, are not generally treated as candidates for incorporation.

Debate over total incorporation

Justice Hugo Black argued in his dissent in Adamson v. California (1947) that the framers of the Fourteenth Amendment intended total incorporation of the first eight amendments. Justice Felix Frankfurter defended the selective approach. Although Black's view never commanded a majority, selective incorporation has in practice produced nearly the same result, and modern decisions such as McDonald and Timbs (the latter unanimous) have continued the process. In Ramos v. Louisiana (2020) the Court held that incorporated rights apply against the states with the same content they have against the federal government, rejecting "watered-down" versions of rights.
