Fermi–Dirac statistics

Fermi–Dirac statistics is a type of quantum statistics that applies to the physics of a system consisting of many identical particles that obey the Pauli exclusion principle — particles called fermions, which have half-integer spin. It is named after Enrico Fermi and Paul Dirac, who each derived the distribution independently in 1926.

The distribution

The Fermi–Dirac distribution gives the probability that an energy state of energy ε is occupied at temperature T: it depends on the chemical potential (the Fermi energy at zero temperature), Boltzmann's constant, and temperature. At absolute zero, all states below the Fermi energy are filled and all above are empty, producing a sharp "Fermi surface"; at higher temperatures the occupation falls off smoothly over a range of about kBT.

Applications

Fermi–Dirac statistics describes electrons in metals and semiconductors, nucleons in nuclei and neutron stars, and other degenerate fermion gases. It explains the electronic heat capacity of metals, thermionic emission, and the pressure (degeneracy pressure) that supports white dwarfs and neutron stars against gravitational collapse. It contrasts with Bose–Einstein statistics, which applies to bosons and permits unlimited occupation of a single state.
