Wave theory of light

The wave theory of light holds that light is a wave — in its modern form, an electromagnetic wave — rather than a stream of material particles. It explains reflection, refraction, interference, diffraction, and polarization, and was established during the 19th century through the work of Thomas Young, Augustin-Jean Fresnel, and James Clerk Maxwell.

Origins

Christiaan Huygens proposed in his Traité de la Lumière (1690) that light travels as waves through an all-pervading medium, the luminiferous aether, and showed how wavefronts account for reflection and refraction. Isaac Newton favoured a corpuscular (particle) theory, whose authority long eclipsed the wave view.

Vindication

Young's double-slit experiment of 1801 demonstrated interference fringes, a hallmark of waves. Fresnel's wave mathematics predicted the bright spot at the centre of a circular obstacle's shadow — the "Poisson spot" — which was observed in 1818, decisively supporting the wave theory. Measurements by Léon Foucault in 1850 showed light travels slower in water than in air, as the wave theory predicted and the corpuscular theory did not.

Electromagnetic synthesis

Maxwell's equations (1865) predicted electromagnetic waves travelling at the measured speed of light, identifying light as such a wave. The theory's one failure — the luminiferous aether — was resolved by Einstein's special relativity (1905), which removed the need for a medium, while the photoelectric effect restored a particle aspect to light, leading to the modern concept of wave–particle duality.
