Wave-particle duality

Wave-particle duality is the concept in quantum mechanics that fundamental entities such as photons and electrons exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties, depending on how they are measured. A central principle of the theory, it showed that the classical categories of "wave" and "particle" are inadequate to describe quantum behaviour.

History

The nature of light was debated for centuries: Newton's corpuscular theory competed with the wave theory of Huygens, which triumphed after Young's double-slit experiment (1801) and Fresnel's work demonstrated interference and diffraction. Maxwell's equations then identified light as an electromagnetic wave. However, the photoelectric effect, explained by Einstein in 1905, required light to arrive in discrete quanta — photons — with energy E = hf. In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that matter likewise has wave properties, with wavelength λ = h/p; electron diffraction, observed by Davisson and Germer in 1927, confirmed this.

Key experiments

In the double-slit experiment, single electrons or photons fired one at a time at two slits build up an interference pattern over time, as though each particle passes through both slits as a wave and interferes with itself — yet each is detected at a single point, like a particle. Attempts to observe which slit the particle traverses destroy the interference pattern. Compton scattering (1923) demonstrated the particle-like momentum of photons directly.

Interpretation

Niels Bohr framed duality within his principle of complementarity: wave and particle descriptions are complementary aspects, both needed for a full account, but revealed only under mutually exclusive experimental arrangements. In the modern formalism, quantum objects are described by wavefunctions whose squared amplitudes give probabilities for particle-like detection events; duality is thus an expression of the probability-wave structure of quantum theory rather than of objects literally being classical waves or particles.
