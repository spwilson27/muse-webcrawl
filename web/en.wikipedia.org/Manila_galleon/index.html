Manila galleon

The Manila galleons were Spanish trading ships which for two and a half centuries linked the Philippines with Mexico across the Pacific Ocean, making one or two round-trip voyages per year between Manila and Acapulco. The trade began in 1565, when the Augustinian friar and navigator Andrés de Urdaneta found the return route (the tornaviaje) by sailing north to catch the Kuroshio current and the westerlies, and ended in 1815, when the last galleon, the Magallanes, arrived in Manila as the Mexican War of Independence disrupted the route.

The trade

Westward cargoes from Acapulco consisted overwhelmingly of American silver — from the mines of Mexico and Peru — which purchased Chinese goods brought to Manila by junks from Fujian: silk, porcelain, lacquerware, spices, ivory and cotton textiles. Eastbound goods were distributed through Mexico and onward to Spain and Europe. The galleon trade made Manila one of the first truly global entrepôts and effectively integrated the Philippines into world commerce, though the Spanish crown limited the trade by quota and the colony remained heavily dependent on the annual silver subsidy (situado) from New Spain.

The ships and the voyage

The galleons were among the largest wooden ships of their age, built largely in Philippine shipyards at Cavite from hardwoods such as teak and molave, displacing up to 2,000 tons. The eastbound passage to Acapulco took five to seven months and was notoriously deadly, with scurvy, thirst and storms common; the westbound run, riding the trade winds, took about three months. Storms, reefs and foreign raiders claimed many ships — roughly a quarter of all galleons were lost or captured, among them Thomas Cavendish's prize Santa Ana (1587) and the treasure ships taken during the British occupation of Manila in 1762.

Legacy

The galleon route carried not only goods but people, languages, religious imagery and crops — maize, sweet potato, peanuts and tobacco to Asia; mango and other Asian plants to the Americas — making it a foundational channel of the Columbian exchange across the Pacific. Trade voyages of the Manila galleons were inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World register in 2015.
