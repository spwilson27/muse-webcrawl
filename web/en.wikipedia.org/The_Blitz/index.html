The Blitz

The Blitz (from German Blitzkrieg, "lightning war") was a German bombing campaign against the United Kingdom in 1940 and 1941, during the Second World War. The term was first used by the British press, and originated as an informal name for the strategic bombing carried out by the Luftwaffe. Air raids on London began on 7 September 1940 and continued for 56 of the following 57 nights.

Course of the campaign

After the failure to destroy RAF Fighter Command in the Battle of Britain, Germany shifted to night bombing of cities, ports and industry. London was bombed for 57 consecutive nights from 7 September 1940. Other cities hit in heavy raids included Coventry (devastated on 14–15 November 1940), Liverpool, Birmingham, Bristol, Southampton, Plymouth, Sheffield, Manchester, Glasgow (the Clydebank Blitz) and Belfast. Londoners sheltered in Underground stations, Anderson shelters and public refuges; the slogan "London can take it" and later the myth of the "Blitz spirit" embodied civilian resilience.

Effects

The Blitz killed an estimated 43,000 civilians and destroyed or damaged more than a million London houses alone. Despite the destruction, British war production continued to rise, morale did not collapse, and the campaign failed in its strategic aim of forcing Britain out of the war. It ended in May 1941 as the Luftwaffe redeployed east for Operation Barbarossa, the invasion of the Soviet Union. Sporadic later raids — the "Baedeker Blitz" of 1942 and the V-weapon attacks of 1944–45 — followed.
