Compact group

A compact group is a collection of galaxies in close proximity to one another, typically containing a small number of members (most commonly four to eight) within a small region of space, comparable in size to a single large galaxy. Compact groups are among the densest galaxy environments known, and their members experience strong gravitational interactions.

Examples and catalogues

The best-known example is Stephan's Quintet in the constellation Pegasus, observed by Édouard Stephan in 1877, though one of its apparent members is a foreground galaxy. Systematic catalogues were compiled by Paul Hickson in the 1980s; the Hickson Compact Groups number about one hundred systems selected by criteria of population, isolation, and compactness. Seyfert's Sextet is another famous example.

Properties and evolution

Galaxies in compact groups often show signs of interaction: tidal tails, distorted shapes, and bursts of star formation. The crossing times in compact groups are short, so theory long predicted that members should merge quickly into a single elliptical galaxy; the observed abundance of compact groups therefore poses questions about how they are replenished, possibly by infall of new galaxies from surrounding looser groups. Many compact groups are embedded in larger structures, and some apparent members may be chance projections.
