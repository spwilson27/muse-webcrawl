Shotgun


A pump-action shotgun, one of the most common modern types.

A shotgun (also known as a scattergun or historically as a fowling piece) is a long gun designed to be fired from the shoulder, which uses the energy of a fixed shell to fire a number of small spherical pellets called shot, or a solid projectile called a slug. Shotguns have smooth bores, typically in gauges such as 12, 20, or .410 bore, and come in a wide variety of sizes and operating mechanisms, including break-action (single-shot, double-barrelled side-by-side and over-and-under), pump-action, lever-action, bolt-action, and semi-automatic designs.

Ammunition

A shotgun shell consists of a case (usually plastic with a brass base) containing primer, propellant, a wad, and the shot charge. Pellet sizes range from large buckshot used for hunting big game and for defensive use, through medium sizes for waterfowl, to fine birdshot for small birds and clay targets. The spread of the pattern is controlled by the choke, a constriction at the muzzle. Shotguns can also fire slugs for greater range and specialised loads such as breaching rounds, bean bags, and flares.

Uses

The shotgun is one of the most versatile firearms. It is used for hunting birds and game, for shooting sports (skeet, trap, and sporting clays), by police and military forces for close-quarters combat and breaching, and for home defence. Short-barrelled sawed-off shotguns and combat shotguns have a long and controversial history; the trench guns of the First World War prompted a German diplomatic protest in 1918.

History

Shotguns descend from the blunderbuss and the muzzle-loading fowling pieces carried by sportsmen from the 17th century onward. The 19th century brought breech-loading, hammerless actions, and paper and later plastic cartridges, while John Browning's designs — the Winchester Model 1893/1897 pump guns and the Auto-5 semi-automatic — defined the modern forms. Shotguns remain among the most widely owned firearms worldwide.

Legal status

Regulation varies widely: many countries treat shotguns more leniently than rifled firearms because of their sporting role, while others restrict barrel length, capacity, or particular types. In the United States, short-barrelled shotguns are regulated under the National Firearms Act of 1934.
