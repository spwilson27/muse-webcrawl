Saint Lucian Creole (Kwéyòl) is a French-based creole language spoken by the majority of the population of Saint Lucia. It is a variety of Antillean Creole, closely related to the creoles spoken in Martinique, Guadeloupe, Dominica, and Grenada, and is mutually intelligible to a large extent with them.

History

The language developed during the period of French colonization of Saint Lucia, when enslaved Africans combined French vocabulary with African grammatical structures. Although Britain took permanent control of the island in 1814 and English became the official language, the French-based creole remained the vernacular of most Saint Lucians, particularly in rural areas. Use of Kwéyòl was long discouraged in schools, but since the late twentieth century it has undergone a revival, with an official orthography, radio broadcasting, and an annual festival, Jounen Kwéyòl (Creole Day), held each October.

Characteristics

Like other Antillean creoles, Saint Lucian Creole draws the great majority of its vocabulary from French, while its grammar differs sharply: verbs are uninflected, and tense, aspect, and mood are marked by preverbal particles such as ka (progressive/habitual), té (past), and ké (future). Plurality is commonly marked with sé ... la around the noun phrase. A standardized phonemic orthography writes the language largely as it sounds, so French-derived chat becomes chat "cat" but "tooth" is dan, from French dent.

Status

English remains Saint Lucia's sole official language, used in government, law, and education, while Kwéyòl predominates in informal speech, folk culture, and political campaigning. Efforts to raise its status include creole literacy programmes and a translation of the New Testament. See also Antillean Creole and Saint Lucia.
