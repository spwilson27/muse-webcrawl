S cell

S cells are a type of enteroendocrine cell found in the epithelium of the small intestine, concentrated in the duodenum and jejunum. They are responsible for secreting the hormone secretin.

Function

S cells sense the acidity of the intestinal lumen. When acidic chyme arrives from the stomach and lowers duodenal pH below about 4.5, S cells release secretin into the bloodstream. Secretin stimulates the pancreas and biliary ducts to secrete a bicarbonate-rich fluid that neutralises the acid, promotes secretion of pancreatic digestive enzymes, and inhibits gastric acid secretion and gastric emptying. This negative feedback loop protects the duodenal mucosa and creates the near-neutral pH at which pancreatic enzymes work best.

History

Secretin itself was the first hormone ever identified: in 1902 William Bayliss and Ernest Starling showed that acid in the duodenum released a blood-borne chemical messenger that stimulated pancreatic secretion, coining the concept of hormonal signalling. The S cell as its source was characterised later with immunohistochemistry and electron microscopy, which showed the cells' characteristic secretory granules.

Characteristics

S cells are flask-shaped "open" enteroendocrine cells whose apical surface reaches the gut lumen, letting them sample luminal contents directly. They contain membrane-bound secretory granules, denser and more uniform than those of neighbouring enteroendocrine types. Conditions that alter gastric acid load, such as Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, can drive increased secretin release.
