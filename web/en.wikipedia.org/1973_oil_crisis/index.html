The 1973 oil crisis, or first oil shock, began in October 1973 when the Arab members of OPEC, organised as OAPEC, proclaimed an oil embargo against nations that had supported Israel during the Yom Kippur War. The embargo, combined with production cuts, caused the price of oil to rise roughly fourfold, from about US$3 per barrel to nearly $12, and triggered shortages, rationing and economic disruption across the industrialised world until the embargo was lifted in March 1974.

Background

By the early 1970s Western economies had become heavily dependent on cheap Middle Eastern oil, while American domestic production had peaked in 1970. The Tehran and Tripoli agreements of 1971 raised prices modestly, and the devaluation of the US dollar further reduced producers' real income. When Egypt and Syria attacked Israel on 6 October 1973 and the United States resupplied Israel during the war, the Arab oil ministers voted to cut production and embargo shipments to the United States, the Netherlands and other countries.

Effects

In the United States, the embargo produced long queues at petrol stations, odd–even rationing based on licence plates, a national maximum speed limit of 55 mph, and year-round daylight saving time as energy-saving measures. European governments banned Sunday driving and restricted heating. Japan, which depended on imports for nearly all its oil, was hit especially hard. The crisis contributed to stagflation — simultaneous inflation and recession — in much of the West and ended the long post-war economic boom.

Long-term consequences

The crisis led to the creation of the International Energy Agency and national strategic petroleum reserves, spurred fuel-efficiency standards and smaller cars, accelerated North Sea and Alaskan oil development, and prompted lasting investment in nuclear power in countries such as France. It also permanently increased OPEC's influence over world energy markets.
