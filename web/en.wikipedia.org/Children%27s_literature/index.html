Children's literature

Children's literature comprises books, magazines and poems written for, or enjoyed by, children. It ranges from picture books for the very young through early readers and chapter books to young adult fiction. Modern children's literature is often divided by age range and by genre, and it forms a major sector of publishing worldwide.

History

Before the eighteenth century, children read material written mainly for adults, such as fables and religious instruction. John Newbery's A Little Pretty Pocket-Book (1744) is often credited as the first book produced specifically for children's entertainment. The nineteenth century brought the Brothers Grimm's fairy tales, Hans Christian Andersen, and Lewis Carroll's Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865), widely seen as a landmark of playful, non-didactic writing for children.

Twentieth century and beyond

Twentieth-century classics include works by A. A. Milne, Beatrix Potter, Dr. Seuss, Roald Dahl and Maurice Sendak, while series such as J. K. Rowling's Harry Potter demonstrated the enormous global market for children's and young adult fiction. Picture books, poetry, and graphic novels for children have all gained critical recognition.

Awards

Major honours include the Newbery and Caldecott Medals in the United States, the Carnegie and Kate Greenaway Medals in the United Kingdom, and the international Hans Christian Andersen Award.
