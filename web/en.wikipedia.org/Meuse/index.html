The Meuse (French: Meuse; Dutch and German: Maas) is a major European river rising in France and flowing through Belgium and the Netherlands before draining into the North Sea from the Rhine–Meuse–Scheldt delta. It is 925 km (575 miles) long and has a drainage basin of about 34,500 km². The river gave its name to the Mosasaurus, the giant marine reptile whose fossils were found near Maastricht in 1764.

Course

The Meuse rises on the Langres plateau at Pouilly-en-Bassigny, France, and flows north past Verdun, Sedan, and Charleville-Mézières, then into Belgium, where it passes Dinant, Namur (where the Sambre joins it), and Liège. It crosses into the Netherlands near Maastricht, flows past Venlo, and in the delta merges with distributaries of the Rhine — including the Waal at the Afgedamde Maas — before reaching the sea via the Hollands Diep and Haringvliet. Since the Delta Works, its main outflow is managed with the Rhine's.

History

The Meuse valley was a corridor of early industry — coal and steel around Liège and Charleroi — and a repeated invasion route: the Battles of Verdun (1916) on its French course, the German breakthrough at Sedan (1870 and 1940), and the Battle of the Bulge crossings of 1944. The 1993 and 1995 floods in the Netherlands prompted the "Room for the River" programme. The river's name is also embedded in French wine country (Côtes de Meuse) and in the Meuse valley fog disaster of 1930, a landmark air-pollution event.

Navigation

Canalised in France and Belgium, the Meuse is navigable for barges for most of its length and linked by canals to the Rhine, Scheldt, and French canal networks. The Albert Canal (1939) provides a direct link from Liège to Antwerp.

Tributaries

Principal tributaries include the Sambre, Ourthe, Lesse, Chiers, Semois, and the Roer (Rur). In the Netherlands the Niers and Dommel also join its basin.
