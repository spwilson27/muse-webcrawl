Freehold (law)

A freehold, in English common law, is an estate in land of uncertain duration — as opposed to a leasehold, which is for a fixed term. Historically, freehold estates were those held by a free tenure rather than a villein (servile) tenure, and the concept of "freehold" remains fundamental in property law in England and Wales and in other common-law jurisdictions.

Types of freehold estate

The principal freehold estates are:

  • Fee simple absolute — the largest estate, potentially lasting forever, freely transferable, and inheritable; the closest common-law equivalent of outright ownership.
  • Fee tail — an estate limited to the holder's direct (lineal) descendants, historically used to keep land in a family; largely abolished or restricted in most jurisdictions.
  • Life estate — lasting only for the lifetime of a specified person, after which the land passes to a remainderman or reverts to the grantor.

Freehold and leasehold

In modern English land law, only two estates in land exist in law: the fee simple absolute in possession (freehold) and the term of years absolute (leasehold). Owners of leasehold property — common for flats in England and Wales — hold the property for a fixed term under a lease, often paying ground rent to the freeholder, a distinction that has generated continuing political controversy and reform proposals. In Scotland, feudal tenure was abolished with effect from 2004, leaving a system closer to outright ownership.
