Plagiocephaly

Plagiocephaly, also known as flat head syndrome, is a condition characterized by an asymmetrical distortion (flattening of one side) of the skull. The term derives from the Greek words plagios (oblique) and kephale (head). It is a common finding at birth and can be caused by a restrictive intrauterine environment. It may also describe the opposite side pattern caused when rapidly growing parts of the brain push against areas of the skull that do not grow as fast.

Causes

Most cases are deformational (positional) plagiocephaly, arising when an infant spends prolonged time lying on one side of the head. Rates rose notably after safe-sleep campaigns recommended placing babies on their backs to reduce the risk of sudden infant death syndrome. A smaller proportion of cases are synostotic, caused by the premature fusion of one or more cranial sutures (craniosynostosis), which requires different management. Congenital muscular torticollis, in which a tightened neck muscle biases head position, is a frequent contributing factor.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical, based on the characteristic head shape — often a parallelogram form when viewed from above in positional cases — sometimes supplemented by skull measurements or imaging when synostosis is suspected. Mild positional plagiocephaly often resolves with repositioning and increased supervised "tummy time". More persistent cases may be treated with physiotherapy for associated torticollis or with a custom cranial remoulding orthosis (helmet) during the months of rapid skull growth.

Prognosis

Positional plagiocephaly is primarily a cosmetic concern and usually improves as the child grows, spends more time upright, and develops motor skills. Synostotic forms may require surgical correction to permit normal brain and skull growth.
