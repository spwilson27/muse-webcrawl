Alfred Jewel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Material: Gold, enamel, rock crystal

Created: Late 9th century

Discovered: 1693, North Petherton, Somerset

Present location: Ashmolean Museum, Oxford

The Alfred Jewel is a small Anglo-Saxon ornament made of gold, cloisonné enamel and rock crystal, dating to the reign of Alfred the Great, king of Wessex (871–899). It is one of the most famous survivals of Anglo-Saxon craftsmanship and is now kept in the Ashmolean Museum in Oxford.

Description

The jewel is tear-drop shaped, about 6.4 cm long. A translucent plaque of rock crystal covers a cloisonné enamel image of a figure — possibly representing Sight, or a saint, or Christ — holding two sceptre-like plants. Around the edge runs a gold frame inscribed in Old English: + ÆLFRED MEC HEHT GEWYRCAN, "Alfred ordered me to be made". The base terminates in a hollow socket decorated with a beast's head, suggesting the object fitted onto the end of a rod or staff.

Interpretation

The jewel is widely believed to be an æstel — a pointer or reading aid of the kind Alfred is recorded as having sent, together with his translation of Pope Gregory's Pastoral Care, to each bishopric in his kingdom, each said to be worth fifty mancuses. Several similar but plainer jewels have since been found, supporting this theory. The Alfred Jewel was discovered in 1693 at North Petherton in Somerset, only a few miles from Athelney, where Alfred took refuge from the Vikings in 878.
