Zoroaster, also known as Zarathustra (Avestan: Zaraθuštra), was an Iranian prophet who founded the religion that bears his name, Zoroastrianism. He reformed the ancient Iranian religion into the worship of one supreme god, Ahura Mazda, and composed the Gathas, hymns that form the oldest core of the Avesta.

Life and dating

Zoroaster's dates are among the most debated in religious history. Greek authors placed him thousands of years before their own time; Zoroastrian tradition dates him "258 years before Alexander" (i.e. c. 628–551 BCE), while many modern scholars, judging by the archaic language of the Gathas, place him around 1000 BCE in eastern Iran or Central Asia. Tradition records that he was born to Pourušaspa and Dugdōwā, trained as a priest (zaotar), received his revelation from Ahura Mazda at the age of thirty, and after years of rejection won the patronage of King Vishtaspa, whose court adopted the new faith.

Teachings

In the Gathas, Zoroaster proclaims Ahura Mazda as the uncreated creator and sets out a cosmic struggle between asha (truth, order) and druj (the lie). Human beings must choose freely between good and evil, guided by the ethical triad of good thoughts, good words, and good deeds, and face judgment after death at the Chinvat Bridge. His reform elevated monotheism and moral dualism and decisively shaped the state religions of the Achaemenid, Parthian, and Sasanian empires.

Legacy

Zoroaster was famous in the classical world as a sage and supposed inventor of magic and astrology. Through Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, elements associated with his teaching — heaven and hell, angels, final judgment, and resurrection — entered Western religious thought, while Friedrich Nietzsche borrowed his Greek name for the prophet of Thus Spoke Zarathustra.
