Hamiltonian mechanics

Hamiltonian mechanics is a reformulation of classical mechanics introduced by William Rowan Hamilton in 1833–34, growing out of Lagrangian mechanics. Instead of generalised coordinates and velocities, it describes a system by generalised coordinates q and conjugate momenta p, evolving in phase space according to Hamilton's equations.

The Hamiltonian

The Hamiltonian function H(q, p, t) is obtained from the Lagrangian by a Legendre transformation, H = Σ piq̇i − L. For many systems it equals the total energy. The dynamics are governed by the canonical equations dqi/dt = ∂H/∂pi and dpi/dt = −∂H/∂qi, which are symmetric and first-order.

Properties

Phase-space flow preserves volume (Liouville's theorem) and the symplectic structure of the space. Canonical transformations, Poisson brackets, and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation all arise naturally in this framework. Symmetries of the Hamiltonian correspond directly to conserved quantities via Noether's theorem.

Applications

Hamiltonian mechanics underlies statistical mechanics and provides the bridge to quantum mechanics, where Poisson brackets are replaced by commutators and the Hamiltonian becomes the operator generating time evolution through the Schrödinger equation.
