Sainsbury Wing

The Sainsbury Wing is an extension of the National Gallery in London, England, located on the west side of the main building in Trafalgar Square. Designed by the American architects Robert Venturi and Denise Scott Brown of Venturi, Scott Brown and Associates, it opened in 1991 and houses the Gallery's collection of early Renaissance paintings. It is one of the most debated British buildings of the late twentieth century and a key work of architectural postmodernism. Following a major refurbishment and reconfiguration of its entrance sequence, the wing was renamed the Sainsbury Wing again upon its reopening in May 2025 as the Gallery's main entrance.

Origins and competition

The site west of the National Gallery, formerly occupied by a furniture store destroyed in the Blitz, had long been earmarked for an extension. A 1982 design competition was won by Ahrends, Burton and Koralek with a modernist scheme; after Prince Charles famously described the design as a "monstrous carbuncle on the face of a much-loved and elegant friend" in a 1984 speech, the scheme was abandoned. A new competition, combined with a donation from the Sainsbury family — Simon, David, and John Sainsbury — led to the appointment of Venturi, Scott Brown and Associates in 1987.

The Sainsbury Wing facade, echoing the classical language of the main Gallery building.

Architecture

The wing is clad in Portland stone to match the 1830s Gallery building by William Wilkins. Its east facade continues the main building's Corinthian pilasters, but the classical elements gradually flatten, shift rhythm, and dissolve toward the new entrance — a characteristically postmodern play on the classical language. Inside, the top-floor galleries are arranged as an enfilade of daylit rooms with grey pietra serena stone surrounds, modelled on Italian Renaissance church interiors, which display the early Renaissance collection in roughly chronological order. A grand staircase descends past a large window framing the square.

Reception and refurbishment

Reactions to the building were sharply divided on its opening: some critics praised its wit and contextual sensitivity, while others found its applied classicism mannered. In 2018 it was listed at Grade I, an unusually early listing for a postmodern building. A refurbishment by Selldorf Architects, completed in 2025 as part of the Gallery's bicentenary NG200 project, opened up the foyer, increased natural light, and made the Sainsbury Wing the principal entrance to the museum.
