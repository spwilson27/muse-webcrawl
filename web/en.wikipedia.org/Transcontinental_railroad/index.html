A transcontinental railroad or transcontinental railway is contiguous railroad trackage that crosses a continental land mass and has terminals at different oceans or continental borders. Such networks can be via the tracks of either a single railroad or over those owned or controlled by multiple railway companies along a continuous route.

The term is most famously associated with the first transcontinental railroad in the United States, completed in 1869, which connected the existing eastern rail network at Council Bluffs/Omaha, Nebraska with the Pacific coast at the Oakland Long Wharf in California. Its completion revolutionised the settlement and economy of the American West, cutting cross-country travel from months to about a week.

Construction of the US line

Authorised by the Pacific Railroad Acts of 1862 and 1864, construction was undertaken by two companies racing toward each other: the Central Pacific Railroad building east from Sacramento over the Sierra Nevada—largely with Chinese immigrant labour—and the Union Pacific Railroad building west from Omaha across the Great Plains, employing many Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans. The federal government subsidised the work with bonds and grants of public land along the right of way.

The two lines met at Promontory Summit, Utah, on 10 May 1869, where Leland Stanford drove the ceremonial golden spike. The event was announced nationwide by telegraph, synchronised with the spike's blows.

Other transcontinental railways

  • Canada: the Canadian Pacific Railway, completed in 1885 with the Last Spike at Craigellachie, British Columbia.
  • Russia: the Trans-Siberian Railway, built 1891–1916, linking Moscow with Vladivostok—the longest railway line in the world.
  • Australia: the Trans-Australian Railway, completed in 1917 across the Nullarbor Plain.
  • Panama: the Panama Railroad (1855), the first transcontinental line in the Americas, connecting the Atlantic and Pacific across the isthmus.

Impact

Transcontinental railroads bound continents together politically and economically, standardised time (railway time zones in North America date from 1883), enabled mass migration and trade, and profoundly displaced indigenous peoples whose lands the lines crossed.
