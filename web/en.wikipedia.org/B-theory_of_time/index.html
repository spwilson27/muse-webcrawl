B-theory of time

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The B-theory of time is the name given to one of two positions regarding the temporal ordering of events in the philosophy of time. B-theorists argue that the flow of time is only a subjective illusion of human consciousness, that the past, present, and future are equally real, and that time is tenseless: events are ordered by the non-indexical relations earlier than, simultaneous with, and later than, collectively called the B-series.

Background

The terminology derives from J. M. E. McTaggart's 1908 paper "The Unreality of Time," which distinguished the A-series (events ordered as past, present, or future) from the B-series (events ordered as earlier or later than one another). Defenders of the rival A-theory hold that the division of events into past, present, and future reflects an objective feature of reality, whereas B-theorists hold that such A-determinations depend on the perspective of an observer located within time.

Relation to physics

The B-theory is closely associated with eternalism and the block universe interpretation of spacetime suggested by the special theory of relativity. Because simultaneity is relative to an observer's frame of reference, there is no single objective "now" slicing the universe; events that are future relative to one observer may be past relative to another. Hermann Minkowski's formulation of spacetime is often taken to support this picture, though the inference from physics to metaphysics remains contested.

Arguments and objections

B-theorists offer paraphrase strategies for tensed language: a statement like "the meeting starts now" can be understood token-reflexively, in terms of the utterance's own temporal location, without positing objective tense. Critics object that the theory cannot account for temporal experience, the apparent asymmetry of time, or the special significance of the present. A-theorists such as presentists and growing-block theorists defend various forms of temporal becoming instead.
