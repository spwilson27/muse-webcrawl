Foucault pendulum

Foucault pendulum
Inventor: Léon Foucault
First demonstrated: 1851 (Paris Observatory; Panthéon)
Purpose: Demonstrate Earth's rotation
Rotation period: 24h / sin(latitude)

The Foucault pendulum is a simple device named after French physicist Léon Foucault, conceived as an experiment to demonstrate the rotation of the Earth. It is a tall pendulum free to swing in any vertical plane; its plane of oscillation appears to rotate, at a rate proportional to the sine of the latitude.

History

Foucault first demonstrated the effect in February 1851 at the Paris Observatory, then suspended a 28 kg bob on a 67 m wire from the dome of the Panthéon in Paris before an invited audience including Napoleon III. The rotation of the swing plane — about 11° per hour at Paris — was the first direct, tabletop-scale evidence of Earth's rotation.

Explanation

In the rotating frame of the Earth, the Coriolis effect deflects the pendulum's motion. At the poles the plane rotates fully once per sidereal day; at the equator it does not rotate at all; the sense of rotation reverses between hemispheres.

Installations

Foucault pendulums are popular museum exhibits worldwide, including at the United Nations Headquarters and the Griffith Observatory.
