Buried treasure

Buried treasure is a literary trope and a concept in folklore concerning caches of gold, silver, jewels, or other valuables hidden underground, usually by pirates or outlaws. The image of the buried pirate hoard — marked on a secret treasure map with an "X" — was popularised by nineteenth-century adventure fiction, most famously Robert Louis Stevenson's Treasure Island (1883) and Washington Irving's tales of Captain Kidd.

Historical basis

Although pirates rarely buried their loot, preferring to spend or divide it quickly, a few genuine cases exist. The pirate William Kidd is said to have buried treasure on Gardiners Island, New York, before his arrest in 1699; a cache was indeed recovered there and used as evidence at his trial. Other famous alleged hoards include the treasure of Lima, supposedly buried on Cocos Island, and the Oak Island "Money Pit" in Nova Scotia, which has attracted excavators since the 1790s without any confirmed discovery.

Real caches

Archaeologists routinely uncover deliberately buried valuables known as hoards, such as the Hoxne Hoard of Roman gold and silver found in England in 1992. Such deposits were often hidden in times of war or instability with the intention of later recovery. Modern enthusiasts search for lost caches with metal detectors, a hobby connected to geocaching and magnet fishing.

In popular culture

Buried treasure is a central device in works ranging from Edgar Allan Poe's "The Gold-Bug" to the films The Goonies and the Pirates of the Caribbean series. The enduring appeal lies in the combination of mystery, codes and maps, and the promise of sudden wealth.
