Canis Major is a constellation in the southern celestial hemisphere. In the second century it was included in Ptolemy's 48 constellations, and it remains one of the 88 modern constellations. Its name is Latin for "greater dog"; it represents the larger of the two hunting dogs that follow the constellation Orion the Hunter, the other being Canis Minor, the "lesser dog".

Canis Major contains Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, known as the "dog star". Because of its brightness, Sirius has played a prominent role in many cultures; its heliacal rising marked the annual flooding of the Nile in ancient Egypt and the "dog days" of summer in Greek and Roman tradition. The Milky Way passes through Canis Major, giving the constellation a wealth of open clusters and nebulae.

Notable stars

Sirius (Alpha Canis Majoris) is a binary system 8.6 light-years from Earth: the primary is a white main-sequence star and its companion, Sirius B, was the first white dwarf discovered. Adhara (Epsilon Canis Majoris) is a bright ultraviolet source; Wezen (Delta) is a yellow supergiant whose name derives from the Arabic for "weight"; Murzim (Beta) is a Beta Cephei variable; and Aludra (Eta) is a luminous blue supergiant. The red hypergiant VY Canis Majoris is among the largest known stars, with a radius hundreds of times that of the Sun. Within the constellation also lies the Canis Major Dwarf Galaxy, a disputed satellite galaxy of the Milky Way about 25,000 light-years away, discovered in 2003.

Deep-sky objects

The open cluster Messier 41 lies about four degrees south of Sirius and contains roughly a hundred stars some 2,300 light-years away. Other objects include the open cluster NGC 2362 around Tau Canis Majoris and the emission nebula NGC 2359, nicknamed "Thor's Helmet", a bubble blown by a massive Wolf–Rayet star.

Mythology

In Greek mythology Canis Major was identified with Laelaps, the dog that never failed to catch its prey, given by Zeus to Europa; in another tradition the constellation is the dog of Orion. Its counterpart Canis Minor represents the second hound. The pairing appears across many astronomies: Mesopotamian texts associated the stars with a bow and arrow, and Chinese astronomy distributed them among several asterisms including Junshi, the military market.
