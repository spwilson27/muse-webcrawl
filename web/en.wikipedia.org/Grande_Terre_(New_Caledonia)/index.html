Grande Terre is the largest island of New Caledonia, a French overseas collectivity in the southwest Pacific Ocean. It accounts for the great majority of the territory's land area and population, and contains the capital and largest city, Nouméa, on its southeastern coast.

Geography

The island is long and narrow, stretching roughly 400 kilometres from northwest to southeast and between 50 and 70 kilometres wide. A mountain range, the Chaîne Centrale, runs along its spine and divides a humid, lush east coast from a drier west coast of savanna and plains. The highest peak is Mont Panié at 1,629 metres. Grande Terre is surrounded by one of the world's largest lagoon systems and an extensive barrier reef, parts of which are inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Geology

Grande Terre is a fragment of the ancient supercontinent Gondwana, separated from Australia tens of millions of years ago. A large portion of the island is covered by ultramafic rock rich in nickel, giving the soils their characteristic red colour and making New Caledonia one of the world's leading nickel producers. The island's long isolation has produced remarkable levels of endemism in its flora and fauna.

History

The island was inhabited by the Kanak people, the indigenous Melanesian population, for millennia before European contact. Captain James Cook sighted and named New Caledonia in 1774, and France annexed the territory in 1853, establishing Nouméa (as Port-de-France) in 1854 and using the island as a penal colony. Grande Terre remains the political and economic centre of the collectivity, which has held a series of independence referendums under the Nouméa Accord.
