In ancient Roman religion and mythology, Terra (Latin for "earth") — more commonly Tellus or Terra Mater ("Mother Earth") — is the primordial goddess personifying the Earth and a deity of fertility and agricultural productivity. She is the Roman counterpart of the Greek Gaia.

Cult and worship

Tellus received a temple on the Carinae in Rome, vowed in 268 BC after an earthquake during the Picentine War. Her festivals were agricultural: the Fordicidia on 15 April, in which pregnant cows were sacrificed to secure fertility of crops and herds, and the movable Sementivae in January for the sowing season. Oaths were sworn by Tellus, and she was invoked with Jupiter at the beginnings of histories — Livy, for example, names her among the deities witnessing treaties.

Mythology and iconography

As a primordial deity, Terra figures in Roman cosmogony as the counterpart of Caelus (the Sky): from their union came Saturn, Ops, and the other Titans, while alone she produced the sea (Pontus) and the mountains. In art she is shown as a matronly woman amid fruits, a cornucopia, and children representing the seasons, most famously on the Ara Pacis in Rome, whose "Tellus panel" depicts a mother goddess with two infants surrounded by symbols of earth's abundance.
