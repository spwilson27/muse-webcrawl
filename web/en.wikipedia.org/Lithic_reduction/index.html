Lithic reduction

Lithic reduction is the process of shaping stone into tools by removing flakes from a larger piece, called a core, through percussion or pressure. Practised for more than three million years, it is the defining technology of the Stone Age and the means by which early humans and their ancestors produced handaxes, blades, points and scrapers.

Mechanics

Reduction exploits the conchoidal fracture of fine-grained, brittle rocks such as flint, chert, obsidian and quartzite. Striking a core near its edge with a hammerstone (hard percussion) or an antler billet (soft percussion), or applying pressure with a pointed tool (pressure flaking), detaches a flake with a characteristic bulb of percussion. The knapper controls flake size and shape by preparing striking platforms and reading the geometry of the core.

Reduction strategies

Archaeologists recognise several strategies:

  • Oldowan reduction — simple choppers and flakes struck with a few blows, from about 2.6 million years ago.
  • Acheulean shaping — bifacial thinning to produce symmetrical handaxes.
  • Levallois technique — careful preparation of a core so that a single blow detaches a flake of predetermined shape.
  • Blade production — systematic removal of long, parallel-sided blades from cylindrical cores, characteristic of the Upper Palaeolithic.
  • Bipolar reduction — resting the core on an anvil and striking from above, common where raw material is scarce.

Study

Debris from reduction (debitage) dominates most Stone Age sites. Through refitting, use-wear analysis and experimental knapping, archaeologists reconstruct the chaîne opératoire — the sequence of decisions a knapper made — and infer aspects of prehistoric cognition and learning.
