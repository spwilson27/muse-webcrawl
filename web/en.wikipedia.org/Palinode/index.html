Palinode

A palinode is an ode, song, or poem in which the author retracts or recants a view or sentiment expressed in an earlier work. The term derives from the Greek palin ("again") and ode ("song"), and is sometimes applied to prose retractions as well.

History

The most famous early palinode is attributed to the Greek lyric poet Stesichorus (c. 630–555 BC). According to legend preserved by Plato in the Phaedrus, Stesichorus had composed a poem blaming Helen of Troy for the Trojan War and was struck blind for his impiety. He then wrote a recantation — declaring that Helen never sailed to Troy at all — and recovered his sight. The opening lines, "That story is not true," became proverbial.

Later examples

Geoffrey Chaucer ended The Canterbury Tales with a "Retraction" in which he asked forgiveness for his more frivolous works. John Donne and other early modern poets wrote palinodes, and the form was used by later writers including Robert Southey. In a looser sense, any formal poetic recantation — including apologies for earlier verses — may be called a palinode.
