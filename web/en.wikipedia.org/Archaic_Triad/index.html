The Archaic Triad is a hypothetical grouping of the three earliest gods of Roman religion — Jupiter, Mars and Quirinus — proposed by the scholar Georges Dumézil as the Roman expression of his trifunctional hypothesis about Indo-European religion. The three deities corresponded respectively to the functions of sacred sovereignty, warfare and production or fertility.

The three gods

Jupiter, god of the sky and supreme ruler, represented the sovereign and juridical function, exercised with his priest the flamen Dialis. Mars, god of war but also of agricultural protection, represented military force, served by the flamen Martialis. Quirinus, an obscure deified form associated with Romulus and the assembled citizen body, represented the productive populace and was served by the flamen Quirinalis. These three priests were the flamines maiores, the senior of Rome's fifteen flamens, and their order of precedence preserved the ranking of the triad.

Dumézil's hypothesis

Dumézil argued that the triad reflected an inherited Indo-European ideology dividing society into priests, warriors and producers, with parallels in Vedic India and Norse mythology. The Archaic Triad was later overshadowed by the Capitoline Triad of Jupiter, Juno and Minerva, which incorporated Etruscan influence. Modern scholars debate how far the trifunctional scheme was a genuine feature of early Roman cult rather than a scholarly reconstruction, but the grouping of the three flamines maiores is securely attested in Roman sources.
