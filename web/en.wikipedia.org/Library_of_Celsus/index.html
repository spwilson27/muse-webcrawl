The ornate facade of the Library of Celsus is one of the most famous remains of the ancient world.

The Library of Celsus is an ancient Roman building in Ephesus, Anatolia, now part of Selçuk, Turkey. It was built in honour of the Roman Senator Tiberius Julius Celsus Polemaeanus by his son Gaius Julius Aquila Polemaeanus, and completed around AD 114–117. Celsus is buried in a crypt beneath the library. The building stored some 12,000 scrolls and was the third-largest library of the ancient world, after those of Alexandria and Pergamum.

Architecture

The east-facing marble facade, richly decorated with carved ornament and statues personifying Wisdom, Knowledge, Intelligence and Virtue, was carefully reconstructed by Austrian archaeologists in the 1970s. The single large rectangular reading room (about 17 by 10 metres) had niches in its thick walls for scroll storage in armaria; double walls behind the niches helped protect the scrolls from humidity. Statues from the facade are now in Vienna.

History

The interior was destroyed by fire in the 3rd century (often dated to a Gothic invasion in AD 262 or 268), though the facade survived. In late antiquity a fountain was built in front of it. The facade became an iconic image of classical antiquity and appears on the reverse of the Turkish 20-lira banknote (2005–2009 series).
