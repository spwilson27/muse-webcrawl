Fictive kinship

Fictive kinship
FieldAnthropology, sociology
DefinitionKinship ties not based on blood or marriage
ExamplesGodparenthood, compadrazgo, milk kinship, blood brotherhood, chosen family

Fictive kinship is a term used by anthropologists and sociologists to describe forms of kinship and social relationship that are not based on consanguinity (blood ties) or affinity (marriage ties), but are nevertheless treated as kinship — with the rights, obligations and emotional weight of family relationships. Fictive kin may be addressed with kinship terms ("auntie", "brother"), included in family rituals, and relied upon for childcare, material support and social belonging.

Forms

Examples across cultures include godparenthood and the Latin American institution of compadrazgo, in which co-parents are bound by ritual sponsorship; milk kinship in Islamic and other traditions, in which a child nursed by a woman becomes kin to her and her children; blood brotherhood pacts; adoption and fosterage in societies where child circulation is common; sworn brotherhoods; and the "fictive kin" networks documented in urban communities, where close friends and neighbours exchange the services and support of family. In LGBTQ communities, "chosen family" describes similar deliberately constructed kin networks.

Functions

Fictive kinship extends support networks beyond blood relatives, creates alliances between families and groups, integrates outsiders, and provides resilience in the face of migration, poverty or family disruption. Studies of African American communities, notably Carol Stack's All Our Kin (1974), showed how extensive fictive-kin networks organise childcare, money and goods among households. Scholars debate the term "fictive" itself, since such ties are experienced as entirely real by participants; terms such as "voluntary kin" or "kinship by choice" are sometimes preferred.
