Brachiation
DefinitionArboreal locomotion by swinging hand over hand beneath branches
Primary practitionersGibbons and siamangs (family Hylobatidae)
Typical speedUp to ~55 km/h (gibbons); leaps up to 15 m
Key adaptationsLong arms, hook-like hands, rotating wrists, short lumbar spine
A gibbon swinging beneath branches using continuous brachiation

Brachiation (from Latin brachium, "arm") is a form of arboreal locomotion in which primates swing from tree limb to tree limb using only their arms, the body suspended below the hands and propelled by pendulum-like motion. The lesser apes — gibbons and siamangs — are the true specialists, and brachiation is considered their primary means of travel through the forest canopy.

Mechanics

In continuous brachiation the hands grip successive supports in alternation without a free-flight phase; in ricochetal brachiation, seen in fast-moving gibbons, the body is airborne between handholds. The motion works like an inverted pendulum, exchanging kinetic and potential energy much as in human walking, which makes it efficient for its speed. Gibbons can move at up to about 55 km/h and cover gaps of 15 metres in a single leap.

Anatomy

Brachiators have long forelimbs relative to body size, long curved fingers forming hooks, reduced thumbs that do not interfere with the hook grip, short fingernails, ball-and-socket wrist joints permitting wide rotation, and a short, stiff lower back. These features contrast with those of the great apes: chimpanzees, gorillas, and orangutans climb and occasionally brachiate — orangutans use a slow, four-limbed variant sometimes called quadrumanous scrambling — but none matches the gibbon's specialisation. Spider monkeys brachiate with the aid of a prehensile tail acting as a fifth limb.

Evolutionary significance

Brachiation has featured in theories of human evolution: the brachiating ancestor hypothesis proposed that features humans share with apes — upright trunk, mobile shoulder, grasping hands — arose from an arboreal, arm-swinging stage before bipedalism. Modern palaeoanthropology treats the relationship more cautiously, but brachiation remains an important model in studies of primate locomotor evolution.
