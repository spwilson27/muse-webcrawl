Howard Scott

Howard Scott (1 April 1890 – 1 January 1970) was an American engineer and the founder of the Technocracy movement in North America. In the aftermath of the Great Depression, his proposal to replace the price system with an energy-based accounting system, administered by engineers and scientists, briefly attracted mass attention across the United States and Canada.

Howard Scott led the technocracy movement during the 1930s.

Career

Scott's background before 1919 was obscure; he worked in various engineering and industrial jobs and cultivated an image of practical expertise. In 1919–1920 he organised the Technical Alliance in New York, whose associates included academics interested in the ideas of Thorstein Veblen, who had suggested that engineers might one day run industry. The Alliance began an "Energy Survey of North America" intended to document the continent's physical resources and productive capacity.

Technocracy

In 1932, at Columbia University, Scott's Committee on Technocracy publicised the survey's findings, arguing that mechanisation was destroying jobs faster than new ones were created and that the price system could no longer distribute abundance. Scott's radio addresses and a celebrated speech at New York's Hotel Pierre in January 1933 made "technocracy" a newspaper sensation — though hostile press coverage of the speech, and disputes over Scott's credentials, quickly damaged the movement's standing.

In 1933 Scott founded Technocracy Incorporated, which promoted the creation of a "Technate" of North America run on energy accounting, with citizens receiving energy certificates rather than money. The movement persisted for decades as a membership organisation, and Scott remained its director general until his death in 1970.
