Retributive justice is a legal and philosophical theory that considers proportionate punishment a morally acceptable and satisfactory response to crime. It holds that when an offender breaks the law, justice requires that they suffer in return, and that the response to a crime is proportional to the offence — a principle summarised in the ancient formula lex talionis, "an eye for an eye". Retribution is distinct from revenge: it is impersonal, administered by a lawful authority, limited by proportionality, and directed only at wrongdoing that has been proven.

The theory is often associated with Immanuel Kant, who argued that punishment is justified because the criminal deserves it, and that failing to punish would treat the criminal as a means rather than an end. Hegel viewed punishment as the annulment of the crime, restoring the legal order the offence denied.

The scales of justice symbolise proportionate, measured punishment.

Principles

  • Desert: those who commit wrongful acts morally deserve to suffer in proportion to their wrongdoing.
  • Proportionality: punishment must fit the severity of the offence — neither excessive nor trivially lenient.
  • Equality: similar offences by similar offenders should receive similar punishments.

Retribution and other theories

Retributivism contrasts with consequentialist justifications of punishment, such as deterrence, incapacitation and rehabilitation, which justify punishment by its future benefits, and with restorative justice, which focuses on repairing harm to victims and communities. Many modern sentencing systems combine retributive limits with consequentialist aims.

Criticism

Critics argue that retributivism can rationalise harsh punishments, that "desert" is difficult to measure, and that it neglects social causes of crime and the needs of victims. Defenders, such as Michael Moore, respond that retribution is a genuine moral intuition and that it constrains state power by forbidding punishment beyond what is deserved.
