Cross-hatch

Cross-hatching is an artistic technique used in drawing, engraving, etching, and other printmaking to create tonal or shading effects. The artist first draws a set of closely spaced parallel lines (hatching) and then overlays one or more additional sets of parallel lines at an angle to the first. The denser and more layered the lines, the darker the resulting area appears, allowing a full range of values to be built up from black lines on a light ground.

Technique and history

Hatching developed in medieval woodcut and reached sophistication in the engravings of Renaissance masters such as Albrecht Dürer and, in pen drawing, Leonardo da Vinci and Rembrandt van Rijn, whose etchings use cross-hatching extensively to model form and light. Variations include contour hatching, where lines follow the shape of the subject, and scribble hatching. Because printed lines cannot easily reproduce continuous grey tones, cross-hatching remained essential to printmaking and illustration — and survives in comic art and technical illustration — long after the invention of halftone screening.
