Cosmological constant

In cosmology, the cosmological constant (usually denoted by the Greek capital letter lambda, Λ) is the simplest possible form of dark energy. It was originally introduced by Albert Einstein in 1917 as a modification of his field equations of general relativity, to achieve a stationary universe. Einstein later abandoned the concept after the observation of the expansion of the universe, but it returned to prominence in the 1990s when observations showed that cosmic expansion is accelerating.

Role in the field equations

Adding the term Λgμν to Einstein's field equations produces an effective repulsive gravitational force proportional to distance. In the standard ΛCDM model, the cosmological constant accounts for roughly 68% of the total energy density of the present-day universe, with dark matter making up most of the remainder besides ordinary matter.

The cosmological constant problem

Interpreting Λ as the energy density of the vacuum leads to a notorious discrepancy: naive quantum field theory estimates of vacuum energy exceed the observed value by many orders of magnitude — often quoted as up to 10120 — one of the largest disagreements between theory and observation in physics. Whether the acceleration is instead caused by a dynamical field such as quintessence remains an open question.
