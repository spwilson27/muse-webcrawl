Logical positivism

Logical positivism, later called logical empiricism, is a philosophical movement whose central thesis was the verification principle (also called the verifiability criterion of meaning): a statement is cognitively meaningful only if it is either analytic or can be verified through empirical observation. The movement emerged in the 1920s around the Vienna Circle — including Moritz Schlick, Rudolf Carnap, Otto Neurath and Friedrich Waismann — and the Berlin Circle of Hans Reichenbach.

Doctrine

Influenced by Ludwig Wittgenstein's Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus and by the logic of Frege and Russell, the positivists sought a scientific world-conception: metaphysics, theology and much traditional philosophy were dismissed as meaningless; philosophy was to become the logical analysis of the language of science. A. J. Ayer's Language, Truth and Logic (1936) popularised the view in English.

Criticism and legacy

The verification principle faced the objection that it is itself neither analytic nor verifiable, and Karl Popper's falsifiability was proposed as a criterion of science rather than of meaning. Members dispersed under Nazism, principally to the United States, where logical empiricism profoundly shaped analytic philosophy and philosophy of science even as its strict programme was abandoned.
