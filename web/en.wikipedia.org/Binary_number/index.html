A binary number is a number expressed in the base-2 numeral system or binary numeral system, a method for representing numbers that uses only two symbols for the natural numbers: typically "0" (zero) and "1" (one). The base-2 system is a positional notation with a radix of 2. Each digit is referred to as a bit, or binary digit.

Because of its straightforward implementation in digital electronic circuitry using logic gates, the binary system is used by almost all modern computers and computer-based devices, as a preferred system of use, over various other human techniques of communication, because of the simplicity of the language and the noise immunity in physical implementation.

Counting and conversion

In binary, as in decimal, the value of a digit depends on its position: successive places are worth 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and so on. Thus the binary number 1011 equals 8 + 0 + 2 + 1 = 11 in decimal. Counting proceeds 0, 1, 10, 11, 100, 101, ... Fractions can be expressed after a binary point with places worth 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, though many decimal fractions (such as 0.1) have no exact binary representation — a recurring source of floating-point rounding surprises.

Arithmetic

Binary addition and multiplication follow simpler tables than decimal: addition has the single carry rule 1 + 1 = 10, and multiplication reduces to copying and shifting. Negative numbers are typically stored in two's complement form, and computers group bits into bytes (8 bits) and words.

History

Binary arithmetic was described in ancient China in connection with the hexagrams of the I Ching, and by the Indian scholar Piṅgala. In the West, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz published the modern binary system in his 1703 paper "Explication de l'Arithmétique Binaire". George Boole's algebra of logic (1854) and Claude Shannon's 1937 thesis showing that Boolean algebra could design switching circuits laid the theoretical foundations for binary digital computing.
