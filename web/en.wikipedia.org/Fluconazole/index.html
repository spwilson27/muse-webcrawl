Fluconazole

Fluconazole is an antifungal medication used for a number of fungal infections, sold under the brand name Diflucan among others. This includes candidiasis, blastomycosis, coccidioidomycosis, cryptococcosis, histoplasmosis, dermatophytosis, and tinea versicolor. It is also used to prevent candidiasis in those who are at high risk such as following organ transplantation, low birth weight babies, and those with low blood neutrophil counts. It is given either by mouth or by injection into a vein.

Medical uses and mechanism

Fluconazole is a triazole antifungal. Like other azole antifungals, it works by inhibiting the fungal cytochrome P450 enzyme 14α-demethylase, which blocks the synthesis of ergosterol, an essential component of the fungal cell membrane. It is fungistatic against most Candida species and is notable for excellent oral bioavailability and penetration into cerebrospinal fluid, making it a mainstay of treatment and long-term suppression for cryptococcal meningitis, particularly in people with HIV/AIDS. A single 150 mg oral dose is a standard treatment for uncomplicated vaginal candidiasis.

Adverse effects and interactions

Common side effects include nausea, headache, abdominal pain, dizziness and rash; more serious effects include liver toxicity, QT prolongation and severe skin reactions. Because it inhibits human cytochrome P450 enzymes (notably CYP2C9, CYP2C19 and CYP3A4), fluconazole interacts with many medicines, including warfarin and some statins. Use in pregnancy, particularly in the first trimester at high doses, is associated with birth defects.

History

Fluconazole was developed by Pfizer, patented in 1981, and came into commercial use in 1990 (approved in the United States as Diflucan in 1990). It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is available as a generic medication worldwide.
