Magdalena Carmen Frida Kahlo y Calderón (6 July 1907 – 13 July 1954) was a Mexican painter known for her many portraits, self-portraits, and works inspired by the nature and artifacts of Mexico. Inspired by the country's popular culture, she employed a naïve folk art style to explore questions of identity, postcolonialism, gender, class, and race in Mexican society.

Life

Kahlo was born in Coyoacán, Mexico City, the daughter of a German immigrant photographer father and a Mexican mother. At eighteen she was gravely injured when a streetcar collided with the bus she was riding; she suffered fractures of the spine, pelvis, and leg, and endured pain and repeated surgeries for the rest of her life. She began painting during her convalescence. In 1929 she married the muralist Diego Rivera; their marriage was turbulent, ending in divorce and remarriage. Kahlo's work was largely unappreciated during her lifetime, though she exhibited in Mexico, New York, and Paris, and was the first Mexican artist whose work was acquired by the Louvre.

Work

Of her 143 paintings, 55 are self-portraits, in which she depicted her own body, injuries, and emotional life with unflinching directness. Notable works include The Two Fridas (1939), Self-Portrait with Thorn Necklace and Hummingbird (1940), The Broken Column (1944), and Henry Ford Hospital (1932). She drew on Mexican folk imagery, retablos, pre-Columbian mythology, and Catholic iconography.

Legacy

Beginning in the 1970s, Kahlo was rediscovered by feminist and Chicano art historians, and she has become an icon of Mexican national and indigenous traditions and of the feminist movement. Her Blue House in Coyoacán is now the Frida Kahlo Museum.
