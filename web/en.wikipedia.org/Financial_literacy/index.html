Financial literacy

Financial literacy is the possession of skills, knowledge, and behaviors that allow an individual to make informed decisions regarding money: earning, budgeting, saving, investing, borrowing, and protecting against risk and fraud. The OECD defines it as a combination of awareness, knowledge, skill, attitude, and behavior necessary to make sound financial decisions and ultimately achieve individual financial well-being.

Measurement

The best-known measures are the "Big Three" questions developed by Annamaria Lusardi and Olivia Mitchell, testing understanding of compound interest, inflation, and risk diversification. Surveys using these questions (including the S&P Global Financial Literacy Survey) find that only about one in three adults worldwide is financially literate, with wide variation by country, gender, and education. Low literacy correlates with higher borrowing costs, less retirement saving, and greater financial fragility.

Education and policy

Governments promote financial literacy through school curricula mandates, national strategies coordinated by bodies such as the OECD's International Network on Financial Education, and consumer-protection agencies. A growing research literature finds that classroom financial education produces modest but real improvements in knowledge and some behaviors, with effects strengthened by "just-in-time" delivery and by rules of thumb rather than abstract instruction.

Criticisms

Critics argue that emphasizing individual literacy can shift responsibility from product design and regulation to consumers, and that education alone cannot overcome low income, complex predatory products, or cognitive biases. This has prompted complementary approaches: simplified disclosure, default options (such as auto-enrollment in pensions), and behavioral "nudges."
