The Choctaw (Choctaw: Chahta) are a Native American people originally based in the Southeastern United States, in present-day Mississippi, Alabama and Louisiana. They were one of the "Five Civilized Tribes". Their language belongs to the Muskogean family. Today the largest group is the Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma, the third-largest federally recognised tribe in the United States.

History

The Choctaw descend in part from the Mississippian culture centred on sites such as Nanih Waiya, their sacred mother mound. Allied with the French in the 18th century against the Chickasaw and British, they became major agricultural producers and adopted a constitutional government. By the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek (1830) — the first removal treaty under the Indian Removal Act — they ceded some 11 million acres and most Choctaw removed west in 1831–34, an ordeal that contributed the word to the broader Trail of Tears. Those who stayed in Mississippi became the ancestors of the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians.

Later history and culture

In Indian Territory the Choctaw rebuilt schools and government; during the First World War, Choctaw soldiers served as the original "code talkers", a role repeated in the Second World War. The Choctaw Nation, headquartered in Durant, Oklahoma, and the Mississippi Band (centred on Pearl River) run extensive health, cultural and economic programmes, and Choctaw language classes support revitalisation. In 1847, during the Irish Great Famine, the Choctaw collected money for Irish relief — an act of generosity commemorated by the Kindred Spirits sculpture in Ireland and by scholarship exchanges.
