The Battle of Lechaeum was fought in 391 BC during the Corinthian War, near Lechaeum, the western port of Corinth. The Athenian general Iphicrates, commanding a force composed almost entirely of light-armed peltasts — javelin-armed mercenaries of his own reforming style — caught a Spartan mora (regiment) of hoplites without cavalry or light-troop support and destroyed about half of it through repeated hit-and-run attacks. The victory was a landmark in Greek military history: it demonstrated that mobility and missile troops could defeat the once-invincible Spartan heavy infantry in open battle, and it grievously damaged Sparta's military prestige.

Background

Since 392 BC Iphicrates had been operating around Corinth with his peltasts, harassing Spartan and allied forces based at Lechaeum, which Sparta had seized to control the Gulf of Corinth. Agesilaus, the Spartan king, campaigned in the Corinthia and then departed for Sparta, leaving troops to garrison Lechaeum.

The battle

As narrated by Xenophon in the Hellenica, a Spartan mora of about 600 hoplites was escorting some Amyclaeans home from Lechaeum for the Hyacinthia festival when Iphicrates's peltasts confronted it. The Spartan cavalry attached to the column was too weak to protect it. The peltasts hurled their javelins from beyond spear range and retreated whenever the hoplites charged; the Spartans, exhausting themselves in repeated pursuits, could neither catch the nimble peltasts nor withdraw in order. Each rush thinned the mora — the fastest runners who led the charges were picked off first. A relief force from Lechaeum eventually drove the Athenians off, but by then some 250 Spartan hoplites lay dead, a severe blow to a citizen body already suffering from oliganthropia (shortage of full citizens).

Significance

Lechaeum, together with Iphicrates's subsequent destruction of a Corinthian-allied force, cemented the reputation of peltasts as a battle-winning arm and encouraged the professionalisation and tactical flexibility that characterised fourth-century BC Greek warfare, foreshadowing the combined-arms armies of Philip II of Macedon. Spartan hoplites thereafter rarely operated without screens of cavalry and light troops.
