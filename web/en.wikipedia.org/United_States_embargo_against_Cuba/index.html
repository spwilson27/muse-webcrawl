The United States embargo against Cuba — known in Cuba as el bloqueo, "the blockade" — is a series of trade and financial restrictions imposed by the United States on Cuba beginning in 1960. It is the most enduring trade embargo in modern history. The embargo prohibits most commerce between the two countries, restricts American travel to Cuba, and penalizes certain transactions by foreign firms. Despite the embargo, the United States has since 2000 been a significant exporter of food and agricultural goods to Cuba under a statutory exception.

History

After Fidel Castro's government came to power in 1959, it nationalized American-owned properties in Cuba without compensation. In response, President Dwight D. Eisenhower imposed a partial trade embargo on 19 October 1960, exempting only food and medicine. On 3 February 1962, President John F. Kennedy expanded it into a comprehensive embargo under the Foreign Assistance Act, effective 7 February 1962, and the Cuban Assets Control Regulations followed in 1963.

The embargo was tightened by the Cuban Democracy Act of 1992 (Torricelli Act), which barred foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies from trading with Cuba and imposed the "180-day rule" on shipping, and codified into statute by the Cuban Liberty and Democratic Solidarity (Helms–Burton) Act of 1996, which also allowed U.S. nationals to sue companies "trafficking" in confiscated property. Because the embargo is now statutory, the president cannot lift it in full without Congress.

Thaw and reversal

On 17 December 2014, Presidents Barack Obama and Raúl Castro announced the restoration of diplomatic relations — the "Cuban thaw" — and the U.S. embassy in Havana reopened in 2015; Cuba was removed from the U.S. list of state sponsors of terrorism. Beginning in 2017, the Trump administration reversed many of the openings, restricting travel and remittances and, in January 2021, redesignating Cuba a state sponsor of terrorism. Title III of Helms–Burton, long waived by presidents, was allowed to take effect in 2019.

Effects and debate

The Cuban government attributes severe economic damage to the embargo, estimating cumulative losses well over $100 billion. Supporters argue the embargo pressures Cuba on human rights and democracy; critics contend it has failed to dislodge the government while harming ordinary Cubans and U.S. business interests. Since 1992, the UN General Assembly has voted annually, by overwhelming margins, for a resolution calling for an end to the embargo.
