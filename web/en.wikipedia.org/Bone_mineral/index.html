Bone mineral

Bone mineral (also called bone salt) is the inorganic component of bone tissue. It consists predominantly of crystalline hydroxyapatite, a calcium phosphate mineral with the approximate chemical formula Ca5(PO4)3(OH), along with smaller amounts of carbonate, magnesium, sodium, fluoride, and other ions substituted into the crystal lattice.

Structure and function

Bone is a composite material: the mineral phase provides stiffness and compressive strength, while the organic matrix — mainly type I collagen — provides tensile strength and toughness. Mineral crystals are deposited within and between collagen fibrils during the process of mineralization, regulated by osteoblasts and by matrix vesicles. Hydroxyapatite makes up roughly 65–70 percent of the dry weight of mature bone.

Physiology

Bone mineral serves as the body's principal reservoir of calcium and phosphate, which are exchanged with the blood under the control of parathyroid hormone, calcitonin, and vitamin D to maintain mineral homeostasis. Continuous remodeling by osteoblasts and osteoclasts replaces mineral throughout life.

Clinical relevance

Bone mineral density (BMD) is measured clinically, typically by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA), to diagnose osteoporosis and assess fracture risk. Defective mineralization produces rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults, often from vitamin D deficiency.
