Chronobiology

Chronobiology is the field of biology that examines biological rhythms — the cyclical, time-based phenomena in living organisms — and their adaptation to solar- and lunar-related cycles. These cycles include circadian (roughly 24-hour), ultradian (shorter than a day) and infradian (longer than a day, such as circannual seasonal) rhythms.

Circadian rhythms

The best-studied biological rhythms are circadian rhythms, endogenous cycles of about 24 hours that govern sleep and wakefulness, body temperature, hormone secretion and many other processes. In mammals the master clock is the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, synchronised to the light-dark cycle by the eyes; peripheral clocks in organs such as the liver run under molecular feedback loops of "clock genes" including PER and CRY. The 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young for elucidating these molecular mechanisms in fruit flies.

Applications

Disruption of biological rhythms is associated with jet lag, shift-work sleep disorder and delayed sleep phase disorder, and suspected links to metabolic and mood disorders. Chronobiology informs chronotherapy — timing drug administration to the body's rhythms to improve efficacy — and the design of lighting in workplaces and hospitals.

History

Observations of daily rhythms date to the eighteenth century, when Jean-Jacques d'Ortous de Mairan showed that mimosa leaves continue to open and close in darkness. The field was formalised in the mid-twentieth century by researchers including Franz Halberg, who coined the term "circadian", and Colin Pittendrigh.
