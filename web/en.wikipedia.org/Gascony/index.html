Gascony (French: Gascogne; Gascon: Gasconha) is a historical and cultural region in southwest France, roughly the area between the Garonne river and the Pyrenees. Its inhabitants, the Gascons, speak (or historically spoke) Gascon, a distinctive variety of the Occitan language. The name derives from the Vascones, a people related to the Basques who settled in the area in late antiquity.

History

In Roman times the region was known as Novempopulania, the "land of nine peoples", part of Aquitania. The Duchy of Gascony emerged in the early Middle Ages under Frankish suzerainty. From 1152, through the marriage of Eleanor of Aquitaine to Henry II of England, Gascony became a possession of the English crown, remaining so — prized for its wine trade through Bordeaux — until the end of the Hundred Years' War in 1453, when it passed definitively to France. Gascons gained a literary reputation for swagger and wit, embodied by d'Artagnan of Dumas's The Three Musketeers and by Cyrano de Bergerac.

Geography and culture

Gascony spans much of the modern departments of Gers, Landes and Lot-et-Garonne and parts of neighbouring departments. It is largely rural, known for foie gras, Armagnac brandy (among the oldest distilled spirits in France), duck and goose farming, bastide towns, and the bullfighting traditions of the Landes. Auch, with its cathedral, is the historic capital; other towns include Dax, Mont-de-Marsan and Condom.
