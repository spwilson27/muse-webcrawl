Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1 July 1646 – 14 November 1716) was a German polymath active as a mathematician, philosopher, scientist and diplomat. He is regarded as one of the most important figures of the Enlightenment and a founder of modern logic and analytic philosophy. Independently of Isaac Newton, he developed differential and integral calculus, and his notation — including the integral sign ∫ and the d of differentials — remains in universal use.

Life

Born in Leipzig, Leibniz studied law and philosophy, earned a doctorate in law at Altdorf, and entered the service of the Elector of Mainz before moving to Hanover in 1676, where he served as librarian, counsellor and historian to the House of Brunswick. He corresponded with hundreds of scholars across Europe and travelled widely on diplomatic and scholarly missions.

Philosophy

Leibniz's metaphysics centres on the monads — simple, indivisible, windowless substances that mirror the universe from individual points of view and harmonise through a pre-established harmony ordained by God. He defended the principle of sufficient reason, the identity of indiscernibles, and the doctrine that this is "the best of all possible worlds", later satirised by Voltaire in Candide.

Mathematics and science

Besides calculus, Leibniz formalised the binary number system, designed a stepped reckoner capable of multiplication and division, contributed to dynamics and geology, and sketched ideas anticipating mathematical logic and computing, including the vision of a universal characteristic (characteristica universalis) in which reasoning could be reduced to calculation.
