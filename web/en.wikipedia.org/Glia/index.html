Glia

Glial cells (including astrocytes and oligodendrocytes) interacting with a neuron.

Glia, also called glial cells or neuroglia, are non-neuronal cells in the central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) and the peripheral nervous system that do not produce electrical impulses. They maintain homeostasis, form myelin, and provide support and protection for neurons. In the central nervous system, glial cells include astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia, and ependymal cells. In the peripheral nervous system, glial cells include Schwann cells and satellite cells.

History

Glia were first described in 1856 by the pathologist Rudolf Virchow, who conceived of them as a kind of connective "nerve glue" (from the Greek glia, "glue") holding neurons in place. For much of the following century they were regarded as passive support cells. Modern research has shown that glia are far more active than once thought: they regulate the chemical environment around synapses, modulate synaptic transmission, prune synapses during development, and participate in learning, memory, and immune defense of the brain.

Types

  • Astrocytes — star-shaped cells that regulate the blood–brain barrier, recycle neurotransmitters, buffer potassium ions, and support synapses.
  • Oligodendrocytes — produce the myelin sheaths that insulate axons in the central nervous system.
  • Microglia — the resident immune cells of the brain, scavenging debris, pathogens, and dead cells.
  • Ependymal cells — line the ventricles of the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord, helping circulate cerebrospinal fluid.
  • Schwann cells — produce myelin in the peripheral nervous system.
  • Satellite cells — surround neuron cell bodies in peripheral ganglia.

Numbers

The old textbook claim that glia outnumber neurons ten to one is a myth. Careful counting using the isotropic fractionator suggests the human brain contains roughly 86 billion neurons and a comparable number of glial cells, though the ratio varies greatly between brain regions.

Clinical significance

Glial dysfunction is implicated in many diseases. Loss of myelin-producing cells underlies multiple sclerosis; gliomas are tumors arising from glial cells; and reactive astrocytes and activated microglia are involved in neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease.
