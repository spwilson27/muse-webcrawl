Assumption of Mary

The Assumption of Mary is the belief, held by the Catholic Church, Eastern Orthodoxy (as the Dormition), Oriental Orthodoxy and parts of Anglicanism, that the Virgin Mary, having completed her earthly life, was assumed (taken up) body and soul into heavenly glory. In the Catholic Church the doctrine was defined as dogma by Pope Pius XII in the apostolic constitution Munificentissimus Deus of November 1, 1950.

History of the belief

The doctrine is not recorded in Scripture; it developed from early apocryphal Transitus Mariae narratives (4th–6th centuries) describing Mary's death and bodily assumption. The feast, celebrated on August 15, spread from the East — where the Emperor Maurice fixed the Dormition feast — to the West by the 7th century. Medieval theologians including Thomas Aquinas and Bonaventure supported the belief, and popular devotion grew through art and liturgy.

Doctrine

The 1950 definition states that Mary "having completed the course of her earthly life, was assumed body and soul into heavenly glory". It deliberately leaves open whether Mary died before being assumed. The Assumption is closely related to the dogma of the Immaculate Conception (1854) and to Mary's role as Theotokos; the feast is a holy day of obligation in many countries and a public holiday in several.
