Folk music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Folk music is a music genre that includes traditional folk music and the contemporary genre that evolved from the former during the 20th-century folk revival. Some types of folk music may be called world music. Traditional folk music has been defined in several ways: as music transmitted orally, music with unknown composers, music that is played on traditional instruments, and music about cultural or national identity.

Traditional folk music

Traditional folk music typically arises within a community and is passed down through generations by oral tradition. It is generally performed by non-professional musicians, often at social gatherings, and its songs deal with everyday life, work, love, and historical events. Because it is transmitted by ear rather than through written notation, traditional folk music tends to exist in many variants, with melodies and lyrics evolving over time.

Every culture has its own folk traditions. In the British Isles these include ballads and sea shanties; in the United States, folk music draws on English, Scottish, and Irish ballads, African American spirituals and blues, and traditions brought by immigrants from many countries.

The folk revival

During the mid-20th century, a folk music revival brought traditional styles to wide popular audiences. Performers such as Woody Guthrie, Pete Seeger, and Lead Belly influenced a generation of singer-songwriters, and the revival of the 1950s and 1960s produced artists including Joan Baez and Bob Dylan, whose work blended folk traditions with contemporary songwriting and social commentary.

Characteristics and instruments

Folk music is commonly performed on acoustic instruments such as the guitar, banjo, fiddle, mandolin, harmonica, and accordion. Songs are often strophic, built around simple chord progressions, and intended for communal singing. Although the boundaries of the genre are debated, folk music is frequently contrasted with commercial pop music by its emphasis on tradition, storytelling, and community participation.
