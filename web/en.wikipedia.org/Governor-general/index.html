Governor-general

A governor-general (plural governors-general) is the representative of a monarch in an independent state. The term is used principally in the Commonwealth realms—countries such as Canada, Australia, and New Zealand where the British monarch is head of state—where the governor-general performs the monarch's constitutional and ceremonial duties on their behalf.

History

The office originated in colonial administration, where governors-general were appointed by the imperial power to govern large colonies or federations, such as British India, French Indochina, and the Dutch East Indies. As colonies gained self-government, the role transformed: after the 1926 Balfour Declaration and the Statute of Westminster 1931, governors-general of the Dominions ceased to be agents of the British government and became personal representatives of the monarch, appointed on the advice of the local prime minister.

Role and powers

A governor-general typically appoints the prime minister, assents to legislation, summons and dissolves parliament, and acts as commander-in-chief, almost always on the advice of ministers. Reserved or discretionary powers are exercised only in exceptional circumstances; the most famous instance was the 1975 Australian constitutional crisis, when Governor-General Sir John Kerr dismissed the government of Gough Whitlam. Similar offices exist under other titles, including the governor general styled without a hyphen in some jurisdictions' official usage.

Current offices

Governors-general currently serve in realms including Antigua and Barbuda, Australia, the Bahamas, Belize, Canada, Grenada, Jamaica, New Zealand, Papua New Guinea, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Saint Lucia, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, the Solomon Islands, and Tuvalu. Several former realms—such as India (until 1950), Pakistan, and Ireland—abolished the office on becoming republics; the title governor-general was also used for the head of state of the Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland and in some non-Commonwealth contexts.
