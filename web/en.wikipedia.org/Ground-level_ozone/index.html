Ground-level ozone

Ground-level ozone (O3), also known as surface-level ozone and tropospheric ozone, is a trace gas in the troposphere (the lowest level of Earth's atmosphere), with a concentration that varies by location and season. It is not emitted directly but is formed by chemical reactions between oxides of nitrogen (NOx) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in the presence of sunlight. As a key component of photochemical smog, elevated ground-level ozone is a significant air pollutant harmful to human health, vegetation and ecosystems.

Formation

Sunlight photolyses nitrogen dioxide (NO2) to nitric oxide (NO) and an oxygen atom, which combines with O2 to form ozone. VOCs — emitted by vehicles, solvents, industry and vegetation — perturb the balance so that ozone accumulates rather than being destroyed by NO. Concentrations therefore peak downwind of cities on hot, sunny, stagnant days. Methane oxidation provides a global background level, and occasional intrusions of stratospheric ozone also contribute. Ground-level ozone is distinct from the stratospheric ozone layer, which shields the Earth from ultraviolet radiation.

Health and environmental effects

Ozone irritates the respiratory system, aggravates asthma and other lung diseases, and is associated with premature mortality from respiratory and cardiovascular causes. It damages plant tissues, reducing yields of crops such as wheat, soy and maize, and injuring forests; it also degrades rubber and other materials. Tropospheric ozone is the third most important anthropogenic greenhouse gas after carbon dioxide and methane.

Regulation and trends

Air-quality standards in the United States, the European Union and elsewhere set maximum 8-hour average concentrations; the US EPA standard is 70 parts per billion (set in 2015). Peak ozone levels have declined in North America and Europe since the 1980s owing to controls on vehicle and industrial emissions, but have risen in parts of Asia, and background levels continue to increase.
