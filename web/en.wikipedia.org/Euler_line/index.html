Euler line

Named forLeonhard Euler
Published1765
Points on the lineOrthocenter (H), centroid (G), circumcenter (O), nine-point center (N)
Key relationOH = 3·OG, i.e. HG = 2·GO

In geometry, the Euler line is a line determined from any triangle that is not equilateral. It passes through several important points determined from the triangle: the orthocenter, the centroid, the circumcenter, the nine-point center, and the centre of the circle passing through the vertices of the medial triangle. Leonhard Euler proved in 1765 that in any triangle these points are collinear.

Properties

The centroid divides the segment between the orthocenter and circumcenter internally in the ratio 2:1: the segment OH is trisected by G, so that HG = 2·GO. The nine-point center is the midpoint of the segment OH. In vector terms, with the circumcenter at the origin, the position vector of the orthocenter equals the sum of the position vectors of the vertices: h = a + b + c, from which the collinearity follows directly.

Special cases

In an equilateral triangle all the centres coincide, and the Euler line is undefined. In an isosceles triangle the Euler line coincides with the axis of symmetry. In a right triangle the circumcenter is the midpoint of the hypotenuse and the orthocenter is the right-angle vertex, so the Euler line is the median to the hypotenuse. The de Longchamps point (the reflection of the orthocenter across the circumcenter) also lies on the line, and Euler's line is perpendicular to the corresponding Simson line relationships studied in triangle geometry.

Generalizations

Analogues of the Euler line exist in other geometries and for tetrahedra; in a tetrahedron, the corresponding points (centroid and circumcenter) define the Monge point configuration, though full collinearity is replaced by a line through the centroid and Monge point in special (orthocentric) cases.
