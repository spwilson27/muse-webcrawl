Magnetic stripe card

A magnetic stripe card is a type of card capable of storing data by modifying the magnetism of tiny iron-based magnetic particles on a band of magnetic material on the card. The stripe — usually black or brown and located on the reverse — is read by swiping it through a reader, in which a magnetic reading head detects the pattern of flux reversals, an application of magnetic flux.

History

Magnetic recording on steel tape and wire dates to the early 1900s. In the late 1960s, IBM engineer Forrest Parry attached magnetic tape to a plastic card, creating the modern stripe card. The technology was standardised under ISO/IEC 7811, which defines up to three data tracks along the stripe.

Uses and standards

Stripe cards became ubiquitous as bank cards, credit and debit cards, driving licences, transit tickets, hotel key cards, and gift cards. Track 1 was developed by the airline industry, Track 2 by banking, and Track 3 for miscellaneous use. Because stripes store only a small amount of static data and can be cloned easily ("skimming"), payment systems in most countries have migrated to EMV chip cards and contactless payments, though stripes remain as a fallback in some markets, notably the United States.
