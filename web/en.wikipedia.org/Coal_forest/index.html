Coal forest

The coal forests were vast tropical wetlands that covered much of the Earth's tropical land during the late Carboniferous and early Permian periods, roughly 300 million years ago. The accumulated plant matter of these forests, buried and compressed over geological time, formed the coal seams that fueled the Industrial Revolution.

Flora

The forests were dominated by plants unlike those of modern forests. Giant clubmosses such as Lepidodendron—trees up to 40 metres tall with scaly, patterned trunks—formed the canopy, alongside tree ferns, horsetails such as Calamites, and early seed plants including seed ferns and cordaites. These plants grew in waterlogged, low-lying swamps near the equator, where the continents of Euramerica were then located.

Coal formation

In the stagnant, oxygen-poor swamp waters, dead plant material accumulated as peat faster than it could decay. Repeated cycles of subsidence and flooding buried the peat under sediment; heat and pressure then converted it through peat, lignite, and bituminous stages into coal. The alternation of coal seams with marine and river sediments records rhythmic changes in sea level, probably driven by glaciation in the southern continent of Gondwana.

Fauna and collapse

The coal forests supported giant arthropods—dragonfly-like insects with wingspans over half a metre and millipede relatives more than two metres long—fostered by the era's high atmospheric oxygen, as well as early amphibians and reptiles. The ecosystem collapsed near the end of the Carboniferous as the climate became drier, an event known as the Carboniferous rainforest collapse, fragmenting the wetlands and driving many species extinct.
