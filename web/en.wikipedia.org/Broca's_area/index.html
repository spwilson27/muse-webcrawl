Broca's area

Broca's area in the inferior frontal gyrus (Brodmann areas 44 and 45)

Broca's area is a region of the frontal lobe of the dominant hemisphere of the brain — usually the left — associated with speech production and aspects of language processing. It comprises the pars opercularis and pars triangularis of the inferior frontal gyrus, corresponding roughly to Brodmann areas 44 and 45. Damage to the region causes Broca's aphasia, in which speech is effortful and telegraphic while comprehension is relatively preserved.

History

The area is named for French physician Paul Broca, who in 1861 reported the case of a patient, Louis Victor Leborgne — nicknamed "Tan" because that was nearly the only syllable he could utter — whose post-mortem examination revealed a lesion in the left inferior frontal lobe. A second patient, Lelong, supported the localisation, and Broca's work became foundational evidence for cerebral localisation of function and left-hemisphere language dominance.

Function

Modern neuroimaging shows Broca's area is active in speech production, syntactic processing, verbal working memory, and also in action observation and imitation, where it forms part of the human mirror-neuron system. Current models treat language as a distributed network: Broca's area works with Wernicke's area in the temporal lobe, connected by the arcuate fasciculus, and with parietal and subcortical structures.

Clinical significance

Strokes affecting the middle cerebral artery can damage Broca's area, producing expressive aphasia; tumours and neurodegenerative disease (primary progressive aphasia) may affect it as well. Pure lesions confined to Broca's area often cause milder, more transient deficits than the classic syndrome, which usually involves surrounding tissue.
