Bias–variance tradeoff

In statistics and machine learning, the bias–variance tradeoff describes the relationship between a model's complexity, the accuracy of its predictions, and how well it can make predictions on previously unseen data. The tradeoff is the property of a model that the variance of the parameter estimated across samples can be reduced by increasing the bias in the estimated parameters.

For a learned predictor, the expected squared prediction error decomposes into three terms:

Expected error = (bias)² + variance + irreducible error

Bias

Bias is the error introduced by approximating a real-life problem with a simplified model. High bias can cause an algorithm to miss relevant relations between features and target outputs — a regime called underfitting. Linear regression applied to a strongly nonlinear relationship is an example.

Variance

Variance is the error from sensitivity to small fluctuations in the training set. High variance can cause a model to capture random noise in the training data — overfitting. A high-degree polynomial fit is a classic example.

Implications

The tradeoff guides model selection: cross-validation, regularization, dimensionality reduction and ensemble methods such as bagging (which reduces variance) and boosting (which primarily reduces bias) are all responses to it. Increasing either the quality or quantity of training data generally reduces variance and total error. Modern deep learning has complicated the classical picture through phenomena such as double descent, where test error can fall again in the heavily over-parameterised regime.
