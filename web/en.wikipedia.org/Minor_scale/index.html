Minor scale

In music theory, the minor scale refers to three scale patterns — the natural minor scale (or Aeolian mode), the harmonic minor scale, and the melodic minor scale (ascending or descending) — rather than just one. In each of these the first, third, and fifth scale degrees create a minor triad, contrasting with the major triad of the major scale.

The natural minor scale follows the pattern of whole and half steps W–H–W–W–H–W–W. For example, A natural minor consists of the notes A–B–C–D–E–F–G, using no sharps or flats; it is the relative minor of C major.

Natural minor

The natural minor scale (the Aeolian mode) shares its key signature with its relative major, a minor third above. Its characteristic sound comes from the flattened third, sixth, and seventh degrees relative to the major scale.

Harmonic minor

The harmonic minor scale raises the seventh degree by a semitone, producing a leading tone that strengthens dominant harmony (V) in minor keys. The interval of an augmented second between the sixth and raised seventh degrees gives the scale a distinctive colour. In A harmonic minor, the notes are A–B–C–D–E–F–G♯.

Melodic minor

The melodic minor scale smooths the augmented second by raising the sixth degree as well when ascending: A–B–C–D–E–F♯–G♯. Classically, the descending form reverts to the natural minor, reflecting the idea that the raised degrees serve melodic motion toward the tonic. In jazz, the ascending form is used in both directions (the "jazz melodic minor") and generates modes used in improvisation, such as the Lydian dominant scale.

Use

Minor scales underpin a vast repertoire: much Baroque, Classical, and Romantic music alternates between minor and major keys, and the minor mode is pervasive in folk music, heavy metal (often using harmonic minor and its modes), and popular song, where it is frequently associated with sombre or serious moods.
