A cherry is the fleshy drupe (stone fruit) of many plants of the genus Prunus, especially the sweet cherry (Prunus avium) and sour cherry (Prunus cerasus). Cherries have been cultivated since prehistoric times around the Black and Caspian seas and were spread through Europe by the Romans.

Cultivation

Commercial orchards grow sweet cherries for fresh eating and sour (tart) cherries for cooking, juice and pies; famous cultivars include Bing, Rainier, Stella and Montmorency. Trees bloom in spring and most sweet varieties require cross-pollination and a period of winter chill. Birds are a major orchard pest, and rain near harvest can split the fruit. Turkey is the largest producer, followed by the United States, where Michigan dominates tart cherry output and the Pacific Northwest leads in sweet cherries.

Ornamental cherries

Ornamental flowering cherries, notably Japanese sakura such as Prunus serrulata, are celebrated in the spring hanami tradition and in festivals from Washington, D.C. to Kyoto. Cherry wood is prized in furniture-making, and the fruit's seeds contain amygdalin, giving bitter almond flavours used carefully in liqueurs such as maraschino.
