Magnetohydrodynamics

Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD), also called magneto-fluid dynamics, is the study of the magnetic properties and behaviour of electrically conducting fluids — including plasmas, liquid metals, and salt water — combining the Navier–Stokes equations of fluid dynamics with Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism. The field was founded by Hannes Alfvén, who received the 1970 Nobel Prize in Physics for his work.

Principles

In MHD the fluid is treated as a single conducting continuum. Its motion induces electric currents, whose magnetic fields in turn exert Lorentz forces on the fluid. A key idealisation, ideal MHD, assumes infinite electrical conductivity, in which case magnetic field lines are "frozen into" the flow (Alfvén's theorem) and move with the plasma. Departures from ideal behaviour, described by resistivity and the magnetic Reynolds number, permit magnetic reconnection, in which field lines break and rejoin, releasing energy.

Phenomena

MHD describes Alfvén waves — oscillations of field lines analogous to vibrations of a plucked string — as well as solar flares and coronal mass ejections, the generation of planetary magnetic fields by core dynamos, the behaviour of interstellar plasma, and accretion disks around black holes (via the magnetorotational instability).

Applications

Engineering applications include MHD generators for direct conversion of heat to electricity, electromagnetic pumps for liquid-metal coolants, plasma confinement in fusion devices such as tokamaks and stellarators, and proposed plasma propulsion for spacecraft. Geophysicists use MHD dynamo theory to explain the geodynamo that sustains Earth's magnetic field.
