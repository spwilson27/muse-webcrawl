Cincinnatus

Lucius Quinctius Cincinnatus (c. 519 – c. 430 BC) was a Roman patrician, statesman and military leader of the early Roman Republic. In Roman tradition he became a model of civic virtue: although granted dictatorial power in a crisis, he defeated Rome's enemies and immediately surrendered power, returning to his farm.

Legend

According to Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Cincinnatus had retired to a small farm across the Tiber after the condemnation of his son. In 458 BC, when a Roman consul's army was trapped by the Aequi at Mount Algidus, the Senate appointed Cincinnatus dictator. Messengers found him ploughing; he accepted, raised an army, defeated the Aequi within days, celebrated a triumph, and resigned the dictatorship after only about fifteen days, well before his six-month term expired. Tradition also made him dictator a second time in 439 BC to suppress the alleged conspiracy of Spurius Maelius.

Legacy

Cincinnatus was invoked for centuries as the exemplar of the citizen who serves only from duty. The American city of Cincinnati, Ohio, is named for the Society of the Cincinnati, a hereditary association of Revolutionary War officers who took him as their emblem; George Washington, its first president general, was widely compared to Cincinnatus for relinquishing power.
