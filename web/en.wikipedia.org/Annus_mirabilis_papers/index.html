Annus mirabilis papers
AuthorAlbert Einstein
Published1905, in Annalen der Physik
SubjectPhotoelectric effect; Brownian motion; special relativity; mass–energy equivalence

The annus mirabilis papers (from the Latin annus mirabilis, "miraculous year") are the four ground-breaking papers written by Albert Einstein and published in the journal Annalen der Physik in 1905, when Einstein was 26 years old and working as a technical expert at the Swiss patent office in Bern. The papers revolutionised physics, introducing the quantum of light, proving the existence of atoms through Brownian motion, and founding the theory of special relativity and the mass–energy equivalence expressed by E = mc². The year 1905 is consequently known as Einstein's annus mirabilis, and 2005 was celebrated as the World Year of Physics.

The four papers

  • Photoelectric effect ("On a Heuristic Point of View Concerning the Production and Transformation of Light", received 18 March) — proposed that light consists of discrete quanta (later called photons) whose energy is proportional to frequency, explaining the photoelectric effect. This paper, more than relativity, was cited in Einstein's 1921 Nobel Prize.
  • Brownian motion ("On the Motion of Small Particles Suspended in a Stationary Liquid, as Required by the Molecular Kinetic Theory of Heat", received 11 May) — gave a theoretical account of the random motion of pollen grains in water as evidence for the physical reality of atoms and molecules, enabling experimental determination of Avogadro's number.
  • Special relativity ("On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies", received 30 June) — reconciled Maxwell's equations with the principle of relativity, abolishing the luminiferous aether and showing that measurements of space and time depend on the observer's motion.
  • Mass–energy equivalence ("Does the Inertia of a Body Depend Upon Its Energy Content?", received 27 September) — a short sequel deriving E = mc², the equivalence of mass and energy.

Background and impact

Einstein had worked on these problems for years, discussing them with his friends in the self-styled "Olympia Academy" in Bern and with his wife Mileva Marić and colleague Michele Besso, whom he thanked in the relativity paper. A fifth work, his doctoral dissertation on molecular dimensions, was also completed in 1905. The papers were initially received quietly, but Max Planck's advocacy brought relativity to attention, and within a few years Einstein was recognised as the leading theoretical physicist of his generation. Together the four papers transformed the foundations of physics and of the scientific understanding of space, time, matter and light.
