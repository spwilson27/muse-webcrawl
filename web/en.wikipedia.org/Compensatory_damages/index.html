Compensatory damages

Compensatory damages are money paid to a claimant to compensate for damage, injury, or another incurred loss, within the legal process. They are the most common form of damages in civil litigation and are intended, so far as money can do it, to place the injured party in the position they would have occupied had the wrong not occurred — in contrast to punitive damages, which aim to punish the defendant and deter similar conduct.

Types

Compensatory damages are commonly divided into two categories. Special damages (or economic damages) cover quantifiable monetary losses such as medical expenses, lost wages, property damage, and loss of future earning capacity. General damages (or non-economic damages) compensate for intangible harms such as pain and suffering, emotional distress, loss of consortium, and reputational injury. In contract cases, compensatory damages usually take the form of expectation damages, protecting the benefit of the bargain; reliance and restitutionary measures are used in specific circumstances.

Proof and limitations

A plaintiff must prove the fact and amount of loss with reasonable certainty, and has a duty to mitigate damages by taking reasonable steps to avoid further loss. Damages for breach of contract are limited to losses within the contemplation of the parties at the time of contracting, per the rule in Hadley v Baxendale (1854). Some jurisdictions cap non-economic damages by statute, particularly in medical malpractice cases.
