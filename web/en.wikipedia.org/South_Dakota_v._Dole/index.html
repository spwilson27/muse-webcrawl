HoldingCongress may condition federal highway funds on states adopting a drinking age of 21

South Dakota v. Dole, 483 U.S. 203 (1987), is a United States Supreme Court case concerning the federal spending power. The Court upheld the National Minimum Drinking Age Act of 1984, which directed the Secretary of Transportation (Elizabeth Dole) to withhold 5% of federal highway funds from states in which the legal drinking age was below 21.

Background

South Dakota permitted 19-year-olds to purchase beer containing up to 3.2% alcohol. Facing the loss of highway funds, the state sued, arguing that the condition exceeded Congress's spending power and violated the Twenty-first Amendment, which had returned control of alcohol regulation to the states.

Decision

Writing for a 7–2 majority, Chief Justice Rehnquist held that conditions on federal grants are permissible if they (1) serve the general welfare, (2) are unambiguous, (3) are related to the federal interest in the programme, and (4) do not violate an independent constitutional bar — and if the financial inducement is not so coercive as to pass into compulsion. The drinking-age condition, related to safe interstate travel, satisfied these limits, and the Twenty-first Amendment did not forbid so mild an indirect incentive. Justices Brennan and O'Connor dissented.

Significance

The case remains the leading statement of the scope and limits of conditional federal spending; its "coercion" caveat later mattered in NFIB v. Sebelius (2012).
