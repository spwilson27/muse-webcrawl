Object relations theory

Object relations theory is a school of thought in psychoanalytic theory and psychoanalysis, centered on the idea that the psyche is composed of internalized images of the self and of significant others ("objects"), and that relationships with these internal objects shape personality and later relationships. It grew out of the work of Melanie Klein, Ronald Fairbairn, Donald Winnicott, and others, shifting emphasis from Freud's drive theory toward the individual's need for relationships from infancy.

Key concepts

In this framework, an "object" is an internal mental representation of a person, often formed in early childhood from interactions with caregivers. Through processes such as introjection, splitting, and projective identification, infants organize experiences of "good" and "bad" objects. Otto Kernberg later integrated object relations with ego psychology, applying it to the understanding and treatment of borderline personality organization.

Influence

Object relations theory influenced attachment theory, developmental psychology, and modern psychodynamic psychotherapy, which uses the therapeutic relationship itself as a vehicle for insight and change.
