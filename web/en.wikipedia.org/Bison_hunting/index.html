Bison hunting

Bison hunting is the hunting of the American bison (Bison bison) for meat, hides, and other products. For thousands of years, Indigenous peoples of North America hunted bison as a central element of their subsistence and culture. In the nineteenth century, commercial hide hunting by Euro-American settlers brought the species to the brink of extinction, reducing herds that once numbered in the tens of millions to only a few hundred animals.

Indigenous hunting

Native peoples hunted bison on foot long before the arrival of the horse, using techniques such as driving herds over cliffs at buffalo jumps, into corrals or pounds, and stalking animals while disguised in bison or wolf skins. The introduction of the horse in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries transformed Plains hunting, enabling mounted hunters to pursue herds with bow, lance, and later firearms. Nearly every part of the animal was used: meat for food, hides for tipis and clothing, bones for tools, and sinew for bowstrings.

Commercial hunting and near-extinction

During the nineteenth century, demand for bison hides in eastern and European markets, together with railroad expansion and deliberate United States government policy aimed at undermining Plains Indian resistance, led to industrial-scale slaughter. Professional hunters using large-caliber rifles such as the Sharps .50 killed millions of animals; the herds of the southern plains were destroyed by about 1875 and the northern herds by the mid-1880s. By 1884, only a few hundred wild bison remained.

Conservation and modern hunting

Conservationists including William Hornaday helped preserve remnant herds in places such as Yellowstone National Park, and ranchers bred bison privately. Today the population has recovered to several hundred thousand, mostly in commercial herds. Regulated hunting of wild bison occurs in a few jurisdictions, including around Yellowstone and in Alaska and Canada, and bison products are sold commercially.
