Tabun (nerve agent)

Chemical classOrganophosphorus compound
NATO designationGA
AppearanceColorless to brown liquid
Mode of actionAcetylcholinesterase inhibitor

Tabun (NATO designation GA) is an extremely toxic synthetic organophosphorus compound and the first of the G-series nerve agents to be discovered. It is a clear, colorless, and tasteless liquid with a faint fruity odor. Tabun is classified as a weapon of mass destruction and its production and stockpiling are outlawed by the Chemical Weapons Convention of 1993.

Structure of tabun, an organophosphate nerve agent

History

Tabun was discovered in 1936 by the German chemist Gerhard Schrader, who was researching organophosphate insecticides at IG Farben. Recognizing its extreme toxicity to mammals, Schrader reported it to the German military, and the Nazi regime developed it in secret as a chemical weapon, building a dedicated production plant at Dyhernfurth in Silesia. Although Germany stockpiled thousands of tons of tabun-filled munitions, it was never used in combat during the Second World War.

Effects

Like other nerve agents, tabun irreversibly inhibits the enzyme acetylcholinesterase, causing acetylcholine to accumulate at synapses. Symptoms include constricted pupils, salivation, convulsions, and ultimately death by respiratory failure. Antidotes include atropine and pralidoxime, administered as quickly as possible after exposure.

Later use

Tabun was used by Iraq against Iranian troops during the Iran–Iraq War in the 1980s, marking the first confirmed battlefield use of nerve agents since the First World War era. Iraq's stockpiles were destroyed under United Nations supervision after the Gulf War.
