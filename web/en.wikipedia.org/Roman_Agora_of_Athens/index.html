The Roman Agora (Greek: Ρωμαϊκή Αγορά) is an ancient marketplace in Athens, Greece, located to the east of the ancient Agora of Athens and north of the Acropolis, in the modern Plaka district. It was built in the late 1st century BC with funds donated by Julius Caesar and the emperor Augustus, and it took over the commercial functions of the older agora.

Description

The Roman Agora occupied a rectangular courtyard roughly 111 by 98 metres, surrounded by stoas (colonnades) on all four sides, behind which stood shops. Its main entrance was the Gate of Athena Archegetis on the west side, a monumental gateway of Pentelic marble with four Doric columns, dedicated in 11 BC. A second, Ionic propylon stood on the east side.

The square was paved with marble slabs and administered by market officials (agoranomoi), who supervised weights, measures and the trade in grain, oil and wine. Inscriptions record regulations, including a law of the emperor Hadrian concerning the tax on olive oil sales.

Tower of the Winds

Just east of the agora stands the Tower of the Winds (Horologion of Andronikos Kyrrhestes), an octagonal marble clocktower built in the 2nd or 1st century BC. It combined a water clock, sundials and a wind vane, and each of its eight sides carries a relief of the corresponding wind deity. Though slightly earlier than the agora itself, it became closely associated with the complex.

Later history

The agora continued in use through late antiquity. In the Ottoman period the Fethiye Mosque was built on its northern side in 1456, shortly after the Ottoman conquest of Athens. Excavations by the Greek Archaeological Society in the 19th and 20th centuries cleared the site, which is now an open-air archaeological area.
