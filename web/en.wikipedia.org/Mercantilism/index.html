Mercantilism

Mercantilism is a nationalist system of trade policies that dominated European economic thought and state practice from the 16th to the 18th centuries. It held that a nation's wealth and power were best served by increasing exports and limiting imports, thereby accumulating precious metals ("bullion") and achieving a favourable balance of trade.

Policies

Mercantilist governments imposed high tariffs and quotas on manufactured imports, banned the export of gold, silver and raw materials, subsidised domestic manufactures and export industries, and granted monopoly charters to trading companies such as the English and Dutch East India Companies. Colonies were expected to supply raw materials to the mother country and to buy its manufactures, under laws such as England's Navigation Acts (from 1651).

Ideas

Writers such as Thomas Mun (England's Treasure by Forraign Trade, written c. 1630) argued that trade was zero-sum: one nation's gain was another's loss. In France, Jean-Baptiste Colbert, finance minister to Louis XIV, systematised the approach — sometimes called Colbertism — with state factories, quality regulations and infrastructure. Not all mercantilist writers equated wealth with bullion; later ones emphasised employment and the balance of trade more broadly.

Decline and criticism

Physiocrats in France and Adam Smith in The Wealth of Nations (1776) attacked mercantilism as a system of producer privilege that confused money with wealth and harmed consumers. David Ricardo's theory of comparative advantage further undermined its premises. Most European states moved toward freer trade in the 19th century, though protectionist ideas echoing mercantilism — sometimes called neo-mercantilism — recur in later trade politics.
