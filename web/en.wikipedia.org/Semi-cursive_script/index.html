Semi-cursive script

Semi-cursive script, also known as running script (Chinese: 行書; pinyin: xíngshū), is a style of Chinese calligraphy and East Asian handwriting that emerged during the Han dynasty. It is a cursive style intermediate between the regular script (kaishu) and the highly abbreviated cursive script (caoshu). Characters written in semi-cursive script retain recognizable structure while strokes are linked or simplified for speed and fluidity.

Characteristics

In semi-cursive writing, strokes within a character may be connected, some components are written with fewer strokes, and the brush is lifted less often than in regular script. Unlike full cursive, the characters remain readily legible to readers familiar with the regular forms. The style is widely used for informal writing, notes, letters, and much expressive calligraphy.

History

The style is traditionally traced to the late Han dynasty, with the calligrapher Liu Desheng often credited as an early formulator. It reached a celebrated peak in the work of Wang Xizhi (303–361) of the Eastern Jin dynasty, whose Preface to the Poems Composed at the Orchid Pavilion, written in semi-cursive script, is regarded as the most famous work of Chinese calligraphy.

Use

Semi-cursive forms influenced handwriting in Japan, where the style is known as gyōsho, one of the three main styles of Japanese calligraphy alongside kaisho and sōsho.
