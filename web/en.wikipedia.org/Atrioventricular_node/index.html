Atrioventricular node


The heart's conduction system, with the AV node

The atrioventricular node (AV node) is part of the electrical conduction system of the heart that electrically connects the atria and ventricles. It lies in the interatrial septum near the opening of the coronary sinus, within the region known as the triangle of Koch. The AV node receives impulses from the sinoatrial (SA) node and, after a short delay, passes them through the bundle of His to the ventricles.

Function

The node's most important role is to slow the electrical impulse — by roughly a tenth of a second — before it reaches the ventricles. This delay allows the atria to finish contracting and empty their blood into the ventricles before ventricular contraction begins. The AV node also acts as a protective filter: during very rapid atrial rhythms such as atrial fibrillation, it blocks many impulses, preventing dangerously fast ventricular rates. If the SA node fails, the AV node can itself act as a pacemaker, firing at about 40–60 beats per minute.

Clinical significance

Disease or damage of the AV node causes heart block, graded from first-degree (prolonged conduction) to complete heart block, in which no impulses pass and a slower escape rhythm or an artificial pacemaker must sustain the heartbeat. AV nodal reentrant tachycardia (AVNRT) is the most common regular supraventricular tachycardia; it is often treated by catheter ablation of one of the node's pathways. Drugs that slow AV conduction, including beta blockers and certain calcium channel blockers, are used to control heart rate.
