Mesoamerican pyramids

Mesoamerican pyramids form a prominent part of ancient Mesoamerican architecture. Although similar in appearance to Egyptian pyramids, the Mesoamerican examples are typically stepped pyramids with flat tops, on which temples were built, rather than tombs with smooth faces. They were constructed by cultures including the Olmec-influenced peoples, Maya, Zapotec, Teotihuacanos and Aztecs (Mexica), from the first millennium BC until the Spanish conquest.

Design and construction

Most Mesoamerican pyramids consist of a rubble core faced with cut stone and lime plaster, rising in stepped tiers, often in the talud-tablero style at Teotihuacan. A broad stairway, sometimes with sculpted balustrades, led to a temple on the summit. Pyramids were frequently rebuilt in successive enlargements, with a new shell built over the older structure, and many contain burials of rulers, as at Palenque's Temple of the Inscriptions.

Notable examples

The Great Pyramid of Cholula in Puebla is the largest pyramid by volume in the world, exceeding the Great Pyramid of Giza. Others include the Pyramids of the Sun and Moon at Teotihuacan, El Castillo at Chichen Itza — famous for the equinox effect of light and shadow on its stairway — the Templo Mayor of Tenochtitlan, and the great pyramid temples of Tikal and Calakmul. At La Venta, the Olmec built one of the earliest known Mesoamerican pyramids, around 900 BC.
