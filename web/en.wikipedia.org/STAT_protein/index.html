STAT protein

The STAT proteins (Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription) are a family of intracellular transcription factors that mediate signalling by many cytokines and growth factors, principally through the JAK-STAT signaling pathway. In mammals there are seven STAT genes: STAT1, STAT2, STAT3, STAT4, STAT5A, STAT5B and STAT6. They were discovered through studies of interferon signalling in the early 1990s.

Activated STATs dimerise, enter the nucleus and switch on target genes.

Mechanism

Latent STATs reside in the cytoplasm. When a cytokine binds its cell-surface receptor, receptor-associated Janus kinases (JAKs) phosphorylate the receptor and recruit STATs, which are then phosphorylated on a key tyrosine residue. Phosphorylated STATs form homo- or heterodimers via their SH2 domains, translocate into the nucleus, bind specific DNA sequences (such as GAS elements), and activate transcription. Signalling is terminated by phosphatases, SOCS proteins and PIAS inhibitors.

Functions and disease

Different STATs serve different pathways: STAT1 and STAT2 mediate interferon responses and antiviral defence; STAT3 transduces interleukin-6 family signals and is constitutively activated in many cancers, making it a drug target; STAT4 and STAT6 direct T-helper cell differentiation (Th1 and Th2 respectively); STAT5 responds to prolactin, growth hormone and interleukin-2. Loss-of-function mutations cause immunodeficiencies — for example, STAT3 mutations cause hyper-IgE (Job's) syndrome in its dominant-negative form — while gain-of-function mutations can cause autoimmunity and lymphoproliferation.
