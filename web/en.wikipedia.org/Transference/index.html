Transference is a phenomenon in psychoanalysis in which a patient unconsciously redirects feelings and desires — originally attached to important figures from childhood, especially parents — onto the analyst. Sigmund Freud first described the concept in the 1890s and 1900s, notably in the Dora case and the 1912 paper "The Dynamics of Transference".

Role in treatment

Freud initially saw transference as an obstacle, but came to regard it as the central vehicle of therapy: in the "transference neurosis", the patient's conflicts are re-enacted within the analytic relationship, where they can be observed, interpreted, and worked through rather than merely remembered.

Forms

Positive transference involves feelings of affection, trust, or idealization; negative transference, hostility and distrust; erotic transference, romantic or sexual feelings. Each can be clinically informative and each can also produce resistance to the work of analysis.

Countertransference

The analyst's emotional responses to the patient — initially treated as interference to be suppressed — came to be seen by later analysts, following Paula Heimann and Heinrich Racker, as an important diagnostic instrument for understanding the patient's inner world.

Beyond psychoanalysis

The term is used more broadly for the redirection of feelings toward any person reminiscent of an earlier figure, and related ideas appear in cognitive psychology's research on person perception and schemas.
