Sir James George Frazer OM FRS FRSE FBA (1 January 1854 – 7 May 1941) was a Scottish social anthropologist and folklorist, influential in the early stages of the modern studies of mythology and comparative religion. His monumental work The Golden Bough: A Study in Magic and Religion (first published 1890) remains his best-known achievement and had a wide influence on literature and thought well beyond anthropology.

Life

Frazer was born in Glasgow, the son of a chemist, and educated at Springfield Academy, Larchfield Academy, the University of Glasgow and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he read classics and became a fellow in 1879. He spent almost his whole career at Cambridge and Liverpool (where he was the first professor of social anthropology, 1907), working as an "armchair anthropologist" who synthesised reports from missionaries, colonial officials and classical texts rather than doing fieldwork himself.

The Golden Bough

The book grew from an attempt to explain the strange custom at Nemi in Italy, where a priest-king — the King of the Wood — held office until slain by a successor who had plucked a golden bough from a sacred tree. From this starting point Frazer ranged across the world's cultures, arguing that human thought evolves through three stages: magic, then religion, then science. He distinguished sympathetic magic — based on similarity and contact — and collected vast evidence on sacred kingship, dying and reviving gods, scapegoats, taboo and fertility ritual. The work grew from two volumes (1890) to three (1900) and then to twelve volumes (1906–1915); Frazer's own one-volume abridgement (1922) reached a mass readership.

Influence and criticism

The Golden Bough profoundly influenced writers including T. S. Eliot, Ezra Pound, D. H. Lawrence and the myth criticism of the twentieth century, as well as Freud's Totem and Taboo. Later anthropologists, notably Malinowski and the fieldwork generation, rejected Frazer's evolutionary scheme and his decontextualised comparisons, and Ludwig Wittgenstein criticised his treatment of ritual as primitive error. Nevertheless, Frazer's erudition and literary power secured the work a lasting place in the humanities. His other books include Totemism and Exogamy (1910), The Belief in Immortality and the Worship of the Dead (1913–1924) and editions of classical authors.
