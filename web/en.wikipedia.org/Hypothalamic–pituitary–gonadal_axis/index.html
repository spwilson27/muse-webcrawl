The hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis (HPG axis, also known as the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian/testicular axis in females and males respectively) is a neuroendocrine system linking the hypothalamus, the pituitary gland, and the gonads. It controls the development and regulation of the reproductive system and is activated at puberty, remaining active throughout adult reproductive life.

Mechanism

Specialised neurons in the hypothalamus—regulated upstream by kisspeptin-producing KNDy neurons—secrete gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) in pulses into the hypophyseal portal circulation. GnRH stimulates the anterior pituitary to release the gonadotropins luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). These act on the gonads: in the testes, LH drives testosterone production by Leydig cells and FSH supports spermatogenesis in Sertoli cells; in the ovaries, LH and FSH govern follicular development, ovulation, and the production of estradiol and progesterone. The gonadal steroids and the peptide inhibin feed back on the hypothalamus and pituitary, generally inhibiting further GnRH, LH, and FSH secretion; in females, sustained high estradiol paradoxically triggers the mid-cycle LH surge that causes ovulation.

Development and disorders

The axis is active in infancy ("mini-puberty"), then becomes quiescent during childhood before its reactivation initiates puberty. Disorders of the axis include hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (as in Kallmann syndrome, where GnRH neurons fail to migrate), precocious or delayed puberty, and suppression of the axis by stress, undernutrition, or excessive exercise (functional hypothalamic amenorrhea). Exogenous sex steroids, including anabolic steroids and hormonal contraceptives, suppress the axis by negative feedback.
