The spike protein (S) of SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes COVID-19, is a large trimeric glycoprotein that protrudes from the viral envelope, giving coronaviruses their crown-like appearance. It mediates attachment to and entry into host cells and is the principal target of neutralising antibodies and of COVID-19 vaccines.

Structure

Each spike monomer contains 1,273 amino acids and is cleaved into two subunits. The S1 subunit contains the receptor-binding domain (RBD), which attaches to the human ACE2 receptor, and the N-terminal domain. The S2 subunit contains the fusion machinery that merges the viral and cell membranes after further cleavage at the S2′ site by host proteases such as TMPRSS2 or cathepsins. Unusually among related coronaviruses, SARS-CoV-2 spike has a polybasic (furin) cleavage site at the S1/S2 boundary, which enhances its processing and transmissibility. The protein is heavily glycosylated, with 22 N-linked glycan sites per monomer that shield it from antibodies.

Function and entry

The RBD switches between "down" (closed, immune-hidden) and "up" (receptor-accessible) conformations. Binding to ACE2, followed by proteolytic priming, triggers a dramatic refolding of S2 that drives membrane fusion and release of the viral genome into the cell.

Variants and immunity

Because spike is exposed on the virion surface, it accumulates mutations in variants of concern — for example D614G early in the pandemic, and the heavily mutated spikes of Delta and Omicron — many of which increase ACE2 affinity or escape antibody neutralisation. Most authorised COVID-19 vaccines (mRNA, adenoviral vector and protein subunit) present the spike antigen, often stabilised in its prefusion form by proline substitutions ("2P"), to elicit immunity.
