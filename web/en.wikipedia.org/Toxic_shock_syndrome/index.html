Toxic shock syndrome

Toxic shock syndrome (TSS) is a rare but potentially fatal condition caused by bacterial toxins, most often the TSST-1 toxin produced by Staphylococcus aureus or toxins of group A Streptococcus. The toxins act as superantigens, triggering a massive, uncontrolled immune response that can lead to fever, a characteristic rash, low blood pressure, and multi-organ failure.

Signs and symptoms

TSS typically begins abruptly with high fever, confusion, muscle aches, nausea, and a diffuse sunburn-like rash. Blood pressure may fall dangerously (shock), and the rash is often followed one to two weeks later by peeling of the skin, especially of the palms and soles. Streptococcal TSS is often associated with a painful skin or soft-tissue infection.

Causes and risk factors

The syndrome gained public attention in 1980, when an outbreak among menstruating women was linked to high-absorbency tampons, particularly the Rely brand, which was withdrawn from the market. Changes in tampon manufacture and labelling were followed by a sharp decline in menstrual cases. TSS can also occur after surgery, childbirth, burns, or skin wounds, and in association with nasal packing, contraceptive sponges, or influenza. Only people lacking antibodies to the toxin are susceptible, and most adults have such antibodies.

Treatment

TSS is a medical emergency. Treatment involves removing the source of infection (such as a tampon or packing), aggressive intravenous fluids, antibiotics, and supportive care in an intensive care unit; intravenous immunoglobulin is sometimes used. With prompt treatment most patients recover, but streptococcal TSS in particular carries a substantial mortality rate.
