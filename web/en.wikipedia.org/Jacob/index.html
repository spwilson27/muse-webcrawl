Jacob (Hebrew: יַעֲקֹב, Yaʿăqōḇ), later given the name Israel, is a patriarch of the Israelites and a central figure in the Book of Genesis. He is the son of Isaac and Rebekah, the grandson of Abraham and Sarah, and the father of the twelve sons who became the ancestors of the Twelve Tribes of Israel.

Life in Genesis

Jacob and his twin brother Esau were born to Isaac and Rebekah, with Jacob emerging holding Esau's heel — the folk etymology given for his name. As a young man Jacob, at his mother's urging, obtained both Esau's birthright (traded for a meal of lentil stew) and Isaac's blessing, intended for the elder son.

Fleeing Esau's anger, Jacob travelled to Haran, where on the way he dreamed of a ladder reaching to heaven at Bethel. In Haran he worked for his uncle Laban for twenty years, marrying Laban's daughters Leah and Rachel and acquiring great flocks. He fathered twelve sons and a daughter, Dinah.

Wrestling and the name Israel

Returning to Canaan, Jacob wrestled through the night with a mysterious figure — described variously as a man, an angel, or God — at the ford of the Jabbok river. At daybreak the figure renamed him Israel, "for you have striven with God and with men, and have prevailed." The episode, known as Jacob's ladder's counterpart in his maturity, gave the Israelite people their name. Jacob was reconciled with Esau, settled in Canaan, and favoured his son Joseph, whose brothers sold him into slavery in Egypt.

Later years

In old age Jacob migrated to Egypt during a famine, at Joseph's invitation, and blessed Pharaoh. Before his death he blessed Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh and pronounced blessings — really character sketches and prophecies — over each of his own sons in Genesis 49. He died in Egypt after seventeen years there, and his body was carried back to be buried in the cave of Machpelah at Hebron beside Abraham, Isaac, and his wife Leah.

In tradition

Jacob is revered in Judaism as the third patriarch, in Christianity as an ancestor of Jesus, and in Islam as the prophet Yaqub. Scholars of the documentary hypothesis regard the Jacob cycle as a composite of traditions from different sources, reflecting among other things the relations between Israel and Edom.
