Chanson

A chanson (French for "song") is a lyric-driven French song. The term spans three broad historical meanings: the late medieval and Renaissance polyphonic song; the narrative epic poems of the chansons de geste; and, most familiarly today, the classic French popular song of the cabaret and music-hall tradition, with the singer known as a chansonnier.

Medieval and Renaissance chanson

In the Middle Ages the chanson included monophonic songs of the troubadours and trouvères and the epic chansons de geste such as the Chanson de Roland. From the 14th century, composers such as Guillaume de Machaut and later Josquin des Prez, Clément Janequin and Orlando di Lasso made the polyphonic chanson a central genre, set to courtly love poetry in the fixed forms (ballade, rondeau, virelai) and later to freer texts. Janequin's programmatic chansons, imitating battles and birdsong, were especially famous.

Modern chanson

The modern chanson emerged in the Paris cabarets and music halls of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, emphasizing poetic lyrics, emotional realism and the persona of the singer-songwriter. Its defining artists include Aristide Bruant, Édith Piaf, Charles Trenet, Georges Brassens, Jacques Brel (Belgian), Léo Ferré, Charles Aznavour, Barbara and Serge Gainsbourg. The "chanson réaliste" of Piaf's era sang of love, poverty and fate among the Parisian poor; the post-war generation of auteurs-compositeurs-interprètes elevated the genre's literary ambitions, and chanson remains a touchstone of French-language popular music.
