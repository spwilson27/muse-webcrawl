Medial rectus muscle

Medial rectus muscle
Origin: Annulus of Zinn at the orbital apex
Insertion: Medial surface of the eyeball, ~5.5 mm from the limbus
Nerve: Oculomotor nerve (CN III), inferior division
Action: Adduction of the eyeball

The medial rectus muscle is one of the six extraocular muscles that control movement of the eye. It lies on the medial side of the orbit and is the largest and strongest of the extraocular muscles. Its sole action is to adduct the eye — to rotate it toward the nose — as when looking cross-eyed or converging on a near object.

Structure

The muscle arises from the common tendinous ring (annulus of Zinn) at the apex of the orbit and runs forward along the medial orbital wall, inserting into the sclera on the medial side of the globe about 5.5 mm behind the corneal limbus — the closest insertion of any rectus muscle to the limbus. It is supplied by the inferior division of the oculomotor nerve, which enters its lateral (orbital) surface.

Clinical significance

Palsy of the oculomotor nerve paralyses the medial rectus, leaving the eye deviated outward (exotropia) under the unopposed action of the lateral rectus. In infants, congenital esotropia involves medial rectus tightness and is treated partly by weakening the muscle surgically (medial rectus recession). Entrapment of the medial rectus in a medial orbital wall (blowout) fracture restricts gaze and causes diplopia.
