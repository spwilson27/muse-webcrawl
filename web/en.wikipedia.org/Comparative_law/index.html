Comparative law

Comparative law is the study of differences and similarities between the law of different countries. More specifically, it involves the study of the different legal systems in existence in the world, including the common law, the civil law, socialist law, Canon law, Jewish Law, Islamic law, Hindu law, and Chinese law. It includes the description and analysis of foreign legal systems, even where no explicit comparison is undertaken.

Purposes

Comparative law serves several aims: it deepens understanding of one's own legal system, assists legislators in law reform, supports the unification and harmonisation of law (as in the European Union and UNIDROIT), and informs courts deciding cases with foreign elements. Increased globalisation has made the discipline more important to practising lawyers and scholars alike.

History

Comparison of laws is ancient — Aristotle surveyed Greek constitutions and Montesquieu's The Spirit of the Laws (1748) was an early comparative work. The modern discipline is usually dated to the first International Congress of Comparative Law in Paris in 1900, associated with Édouard Lambert and Raymond Saleilles. Influential 20th-century scholars include Konrad Zweigert and Hein Kötz, whose functional method compares legal rules by the problems they solve.

Legal families

Comparatists commonly group jurisdictions into legal families or traditions: the Romano-Germanic (civil law) family, the common law family, socialist law, and religious or traditional systems. Many jurisdictions are mixed, combining elements of several traditions.

Methods

The dominant approach is functionalism, which assumes that societies face similar problems and that differing doctrines may reach comparable results. Critics propose cultural, economic and postcolonial perspectives that question the universality of legal solutions.
