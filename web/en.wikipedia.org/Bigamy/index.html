Bigamy

Bigamy is the act of entering into a marriage with one person while still legally married to another. A legal or de facto separation of the couple does not alter their marital status as married persons. In countries that practice monogamous marriage, bigamy is generally illegal and can constitute a criminal offence, distinct from polygamy, which describes the broader practice of having multiple spouses.

Legal treatment

Most Western jurisdictions criminalise bigamy; for example, in England and Wales it is an offence under the Offences against the Person Act 1861, punishable by up to seven years' imprisonment, and in the United States it is a felony or misdemeanour depending on the state. A second marriage entered into while a prior marriage subsists is usually void.

History

Monogamy was institutionalised in Roman law and reinforced by the Christian church in Europe. In the 19th-century United States, bigamy laws were central to the federal campaign against plural marriage in the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, leading to the Morrill Anti-Bigamy Act of 1862 and the church's 1890 Manifesto abandoning the practice.

Defences and exceptions

Common defences include a good-faith belief that the prior marriage was dissolved by death or divorce, and the prolonged absence of a spouse — seven years' absence without knowledge that the spouse is alive has historically been a statutory defence in England. In some legal systems, religious or customary marriages create complications for prosecution where civil registration is incomplete.
