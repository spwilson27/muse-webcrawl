Student-centred learning

Student-centred learning, also known as learner-centred education, broadly encompasses methods of teaching that shift the focus of instruction from the teacher to the student. In original usage, student-centred learning aims to develop learner autonomy and independence by putting responsibility for the learning path in the hands of students, guided and facilitated by the teacher.

Background

The approach draws on the educational theories of John Dewey, Jean Piaget, and Lev Vygotsky, whose constructivist ideas hold that learners actively build knowledge rather than passively receive it. Student-centred methods contrast with teacher-centred instruction, in which the teacher is the main authority and primary deliverer of information.

Characteristics

Student-centred classrooms typically feature active learning activities such as discussion, problem-solving, projects, and peer teaching; learners often have some choice over content, pace, or the form of assessment; and the teacher acts as a "guide on the side" rather than a "sage on the stage". Assessment tends to include formative and self-assessment components.

Applications

Specific instructional models associated with student-centred learning include problem-based learning, inquiry-based learning, flipped classrooms, and personalised learning supported by technology.

Criticism

Critics, including proponents of direct instruction, argue that minimally guided approaches can overload novice learners' working memory and that the evidence favours explicit teaching for foundational knowledge, while supporters respond that well-designed student-centred learning includes substantial guidance and scaffolding.
