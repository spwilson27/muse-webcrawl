Malleus Maleficarum

AuthorHeinrich Kramer (attributed; possibly with Jacob Sprenger)
Publishedc. 1486–1487 (Speyer)
LanguageLatin
SubjectWitchcraft; witch trials

The Malleus Maleficarum, usually translated as the "Hammer of Witches", is the most famous treatise on witchcraft. It was written by the German Catholic clergyman and inquisitor Heinrich Kramer and first published in Speyer in about 1486–1487; the Dominican Jacob Sprenger was long named as co-author, though his contribution is disputed by modern historians. The book was prefaced by the papal bull Summis desiderantes affectibus (1484) of Pope Innocent VIII, which authorised Kramer and Sprenger's inquisitorial work.

Contents

The work is divided into three parts: a theological argument that witchcraft is real and heretical (directed against sceptics); a description of witches' practices — pacts with the Devil, maleficia (harmful magic), and remedies; and a legal guide to conducting witch trials, including interrogation, torture and sentencing. It is notorious for its misogyny, arguing that women are more susceptible to witchcraft because of supposed weakness of faith and intellect.

Influence

Reprinted in dozens of editions across Europe over the following two centuries, the Malleus became the standard handbook of witch-hunters during the peak of the European witch trials, though its influence varied by region and some authorities, including the Spanish Inquisition, treated it with caution.
