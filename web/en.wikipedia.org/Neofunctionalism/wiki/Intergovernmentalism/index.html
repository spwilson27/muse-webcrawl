Intergovernmentalism

In political science, intergovernmentalism treats states, and national governments in particular, as the primary actors in integration processes. Applied to Europe by Stanley Hoffmann in the 1960s and developed as liberal intergovernmentalism by Andrew Moravcsik, it is the main rival to neofunctionalism.

Intergovernmentalism
Key thinkersStanley Hoffmann; Andrew Moravcsik
Core claimStates control integration outcomes

Liberal intergovernmentalism

Moravcsik's three-stage model explains major EU bargains by (1) national preference formation driven by economic interests, (2) interstate bargaining reflecting relative power, and (3) institutional choice to secure credible commitments.

Critique and debate

Neofunctionalists counter that supranational actors and spillover shape preferences and outcomes beyond state control. The theories frame the long-running debate over who drives European integration.
