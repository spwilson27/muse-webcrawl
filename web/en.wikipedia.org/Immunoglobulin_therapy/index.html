Immunoglobulin therapy

Immunoglobulin therapy
Routes: Intravenous (IVIG), subcutaneous (SCIG), intramuscular
Source: Pooled human plasma from thousands of donors
Main component: IgG antibodies

Immunoglobulin therapy is the use of a mixture of antibodies (immunoglobulins) to treat a number of health conditions. The preparation is made from the blood plasma of large numbers of healthy human donors and consists mainly of IgG.

Uses

The therapy serves two broad purposes. As replacement therapy, it supplies missing antibodies to people with primary immunodeficiencies such as common variable immunodeficiency or X-linked agammaglobulinaemia. As immunomodulatory therapy, high doses are used in autoimmune and inflammatory conditions including immune thrombocytopenic purpura, Kawasaki disease, Guillain–Barré syndrome, and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy.

Administration

Products may be given intravenously (IVIG), typically every three to four weeks in hospital, or subcutaneously (SCIG) at home on a more frequent schedule. Specific hyperimmune globulins—concentrated against one pathogen, such as anti-D, rabies, or tetanus immunoglobulin—are used after exposure or for prevention.

Adverse effects

Common reactions include headache, fever, chills, and flushing, usually related to infusion rate. Rare serious effects include anaphylaxis (especially in IgA-deficient patients with anti-IgA antibodies), kidney injury, and thrombotic events. See also Immunoglobulin A and Blood plasma.
