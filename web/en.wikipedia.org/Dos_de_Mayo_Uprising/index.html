Dos de Mayo Uprising

Francisco Goya, The Second of May 1808: The Charge of the Mamelukes.

The Dos de Mayo Uprising (2 May 1808) was a spontaneous rebellion by the people of Madrid against the French occupation of the city during the Peninsular War. News that the last members of the Spanish royal family were to be taken to France triggered fighting in the streets around the Royal Palace and the Puerta del Sol. French troops under Joachim Murat suppressed the rising with great brutality, and mass executions followed on 3 May.

Background

Under the Treaty of Fontainebleau (1807), French troops had entered Spain ostensibly to invade Portugal, but by early 1808 they occupied Madrid and other strongholds. King Ferdinand VII was forced to abdicate and was held at Valençay. When Murat moved to remove the remaining infantes, including the young Francisco de Paula, crowds gathered in protest on the morning of 2 May.

The fighting and reprisals

Mobs attacked French soldiers and the feared Mameluke cavalry of the Imperial Guard. Spanish regular troops mostly remained in barracks, though artillery captains Luis Daoíz and Pedro Velarde led a defence of the Monteleón barracks. Murat's troops cleared the city, and a military commission ordered summary executions through the night of 2–3 May at sites including Príncipe Pío hill.

Consequences

News of the uprising and its suppression ignited revolts across Spain and the formation of provincial juntas, turning the occupation into the full-scale Peninsular War. Francisco Goya commemorated the events in his paintings The Second of May 1808 and The Third of May 1808. The 2nd of May is now a public holiday in the Community of Madrid.
