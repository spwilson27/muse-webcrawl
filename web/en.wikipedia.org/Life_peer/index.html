Life peer

Life peers sit in the House of Lords but cannot pass their titles to their children.

A life peer is a member of the British peerage whose title lasts only for their own lifetime and cannot be inherited. Life peerages are created by the monarch, on the advice of the prime minister, under the Life Peerages Act 1958. All life peers hold the rank of baron or baroness and are entitled to sit and vote in the House of Lords.

History

Before 1958, membership of the House of Lords was almost entirely hereditary (apart from the Lords Spiritual and the Law Lords, who gained life peerages under the Appellate Jurisdiction Act 1876 in order to serve as judges). The Life Peerages Act allowed the creation of peerages for women as well as men and opened the Lords to people of achievement in public life, the professions, business, the arts and academia.

Since the House of Lords Act 1999 removed most hereditary peers, life peers have formed the great majority of the chamber's membership. New life peers are nominated by political parties (including "working peers" and peers for the crossbenches), and since 2000 the House of Lords Appointments Commission has vetted nominations and recommended non-party-political appointees.

Styles and titles

A life peer's title typically takes the form "Baron X, of Y in the County of Z"; the territorial designation distinguishes holders of the same surname. Life peers are styled "The Right Honourable the Lord X". Their children are entitled to the courtesy style "The Honourable" but inherit no title.

Numbers and reform

Several hundred living life peers are entitled to attend the Lords, making it one of the largest legislative chambers in the world. Proposals to cap the size of the House, introduce retirement ages, or replace it with an elected chamber have been debated repeatedly; the House of Lords (Hereditary Peers) Bill of 2024 proposed removing the remaining hereditary peers.
