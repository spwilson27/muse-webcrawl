Kőnig's line coloring theorem

In graph theory, Kőnig's line coloring theorem, proved by Dénes Kőnig in 1916, describes an equivalence between edge coloring problems and matching problems in bipartite graphs. It states that in any bipartite graph, the number of colors needed for an edge coloring equals the maximum degree of the graph. Equivalently, the edge chromatic number of a bipartite graph G is equal to Δ(G), its maximum vertex degree.

Statement and significance

For general graphs, Vizing's theorem says the edge chromatic number is either Δ or Δ + 1. Kőnig's theorem shows that bipartite graphs always attain the lower bound. Because edge coloring of a bipartite graph corresponds to decomposing the graph into matchings, the theorem is equivalent to the statement that every regular bipartite graph has a perfect matching — closely related to Hall's marriage theorem and to Kőnig's theorem on vertex covers.

Applications

The theorem underlies scheduling results: for example, in timetabling problems modelled by bipartite graphs (teachers and classes), the colors correspond to time slots, and the theorem guarantees an optimal schedule using exactly Δ slots. It also gives efficient algorithms for edge coloring bipartite graphs.
