Nickel electroplating

Nickel electroplating
TypeElectroplating / surface finishing process

PurposesCorrosion protection, wear resistance, undercoat for chromium, decoration, engineering build-up

Nickel electroplating is a technique of electroplating a thin layer of nickel onto a metal object. The nickel layer can be decorative, provide corrosion resistance and wear resistance, salvage worn or undersized parts, or serve as an undercoat for subsequent chromium plating. It is one of the most widely used electroplating processes in industry.

The object to be plated is made the cathode in an electrolyte containing nickel ions, usually with soluble nickel anodes that dissolve to replenish the bath. Direct current reduces Ni²⁺ ions at the cathode surface to metallic nickel.

Bath types

The Watts bath, developed by Oliver P. Watts in 1916, contains nickel sulfate, nickel chloride, and boric acid as a buffer and remains the workhorse for bright and semi-bright deposits; organic brighteners such as saccharin produce mirror-bright finishes. The sulfamate bath gives low-stress, ductile deposits preferred for electroforming and engineering applications (electroformed nickel is also produced electrolessly via the related, but chemically distinct, electroless nickel process).

Applications

Decorative nickel underlies most "chrome" trim on automobiles, plumbing fixtures, and hardware — typically as duplex or triplex nickel layers beneath a thin chromium topcoat for corrosion performance. Engineering uses include rebuilding worn shafts, coating chemical plant components, and providing a diffusion barrier in electronics. Health and environmental considerations — nickel is a common contact allergen, and its salts are regulated — govern bath operation and wastewater treatment.

History

Nickel plating was experimented with in the 1840s (Goldwin Smith; the Bottger and Adams processes) and commercialised in the United States by Isaac Adams Jr. in the 1860s–70s, becoming a major finishing industry by the early 20th century.
