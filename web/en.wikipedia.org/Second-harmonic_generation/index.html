Second-harmonic generation

Second-harmonic generation (SHG), also called frequency doubling, is a nonlinear optical process in which two photons of the same frequency interact with a nonlinear material and combine to form a single photon with twice the energy, and therefore twice the frequency and half the wavelength, of the original photons.

Theory

SHG arises from the second-order nonlinear susceptibility of a medium. It can occur only in materials without inversion symmetry, such as certain crystals (beta barium borate, lithium niobate, potassium dihydrogen phosphate). Efficient conversion requires phase matching, so that the fundamental and harmonic waves stay in step through the crystal; techniques include angle tuning of birefringent crystals and quasi-phase-matching in periodically poled materials.

History and applications

Second-harmonic generation was first demonstrated in 1961 by Peter Franken and co-workers at the University of Michigan, shortly after the invention of the laser, using a ruby laser and a quartz crystal. It is widely used to produce green laser light by doubling the 1064 nm output of Nd:YAG lasers, in microscopy (SHG imaging of collagen and other ordered structures), and in spectroscopy and materials characterisation.
