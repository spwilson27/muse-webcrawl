The Act of Abjuration (Dutch: Plakkaat van Verlatinghe, "Placard of Desolation") is the declaration of independence by the Dutch provinces signed on 26 July 1581 in The Hague. It proclaimed that Philip II of Spain, by oppressing his subjects and violating their ancient privileges, had forfeited his sovereignty over the Seventeen Provinces, and that the States General therefore abjured their allegiance to him. It is regarded as the founding document of Dutch independence and a milestone in the Dutch Revolt.

Background

The provinces of the Habsburg Netherlands had risen in revolt against Philip II over taxation, centralisation and the persecution of Protestants. In 1579 the northern provinces formed the Union of Utrecht, while attempts at reconciliation with Spain failed. The Act followed the Union's logic: since a prince is constituted by God to govern his subjects "as a shepherd his sheep", a prince who oppresses them is no longer a prince but a tyrant, whom the States may lawfully depose.

Content and influence

Drafted under the direction of the States' pensionary and influenced by monarchomach resistance theory, the Act catalogued Philip's violations of the provinces' charters, including the Joyous Entry of Brabant, and concluded that the subjects were released from their oath. Several of its formulations — a ruler bound by contract, a people entitled to cast off a tyrant — anticipate arguments later used in the American Declaration of Independence of 1776, with which it is often compared.

Aftermath

The States General offered sovereignty to Francis, Duke of Anjou, and later to Elizabeth I of England (declined) and the Earl of Leicester, before effectively becoming a republic by 1588. Spain did not recognise Dutch independence until the Peace of Münster in 1648. The original document is held by the National Archives in The Hague.
