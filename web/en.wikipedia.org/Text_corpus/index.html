Text corpus

In linguistics, a corpus (plural corpora) or text corpus is a large and structured set of texts, usually stored electronically, used to carry out statistical analysis and hypothesis testing about a language. Corpora are the primary resource of corpus linguistics and are used in lexicography, language teaching, translation and natural language processing, including the training of large language models.

Characteristics

A corpus is designed to be representative of a language or language variety, balancing genres, registers, time periods and speakers. Texts may be annotated (or "tagged") with linguistic information such as part of speech, syntactic structure or semantic fields. Well-known corpora include the Brown Corpus of American English (1961), the British National Corpus (100 million words), the Corpus of Contemporary American English, and web-derived corpora containing billions of words.

Analysis

Corpus tools generate word-frequency lists, concordances (keyword-in-context displays), and collocation statistics showing which words habitually occur together. Findings such as Zipf's law describe the distribution of word frequencies. Corpus evidence transformed dictionary-making: the COBUILD project (1980s) was the first to write definitions based on large-scale corpus data, revealing patterns of usage that intuition alone had missed.

Types of corpora

Corpora may be monolingual or multilingual; parallel corpora align texts with their translations, supporting machine translation. Specialised corpora cover learner language, historical texts, spoken conversation or particular domains such as law and medicine. In recent years, enormous corpora scraped from the web have served as training data for neural language models, raising questions about representativeness, copyright and bias.
