The Nineteenth Amendment (Amendment XIX) to the United States Constitution prohibits the United States and its states from denying the right to vote to citizens on the basis of sex, in effect recognising women's suffrage nationwide. It was adopted on 18 August 1920, when Tennessee became the 36th state to ratify it, and certified on 26 August 1920 — a date now commemorated as Women's Equality Day.

The amendment's text, first introduced in Congress in 1878 by Senator Aaron A. Sargent and often called the Susan B. Anthony Amendment, reads: "The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex. Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation."

Background

The campaign for women's voting rights began in earnest at the Seneca Falls Convention of 1848, led by Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott, and continued through the rival suffrage organisations that merged in 1890 as the National American Woman Suffrage Association. Activists such as Susan B. Anthony, Carrie Chapman Catt, and Alice Paul pursued state-by-state victories (Wyoming, Colorado, and others enfranchised women before 1920) alongside the federal amendment strategy; Paul's National Woman's Party added militant picketing of the White House during the First World War.

Adoption and aftermath

The House passed the amendment on 21 May 1919 and the Senate on 4 June 1919. In the decisive Tennessee ratification debate, young legislator Harry T. Burn switched his vote after receiving a letter from his mother urging him to "be a good boy" and support suffrage. In Leser v. Garnett (1922) the Supreme Court unanimously upheld the amendment against procedural challenges. Despite ratification, poll taxes, literacy tests, and intimidation continued to disenfranchise many women — particularly Black women in the Jim Crow South — until the Voting Rights Act of 1965.
