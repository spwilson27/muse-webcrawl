Fruit preserves

Fruit preserves are preparations of fruit whose main preservative is sugar and sometimes acid, stored in glass jars or cans. The category includes jam, jelly, marmalade, conserve, and fruit butter, which differ in the form of the fruit used and whether the result is a smooth gel, a spread containing pieces of fruit, or a homogeneous purée. Sugar inhibits microbial growth by binding water, and cooking the fruit with sugar and its natural pectin produces the characteristic set.

Types

Jam is made from crushed or chopped fruit cooked with sugar. Jelly is made from fruit juice alone, giving a clear, firm gel. Marmalade, in English usage, is a preserve of citrus fruit including shreds of peel. Preserves in the narrower sense contain whole fruits or large pieces suspended in a syrup or lightly gelled juice. Conserves are jams made from a mixture of fruits, often with raisins and nuts; fruit butters are slow-cooked spiced purées, such as apple butter.

Regulation and history

Many jurisdictions legally define these terms — for example, U.S. regulations distinguish jelly from preserves by fruit content and form. Preserving fruit with sugar has ancient roots, but the craft expanded greatly with cheap sugar in the 19th century and with home canning using sealed jars, a tradition still widespread today.
