Islamic finance refers to financial activities that comply with Sharia (Islamic law). A central tenet is the prohibition of riba — usually translated as interest or usury — which means Islamic banks and financial products are structured around trade, leasing, and profit-and-loss sharing rather than interest-bearing loans. Other core prohibitions include gharar (excessive uncertainty or ambiguity in contracts) and maysir (gambling or speculation), as well as investment in prohibited (haram) industries such as alcohol, pork, and conventional gambling.

Principles

Scholars derive Islamic finance principles from the Quran and the Sunnah. Money is treated as a medium of exchange rather than a commodity in itself, so it cannot legitimately generate more money simply through lending. Instead, returns must arise from real economic activity: buying and selling goods, leasing assets, or sharing in the profits and risks of a venture. This underlies the industry's emphasis on asset-backed transactions and risk sharing between financier and entrepreneur.

History

Although classical Islamic jurisprudence developed contracts such as the mudaraba (trust financing) centuries ago, the modern industry dates from the 1960s and 1970s. The Mit Ghamr savings bank in Egypt (1963) is often cited as an early experiment, and the founding of the Islamic Development Bank in 1975 and Dubai Islamic Bank the same year marked the start of institutional Islamic banking. Growth accelerated in the 1990s and 2000s, driven by Gulf oil wealth and Malaysia's deliberate development of a dual banking system. The global industry grew to hold assets measured in the trillions of US dollars by the 2010s–2020s.

Products and instruments

  • Murabaha — cost-plus financing: the bank buys an asset and sells it to the customer at a disclosed markup, payable in instalments. The most widely used instrument.
  • Ijara — leasing, analogous to conventional lease financing, sometimes ending in purchase (ijara wa iqtina).
  • Musharaka — joint venture partnership in which parties share profits and losses in proportion to their contributions.
  • Mudaraba — one party provides capital, the other provides management; profits are shared by agreement, losses fall on the capital provider.
  • Sukuk — certificates representing ownership in underlying assets or projects, often described as Islamic bonds, though structured as asset-backed securities rather than debt.
  • Takaful — cooperative, Sharia-compliant insurance based on mutual guarantee.

Governance and standards

Islamic financial institutions typically employ a Sharia supervisory board of scholars who certify products' compliance. International bodies such as the Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI, founded 1991) and the Islamic Financial Services Board (IFSB, 2002) issue standards, though interpretations differ between regions and scholars — a frequent source of debate and occasional controversy, as when a prominent scholar criticised most sukuk structures in 2007–2008.

Criticism

Critics argue that many Islamic products replicate the economics of conventional interest-bearing finance through legal structuring (hiyal), delivering form without substance, while supporters respond that the industry promotes risk sharing, asset-backing, and ethical screening. Others note higher transaction costs and inconsistency of Sharia rulings across jurisdictions.
