Reefing

Reefing reduces the area of a sail by folding or rolling one edge of the canvas in on itself and attaching the unused portion to a spar or stay. It is the primary means of reducing sail area in strong winds, maintaining speed and control and preventing the vessel from being overpowered, heeling excessively, or damaging sails and rigging. The opposite operation — removing the reef — is called "shaking out" the reef.

Methods

In traditional slab (or "jiffy") reefing, the sail is lowered part-way, the tack and clew at a row of reef points are secured to the boom with reefing lines (pendants), and the loose bundle of sail cloth is tied up with short lengths of line passed through cringles. A sail may have one, two or three sets of reef points, allowing progressive reduction. Modern alternatives include roller reefing and in-mast or in-boom furling, in which the sail is wound around a rotating spar or foil, permitting effectively infinite adjustment but with compromises in sail shape and reliability. Headsails are usually reduced by roller furling or by changing to a smaller jib or storm sail.

Practice

Sailors are advised to reef early — "reef when you first think of it" — because reefing becomes much harder in violent conditions. Reefing also lowers the sail's centre of effort, reducing the heeling moment and weather helm, and is combined with other heavy-weather tactics such as flattening the sail, easing the sheets, and using storm sails.
