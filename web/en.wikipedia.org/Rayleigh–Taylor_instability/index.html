Rayleigh–Taylor instability

Characteristic mushroom-shaped structures of the instability

The Rayleigh–Taylor instability, named for Lord Rayleigh and G. I. Taylor, is an instability of an interface between two fluids of different densities which occurs when the lighter fluid is pushing the heavier fluid. Equivalently, it occurs when a heavier fluid is supported against gravity (or accelerated) by a lighter one; small perturbations of the interface then grow, producing rising plumes ("bubbles") of light fluid and falling spikes of heavy fluid.

Physics

Linear stability analysis shows that a perturbation of wavenumber k grows at a rate depending on the density difference (summarised by the Atwood number), the acceleration, surface tension and viscosity. In the nonlinear stage the spikes and bubbles develop secondary Kelvin–Helmholtz instabilities along their sides, rolling up into mushroom shapes and eventually producing turbulent mixing.

Examples

The instability is observed when water is suspended above oil, in the expansion of supernova remnants such as the Crab Nebula, in volcanic plumes, and in inertial confinement fusion, where it can disrupt the compression of the fuel capsule. It is the underlying mechanism of the familiar behaviour of an inverted glass of water covered by a card: once the card is removed, any disturbance causes the water to fall in growing fingers rather than as a smooth sheet.
