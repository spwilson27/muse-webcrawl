Upton Beall Sinclair Jr. (September 20, 1878 – November 25, 1968) was an American writer, political activist and journalist who wrote nearly one hundred books and other works in several genres. Sinclair's work was well known and popular in the first half of the 20th century, and he won the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction in 1943 for the novel Dragon's Teeth.

Early life

Sinclair was born in Baltimore, Maryland, to a family whose fortunes had declined after the Civil War; his father sold liquor, and the family's poverty contrasted sharply with the wealth of his mother's relatives. He began writing dime novels as a teenager to support himself, graduated from the City College of New York in 1897, and studied law at Columbia while writing fiction.

The Jungle

In 1904 the socialist newspaper Appeal to Reason commissioned Sinclair to investigate Chicago's meatpacking industry. The resulting novel, The Jungle (1906), told through the Lithuanian immigrant Jurgis Rudkus, exposed labour exploitation, poverty and the appalling sanitary conditions of the stockyards. Public revulsion at the descriptions of contaminated meat helped drive passage of the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act in 1906. Sinclair famously remarked, "I aimed at the public's heart, and by accident I hit it in the stomach."

Later career and politics

Sinclair used the proceeds of The Jungle to found the Helicon Home Colony, a cooperative community in New Jersey that burned down in 1907. He wrote many more exposé novels, including King Coal (1917), Oil! (1927) — loosely adapted as the film There Will Be Blood — and the eleven-volume Lanny Budd series. A lifelong socialist, he ran for Congress and for Governor of California; his 1934 campaign for governor on the "End Poverty in California" (EPIC) platform won the Democratic nomination and provoked a pioneering media smear campaign before losing the general election.
