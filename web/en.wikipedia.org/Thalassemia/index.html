Thalassemias are inherited blood disorders characterised by decreased production of one of the globin chains of hemoglobin, resulting in anaemia of varying severity. They are among the most common single-gene disorders worldwide.

Classification

Alpha thalassemia results from deletions or mutations in the HBA1 and HBA2 genes on chromosome 16; severity depends on how many of the four alpha-globin genes are affected, from silent carriage to the fatal hydrops fetalis of haemoglobin Bart's syndrome. Beta thalassemia results from mutations in the HBB gene on chromosome 11 and is classified as minor (trait), intermedia, or major (Cooley's anaemia), the latter requiring lifelong transfusion.

Signs and symptoms

Severe forms present in infancy with failure to thrive, pallor, jaundice, an enlarged spleen and liver, and distinctive skeletal changes from marrow expansion, including frontal bossing and maxillary overgrowth ("chipmunk facies"). Chronic transfusion leads to iron overload damaging the heart, liver and endocrine organs.

Epidemiology and evolution

Thalassemia is most prevalent in the Mediterranean basin, the Middle East, South and Southeast Asia and sub-Saharan Africa — a distribution that largely matches historical malaria endemicity, because carriers enjoy partial protection against severe malaria, a classic example of balancing selection.

Treatment

Management includes regular red-cell transfusions with iron chelation therapy (deferoxamine, deferasirox, deferiprone), splenectomy in selected cases, and luspatercept to reduce transfusion burden. Haematopoietic stem-cell transplantation can be curative, and gene therapies (betibeglogene autotemcel; exagamglogene autotemcel) have been approved since 2019–2023. Cyprus, Sardinia and other high-prevalence regions run carrier-screening programmes that have greatly reduced births of affected children.
