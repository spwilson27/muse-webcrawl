Speleothem

A speleothem (from Greek spēlaion 'cave' and théma 'deposit') is a geological formation made by mineral deposits that accumulate over time in natural caves. Speleothems most commonly form in calcareous caves from calcium carbonate deposits, as limestone is dissolved by slightly acidic water and re-precipitated inside the cave.

Formation

Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and soil, forming a weak carbonic acid that dissolves limestone as it percolates through cracks. When this mineral-laden water reaches a cave chamber, the change in conditions causes carbon dioxide to degas, and calcium carbonate precipitates as calcite, building up formations over thousands of years.

Types

Common speleothems include stalactites, which hang from the cave ceiling, and stalagmites, which grow up from the floor; when the two join they form a column. Other types include flowstone, draperies ("bacon"), straws (thin hollow stalactites), helictites that defy gravity, cave pearls, and rimstone pools.

Climate proxies

Because speleothems grow in layers over very long periods and can be dated precisely with uranium-thorium dating, their isotopic composition is a valuable archive of past climate. Studies of cave deposits in China, for example, have reconstructed the history of the East Asian monsoon over hundreds of thousands of years.
