Bloody April

Bloody April refers to April 1917, the worst month for loss of life for the British Royal Flying Corps (RFC) during the First World War. In support of the Battle of Arras, the RFC flew intensive reconnaissance and artillery-spotting missions over the Western Front and suffered catastrophic casualties at the hands of superior German fighter aircraft and experienced units such as Manfred von Richthofen's Jasta 11.

Background

The British offensive at Arras, launched on 9 April 1917 in support of the French Nivelle Offensive, depended on aerial reconnaissance and photographic mapping. The RFC was equipped largely with obsolete types such as the B.E.2 and F.E.2, slow and poorly armed, while the German Albatros D.III was then the finest fighter at the front. British doctrine, under Hugh Trenchard, insisted on continuous offensive patrols deep over German territory.

Losses

During April 1917 the RFC lost roughly 245 aircraft, with about 211 aircrew killed or missing and 108 taken prisoner. The average life expectancy of a British pilot at the front fell to around 11 days, and replacement pilots sometimes arrived with as little as 17 hours of flying training. Richthofen alone claimed 21 victories that month.

Aftermath

Despite the losses, the RFC completed its reconnaissance tasks for the Arras offensive. New types such as the S.E.5, Sopwith Camel and Bristol F.2 gradually restored competitiveness during the summer of 1917.
