Roman Rite

The Roman Rite (Latin: Ritus Romanus) is the most common ritual family used in the Catholic Church. It is the liturgical rite developed in the Latin Church in the city of Rome and spread throughout the Western world. The vast majority of the world's 1.4 billion Catholics belong to parishes celebrating the Roman Rite, in its ordinary form promulgated after the Second Vatican Council and in the older form codified after the Council of Trent.

History

The rite grew from the early liturgy of Rome, absorbing Gallican and other influences in the early Middle Ages and returning across Europe with the Carolingian reforms. After the Council of Trent, Pope Pius V promulgated a standardised Roman Missal in 1570 (the "Tridentine Mass"), which remained the norm, with minor revisions, until the twentieth century.

Forms of the Mass

Following Vatican II, Pope Paul VI promulgated a revised Missal in 1969–70, celebrated in the vernacular with simplified structure and expanded Scripture readings; this is the form used by most Catholics today. The pre-conciliar form, issued in its last typical edition in 1962, continues to be celebrated in some places, its legal status having been liberalised by Benedict XVI's Summorum Pontificum (2007) and restricted again by Francis's Traditionis custodes (2021). Related uses, such as the Anglican Use of the Ordinariates and the Zaire Use, are authorised variants within the rite.

Other sacraments and offices

Besides the Mass, the Roman Rite includes forms for all sacraments, the Liturgy of the Hours, blessings, and funerals, contained in books such as the Roman Ritual and the Roman Pontifical. Its liturgical year centres on the seasons of Advent, Christmas, Lent, and Easter around the feasts of Christ and the saints.
