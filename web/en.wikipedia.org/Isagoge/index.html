The Isagoge (Greek: Εἰσαγωγή, "Introduction") is a short work on logic by the Neoplatonic philosopher Porphyry (c. 234 – c. 305 AD), written in Sicily around 268–270 AD and sent to Chrysaorios, a Roman senator. It was composed as an introduction to Aristotle's Categories and became, for more than a thousand years, the standard textbook of logic in the Latin West, the Byzantine world, and the Islamic world.

Contents

The Isagoge treats the five "predicables" (quinque voces): genus, species, differentia, property (proprium), and accident. These are the five ways in which a predicate can be related to a subject. Porphyry explains each term, how they resemble and differ from one another, and how genera are divided into species by differentiae — a scheme later visualized as the "Porphyrian tree", in which the highest genus (substance) is successively divided down to the individual.

The problem of universals

In his preface Porphyry raised, but deliberately declined to answer, three questions about genera and species: whether they exist in reality or only in thought; if they exist, whether they are corporeal or incorporeal; and whether they exist separately from sensible things or within them. Boethius, whose Latin translation and commentaries transmitted the work to the medieval West, made these questions central to scholastic philosophy, and they framed the medieval problem of universals — the debate between realists, conceptualists, and nominalists.

Influence and translations

The Isagoge was translated into Latin first by Marius Victorinus (4th century) and again by Boethius (early 6th century), whose version, together with Aristotle's Categories and De interpretatione, formed the logica vetus ("old logic") that constituted the logic curriculum of early medieval schools. It was also translated into Syriac, Arabic, and Armenian; in the Arabic tradition, as the Īsāghūjī, it stood at the head of the standard logic curriculum and generated a vast commentary literature that persisted into the modern period.
