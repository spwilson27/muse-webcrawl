Anthropometry (from Greek ánthrōpos, "human", and métron, "measure") is the systematic measurement of the physical properties of the human body, primarily dimensional descriptors of body size and shape. It is a branch of anthropology that today finds major applications in ergonomics, industrial design, clothing design, architecture, medicine, and nutrition assessment.

Measurements

Core measurements include stature (height), body mass, sitting height, limb lengths and circumferences, skinfold thicknesses, and cranial dimensions such as head length and breadth. Derived indices include the body mass index (BMI) and cephalic index. Modern practice supplements manual instruments (stadiometers, calipers, tape measures) with three-dimensional body scanning.

History

Alphonse Bertillon developed a system of criminal identification in the 1880s based on eleven body measurements ("Bertillonage"), which was superseded by fingerprinting. Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century anthropometry was entangled with racial science and eugenics; contemporary anthropometry repudiates typological race classification and instead studies human variation, growth, nutrition, and adaptation. Anthropometric history uses long-run height and weight data as indicators of living standards.

Applications

Ergonomic design of workstations, vehicle interiors, and clothing relies on population anthropometric databases. In public health, measurements such as mid-upper arm circumference and height-for-age identify child malnutrition. Forensic anthropology uses skeletal measurements to assist identification.
