Poetics (Aristotle)

The Poetics (Greek: Poiētikē; c. 335 BC) by Aristotle is the earliest surviving work of Greek dramatic theory and the first extant philosophical treatise to focus on literary theory. In it Aristotle analyses poetry — chiefly epic poetry and tragedy, with lost discussion of comedy — as forms of mimesis (imitation).

Contents

Aristotle defines tragedy as "an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude", effected through dramatic rather than narrative form, "with incidents arousing pity and fear, wherewith to accomplish the catharsis of such emotions". He ranks the elements of tragedy in order of importance: plot (mythos), character, thought, diction, melody and spectacle. A good plot, he argues, is unified and turns on peripeteia (reversal) and anagnorisis (recognition); the tragic hero falls through hamartia, an error rather than vice, with Sophocles' Oedipus Rex as the model case.

Influence

Preserved through Arabic and Latin transmission, the Poetics profoundly shaped Renaissance and neoclassical criticism; later readers codified Aristotle's remarks into the "three unities" of time, place and action. It remains a foundational text for narratology, screenwriting theory and aesthetics.
