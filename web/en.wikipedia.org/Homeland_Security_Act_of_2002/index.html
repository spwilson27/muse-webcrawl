Homeland Security Act of 2002

The Homeland Security Act of 2002 (Public Law 107-296, 116 Stat. 2135) is an act of the United States Congress that was signed into law by President George W. Bush on November 25, 2002. The Act created the United States Department of Homeland Security (DHS) and the cabinet-level position of Secretary of Homeland Security, in the largest reorganization of the federal government since the creation of the Department of Defense in 1947. It was introduced in the aftermath of the September 11 attacks and the subsequent anthrax attacks.

Background

Following the September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks, President Bush established the Office of Homeland Security within the White House, headed by former Pennsylvania governor Tom Ridge. Congressional proposals to create a full department followed. The bill passed the House of Representatives 299–121 and the Senate 90–9 in November 2002, after contentious debate over the civil-service protections of the new department's employees.

Provisions

The Act consolidated 22 federal agencies or programs into the new Department of Homeland Security, including the Coast Guard, the Secret Service, the Immigration and Naturalization Service (whose functions were split among new bureaus), the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), the Transportation Security Administration, and Customs. It established directorates for border and transportation security; emergency preparedness and response; science and technology; and information analysis and infrastructure protection. The Act also contained provisions concerning critical infrastructure protection, cybersecurity research, and the SAFETY Act's liability protections for anti-terrorism technologies, as well as the restructuring of immigration enforcement under Immigration and Customs Enforcement and Customs and Border Protection.

Criticism

Critics raised concerns about the Act's effect on privacy and civil liberties, and about a provision, inserted late in the drafting process, that shielded pharmaceutical companies from lawsuits over vaccine ingredients; the provision was repealed in 2003. Labor unions objected to the Secretary's authority to waive collective bargaining rights. The department's performance in responding to Hurricane Katrina in 2005 led to further reorganization under the Post-Katrina Emergency Management Reform Act of 2006.
