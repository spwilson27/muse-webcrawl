Novel

A novel is an extended work of narrative fiction, typically written in prose and published as a book. Novels are usually long enough to fill a volume—modern publishing conventions often place the threshold at about 40,000 words—and they tell a connected story, generally following characters through a sequence of events over time. The English word derives from the Italian novella, meaning a short tale or piece of news.

History

Long prose fiction is ancient: works such as the Japanese Tale of Genji (c. 1010) by Murasaki Shikibu and prose romances of the Hellenistic world are often cited as early novels. In Europe, Miguel de Cervantes's Don Quixote (1605, 1615) is widely regarded as the first modern novel. The form took off in 18th-century England with Daniel Defoe's Robinson Crusoe (1719), Samuel Richardson's epistolary Pamela (1740), and Henry Fielding's Tom Jones (1749), helped by rising literacy, circulating libraries, and cheaper print.

The 19th century is often called the novel's golden age, producing Jane Austen, Charles Dickens, Leo Tolstoy, Fyodor Dostoevsky, Gustave Flaubert, and George Eliot. In the 20th century, modernist writers such as James Joyce, Virginia Woolf, and William Faulkner experimented with stream of consciousness and fractured narrative; later movements include postmodernism, magical realism, and the global expansion of the form.

Genres and forms

Novels are classified by genre—including detective fiction, science fiction, fantasy, romance, historical fiction, horror, and the thriller—and by form, such as the epistolary novel, the picaresque, the Bildungsroman (novel of formation), and the roman à clef. Shorter forms include the novella and the novelette.

Elements

Typical elements include plot, character, setting, theme, point of view, and style. Ian Watt's influential study The Rise of the Novel (1957) linked the form's emergence to "formal realism" and the growth of a middle-class reading public.
