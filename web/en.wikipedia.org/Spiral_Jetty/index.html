Spiral Jetty is an earthwork sculpture constructed in April 1970 by American artist Robert Smithson. Built on the northeastern shore of the Great Salt Lake in Utah, it is the best-known work of the land art (earthworks) movement and is considered Smithson's masterpiece.

Construction

Smithson leased a 10-acre site at Rozel Point, an area of abandoned oil exploration and tar seeps. Using dump trucks, a tractor and a front loader, he and a local contractor moved some 6,650 tons of black basalt rock and earth into the lake, forming a coil 1,500 feet (460 m) long and 15 feet (4.6 m) wide that winds counterclockwise from the shore into the reddish water — coloured by salt-loving algae and bacteria. Smithson documented the work in a film and a famous essay, both also titled Spiral Jetty.

Submergence and re-emergence

Within a few years, rising lake levels submerged the jetty completely; it remained underwater for decades, emerging again during drought in the late 1990s, transformed — encrusted white with salt. Fluctuating water levels continue to alter it, an entropic process Smithson regarded as integral to the work. When the lake reached record lows in 2022 the jetty stood far from the water's edge, prompting debate about drought, climate and the work's future.

Stewardship and recognition

The work was donated to the Dia Art Foundation in 1999, which stewards it in partnership with Utah institutions. In 2008 it was threatened by proposed oil drilling nearby, which was vigorously opposed by artists and the public. In 2017 Spiral Jetty was designated Utah's official state work of art.
