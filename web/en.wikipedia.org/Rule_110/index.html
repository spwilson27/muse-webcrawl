Rule 110

TypeElementary cellular automaton
Named byStephen Wolfram (Wolfram code)

Rule 110 is an elementary cellular automaton with interesting behaviour on the boundary between stability and chaos. In 2004 it was proved to be capable of universal computation — that is, Turing complete — making it one of the simplest possible universal computing systems. The rule is named after its Wolfram code, the decimal number 110, which encodes its transition table in binary (01101110).

The rule

An elementary cellular automaton is a one-dimensional array of cells, each in state 0 or 1, updated in discrete time steps according to a rule that depends on a cell's current state and those of its two immediate neighbours. Rule 110's transitions are:

Current neighbourhood111110101100011010001000
New state01101110

Stephen Wolfram investigated Rule 110 beginning in the 1980s and classified it as a "Class 4" automaton, producing complex, localised structures ("gliders") against a periodic background ether. Rule 110 is asymmetrical: its mirror image is Rule 124, and its complement-equivalent variants are Rules 110, 124, 137 and 193.

Universality

Matthew Cook, working with Wolfram, presented a proof at the 1998 Santa Fe Institute conference on cellular automata that Rule 110 can emulate a cyclic tag system, which is known to be Turing complete; the proof was published in the journal Complex Systems in 2004 after litigation over its earlier disclosure. The construction uses collisions between gliders moving through the ether. Because of the enormous overhead of the emulation, Rule 110 is not a practical computing device, but its universality is a celebrated demonstration that very simple rules can generate behaviour of unbounded complexity.
