Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood

The Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood (later known as the Pre-Raphaelites) was a group of English painters, poets, and art critics, founded in 1848 by William Holman Hunt, John Everett Millais and Dante Gabriel Rossetti, with William Michael Rossetti, James Collinson, Frederic George Stephens and Thomas Woolner making up the original seven members.

Principles

The Brotherhood sought a return to the abundant detail, intense colours and complex compositions of Quattrocento Italian art — the art produced "before Raphael" — rejecting what they considered the mechanical approach of Mannerist artists who succeeded Raphael and Michelangelo. In practice this meant painting directly from nature with brilliant colour on a white ground, serious and sincere subject matter drawn from literature, poetry, history and contemporary life, and meticulous, almost photographic detail. Their works were signed with the secret initials "PRB", which provoked curiosity and controversy when revealed.

Reception and development

Early Pre-Raphaelite works were attacked by critics; Charles Dickens condemned Millais's Christ in the House of His Parents (1850), but the critic John Ruskin defended the group in letters to The Times and in his pamphlet on Pre-Raphaelitism, becoming an influential patron. The Brotherhood as such dissolved in the early 1850s, but its approach evolved into the wider Pre-Raphaelite movement: Rossetti's later medievalising work with models such as Elizabeth Siddal and Jane Morris inspired a second phase associated with William Morris, Edward Burne-Jones and the Arts and Crafts movement.

Legacy

Pre-Raphaelitism influenced the Symbolist and Aesthetic movements across Europe and is sometimes described as an early avant-garde. Works such as Millais's Ophelia (1851–52) and Rossetti's Beata Beatrix remain among the most reproduced images of Victorian art.
