Warsaw Ghetto Uprising

The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising was the 1943 act of Jewish resistance in the Warsaw Ghetto in German-occupied Poland during the Second World War, which opposed Nazi Germany’s final effort to transport the remaining ghetto population to the Majdanek and Treblinka extermination camps. It was the largest single revolt by Jews during the war.

Background

Established in 1940, the Warsaw Ghetto confined some 400,000 Jews in appalling conditions. In the summer of 1942, during the Grossaktion Warschau, roughly a quarter of a million ghetto inhabitants were deported to the Treblinka extermination camp and murdered. The remaining Jews, understanding that deportation meant death, formed resistance organisations: the Jewish Combat Organisation (ŻOB), led by the 23-year-old Mordechai Anielewicz, and the Jewish Military Union (ŻZW). In January 1943 armed resistance forced the Germans to halt a renewed deportation after four days.

The uprising

The final liquidation began on 19 April 1943, the eve of Passover. Some 2,000 Waffen-SS and police under SS-Brigadeführer Jürgen Stroop entered the ghetto and were met by fighters armed with pistols, a few rifles, homemade grenades and petrol bombs. Unable to defeat the resistance in street fighting, the Germans systematically burned the ghetto block by block, forcing inhabitants from bunkers with smoke and gas. Anielewicz and many of the ŻOB command died at the bunker at Miła 18 on 8 May. Symbolic resistance ended on 16 May, when Stroop blew up the Great Synagogue of Warsaw and reported that the ghetto was “no more”.

Aftermath

The Stroop Report recorded 56,065 Jews captured; about 7,000 were shot in the ghetto, and most of the rest were deported to Treblinka and Majdanek, where they were murdered. A few fighters escaped through the sewers and joined the Polish underground. The uprising inspired resistance elsewhere, including the revolts at Treblinka and Sobibór in 1943 and the Warsaw Uprising of the Polish Home Army in 1944. Today the Monument to the Ghetto Heroes, unveiled in 1948, and the POLIN Museum of the History of Polish Jews stand on the site.
