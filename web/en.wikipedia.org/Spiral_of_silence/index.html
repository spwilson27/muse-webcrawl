Spiral of silence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The spiral of silence process

The spiral of silence theory is a political science and mass communication theory proposed by the German pollster Elisabeth Noelle-Neumann in 1974. It holds that individuals have a quasi-statistical sense of the prevailing climate of opinion, and that fear of social isolation leads those who perceive their views to be in the minority to remain silent, while those who perceive their views as dominant express them more confidently. Over time this asymmetry makes the apparently dominant opinion seem even stronger and the minority view weaker — a self-reinforcing spiral.

Key concepts

Noelle-Neumann emphasised the role of mass media, whose consonant and cumulative coverage shapes perceptions of which opinions can be voiced in public without risk. Exceptions to the spiral include "hardcore" minorities and avant-gardes who speak out regardless of isolation.

Criticism and research

Empirical support for the theory is mixed: studies find a modest relationship between perceiving one's opinion as minority-held and unwillingness to speak, influenced by culture, issue type and reference groups. The theory has been widely applied to elections, public opinion polling and, more recently, social media behaviour.
