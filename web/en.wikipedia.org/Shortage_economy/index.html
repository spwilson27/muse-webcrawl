Shortage economy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Shortage economy is a term coined by the Hungarian economist János Kornai in his work Economics of Shortage (1980) to describe the systematic and chronic shortages of goods and services in centrally planned economies, such as those of the Soviet Union and its allies. In Kornai's analysis, shortages were not accidental policy failures but a normal, self-reproducing state of the classical socialist system.

Causes

Kornai traced shortages to the institutional structure of the planned economy. State-owned firms operated under soft budget constraints: because the state would always bail them out, firms faced little penalty for inefficiency and had an insatiable "investment hunger", hoarding inputs, labor and materials against future scarcity. Planners set taut output plans, prices were fixed administratively and did not clear markets, and quantity signals replaced price signals. Because buyers could not be sure of supply, they engaged in forced substitution, hoarding, and queueing, which reproduced shortage throughout the system.

Phenomena

Characteristic features of the shortage economy included queues for consumer goods, empty shelves alongside unwanted inventories, sellers' markets in which producers dictated terms, low product quality, suppressed innovation, bribery and informal networks (blat) to obtain goods, and the diversion of effort into a second economy.

Legacy

Kornai contrasted the shortage economy with the surplus economy of capitalism, where the characteristic problem is not shortage but excess supply and unemployment. His framework influenced the economics of transition after 1989, as post-socialist countries hardened budget constraints and liberalized prices.
