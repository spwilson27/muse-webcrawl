Bohemia

Bohemia (Czech: Čechy) is the westernmost and largest historical region of the Czech Republic, comprising roughly the western two-thirds of the country. Together with Moravia and Czech Silesia it forms the Czech lands. Its capital and largest city is Prague. The name derives from the Boii, a Celtic tribe that inhabited the area in antiquity.

Geography

Bohemia is a plateau ringed by mountain ranges: the Šumava (Bohemian Forest) in the southwest, the Ore Mountains (Krušné hory) in the northwest, the Krkonoše (Giant Mountains) in the northeast — home to Sněžka, the highest peak in the Czech Republic at 1,603 metres — and the Bohemian-Moravian Highlands in the southeast. The region is drained by the Elbe (Labe) and its great tributary the Vltava (Moldau), which flows through Prague.

History

Slavic tribes settled Bohemia in the 6th century. The Přemyslid dynasty unified the region by the 10th century, and the Kingdom of Bohemia became a constituent state of the Holy Roman Empire, its king one of the seven prince-electors. Under Charles IV in the 14th century, Prague became the imperial capital and Charles University (1348), the first university in Central Europe, was founded.

In the 15th century Bohemia was convulsed by the Hussite Wars, sparked by the execution of the reformer Jan Hus in 1415. Bohemia later passed to the Habsburgs (1526); the Bohemian revolt of 1618 and the Battle of White Mountain (1620) opened the Thirty Years' War and ended Bohemian autonomy. After 1918 Bohemia formed the core of Czechoslovakia, and since 1993 of the independent Czech Republic.

Culture

Bohemia is famous for its beer (the pilsner style was created at Plzeň in 1842), its glass and crystal, and its spa towns such as Karlovy Vary and Mariánské Lázně. Composers Bedřich Smetana and Antonín Dvořák celebrated the Bohemian landscape in works such as Má vlast and the New World Symphony. In English, "bohemian" came to denote an unconventional artistic lifestyle, through a French association of Bohemia with the Romani people.
