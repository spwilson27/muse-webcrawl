Hybrid fiber-coaxial

Hybrid fiber-coaxial (HFC)
Type: Broadband telecommunications network
Backbone: Optical fiber to neighbourhood nodes
Last drop: Coaxial cable to premises
Standards: DOCSIS (data), used for cable television and internet

Hybrid fiber-coaxial (HFC) is a telecommunications industry term for a broadband network that combines optical fiber and coaxial cable. Fiber carries signals from the operator's headend to optical nodes serving neighbourhoods of typically a few hundred homes; coaxial cable then distributes signals the final distance to subscribers. HFC has been widely used by cable television operators since the early 1990s for television, broadband internet and voice services.

Architecture

Downstream signals are converted to light at the headend, sent over fiber to a node, converted back to radio-frequency signals, and amplified along coaxial trunk and feeder lines to taps at each home. Upstream signals travel the reverse path in a separate frequency band. Nodes can be segmented or split to increase capacity per user as demand grows.

Services

Analog and digital television historically occupied downstream channels; data is carried using DOCSIS standards, which progressively raised speeds — DOCSIS 3.1 supports multi-gigabit downstream using OFDM, and DOCSIS 4.0 extends spectrum and symmetric capacity. Cable telephony and business services also run over HFC.

Advantages and limits

HFC let cable operators upgrade incrementally from all-coax plants while reusing the existing coaxial drop network. Its limits include shared bandwidth among homes on a node, upstream noise, power-hungry amplifiers, and competition from all-fiber (FTTH) networks with higher symmetric capacity and lower maintenance.
