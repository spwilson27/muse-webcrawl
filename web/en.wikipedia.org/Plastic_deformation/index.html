Plastic deformation

In physics and materials science, plasticity is the ability of a solid material to undergo permanent deformation — a non-reversible change of shape — in response to applied forces. Plastic deformation contrasts with elastic deformation, in which the material returns to its original shape when the load is removed.

Mechanisms

In crystalline materials such as metals, plastic deformation occurs mainly through the movement of dislocations — line defects in the crystal lattice — along slip planes, a process called slip. Twinning and grain-boundary sliding contribute in some materials. In amorphous solids such as polymers and glasses, deformation proceeds by the sliding and rearrangement of molecular chains or localised shear transformations.

Yield and flow

Plastic flow begins when the applied stress exceeds the material's yield strength. On a stress–strain curve, the onset of yielding marks the limit of the elastic region; beyond it, many metals harden as deformation proceeds (work hardening) because dislocations multiply and obstruct one another. Necking and fracture eventually follow if deformation continues.

Applications

Controlled plastic deformation is the basis of metal-forming processes such as forging, rolling, drawing, and extrusion, and of the crumple zones that absorb energy in vehicle crashes. Engineers design structures to keep ordinary loads below yield, while relying on ductility to warn of overload before failure.
