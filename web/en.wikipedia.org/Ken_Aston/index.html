Kenneth George Aston (1 September 1915 – 23 October 2001) was an English football referee best known for inventing the system of yellow and red penalty cards used throughout association football.

Life and career

Aston was born in Colchester and educated at Ilford County High School. He worked as a schoolteacher and served in the British Indian Army during the Second World War, reaching the rank of lieutenant colonel. He began refereeing in the 1930s and progressed through the English leagues, becoming a Football League referee after the war.

International refereeing

Aston officiated at the 1962 FIFA World Cup in Chile, including the notorious opening match between Chile and Italy known as the Battle of Santiago, an ill-tempered game with repeated fouls, fighting, and police intervention on the pitch. The experience convinced him that referees needed a clearer, universally understood way to communicate cautions and dismissals to players, spectators, and the press.

Red and yellow cards

According to Aston's own account, the idea came to him while stopped at traffic lights in London: the amber light suggested a warning, "calm down," and the red light a clear instruction to stop. He proposed the colour-coded card system to FIFA, and it was first used at the 1970 FIFA World Cup in Mexico. The cards overcame language barriers and made disciplinary decisions visible to everyone in the stadium. Aston also served on FIFA's Referees' Committee and was responsible for referee training at the 1966 World Cup.

Legacy

Aston later became a senior lecturer for the American Youth Soccer Organization and an influential figure in referee education in the United States. He was appointed MBE in 1997 for services to football. The red and yellow card system he devised remains one of the most recognisable innovations in the laws of the game.
