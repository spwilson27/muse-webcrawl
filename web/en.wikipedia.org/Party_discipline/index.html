Party discipline

Party discipline is the ability of a political party's leadership to control its members in a legislature and compel them to vote together on legislation and other motions. It is a defining feature of parliamentary systems such as those of the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia and India, where the survival of the government depends on maintaining a majority in votes of confidence.

Mechanisms

Discipline is enforced by party whips, officials who communicate the party line, count expected votes and apply pressure to wavering members. Sanctions for defying a whipped vote range from denial of promotion and committee places to the ultimate punishment of expulsion from the party — in the British Parliament, "having the whip withdrawn". In some systems, such as India's anti-defection law, a legislator who defies the whip may lose their seat entirely.

Degrees of discipline

Votes may be strictly whipped (a "three-line whip" in Westminster terminology), loosely encouraged, or left as a free vote (or conscience vote), typically on moral questions such as abortion or capital punishment. Party discipline is generally much weaker in the United States, where legislators vote independently far more often, and stronger in closed-list proportional systems, where members' re-election depends on their position on a party list.

Criticism and defence

Critics argue that strict discipline reduces representatives to lobby fodder, suppresses independent judgement and concentrates power in the party leadership. Defenders respond that voters elect parties on manifestos, that discipline makes collective accountability possible, and that governments could not deliver coherent programmes without it.
