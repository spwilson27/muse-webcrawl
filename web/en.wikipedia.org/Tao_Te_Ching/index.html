Tao Te Ching

The Tao Te Ching (Chinese: 道德經; pinyin: Dàodé Jīng) is a Chinese classic text and the foundational work of Taoism. Traditionally credited to Laozi (Lao Tzu), a sage said to have been an elder contemporary of Confucius, it is now generally regarded by scholars as a compilation of sayings assembled between the 4th and 3rd centuries BCE. Together with the Zhuangzi, it is the principal source for philosophical Taoism, and it profoundly influenced Chinese religion, philosophy, and culture.

Content

The text comprises 81 short chapters divided into two parts: the Tao Ching (chapters 1–37) on the nature of the Tao ("the Way"), and the Te Ching (chapters 38–81) on Te ("virtue" or "power"). Its famous opening declares:

"The Tao that can be told is not the eternal Tao; the name that can be named is not the eternal name."

Central concepts include wu wei (non-action or effortless action), naturalness and spontaneity (ziran), simplicity (pu, the "uncarved block"), and the softness and receptivity of water, which overcomes the hard and strong. The text also offers advice to rulers: govern lightly, keep taxes and interventions low, and let the people order themselves.

Textual history

The oldest surviving fragments were found among the Guodian Chu Slips (c. 300 BCE), containing portions of about a third of the text. Silk manuscripts excavated at Mawangdui in 1973 contain two near-complete copies (c. 168 BCE) in which the Te section precedes the Tao section. The standard received text descends from the recension of Wang Bi (3rd century CE), while the commentary of Heshang Gong became central to religious Taoism.

Influence

The Tao Te Ching is among the most translated works in world literature, with well over two hundred translations into English alone. It influenced Confucian and Buddhist thinkers in China (and Zen in Japan), Chinese landscape painting and poetry, and, in the West, figures ranging from Tolstoy to modern practitioners of leadership theory and psychotherapy.
