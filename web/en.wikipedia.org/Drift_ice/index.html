Drift ice, also called pack ice when it covers a large area, is sea ice that floats on the surface of the water and drifts with winds and currents, as opposed to fast ice, which is attached ("fastened") to a shore or the sea floor. Drift ice consists of individual floes, ranging from a few metres to many kilometres across, that collide, raft over one another, and freeze together or break apart with the seasons.

Properties and terminology

Drift is described by its concentration — the fraction of the sea surface covered, measured in tenths — and by floe size and thickness. Under the WMO sea-ice nomenclature, very open drift covers 1–3/10 of the surface, open drift 4–6/10, close pack 7–8/10, and very close pack 9–10/10. Wind-driven motion, described by the Nansen number and the Ekman spiral, typically moves ice at about 2% of the wind speed, deflected by the Coriolis effect. Pressure between floes builds ridges and keels; open-water leads and polynyas form where floes diverge.

Significance

Drift ice governs polar ecosystems: algae growing beneath floes feed krill and fish, and seals, walrus, and polar bears depend on it as a platform. It has historically blocked navigation — trapping ships from Franklin's expedition to Shackleton's Endurance — and remains a hazard managed by icebreaker escort and ice charts. The transpolar drift stream carries ice across the Arctic Ocean, a motion Fridtjof Nansen deliberately exploited with the Fram in 1893–96, and later studied by drifting stations and the MOSAiC expedition of 2019–20. Declining Arctic drift ice extent and thickness are key indicators of climate change.
