Ives–Stilwell experiment

The Ives–Stilwell experiment, first performed by Herbert E. Ives and G. R. Stilwell in 1938, measured the transverse Doppler effect — the relativistic time dilation of moving clocks — using canal rays of hydrogen ions. It was one of the first direct experimental confirmations of time dilation predicted by special relativity.

The experiment

Ives and Stilwell accelerated hydrogen ions to high speeds and compared the wavelengths of light they emitted in the forward and backward directions. The first-order (classical) Doppler shifts in the two directions are opposite, so their average cancels out, leaving only the second-order shift caused by time dilation. The measured shift matched the relativistic prediction.

Significance and modern versions

Although Ives himself interpreted the result within a Lorentzian ether framework, the experiment is now regarded as a classic test of special relativity. Modern repetitions using fast ion beams and laser spectroscopy at storage rings have confirmed the time-dilation prediction to very high precision.
