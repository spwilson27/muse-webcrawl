Canoe

TypeSmall open watercraft
PropulsionSingle-bladed paddle(s)
Oldest knownPesse canoe, c. 8000 BC (Netherlands)
UsesTransport, fishing, recreation, sport

A canoe is a lightweight, narrow watercraft, typically pointed at both ends and open on top, propelled by one or more paddlers kneeling or sitting facing the direction of travel and using a single-bladed paddle. Canoes are among the oldest types of boat: the Pesse canoe of the Netherlands, dated to around 8000 BC, is the oldest known surviving boat of any kind. Indigenous peoples of the Americas developed sophisticated birchbark and dugout canoes that became essential tools of transport, trade, and exploration.

Design and construction

Traditional canoes were made as dugouts (hollowed logs) or from birch bark stretched over a wooden frame and sealed with resins — light enough to be portaged between waterways. The large dugout canoes of the Pacific Northwest, and the birchbark canoes adopted by French-Canadian voyageurs in the fur trade, illustrate the type's range. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries canoes were built of wood-and-canvas, aluminium, and later fibreglass, polyethylene, and carbon fibre. Modern designs vary widely for flatwater touring, whitewater, and racing; the canoe is distinguished from the kayak principally by the single-bladed paddle and higher seating position.

Use and sport

Canoes remain widely used for wilderness tripping, fishing, and recreation, especially in Canada, where the canoe is a national symbol. As a sport, canoeing includes sprint racing (an Olympic discipline since 1936), slalom, marathon, and freestyle, governed internationally by the International Canoe Federation. Canoe tripping routes such as those in Ontario's Algonquin Provincial Park and the Boundary Waters between Canada and the United States draw paddlers from around the world.
