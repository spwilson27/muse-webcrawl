Regression toward the mean

In statistics, regression toward the mean (also called regression to the mean) is the phenomenon that if a random variable is extreme in its first measurement, it will tend to be closer to the average in its second measurement — and, paradoxically, if it is extreme in its second measurement, it will tend to have been closer to the average in its first. The effect arises whenever measurements contain random variation and is a frequent source of mistaken causal conclusions.

Galton's data: children of unusually tall parents tend to be shorter than their parents.

History

Francis Galton described the phenomenon in the 1880s in studies of heredity, notably his 1886 paper "Regression towards mediocrity in hereditary stature". He found that the children of very tall parents were, on average, tall but shorter than their parents, while children of very short parents were taller than their parents — in both cases closer to the population mean. Galton initially treated this as a biological force, but it is in fact a statistical necessity whenever correlation between the two measurements is imperfect (less than 1).

Examples and misinterpretations

  • The "Sports Illustrated cover jinx" and the sophomore slump: athletes featured after exceptional seasons tend to perform less exceptionally afterwards.
  • Speed cameras installed after an accident spike may appear to work simply because the spike regresses.
  • Patients enrolled in trials at their worst symptoms tend to improve even in control groups — one reason clinical trials need placebo controls and randomisation.
  • Daniel Kahneman's example of flight instructors who concluded praise harms performance: cadets praised after an exceptionally good landing usually did worse next time, and those screamed at after a bad landing usually improved — both by regression alone.

Statistical basis

If two variables are imperfectly correlated, the expected value of one, given an extreme value of the other, lies closer to its mean in standard-deviation units. Regression toward the mean does not imply that the overall distribution shrinks; variance remains stable because extremes in the other direction balance out.
