Freedom Riders

Freedom Riders were civil rights activists who rode interstate buses into the segregated Southern United States in 1961 and subsequent years to challenge the non-enforcement of the United States Supreme Court decisions Morgan v. Virginia (1946) and Boynton v. Virginia (1960), which ruled that segregated public buses were unconstitutional.

The 1961 rides

The first Freedom Ride left Washington, D.C., on 4 May 1961, organised by the Congress of Racial Equality (CORE) under James Farmer. The interracial group of thirteen riders planned to travel through the South to New Orleans. In Anniston, Alabama, one bus was firebombed; in Birmingham and Montgomery, riders were beaten by mobs while police stood aside or were absent. When CORE suspended the ride, students from Nashville, organised by Diane Nash and the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), continued it, insisting that stopping would concede victory to violence.

Jackson and federal action

Riders who reached Jackson, Mississippi, were arrested for breach of the peace and many were sent to the notorious Parchman Farm penitentiary. Over the summer of 1961 more than 400 people participated in the rides. Under pressure from Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy, the Interstate Commerce Commission issued regulations, effective 1 November 1961, banning segregation in interstate bus terminals and on the buses themselves.

Legacy

The Freedom Rides demonstrated the power of nonviolent direct action and of interracial protest, forced federal enforcement of existing law, and trained a generation of civil rights leaders, including John Lewis, who was beaten during the rides and later served in Congress. In 2017 the Freedom Riders National Monument was established in Anniston, Alabama.
