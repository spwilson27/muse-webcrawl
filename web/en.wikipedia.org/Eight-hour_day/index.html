Eight-hour day

Origin: Labour movement, 19th century

Slogan: "Eight hours labour, eight hours recreation, eight hours rest"

The eight-hour day (also known as the 40-hour week movement or the short-time movement) is a social movement to regulate the length of a working day, preventing excesses and abuses of working time. The movement originated in the Industrial Revolution in Britain, where industrial production in large factories transformed working life.

Early advocates

The modern idea is often traced to Welsh social reformer Robert Owen, who in 1817 coined the slogan "Eight hours labour, eight hours recreation, eight hours rest". British factory legislation gradually limited working hours, and trade unions made the shorter day a central demand throughout the nineteenth century.

Milestones

In 1856, stonemasons in Melbourne, Australia won one of the earliest eight-hour days without loss of pay, commemorated by the Eight Hour Day monument. In the United States, the Haymarket affair of 1886 was bound up with the eight-hour campaign; the Ford Motor Company adopted eight-hour days and the five-day week in 1926, and the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 established the 40-hour week in federal law. In 1919 the International Labour Organization's first convention limited hours in industry to eight a day and forty-eight a week.

Modern position

The eight-hour day remains a standard benchmark in labor law in much of the world, though debates continue over overtime, flexible working, and proposals for a four-day week.
