Gravitational-wave astronomy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gravitational-wave astronomy is a branch of observational astronomy that uses gravitational waves — ripples in the fabric of spacetime predicted by Albert Einstein's theory of general relativity in 1916 — to observe astronomical objects and events that are invisible or poorly seen with electromagnetic radiation. It opened an entirely new window on the universe, complementing optical, radio, and other telescopes.

Detectors

The principal instruments are laser interferometers that measure minute changes in distance caused by a passing wave. The LIGO observatories in Hanford, Washington, and Livingston, Louisiana, each have 4 km arms; the European Virgo detector near Pisa has 3 km arms, and Japan's KAGRA detector is built underground. Future facilities include LIGO-India and the space-based LISA mission, planned by the European Space Agency for the 2030s, which will detect lower-frequency waves from supermassive black hole mergers. Pulsar timing arrays, such as NANOGrav, monitor millisecond pulsars to detect nanohertz waves; in 2023 they reported evidence for a stochastic gravitational-wave background.

First detections

On 14 September 2015 LIGO made the first direct detection, GW150914, from the merger of two stellar-mass black holes about 1.3 billion light-years away. The discovery was announced in February 2016 and earned Rainer Weiss, Barry Barish, and Kip Thorne the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics. On 17 August 2017 the detectors observed GW170817, the merger of two neutron stars, whose electromagnetic counterpart was seen across the spectrum — the birth of multi-messenger astronomy with gravitational waves. The event confirmed that such mergers forge heavy elements such as gold and platinum and provided an independent measure of the Hubble constant.

Sources and science

Detected sources include binary black holes, binary neutron stars, and mixed neutron star–black hole systems. By the mid-2020s the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA collaboration had catalogued hundreds of candidate events. Gravitational waves probe extreme gravity, test general relativity, reveal black hole populations, and may eventually reveal signals from the early universe inaccessible to light.
