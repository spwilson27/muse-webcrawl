Voting Rights Act of 1965

Long titleAn Act to enforce the fifteenth amendment to the Constitution of the United States, and for other purposes
Enacted by89th United States Congress
Signed6 August 1965, by President Lyndon B. Johnson
Codification52 U.S.C. §§ 10101–10702
Key provisionsSection 2 (nationwide ban on discriminatory voting practices); Sections 4–5 (coverage formula and preclearance)

The Voting Rights Act of 1965 is a landmark piece of federal legislation in the United States that prohibits racial discrimination in voting. It was signed into law by President Lyndon B. Johnson during the height of the civil rights movement on August 6, 1965, and Congress later amended it five times to expand its protections. The Act is widely considered one of the most effective pieces of federal civil rights legislation ever enacted.

Background

After the American Civil War, the Fifteenth Amendment (1870) prohibited denying the vote on account of race. Yet Southern states circumvented it with poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, and intimidation, effectively disenfranchising nearly all Black voters in the South for decades. The Civil Rights Acts of 1957 and 1964 authorized federal lawsuits against discrimination but enforcement was slow, case-by-case litigation. The violent suppression of the Selma to Montgomery marches in early 1965—in particular "Bloody Sunday" on 7 March 1965—galvanized public opinion and prompted Johnson to send a voting rights bill to Congress.

Provisions

Section 2 imposed a permanent, nationwide prohibition on any voting practice that discriminates on the basis of race or language-minority status. Section 4(b) established a coverage formula identifying jurisdictions with a history of discrimination—those that used a "test or device" and had low voter registration or turnout in the 1964 election. Section 5 required those covered jurisdictions to obtain "preclearance" from the U.S. Attorney General or the D.C. federal court before changing any voting law or procedure. The Act suspended literacy tests in covered areas and authorized federal examiners and observers to supervise registration and elections.

Effects

The Act's impact was immediate: Black voter registration in Mississippi rose from about 6.7 percent of eligible voters in 1964 to nearly 60 percent by 1967, and similar gains occurred across the South. The number of Black elected officials grew from a few hundred in 1965 to many thousands in the following decades. Congress reauthorized and extended the Act in 1970, 1975, 1982, and 2006, each time renewing the preclearance regime.

Shelby County v. Holder

In Shelby County v. Holder (2013), the Supreme Court struck down the Section 4(b) coverage formula as unconstitutional, holding that it was based on decades-old data and exceeded Congress's authority under current conditions. The decision left Section 5 preclearance inoperative unless Congress enacts a new formula, and it prompted several formerly covered states to implement voting changes that had previously been blocked or withdrawn. Debates over restoring or revising the Act remain a major feature of American politics.
