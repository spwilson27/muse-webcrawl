Climate change feedback

Feedback processes can amplify or dampen the response of the climate system to a forcing

A climate change feedback is a process by which an initial change in climate causes a secondary change, which in turn influences the initial change. A feedback that amplifies an initial change is a positive feedback, while a feedback that dampens it is a negative feedback. Feedbacks are central to determining climate sensitivity — how much the Earth's surface temperature ultimately rises in response to a given forcing, such as a doubling of atmospheric carbon dioxide.

Positive feedbacks

The water vapour feedback is the strongest known positive feedback: warmer air can hold more water vapour, itself a greenhouse gas, which causes further warming. The ice–albedo feedback arises because melting snow and sea ice expose darker land and ocean surfaces that absorb more sunlight. The permafrost carbon feedback involves greenhouse gases released from thawing permafrost in Arctic regions, and the possible release of methane from clathrates is a related concern.

Negative feedbacks

The Planck feedback is the dominant stabilizing response: a warmer body radiates more energy to space, following the Stefan–Boltzmann law. The lapse rate feedback and various cloud feedbacks can be positive or negative depending on region and cloud type; changes in high clouds tend to amplify warming while low-cloud changes may dampen it. Over geological timescales, the carbonate–silicate cycle acts as a slow negative feedback regulating atmospheric carbon dioxide.

Significance

Because the fast feedbacks are net positive, the warming caused directly by greenhouse gases is amplified; assessments by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change conclude that feedbacks roughly double or triple the warming that the Planck response alone would produce. Regional patterns, such as those studied for the climate of India, depend on these processes.
