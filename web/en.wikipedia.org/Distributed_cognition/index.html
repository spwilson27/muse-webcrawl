Distributed cognition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Distributed cognition is an approach to cognitive science research developed by Edwin Hutchins in the mid-1980s. It holds that cognitive processes are not confined to the brain of an individual but are distributed across people, artefacts and the environment: knowledge is embedded in the objects, tools and social structures of a setting, and cognition emerges from the interactions among them.

Origins and method

Hutchins developed the approach through ethnographic studies of navigation aboard US Navy ships, published in Cognition in the Wild (1995). He showed that the task of fixing a ship's position could not be understood by studying any single crew member; it was accomplished by a system of people, charts, alidades, bearing logs and standardised procedures that together propagated representational states across media. Distributed cognition analysis examines how information is transformed as it moves between people and artefacts.

Applications

The framework has influenced human–computer interaction, education research, organisation studies and design, where it informs the design of cockpits, control rooms, hospitals and collaborative software. It is related to, but distinct from, situated cognition, extended cognition and activity theory. Critics have debated whether "cognition" should be attributed to systems rather than individuals, and how to delimit the boundaries of a cognitive system.
