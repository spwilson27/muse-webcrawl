Devils Tower

Devils Tower (Lakota: Matȟó Thípila, "Bear Lodge") is a butte in the Bear Lodge Ranger District of the Black Hills, in Crook County, northeastern Wyoming, United States. It rises 867 feet (264 m) above the surrounding terrain and 5,112 feet (1,559 m) above sea level, its summit a nearly flat plateau above dramatic columns of igneous rock. President Theodore Roosevelt proclaimed it the first United States national monument on September 24, 1906, under the newly passed Antiquities Act.

Geology

The tower is composed of phonolite porphyry formed about 50 million years ago, during the Eocene. As the magma cooled underground — geologists debate whether as a laccolith, a stock or a volcanic neck — it contracted into tall polygonal columns, the finest example of columnar jointing of its type. Erosion of the softer surrounding sedimentary rocks of the Belle Fourche River valley exposed the resistant igneous body; fallen columns litter its base as a boulder field.

Indigenous significance and history

The tower is sacred to the Lakota, Cheyenne, Kiowa, Crow, Arapaho and other Plains tribes, whose legends tell of girls fleeing a giant bear whose claws scored the rock as it rose to save them. The English name derives from an 1875 expedition's mistranslation of the native name as "Bad God's Tower", later Devil's Tower; proposals to restore an Indigenous name have been debated. The first recorded ascent was on July 4, 1893, by ranchers William Rogers and Willard Ripley using a wooden ladder pegged into a crack; the tower is now a celebrated rock-climbing destination, though the Park Service asks climbers to refrain voluntarily during June ceremonies.

In popular culture

Devils Tower became famous worldwide as the landing site in Steven Spielberg's 1977 film Close Encounters of the Third Kind, whose characters sculpt it from mashed potatoes and shaving cream.
