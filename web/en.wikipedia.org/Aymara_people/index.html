The Aymara (also spelled Aimara or Aimará) are an indigenous people in the Andes and Altiplano regions of South America, numbering about two million. They have lived in the region around Lake Titicaca in modern Bolivia, Peru, Chile and Argentina for centuries, in a high-altitude homeland near their sacred lake. They speak the Aymara language and, increasingly, Spanish.

History

The Aymara kingdoms that flourished after the fall of Tiwanaku were absorbed into the Inca Empire in the 15th century and then into the Spanish colonial empire. Under colonial rule Aymara communities were subjected to the mita labour draft in the silver mines of Potosí. Aymara-led resistance recurred, most famously in the great rebellion of Túpac Katari, who besieged La Paz in 1781. In the 20th century Aymara people were central to Bolivian labour and peasant movements, and in 2006 Evo Morales, of Aymara descent, became president of Bolivia.

Culture

Aymara culture is organised around the ayllu, a kin-based community holding land collectively, and around principles of reciprocity such as ayni. Traditional agriculture centres on potatoes, quinoa and other high-altitude crops, and on herding llamas and alpacas. The Wiphala, a square rainbow-patterned banner, is the emblem of Andean indigenous peoples. Catholic feast days are widely celebrated in syncretism with devotion to Pachamama, the earth goddess, to whom offerings are made especially in August.
