Vertebrates are animals with backbones — members of the subphylum Vertebrata within the phylum Chordata. They are distinguished by a vertebral column (backbone) of vertebrae surrounding a spinal cord, a cranium protecting the brain, and an internal skeleton of bone or cartilage. Vertebrates range from tiny frogs a few millimetres long to the blue whale, the largest animal known ever to have existed.

Groups

Living vertebrates are conventionally divided into fishes (jawless fish such as lampreys and hagfish; cartilaginous fish such as sharks and rays; and the bony fishes, by far the largest group), amphibians, reptiles (including birds phylogenetically), and mammals. Roughly half of all described vertebrate species are ray-finned fishes; birds number about 11,000 species, mammals about 6,400, reptiles about 12,000, and amphibians about 8,000+.

Evolution

The earliest vertebrates appear in the Cambrian explosion around 525 million years ago, represented by small jawless fish-like animals such as Myllokunmingia and Haikouichthys from China. Jaws evolved by the Silurian–Devonian periods, limbs with digits in the Devonian tetrapods, and the amniotic egg in the Carboniferous, enabling full colonisation of land. Vertebrates later produced the dominant large animals of every terrestrial and marine ecosystem.

Anatomy

Common features include bilateral symmetry, a closed circulatory system with a chambered heart, paired sensory organs, gill slits (at least embryonically), and a post-anal tail at some life stage — the chordate body plan shared with invertebrate relatives such as tunicates and lancelets.
