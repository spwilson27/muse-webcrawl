The opioid epidemic, also called the opioid crisis, is the sustained surge in misuse of and addiction to opioid drugs—and the resulting overdose deaths—that began in the United States in the 1990s and spread to Canada and elsewhere. It has killed hundreds of thousands of people and is widely regarded as one of the gravest public-health crises in modern American history.

Three waves

Health authorities describe the epidemic in waves. The first wave began in the 1990s, when pharmaceutical marketing—most notoriously Purdue Pharma's promotion of OxyContin—reassured doctors that prescription opioids carried low addiction risk, and prescribing soared. The second wave, from about 2010, saw rising heroin deaths as people dependent on pills switched to a cheaper, more available drug. The third wave, from about 2013, is driven by illicitly manufactured synthetic opioids, above all fentanyl—some fifty times more potent than heroin—which is pressed into counterfeit pills and mixed into other drugs, sharply increasing overdose risk. More than 100,000 drug overdose deaths occurred in the United States annually in the early 2020s, most involving opioids.

Causes

Explanations combine aggressive pharmaceutical marketing, permissive prescribing norms and "pill mills," economic distress and "deaths of despair" in deindustrialised communities, inadequate access to addiction treatment, and the adaptability of illicit drug supply. Economists Anne Case and Angus Deaton linked the crisis to cumulative disadvantage among less-educated Americans.

Responses and litigation

Responses include prescription drug monitoring programmes, wider distribution of the overdose antidote naloxone, expanded medication-assisted treatment with methadone, buprenorphine, and naltrexone, and harm-reduction services. Thousands of lawsuits by states, cities, and tribes against manufacturers, distributors, and pharmacies produced settlements exceeding $50 billion. Purdue Pharma pleaded guilty to federal charges and was dissolved in bankruptcy proceedings; members of the Sackler family agreed to pay billions while receiving contested legal immunity, which the U.S. Supreme Court rejected in 2024, prompting a revised settlement.
