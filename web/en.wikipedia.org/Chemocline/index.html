Chemocline

A chemocline is a cline — a layer of water with a steep gradient — caused by differences in the chemistry of water bodies. It is similar to a thermocline, the layer at which the temperature changes more rapidly with depth than it does in the layers above or below it. Chemoclines are characteristic of meromictic lakes and of stratified marine basins such as the Black Sea.

Formation

Chemoclines most commonly form where waters are prevented from mixing: in deep lakes whose lower layer (the monimolimnion) never circulates to the surface, in silled fjords, and in anoxic basins. Differences in salinity, and thus density, between layers maintain the stratification. In the Black Sea, for example, the chemocline separates the oxygenated surface waters from the deep, hydrogen-sulfide-rich anoxic zone at depths of roughly 150–200 metres.

Biology

Chemoclines are biologically active boundaries. At the interface, steep gradients of oxygen, sulfide, and light create niches for specialized microbes: purple and green sulfur bacteria often form dense plates where light reaches sulfide-bearing water, and chemosynthetic communities thrive along the gradient. Some meromictic lakes, such as Lake Cadagno in Switzerland, are studied as natural laboratories for these processes.

Types of stratification

Lakes where a chemocline persists between layers of different salinity are classified by the origin of that salinity — ectogenic, crenogenic, or biogenic meromixis. Chemoclines can break down if climate or hydrology changes, sometimes releasing stored gases or nutrients in dramatic overturn events.
