Social reproduction theory


Care work and domestic labour sustain the workforce and society

Social reproduction theory is a theoretical framework within Marxist feminism that examines how the reproduction of labour power and social life—childbearing and child-rearing, domestic work, care, education, and the maintenance of communities—is integral to the functioning of capitalism, even though much of this work is unpaid or underpaid and is disproportionately performed by women. Key theorists include Lise Vogel, whose Marxism and the Oppression of Women (1983) is a foundational text, Silvia Federici, and Tithi Bhattacharya, editor of the 2017 collection Social Reproduction Theory: Remapping Class, Recentering Oppression.

Origins

The theory builds on remarks in Marx's Capital about the reproduction of labour power and on Friedrich Engels's The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State (1884), which argued that the production of life has a "two-fold character": the production of the means of existence and the production of human beings themselves. In the 1970s, the Wages for Housework campaign and debates over domestic labour raised the question of how unwaged household work relates to capitalist production. Vogel's "unitary theory" rejected dual-systems approaches that treated patriarchy and capitalism as separate, arguing instead for a single framework in which women's oppression arises from their role in reproducing labour power.

Core claims

Social reproduction theorists hold that labour power is not produced by capital but must be continually renewed through life-making activities, largely outside the wage relation; that this work is a necessary condition of accumulation; and that struggles over welfare, healthcare, education, housing, and migration are class struggles over the terms of social reproduction. Contemporary work extends the analysis to race, borders, and the commodification of care through global care chains.

Criticism

Critics within and outside Marxism have questioned whether the framework adequately accounts for forms of oppression not reducible to labour-power reproduction, and some autonomist and intersectionality scholars debate its relationship to other accounts of care and social life. The theory nonetheless remains influential in feminist economics, sociology, and the movement for International Women's Strike campaigns.
