Parasitology

Parasitology is the study of parasites, their hosts, and the relationship between them. As a biological discipline, the scope of parasitology is not determined by the organism or environment in question but by their manner of life. The field draws on methods from cell biology, biochemistry, molecular biology, immunology, genetics, evolution, and ecology.

Parasitism is one of the most successful modes of life on Earth: by some estimates a majority of animal species are parasites at some stage of their life cycle. Parasites studied by parasitologists include single-celled protozoa such as Plasmodium (the cause of malaria), helminths (worms) such as tapeworms, flukes, and roundworms, and ectoparasites such as ticks, lice, and fleas.

Branches

Medical parasitology deals with parasites that infect humans and the major diseases they cause, including malaria, schistosomiasis, leishmaniasis, and soil-transmitted helminth infections — together responsible for an enormous global burden of disease, concentrated in tropical and low-income regions. Veterinary parasitology covers parasites of domestic and farm animals, while structural, quantitative, and molecular parasitology study parasite anatomy, population dynamics, and molecular biology to find drug targets and vaccines.

History

Parasitic worms were described in ancient Egyptian medical papyri, and Avicenna described intestinal worms in the eleventh century. The modern discipline took shape in the nineteenth century when researchers such as Patrick Manson and Ronald Ross unravelled the life cycles of malaria and filarial parasites and demonstrated transmission by mosquitoes — discoveries that founded tropical medicine.
