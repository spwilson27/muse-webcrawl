Lorenz curve

The Lorenz curve is a graphical representation of the distribution of income or of wealth. It was developed by the American economist Max Lorenz in 1905 for representing inequality of the wealth distribution. The curve graphs the cumulative percentage of total income received against the cumulative percentage of recipients, starting with the poorest individual or household.

Definition

The Lorenz curve is commonly drawn in a square with both axes running from 0 to 1 (or 0 to 100%). The horizontal axis shows the cumulative share of the population, ordered from poorest to richest; the vertical axis shows the cumulative share of total income or wealth held by that population share. The diagonal line from the origin to (1, 1) is the line of perfect equality, at which every fraction of the population holds exactly the same share of income. A distribution that is unequal produces a curve that bows below the diagonal; the farther the curve lies below the line, the greater the inequality.

Gini coefficient

The Gini coefficient is derived from the Lorenz curve: it is the ratio of the area between the line of perfect equality and the Lorenz curve to the total area under the line of equality. The coefficient ranges from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (maximal inequality, where one recipient holds everything).

Uses

The Lorenz curve is widely used in economics to compare income and wealth distributions between countries and over time. It is also applied outside economics, including in ecology for species abundance distributions and in business studies for market concentration and customer value analysis.
