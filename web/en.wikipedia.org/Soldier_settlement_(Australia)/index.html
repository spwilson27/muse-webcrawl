Soldier settlement (Australia)

Soldier settlement was a system in Australia by which returned servicemen of the First and Second World Wars were provided with land to farm. State governments, with Commonwealth financial support, acquired and subdivided large estates — in some places taking land, including land belonging to Aboriginal people — and allotted blocks, often with loans for stock, equipment and fencing, to veterans who undertook to live on and develop them.

First World War schemes

Following the First World War, more than 37,000 returned soldiers settled on the land under schemes such as Victoria's at Red Cliffs and in the Mallee, New South Wales' irrigation areas around Griffith, and Western Australia's Group Settlement Scheme. Many blocks were too small, marginal or uncleared to support a family; settlers often lacked farming experience, faced falling produce prices in the 1920s and 1930s, drought, rabbits and heavy debt. A large proportion abandoned their blocks, though some settlements eventually prospered, particularly irrigated fruit-growing districts.

Second World War schemes

After 1945 the War Service Land Settlement scheme applied lessons of the earlier failure: blocks were larger and assessed for viability, training was offered, and closer supervision provided. About 10,000 veterans were settled, more successfully than their predecessors.

Assessment

Historians judge the First World War schemes largely a costly failure in human and financial terms, symbolised in popular memory by hardship and abandonment, while also noting their role in opening new agricultural districts. The schemes also displaced Aboriginal communities from land resumed for settlement.
