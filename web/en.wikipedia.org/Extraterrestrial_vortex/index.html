Extraterrestrial vortex

An extraterrestrial vortex is a vortex (a rotating mass of fluid or gas) that occurs on a planet other than Earth, such as a gas giant. Large extraterrestrial vortices are often associated with areas of low or high pressure and may persist for years or centuries. The most famous example is Jupiter's Great Red Spot, an anticyclonic storm that has been observed since at least the 19th century and possibly since 1665.

Examples

Jupiter

The Great Red Spot is a persistent high-pressure region producing an anticyclonic storm in the atmosphere of Jupiter, located 22 degrees south of the equator. It is the largest known storm in the Solar System; in the 19th century it was wide enough to contain two or three Earths, though it has been shrinking and is now roughly the width of one Earth. Winds at its edges can reach 425 km/h. Smaller vortices on Jupiter include white ovals and brown barges; in 2000, three white ovals merged to form Oval BA, nicknamed "Red Spot Junior".

Saturn

Saturn's north pole hosts a persistent hexagonal jet stream — a standing wave pattern surrounding a polar vortex — first observed by Voyager and imaged in detail by Cassini. Periodically, roughly once per Saturnian year, the planet produces planet-encircling storms known as Great White Spots.

Neptune and Uranus

Neptune's Great Dark Spot, observed by Voyager 2 in 1989, was a dark anticyclonic storm about the size of Earth in Neptune's southern hemisphere, accompanied by bright cirrus-like clouds. Unlike Jupiter's long-lived spot, Neptune's dark spots appear and dissipate over a few years; the original Great Dark Spot had disappeared by the time the Hubble Space Telescope imaged Neptune in 1994, while new dark spots have formed since. Uranus also shows occasional dark spots and bright storm complexes.

Venus and Mars

Venus's poles each host a double-eyed polar vortex, revealed by Venus Express. Mars exhibits polar vortices and large rotating dust devils and dust storms, though these are far weaker than the storms on the gas giants.

Formation and study

Extraterrestrial vortices are studied to understand atmospheric dynamics in regimes very different from Earth's: gas giants have no solid surface to dissipate storms by friction, allowing vortices to persist for centuries. Laboratory experiments with rotating fluids and numerical simulations reproduce many of their features, including the merging of smaller vortices into larger ones and the formation of zonal jets.
