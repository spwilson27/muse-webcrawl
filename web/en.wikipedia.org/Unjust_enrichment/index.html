Unjust enrichment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A courthouse, where restitution claims are adjudicated — placeholder]

In laws of various countries, unjust enrichment is a term of the law of obligations which refers to the situation in which one person is enriched (receives a benefit) at the expense of another in circumstances which the law regards as unjust. The enriched party then comes under an obligation to make restitution of the enrichment, subject to defences such as change of position. Liability for unjust enrichment is independent of both contract and tort: the cause of action is neither wrongdoing nor a promise, but the defendant's unjust retention of a benefit.

Elements

Most legal systems require the claimant to show: (1) the defendant was enriched; (2) the enrichment was at the claimant's expense; (3) the enrichment was unjust — that is, there was an "unjust factor" (such as mistake, failure of consideration, duress, or undue influence) or, in civilian systems, an absence of legal basis (sine causa); and (4) no defence applies.

History

The doctrine traces to Roman law, where actions such as the condictio indebiti allowed recovery of payments not owed. The Roman maxim neminem cum alterius detrimento fieri locupletiorem — no one should be enriched at another's expense — was preserved through the civil law tradition. In English law the subject was long fragmented under "quasi-contract" and the action for money had and received; it was formally recognised as a distinct branch of law by the House of Lords in Lipkin Gorman v Karpnale Ltd [1991].

Restitutionary remedies

The standard remedy is a personal money award measured by the value transferred. In some cases proprietary remedies, such as a constructive trust or equitable lien, may be available, particularly where the claimant's property has been misappropriated.
