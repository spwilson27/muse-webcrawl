Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution

The Fifth Amendment to the Constitution of the United States, part of the Bill of Rights (ratified 1791), creates several constitutional rights limiting governmental power in legal proceedings.

Fifth Amendment
Ratified15 December 1791
Part ofBill of Rights
Key clausesGrand jury, double jeopardy, self-incrimination, due process, takings

The Fifth Amendment's protections are among the most invoked in American law.

Text

"No person shall be held to answer for a capital, or otherwise infamous crime, unless on a presentment or indictment of a Grand Jury… nor shall any person be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy… nor shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself, nor be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law; nor shall private property be taken for public use, without just compensation."

Clauses

The amendment contains a grand jury clause; a double jeopardy clause; a self-incrimination clause (the basis of the phrase "pleading the Fifth"); a due process clause; and a takings (eminent domain) clause.

Landmark interpretations

Miranda v. Arizona (1966) required the famous Miranda warnings; Kelo v. City of New London (2005) addressed the scope of the Takings Clause.
