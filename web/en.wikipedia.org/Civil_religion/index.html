Civil religion

National rituals and memorials are classic expressions of civil religion.

Civil religion is the set of beliefs, symbols and rituals that give a political community a shared sense of sacred purpose and collective identity, functioning alongside — and distinct from — the religions of churches and other faith traditions. The term was used by Jean-Jacques Rousseau in The Social Contract (1762), and it was revived in modern scholarship by the sociologist Robert N. Bellah in his influential 1967 essay "Civil Religion in America".

Rousseau's concept

Rousseau argued that a well-ordered republic needs a minimal civic creed: belief in a beneficent deity, an afterlife in which virtue is rewarded, the sanctity of the social contract and the laws, and the rejection of religious intolerance. For Rousseau, civil religion was a deliberately simple faith meant to bind citizens to the state without empowering a church.

Bellah and American civil religion

Bellah argued that the United States possesses an elaborate civil religion with its own sacred texts (the Declaration of Independence, the Gettysburg Address), prophets and martyrs (Washington, Lincoln, Martin Luther King Jr.), sacred places (Gettysburg, Arlington National Cemetery), and rituals (presidential inaugurations, Memorial Day observances). Its God is a deity of law, order and national purpose rather than the personal God of any denomination. Bellah saw this civil religion as capable both of legitimising the nation and of standing in judgment over it, as in Lincoln's Second Inaugural Address.

Other countries and debate

Analogous phenomena have been identified in many states: republican festivals and the Panthéon in France, Soviet and post-Soviet commemorative cults in Russia, and state Shinto in imperial Japan. Critics debate whether such civic cults foster solidarity or mere nationalism, whether they trivialise traditional faiths, and whether the concept stretches "religion" too far.
