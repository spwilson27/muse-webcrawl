Ionic bond

Formation of sodium chloride: sodium donates an electron to chlorine, producing oppositely charged ions.
TypeChemical bond
MechanismElectrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions
Typical pairMetal + non-metal
ExampleSodium chloride (NaCl)

An ionic bond is a type of chemical bond formed by the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. It typically arises when one atom, usually a metal, transfers one or more electrons to another atom, usually a non-metal, so that both achieve a more stable electron configuration. The resulting cation and anion are held together in a regular three-dimensional lattice.

Formation

Ionic bonding generally occurs between elements with a large difference in electronegativity, conventionally greater than about 1.7 on the Pauling scale. In sodium chloride, a sodium atom (electronegativity 0.93) transfers its single valence electron to a chlorine atom (3.16), forming Na+ and Cl− ions, each with a complete outer shell resembling that of a noble gas.

Properties of ionic compounds

Ionic compounds typically form crystalline solids with high melting and boiling points, reflecting the strength of the lattice energy. They are often brittle, conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water (because the ions become mobile), but do not conduct in the solid state. Many are soluble in polar solvents such as water and insoluble in non-polar solvents.

Comparison with covalent bonding

In a covalent bond, atoms share electrons rather than transferring them. Real bonds lie on a continuum between purely ionic and purely covalent character; bonds with partial ionic character are described as polar covalent. The Fajans rules help predict the degree of covalent character in nominally ionic compounds.
