Nutrient cycle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Flow of nutrients between soil, producers and decomposers

A nutrient cycle (or ecological recycling) is the movement and exchange of inorganic and organic matter back into the production of matter. Energy flows directionally through ecosystems, entering as sunlight and leaving as heat, but nutrients are continually recycled between the living community and the non-living environment.

Major cycles

The principal biogeochemical cycles are those of carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and water. In the nitrogen cycle, bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen into forms plants can use, while other microbes return it to the atmosphere by denitrification. The phosphorus cycle, lacking a significant atmospheric phase, moves through rock weathering, soils, organisms, and sediments.

Reservoirs and fluxes

Each cycle can be described by pools or reservoirs — the atmosphere, oceans, soils, biomass, and sediments — connected by fluxes such as photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, runoff, and burial. Residence times range from days in surface biomass to millions of years in sedimentary rock.

Human impact

Human activity has greatly altered nutrient cycles: industrial nitrogen fixation for fertiliser now rivals natural fixation, phosphorus mining accelerates runoff and eutrophication, and fossil-fuel burning adds carbon to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide.
