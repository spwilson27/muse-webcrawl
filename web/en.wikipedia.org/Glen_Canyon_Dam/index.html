Glen Canyon Dam is a concrete arch-gravity dam on the Colorado River in northern Arizona, United States, near the town of Page. The 710-foot-high (216 m) dam was built by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation from 1956 to 1966 and forms Lake Powell, one of the largest man-made reservoirs in the United States. The dam is named for Glen Canyon, a series of deep, winding gorges now flooded by the reservoir.

History

The dam was authorized under the Colorado River Storage Project Act of 1956, principally to provide water storage and hydroelectric power for the Upper Basin states of the Colorado River Compact. Its construction was controversial: conservationists led by David Brower of the Sierra Club fought to prevent the flooding of Glen Canyon, a struggle often described as the birth of the modern American environmental movement. A compromise protected Dinosaur National Monument upstream while allowing Glen Canyon to be flooded. The reservoir began filling in 1963 and did not reach full pool until 1980.

Power generation

The Glen Canyon Powerplant contains eight generators with a combined capacity of 1,320 megawatts. Electricity is distributed across the western United States by the Western Area Power Administration. Power revenues help repay the cost of the Colorado River Storage Project and fund salinity control and endangered fish recovery programs.

Environmental issues

The dam has profoundly altered the Colorado River ecosystem in Glen Canyon and the Grand Canyon downstream, trapping sediment, changing water temperatures, and affecting native fish such as the humpback chub. Since the early 2000s, prolonged drought in the Colorado River Basin has drawn Lake Powell down dramatically, exposing submerged canyon walls, threatening hydropower generation as levels approach minimum power pool, and prompting emergency releases from upstream reservoirs.
