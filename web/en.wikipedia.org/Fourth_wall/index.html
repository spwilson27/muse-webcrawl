Fourth wall

In a proscenium theatre the "fourth wall" is the imaginary wall through which the audience watches

The fourth wall is a performance convention in which an invisible, imagined wall separates actors from the audience. In a traditional three-walled box set in a proscenium theatre, the fourth wall is the side facing the spectators, through whom the audience sees the action of the play. The actors conventionally behave as if the audience does not exist, sustaining the illusion of reality.

History

The concept is associated with the critic and philosopher Denis Diderot in the 18th century and was taken up by 19th-century realism and naturalism, in which detailed box sets made the audience feel it was watching events through a transparent wall. The convention extended to film and television, where the camera occupies the position of the fourth wall.

Breaking the fourth wall

Breaking the fourth wall occurs when a character acknowledges the audience, the medium, or their own fictionality — a device with roots in ancient theatre (the parabasis of Old Comedy), Shakespearean asides and prologues, and Brecht's alienation effect. It is now common in comedy and metafiction: examples range from the Marx Brothers and Fleabag to Deadpool and direct-address narration in television drama. In video games, the player may occupy or be addressed across the fourth wall in distinctive ways.

Acceptance of the convention is part of the audience's suspension of disbelief.
