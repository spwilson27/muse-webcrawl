Hand saw

A hand saw is a hand tool used to cut material, such as wood, metal or plastic, using a toothed blade drawn back and forth across the workpiece. Hand saws are among the oldest tools, with bronze examples dating to ancient Egypt. The modern Western hand saw typically consists of a tapered steel blade with a wooden or plastic pistol-grip handle, cutting mainly on the push stroke (in contrast to traditional Japanese saws, which cut on the pull stroke).

Types

Common varieties include the crosscut saw, with teeth filed to cut across the grain; the rip saw, with chisel-like teeth for cutting along the grain; the panel saw, a shorter crosscut saw; the back saw or tenon saw, stiffened by a metal spine for joinery; the dovetail saw; the coping saw, with a thin blade in a deep frame for cutting curves; the fret saw; the hacksaw, for metal and plastic; and the keyhole or pad saw for internal cuts. Two-man crosscut saws were historically used in forestry for felling and bucking logs.

Tooth geometry

Saw teeth are characterised by their pitch, usually given as teeth per inch (TPI) or points per inch (PPI), their rake and fleam angles, and their "set" — the slight alternating bending of teeth that widens the kerf so the blade does not bind. Coarse teeth (5–7 TPI) cut fast and rough; fine teeth (12–20 TPI) give smoother finishes in thin stock.

Use and maintenance

A cut is started with short pull strokes to establish a kerf, then long, steady strokes using most of the blade's length. Keeping a saw cutting well requires periodic sharpening with a triangular saw file and re-setting of the teeth; modern hardpoint saws have induction-hardened teeth that stay sharp longer but cannot easily be resharpened.
