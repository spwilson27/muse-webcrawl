The hypothalamus is a small region at the base of the brain, below the thalamus and above the pituitary gland, which links the nervous system to the endocrine system. Despite weighing only about 4 grams in humans, it is the principal control centre for homeostasis, regulating body temperature, hunger, thirst, sleep, circadian rhythms, and emotional and autonomic activity, and it directs the secretion of hormones by the pituitary gland.

Structure

The hypothalamus forms the floor and lower walls of the third ventricle. It is connected to the pituitary by the infundibulum (pituitary stalk) and comprises numerous paired nuclei, including the suprachiasmatic nucleus (the master circadian clock), the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei (which produce oxytocin and vasopressin for release from the posterior pituitary), the arcuate nucleus (appetite and releasing hormones), and the preoptic area (thermoregulation and sleep).

Functions

Through the hypophyseal portal system, hypothalamic releasing and inhibiting hormones—including TRH, CRH, GnRH, GHRH, somatostatin, and dopamine—control the anterior pituitary and thereby the thyroid, adrenal, gonadal (see HPG axis), and growth axes. The hypothalamus also integrates autonomic output: its posterior and lateral parts favour sympathetic arousal, while anterior regions promote parasympathetic activity. Lesions or tumours of the hypothalamus can produce disorders of temperature, appetite (obesity or wasting), sleep, and endocrine function.
