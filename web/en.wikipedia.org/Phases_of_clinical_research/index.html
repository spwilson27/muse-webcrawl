Phases of clinical research

The phases of clinical research are the stages in which scientists conduct experiments with a health intervention to find sufficient evidence for a process considered effective as a medical treatment. For drug development, the clinical phases start with testing for drug safety in a few human subjects, then expand to many study participants (potentially tens of thousands) to determine whether the drug is effective. Clinical research is conducted on drug candidates, vaccine candidates, new medical devices and new diagnostic tests.

Preclinical and Phase 0

Before human testing, candidates undergo preclinical research in vitro and in animals. Phase 0 (exploratory) trials administer subtherapeutic microdoses to a handful of volunteers to gather early pharmacokinetic data without therapeutic intent.

Phase I

Phase I trials test a new drug in a small group for the first time to evaluate safety, determine a safe dosage range and identify side effects. They are often conducted in healthy volunteers, except where the drug is too toxic (as with many cancer drugs), in which case patients are enrolled. Dose escalation proceeds cautiously under close monitoring.

Phase II

Phase II trials explore efficacy in patients with the target condition while continuing safety assessment, and refine dosing. Many candidates fail at this stage for lack of efficacy. Sub-phases IIa (proof of concept) and IIb (dose-finding) are sometimes distinguished.

Phase III

Phase III trials are large, often randomised, double-blind, multicentre studies comparing the intervention with standard treatment or placebo. Successful Phase III results form the core of an application for regulatory approval (for example, an FDA New Drug Application). Only a minority of drugs entering clinical testing reach this stage and approval.

Phase IV

Phase IV studies are conducted after approval, monitoring long-term safety and effectiveness in the general population, detecting rare adverse effects, and exploring new indications — part of pharmacovigilance.
