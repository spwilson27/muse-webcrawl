Ashcan School

Ashcan School

Years active: c. 1895–1918

Location: New York City, United States

Associated artists: Robert Henri, George Bellows, John Sloan, George Luks, William Glackens, Everett Shinn

The Ashcan School was an artistic movement in the United States during the late 19th and early 20th centuries best known for its works portraying scenes of daily life in New York City, often in the city's poorer neighbourhoods. The movement grew out of the circle of newspaper illustrators in Philadelphia who followed the painter Robert Henri.

The Eight

In 1908 five of the painters — Henri, John Sloan, George Luks, William Glackens, and Everett Shinn — exhibited at the Macbeth Galleries in New York with three artists outside the group (Arthur B. Davies, Ernest Lawson, and Maurice Prendergast) as "The Eight", in protest against the conservative exhibition policies of the National Academy of Design. A younger generation, including George Bellows, extended the school's vigorous realism into the 1910s.

Style and subjects

Ashcan artists painted boxers, wrestlers, street children, tenement dwellers, theatre audiences, and crowded sidewalks, using dark palettes and rapid brushwork. Critics who found the subjects vulgar dubbed them the "revolutionary black gang" and later the "Ash Can School", a name derived from a drawing by Bellows of men emptying ash cans, published in 1915. The artists themselves preferred to be seen as chroniclers of modern urban life in the spirit of Walt Whitman.

Legacy

The movement fed into American social realism and helped shift serious American painting toward contemporary city subjects. Many Ashcan painters also participated in the landmark Armory Show of 1913.
