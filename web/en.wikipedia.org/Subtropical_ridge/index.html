Subtropical ridge

The subtropical ridge, also known as the subtropical high or horse latitudes, is a belt of semi-permanent high-pressure systems located roughly between latitudes 25° and 35° in both hemispheres. It forms where air descending from the Hadley cells of the general atmospheric circulation subsides, warming and drying as it sinks.

Characteristics

Areas beneath the subtropical ridge experience weak winds, clear skies, and arid conditions; most of the world's great hot deserts — the Sahara, Arabian, Kalahari, Australian, and Sonoran deserts — lie under its influence. Individual cells of the ridge over the oceans include the Azores (Bermuda) High in the North Atlantic and the Pacific High.

Seasonal movement and effects

The ridge migrates poleward in summer and equatorward in winter, driving seasonal climates: its poleward flank gives Mediterranean climates dry summers, and its movement helps steer tropical cyclones along its periphery. The old sailing term "horse latitudes" for the calm belt may derive from ships becalmed there — legend holds that horses aboard were thrown overboard when water ran short. The ridge separates the trade winds to its equatorward side from the westerlies on its poleward side.
