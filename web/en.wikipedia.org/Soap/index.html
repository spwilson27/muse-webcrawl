Soap

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Bars of soap

Soap is a salt of a fatty acid (sometimes other carboxylic acids) used for cleaning and lubricating products as well as other applications. In a domestic setting, soaps are surfactants usually used for washing, bathing, and other types of housekeeping. In industrial settings, soaps are used as thickeners, components of some lubricants, emulsifiers, and catalysts.

Chemistry

Soaps are typically produced by saponification, the reaction of fats and oils with a strong alkali such as sodium hydroxide or potassium hydroxide. The resulting mixture contains the sodium or potassium salts of fatty acids together with glycerol. Soap molecules have a hydrophilic (water-attracting) head and a hydrophobic (water-repelling) tail, allowing them to emulsify oils and dirt so they can be rinsed away with water.

History

Evidence of soap-like materials dates to around 2800 BC in ancient Babylon. Soap became widely manufactured during the Islamic Golden Age and in medieval Europe; the English word derives from Old English sape. Large-scale commercial production developed in the 19th century with the Leblanc and Solvay processes for making soda ash.

Types

Toilet soaps, laundry soaps, liquid soaps, and industrial soaps differ in alkali, fatty feedstock, and additives such as fragrances, colourants, and moisturisers. Metallic soaps (such as calcium stearates) serve as lubricants and paint driers rather than detergents.
