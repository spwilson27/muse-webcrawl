Synod

A synod (from Greek synodos, “assembly” or “meeting”) is a council of a Christian church convened to decide an issue of doctrine, administration, or application. The word is broadly interchangeable with “council”, though synod often denotes a smaller or more regular body, while the most solemn gatherings are styled ecumenical councils.

In different churches

In the Eastern Orthodox Church, each autocephalous church is governed by a Holy Synod of bishops headed by its patriarch or archbishop. In the Catholic Church, the Synod of Bishops, instituted by Paul VI in 1965, advises the Pope, and dioceses and religious orders hold their own synods; Pope Francis's “Synod on Synodality” (2021–2024) made synodality itself a major theme of Catholic life. Many Protestant churches — Anglican, Lutheran, Reformed, and Methodist among them — are governed wholly or partly by synods that include lay representatives.

Historical examples

Ancient synods shaped Christian doctrine and discipline: the Synod of Elvira (c. 306) issued early canons, the Synod of Whitby (664) settled the English church's Easter dating, and the Synod of Dort (1618–19) condemned Arminianism and produced the Canons of Dort. The Robber Synod of Ephesus (449) is remembered as a synod whose decisions were overturned.
