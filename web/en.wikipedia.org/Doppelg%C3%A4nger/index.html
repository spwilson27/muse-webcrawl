Doppelgänger

A doppelgänger (German: [ˈdɔpl̩ˌɡɛŋɐ], literally "double-goer") is a biologically unrelated look-alike, or a double, of a living person. In fiction and mythology, a doppelgänger is often portrayed as a ghostly or paranormal phenomenon and usually seen as a harbinger of bad luck. In some traditions, meeting one's own doppelgänger is an omen of death, and a doppelgänger seen by friends or relatives of a person may bring illness or danger.

Etymology

The word derives from the German Doppelgänger, a compound noun formed from doppel (double) and Gänger (goer). It was popularized by the German novelist Jean Paul, who used it — as Doppeltgänger — in his 1796 novel Siebenkäs.

In mythology and folklore

The concept of an alter ego or double spirit has ancient roots. In ancient Egyptian mythology, a ka was a tangible "spirit double" sharing the memory and feelings of its living counterpart. In Norse mythology, a vardøger is a ghostly double that precedes a person, performing their actions before they arrive. In Finnish mythology, an etiäinen is a double image of a person that appears ahead of them.

In literature

The doppelgänger is a recurring motif in literature, often representing a character's divided self. Famous examples include Edgar Allan Poe's "William Wilson" (1839), Fyodor Dostoevsky's The Double (1846), Robert Louis Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde (1886), and Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray (1890). Goethe, in his autobiography Dichtung und Wahrheit, recounted riding toward Drusenheim and seeing himself, in strange garments, riding toward his visible self.

In science

The psychiatric phenomenon of seeing one's own double is called heautoscopy, and an irrational belief that a familiar person has been replaced by an identical impostor is known as the Capgras delusion. In a non-paranormal sense, "doppelgänger" is also used for ordinary unrelated look-alikes, a use expanded in the social-media era.
