Sexual dimorphism

Sexual dimorphism is the condition in which males and females of the same species exhibit differences in appearance or form beyond their sexual organs — in size, colour, ornamentation, behaviour or other secondary sex characteristics. It is widespread in the animal kingdom and is usually understood as a product of sexual selection.

Forms

The most conspicuous examples occur in birds: the male peacock's extravagant train contrasts with the cryptic plumage of the peahen, and male birds of paradise and many ducks are brightly coloured while females are drab for camouflage while nesting. In mammals, males are often larger — male elephant seals may weigh several times as much as females — and may bear weapons such as antlers (in deer, borne typically only by males) or the enlarged canines of baboons. Dimorphism can be reversed: in many raptors, spiders, and the deep-sea anglerfish, females are the larger sex, sometimes dramatically so. Insects show elaborate cases, including beetle horns and the entirely dissimilar-looking sexes of some butterflies.

Causes

Charles Darwin devoted much of The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex (1871) to the subject, attributing dimorphism to competition among males for mates (intrasexual selection, favouring size and weapons) and to female choice (intersexual selection, favouring ornaments). Modern theory adds fecundity selection on females, ecological divergence between the sexes, and genetic mechanisms such as sex-linked inheritance and sex-specific gene expression; dimorphic traits can also be by-products of hormonal differences. Conspicuous ornaments are explained as honest signals of quality, sometimes via the handicap principle.

In humans

Humans show moderate dimorphism: on average men are taller and have greater upper-body muscle mass and facial hair, while women have wider pelvises and different fat distribution. The degree and evolutionary interpretation of behavioural sex differences remain debated in psychology and anthropology.
