Highest weight

In representation theory, a branch of mathematics, a highest weight is a special weight of a representation of a Lie algebra or Lie group. Roughly speaking, representations of semisimple Lie algebras are classified by their highest weights, and many constructions in the subject proceed by first understanding the highest-weight theory and then deducing properties of representations from it.

Definition

Let g be a finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebra over the complex numbers, with Cartan subalgebra h and chosen system of positive roots. A weight λ of a g-module V is called a highest weight if there is a non-zero vector v in the λ weight space of V that is annihilated by all the raising operators — that is, by the root vectors corresponding to positive roots — and such that V is generated by v as a g-module. Such a vector is called a highest weight vector.

A weight is dominant integral if it takes non-negative integer values on all coroots and pairs integrally with the root lattice. The fundamental theorem of highest weight theory states that for each dominant integral weight λ there is a unique (up to isomorphism) finite-dimensional irreducible representation of g with highest weight λ, and every finite-dimensional irreducible representation arises this way.

Verma modules

For any weight λ one can construct the Verma module, the universal highest-weight module of weight λ, by inducing from a one-dimensional representation of a Borel subalgebra. Every highest-weight module with highest weight λ is a quotient of the Verma module, and each Verma module has a unique irreducible quotient, usually denoted L(λ). The question of when L(λ) is finite-dimensional is answered by the dominance and integrality conditions above.

For Lie groups

The same classification governs representations of compact connected Lie groups: by the Weyl unitary trick, finite-dimensional representations of a compact group correspond to those of its complexified Lie algebra, and the theorem of the highest weight, proved in this setting by Hermann Weyl, classifies the irreducible representations by dominant integral weights — equivalently, by points of a lattice in a Weyl chamber.

Applications

Highest-weight theory underlies the character formulas of Weyl, the geometry of flag varieties through the Borel–Weil theorem, and the parametrisation of representations appearing in mathematical physics, including the labelling of particle multiplets by weights in the quark model.
