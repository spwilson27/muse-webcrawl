Allotment (gardening)

An allotment garden (British English), or community garden in North America, is a plot of land made available for individual, non-commercial gardening. Allotments are typically formed by dividing a larger piece of land into a few dozen parcels rented to individuals or families, who cultivate fruit, vegetables, and flowers for their own use.

History in Britain

Allotments in the United Kingdom have their roots in the enclosure of common land and nineteenth-century legislation that obliged local authorities to provide land for the labouring poor. The Allotments Acts of the early twentieth century, culminating in the Allotments Act 1925, gave statutory protection to allotment land. Demand surged during both world wars — notably under the "Dig for Victory" campaign of the Second World War — when some 1.4 million plots helped feed the nation. A standard full plot is traditionally 10 rods (about 250 square metres).

Around the world

Similar institutions exist across Europe: the German Kleingarten or Schrebergarten, often with small summer houses; Dutch volkstuinen; and Russian dacha plots. In North America the equivalent community-garden movement, frequently organized by municipalities and nonprofits, likewise saw wartime "victory gardens" and a revival from the 1970s onward.

Modern role

Waiting lists in British cities grew sharply in the twenty-first century as interest in local food, exercise, and green space increased. Allotments are valued for biodiversity, community cohesion, and mental and physical health, although statutory sites remain under pressure from development in some areas.
