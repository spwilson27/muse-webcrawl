Prophylaxis

Prophylaxis (from Greek prophýlaxis, "guard beforehand") is a measure taken for the purpose of preventing disease. In medicine, prophylactic treatment is designed to prevent a disease from occurring, as opposed to treating a disease after it has developed. Examples include vaccines, which build immunity against specific infections; pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) against HIV; antimalarial drugs taken by travellers to endemic regions; and antibiotic prophylaxis given before surgery to reduce the risk of postoperative infection.

Types

Primary prophylaxis prevents the initial occurrence of disease — for instance, vaccination and lifestyle measures against cardiovascular risk. Secondary prophylaxis prevents recurrence or progression after a first event, such as antiplatelet therapy after a heart attack. Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is administered after a known exposure, as with rabies vaccination or antiretrovirals after a needlestick injury. In dentistry, "prophylaxis" also denotes the professional cleaning procedure of scaling and polishing the teeth. The word has entered common usage in other fields as well; in chess, "prophylaxis" describes moves that anticipate and frustrate an opponent's plans.

Considerations

Because prophylactic interventions are given to people who are healthy or at risk rather than ill, their evaluation weighs benefits against harms and costs especially carefully. The number needed to treat, the severity of the disease prevented, and the risk profile of the individual patient all factor into guidelines. Well-known successes of population-level prophylaxis include the eradication of smallpox through vaccination and dramatic reductions in vaccine-preventable childhood diseases.
