Campi Flegrei

The Campi Flegrei (from Greek phlegraios, "burning"; English: Phlegraean Fields) is a large volcanic caldera west of Naples, Italy. About 13 km across, it contains numerous craters and volcanic edifices, including the Solfatara crater with its fumaroles. Much of the caldera lies under the Gulf of Pozzuoli. It is one of the world's most closely monitored volcanoes because roughly half a million people live within the caldera.

Geology

The caldera formed in two great collapses: the Campanian Ignimbrite eruption about 39,000 years ago — one of the largest known in Europe, ejecting over 200 km³ of material — and the Neapolitan Yellow Tuff eruption about 15,000 years ago. Smaller eruptions continued into historical times; the most recent, in 1538, built the Monte Nuovo cone in about a week, accompanied by earthquakes and coastal uplift.

Bradyseism

The area is famous for bradyseism — slow uplift and subsidence of the ground. The Roman market building (the "Temple of Serapis") at Pozzuoli preserves borings by marine mollusks on its columns, recording past submergence and emergence. Crises in 1969–72 and 1982–84 raised Pozzuoli by nearly 2 m in total and led to the evacuation of about 40,000 residents in 1983–84; renewed uplift has been ongoing since about 2005.

Mythology and history

In Greek and Roman myth the Phlegraean Fields were the site of the battle between gods and giants, and nearby Lake Avernus was an entrance to the underworld in Virgil's Aeneid. The area hosted the Greek colony of Cumae, Roman Baiae, and the port of Puteoli (Pozzuoli).

Monitoring

The Vesuvius Observatory of Italy's INGV monitors the caldera with seismic, GPS, and geochemical networks, and civil-protection plans contemplate evacuation of the "red zone" in case of escalating unrest.
