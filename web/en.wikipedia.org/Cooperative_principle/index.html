Cooperative principle

In social science generally and linguistics specifically, the cooperative principle describes how people achieve effective conversational communication in common social situations — that is, how listeners and speakers act cooperatively and mutually accept one another in order to be understood. The philosopher of language Paul Grice introduced the concept in his 1967 William James lectures and his 1975 article "Logic and Conversation".

Grice's formulation

Grice put the principle as follows: "Make your conversational contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged." He supported it with four conversational maxims:

  • Quantity: give as much information as is required, and no more.
  • Quality: do not say what you believe to be false, or that for which you lack adequate evidence.
  • Relation: be relevant.
  • Manner: be perspicuous — avoid obscurity and ambiguity, be brief and orderly.

Implicature

Speakers who appear to violate ("flout") a maxim usually do so deliberately, trusting the hearer to assume the principle still holds and to infer the extra intended meaning — a conversational implicature. Irony, understatement and pointed irrelevance are explained this way: the literal failure of a maxim prompts the listener to compute what the speaker must really mean.

Criticism and development

Later theorists, including proponents of relevance theory, have argued that a single principle of relevance can replace Grice's set, and cross-cultural studies note that the maxims are not applied identically in every society. The framework nevertheless remains foundational in pragmatics.
