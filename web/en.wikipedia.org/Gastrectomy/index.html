A gastrectomy is a partial or total surgical removal of the stomach. It is performed most commonly for stomach cancer, but also for severe peptic ulcer disease, gastrointestinal stromal tumours, perforation, and — in the form of sleeve gastrectomy — as a bariatric (weight-loss) operation.

Types

In a total gastrectomy the entire stomach is removed and the oesophagus is joined directly to the small intestine, usually by a Roux-en-Y reconstruction. A subtotal (partial) gastrectomy removes part of the stomach — the distal portion for tumours of the lower stomach — with reconstruction by Billroth I (gastroduodenostomy), Billroth II (gastrojejunostomy) or Roux-en-Y anastomosis. Sleeve gastrectomy removes roughly 75–80% of the stomach along its greater curvature, leaving a narrow tube; it is now one of the most common bariatric procedures worldwide.

History

The first successful gastrectomy was performed by Theodor Billroth in Vienna in 1881 for pyloric cancer. Early mortality was high, but improvements in anaesthesia, antibiotics, transfusion and nutritional support have made the operation considerably safer.

Consequences and complications

Patients must eat smaller, more frequent meals because the stomach's reservoir function is lost. Common long-term issues include dumping syndrome, weight loss, diarrhoea, and malabsorption of iron, calcium and vitamin B12 — loss of intrinsic factor after total gastrectomy makes lifelong B12 supplementation (usually by injection) necessary to prevent pernicious anaemia. Surgical complications include anastomotic leak, infection and bleeding.
