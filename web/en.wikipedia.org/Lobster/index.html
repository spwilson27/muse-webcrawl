Lobsters are a family (Nephropidae, sometimes also Homeridae) of large marine crustaceans. They have long bodies with muscular tails and live in crevices or burrows on the sea floor. Three of their five pairs of legs have claws, including the first pair, which are usually much larger than the others.

Biology

Lobsters grow throughout their lives by moulting their exoskeletons and can live for many decades; a specimen estimated at 140 years old was reported in 2009, though age estimation is difficult. They are generally solitary, nocturnal scavengers feeding on fish, molluscs and other invertebrates. The two most commercially important species are the American lobster (Homarus americanus) of the northwest Atlantic and the European lobster (Homarus gammarus).

As food

Once considered poverty food fed to prisoners and servants, lobster became a delicacy in the mid-19th century. Lobsters turn red when cooked because heat breaks down the protein binding the pigment astaxanthin. Fisheries in Maine, Atlantic Canada and elsewhere are among the most valuable single-species fisheries in the world, regulated by minimum sizes and protection of egg-bearing females.
