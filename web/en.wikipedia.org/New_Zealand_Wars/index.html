The New Zealand Wars were a series of armed conflicts fought in New Zealand from 1845 to 1872 between the New Zealand colonial government and allied British imperial troops on one side, and Māori tribes (iwi) on the other—though many Māori, the kūpapa, fought alongside the Crown. The wars were sparked by disputes over land sales and sovereignty following the Treaty of Waitangi (1840) and the rapid growth of settler immigration. Their outcome included the confiscation of some 6.5 million acres (about 2.6 million hectares) of Māori land.

Major campaigns

The Northern War (1845–46) in the Bay of Islands involved the chiefs Hōne Heke and Kawiti and saw the cutting down of the flagstaff at Kororāreka. Fighting in the Hutt Valley and Wellington region followed in 1846, and at Whanganui in 1847. The largest conflicts came in the 1860s: the First Taranaki War (1860–61), the Invasion of the Waikato (1863–64) by General Duncan Cameron against the Māori King movement (Kīngitanga), and the Tauranga campaign including the battles of Gate Pā and Te Ranga. The later 1860s and early 1870s saw the campaigns against Tītokowaru in Taranaki and the guerrilla resistance of Te Kooti across the central and eastern North Island.

Māori military achievement

Māori forces, though heavily outnumbered—at the wars' peak some 18,000 imperial and colonial troops faced perhaps 4,000–5,000 Māori fighters at any time—won notable victories using sophisticated trench-and-bunker fortifications, the "modern pā", which anticipated aspects of First World War trench warfare. At Ōhaeawai (1845) and Gate Pā (1864), Māori defenders inflicted costly defeats on British assaults.

Consequences

The wars enabled the colony to seize the initiative in land acquisition. The New Zealand Settlements Act 1863 provided for confiscation (raupatu) of land from tribes deemed in rebellion, a measure that took fertile districts in Waikato, Taranaki, and the Bay of Plenty and remains a central grievance addressed in modern Treaty settlements. The conflicts were largely forgotten in settler public memory for much of the twentieth century, but scholarship—notably James Belich's The New Zealand Wars (1986)—and Waitangi Tribunal inquiries restored their prominence.
