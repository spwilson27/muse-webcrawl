James–Stein estimator

The James–Stein estimator is a biased estimator of the mean of a multivariate normal distribution. Introduced by Willard James and Charles Stein in 1961, building on Stein's 1956 result, it famously dominates the ordinary least-squares (maximum likelihood) estimator whenever the dimension is three or more: that is, the usual sample mean is inadmissible under squared-error loss.

The phenomenon

Suppose X is a p-dimensional observation from a normal distribution with unknown mean θ and identity covariance. The James–Stein estimator shrinks X toward an arbitrary fixed point (without loss of generality, the origin) by a data-dependent factor involving ‖X‖². Stein's lemma shows its risk is strictly less than p, the risk of X itself, for all θ when p ≥ 3 — even though the coordinates may be entirely unrelated, a counterintuitive result often called Stein's paradox or Stein's example.

Positive-part version

The plain estimator can overshoot and shrink past the origin; the "positive-part" James–Stein estimator truncates the shrinkage factor at zero and has still smaller risk.

Interpretation and influence

The result launched the field of shrinkage estimation and connects naturally to empirical Bayes methods, in which the shrinkage factor is estimated from the data as if the means were drawn from a common prior. James–Stein ideas are widely used in statistics, baseball prediction being the classic illustration, and in modern high-dimensional data analysis and machine learning through regularisation.
