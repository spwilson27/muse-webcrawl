Graves' disease, also known as toxic diffuse goiter, is an autoimmune disease that affects the thyroid gland. It is the most common cause of hyperthyroidism, accounting for the majority of cases. The disease is named after Irish surgeon Robert James Graves, who described it in 1835; in German-speaking Europe it is often called Basedow's disease.

Signs and symptoms

In Graves' disease, antibodies bind to and activate the receptor for thyroid-stimulating hormone, driving the thyroid to produce excess thyroid hormone. Symptoms include a diffusely enlarged thyroid (goiter), rapid or irregular heartbeat, weight loss despite normal appetite, tremor, heat intolerance, and anxiety. About a quarter to a third of patients develop Graves' ophthalmopathy, in which inflammation of the tissues behind the eye causes the eyes to bulge (exophthalmos).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is typically made from blood tests showing suppressed TSH with elevated thyroid hormone levels, together with detectable TSH-receptor antibodies. Treatment options include antithyroid medications such as methimazole, radioactive iodine ablation, and surgical removal of the thyroid, followed where needed by thyroid hormone replacement.

Epidemiology

Graves' disease affects roughly 0.5% of males and 3% of females at some point and occurs about seven to eight times more often in women than in men. Onset is usually between the ages of 40 and 60, and genetic susceptibility combined with environmental triggers such as stress, infection, and smoking contributes to risk.
