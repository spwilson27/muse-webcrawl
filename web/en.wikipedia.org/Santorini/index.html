Santorini (Greek: Σαντορίνη), officially Thira (Θήρα), is an island and municipality in the southern Aegean Sea, the southernmost member of the Cyclades group of Greece. The archipelago consists of the crescent-shaped main island and several smaller islets around a flooded volcanic caldera.

The Minoan eruption

Around 1600 BCE the island — then roughly circular and known to later Greeks as Kalliste and Thera — was devastated by one of the largest volcanic eruptions in recorded history, the Minoan (or Thera) eruption. The explosion collapsed the centre of the island into the sea, creating the present caldera, and buried the flourishing town now excavated as Akrotiri, a remarkably preserved Bronze Age settlement of multi-storey frescoed houses. The eruption's effects on Minoan Crete, and possible echoes in Plato's Atlantis story, have been debated for over a century.

History

Dorians, Romans, Byzantines, Venetians (who gave it the name Santorini, from Santa Irini) and Ottomans ruled the island in succession before it joined Greece in the 19th century. Its cliffs of pumice and ash layers are a textbook of volcanic stratigraphy, and smaller eruptions have continued into modern times, forming the central islets of Palea and Nea Kameni.

Tourism

Famous for whitewashed villages — Fira, Oia, Imerovigli — perched on the caldera rim, blue-domed churches and sunsets over the Aegean, Santorini is one of Europe's most visited island destinations. Its arid volcanic soils also produce distinctive Assyrtiko wines, and local specialities include fava and white aubergines.
