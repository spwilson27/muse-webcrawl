Revolving door (politics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In politics, the "revolving door" is a movement of personnel between roles as legislators and regulators, on one hand, and employees and lobbyists of the industries affected by the legislation and regulation, on the other. It is analogous to the movement back and forth between the public and private sectors in many industries and governments around the world.

Critics argue the practice creates conflicts of interest and regulatory capture: officials may favour industries in hope of future employment, while former industry employees may write rules benefiting their old firms. Defenders note that the flow also carries valuable expertise into government and makes regulatory careers more attractive.

Forms

The revolving door operates in both directions. Regulators and legislators leave office to become lobbyists, consultants, or executives ("spinning out"), while industry personnel are appointed to agencies that oversee their former employers. In Japan, the related practice of senior bureaucrats retiring into private-sector posts is known as amakudari ("descent from heaven"). In France, similar movement between the civil service and business is called pantouflage.

Regulation

Many jurisdictions impose "cooling-off" periods before former officials may lobby their old agencies. In the United States, statutes such as 18 U.S.C. § 207 restrict post-employment contacts, and executive orders have periodically tightened or loosened the rules. The European Union and several member states maintain registers of lobbyists and post-employment notifications.
