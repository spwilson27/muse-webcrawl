Ann Lee (29 February 1736 – 8 September 1784), commonly known as Mother Ann Lee, was the founding leader of the United Society of Believers in Christ's Second Appearing, better known as the Shakers. Born in Manchester, England, she emigrated to America in 1774 and established the first Shaker community at Watervliet, New York.

Early life

Ann Lee was born in Toad Lane, Manchester, the second of eight children of John Lee, a blacksmith. She was baptised at Manchester Cathedral in 1742. She received little formal education and worked from childhood in a cotton factory and later as a cook. In 1762 she married Abraham Stanley, a blacksmith, at her father's urging. The couple had four children, all of whom died in infancy — experiences that deeply affected her religious outlook and led her to view marriage and sexual relations as sources of sin.

Conversion and ministry in England

In 1758 Lee joined a religious society led by Jane and James Wardley, former Quakers whose worship involved trembling, dancing and confession of sins; its members became known as the "Shaking Quakers". Lee became a prominent member. In 1770 she was imprisoned in Manchester for disturbing the peace after proclaiming her revelations publicly. According to Shaker tradition, while in prison she received a vision in which she came to believe she was the female embodiment of Christ's second appearing, and that the root of evil was sexual intercourse, even within marriage. Thereafter she taught celibacy as essential to salvation.

Emigration and the American mission

Acting on a revelation, Lee and eight followers sailed from Liverpool aboard the Mariah in May 1774, arriving in New York that August. The group settled in 1776 at Niskayuna (Watervliet), near Albany, where they cleared land and built a community. In 1780, during the American Revolution, Lee's pacifist followers attracted converts after a local religious revival; she then undertook a missionary journey through New England in 1781–1783, winning converts in Massachusetts and Connecticut but also facing mob violence.

Death and legacy

Ann Lee died on 8 September 1784 at Watervliet, weakened, followers believed, by the persecutions she had endured. She was succeeded by James Whittaker and later Joseph Meacham and Lucy Wright, under whom the Shaker movement was organised into communal villages across New England and westward. Shaker theology honours Mother Ann as the female counterpart of Christ, reflecting a belief in a dual, male-and-female nature of God.
