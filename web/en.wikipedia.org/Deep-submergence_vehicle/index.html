Deep-submergence vehicle


DSV Alvin, operated by Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution

A deep-submergence vehicle (DSV) is a deep-diving crewed submersible that is self-propelled, as distinct from a bathyscaphe, which is lowered by cable. Deep-submergence vehicles are used for oceanography, deep-sea exploration, salvage, and military purposes, and are typically launched and recovered from a support ship.

Notable vehicles

The bathyscaphe Trieste reached the bottom of the Mariana Trench in 1960. Alvin, commissioned in 1964 by the United States Navy and operated by Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, can carry three people to 6,500 metres and was used in the exploration of hydrothermal vents and the wreck of the Titanic. Other notable DSVs include the French Nautile, the Russian Mir submersibles, the Japanese Shinkai 6500, the Chinese Jiaolong and Fendouzhe (which reached the Challenger Deep in 2020), and James Cameron's Deepsea Challenger.

Design

Crewed DSVs house their occupants in a thick spherical pressure hull, usually of steel or titanium, surrounded by a buoyant structure and equipped with thrusters, manipulator arms, sample baskets, lights, and cameras. Uncrewed counterparts include remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) and autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs).
