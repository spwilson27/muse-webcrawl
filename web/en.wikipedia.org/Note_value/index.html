Note value

In music notation, a note value indicates the relative duration of a note, using the texture or shape of the notehead, the presence or absence of a stem, and the presence or absence of flags or beams. It expresses duration relative to the beat and the time signature rather than an absolute length in seconds; the actual duration depends on the tempo.

The standard values form a binary hierarchy, each lasting half as long as the one before:

Longer values also exist: the breve (double whole note) and the longa, survivals of mensural notation still occasionally used. Successive beams join groups of short notes within a beat.

Dots and ties

A dot placed after a notehead increases its value by half of itself; a second dot adds a further quarter. A tie joins two notes of the same pitch into a single sustained sound whose duration is the sum of both — a device needed when a duration crosses a bar line or cannot be written with a single value.

Tuplets

Tuplets divide a beat into an irregular number of equal parts: the triplet, three notes in the time of two, is the most common, but quintuplets, sextuplets and other groupings are marked with a numeral, sometimes with a bracket or slur.

Rests

Every note value has a corresponding rest symbol indicating silence of the same relative duration, from the whole rest hanging from a staff line down to heavily flagged short rests.

History

The system descends from the mensural notation of the 13th–16th centuries, in which note shapes (maxima, longa, breve, semibreve, minim and smaller values) had context-dependent durations governed by rules of perfection and imperfection. The modern, strictly proportional system stabilised in the 17th century with the adoption of bar lines and time signatures.
