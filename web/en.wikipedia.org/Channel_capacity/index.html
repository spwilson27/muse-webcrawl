Channel capacity


Channel capacity: the tight upper bound on reliable information transfer through a channel.

Channel capacity, in electrical engineering, computer science and information theory, is the tight upper bound on the rate at which information can be reliably transmitted over a communication channel. Following the terms of the noisy-channel coding theorem, the channel capacity is the highest rate (in bits per unit time) at which information can be sent with an arbitrarily low probability of error.

Definition

Formally, for a channel with input X and output Y, the capacity is the maximum of the mutual information I(X;Y) over all possible input distributions: C = max I(X;Y). The concept was introduced by Claude Shannon in his landmark 1948 paper A Mathematical Theory of Communication.

Shannon–Hartley theorem

For a band-limited channel corrupted by additive white Gaussian noise, the capacity is given by the Shannon–Hartley theorem: C = B log2(1 + S/N), where B is the bandwidth in hertz and S/N is the signal-to-noise ratio. This result, anticipated in part by Harry Nyquist and Ralph Hartley, sets a fundamental limit approached by modern modulation and coding schemes such as turbo codes and LDPC codes.

Examples

The binary symmetric channel, which flips each bit with probability p, has capacity 1 − H(p) bits per use, where H is the binary entropy function. The binary erasure channel has capacity 1 − p where p is the erasure probability.
