The Treaty of Utrecht, or Peace of Utrecht, is a series of peace treaties signed between 1713 and 1715 in the Dutch city of Utrecht that helped end the War of the Spanish Succession (1701–1714). The settlement reshaped the map of Europe and formalised the principle of the balance of power in international diplomacy.

Terms

Philip V, grandson of Louis XIV of France, was recognised as King of Spain, but he and his heirs renounced any claim to the French throne, so that the crowns of France and Spain would never be united. The Spanish Netherlands, Milan, Naples and Sardinia were transferred to the Austrian Habsburgs; Sicily went to the Duke of Savoy; and Prussia's royal title was recognised along with the acquisition of Upper Guelders.

Great Britain emerged as the great gainer: France ceded Newfoundland, Acadia (Nova Scotia) and the Hudson Bay territories in North America, and recognised British sovereignty there. Spain ceded Gibraltar and Menorca to Britain, and granted Britain the asiento — the contract to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America — plus an annual permission ship for trade. The Dutch obtained a "barrier" of fortresses in the Austrian Netherlands against France.

Significance

The Utrecht settlement is regarded as a landmark in the history of diplomacy: the first major peace negotiated by a congress of states, and one that explicitly invoked the "just equilibrium of power" to justify its territorial arrangements. It marked the decline of French hegemonic ambitions under Louis XIV and the rise of Britain as the leading naval and colonial power. Spain, stripped of its European empire, was reduced to a second-rank power.
