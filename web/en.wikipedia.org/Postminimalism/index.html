Postminimalism

Postminimalism is an art movement that emerged in the late 1960s and 1970s, principally in the United States, as a reaction to and extension of minimalism. The term was coined by the critic Robert Pincus-Witten in 1971. Where minimal art prized rigid geometry, industrial fabrication, and impersonality, postminimalist artists retained an interest in simple forms and materials while reintroducing the body, process, chance, and imperfection into the work.

Characteristics

Postminimalist work often uses soft, pliable, or everyday materials — felt, rubber, latex, rope, cheesecloth, lead, and scattered or poured substances — whose final configuration depends on gravity, hanging, stacking, or chance rather than on a preconceived geometric order. Process art, anti-form, land art, and arte povera are closely related tendencies. The emphasis falls on how a work is made and how materials behave, and in the work of Eva Hesse and others, on metaphor, eroticism, and the fragility of the object over time.

Artists

Artists associated with postminimalism include Eva Hesse, Robert Morris, Richard Serra (whose early "splash" and prop pieces exemplify process work), Keith Sonnier, Lynda Benglis, Bruce Nauman, Barry Le Va, and Richard Tuttle. The landmark 1969 exhibitions "Anti-Illusion: Procedures/Materials" at the Whitney Museum and Harald Szeemann's "When Attitudes Become Form" in Bern helped define the moment. In music, "postminimalism" is also used for composers who loosened the strict procedures of minimal music, such as John Adams in his later work.
