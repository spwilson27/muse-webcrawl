Monoamine neurotransmitter

Monoamine neurotransmitters are neurotransmitters and neuromodulators that contain one amino group connected to an aromatic ring by a two-carbon chain. They are derived from aromatic amino acids such as phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan, and play central roles in mood, arousal, reward, and autonomic function.

Principal monoamines

The principal monoamine neurotransmitters are:

  • Catecholamines, derived from tyrosine: dopamine, norepinephrine (noradrenaline), and epinephrine (adrenaline).
  • Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT), an indoleamine derived from tryptophan.
  • Histamine, an imidazoleamine derived from histidine.
  • Melatonin, synthesized from serotonin in the pineal gland, primarily a hormone regulating circadian rhythm.

Synthesis and degradation

Monoamines are synthesized in presynaptic terminals and stored in vesicles by the vesicular monoamine transporter (VMAT). After release, they are cleared from the synaptic cleft by plasma membrane transporters — the serotonin transporter (SERT), dopamine transporter (DAT), and norepinephrine transporter (NET) — or broken down by the enzymes monoamine oxidase (MAO) and catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT).

Clinical significance

Many psychiatric and neurological drugs act on monoamine systems. Monoamine oxidase inhibitors, tricyclic antidepressants, and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) increase monoamine availability and are used to treat depression and anxiety. The monoamine hypothesis proposed that depression results from deficient monoaminergic transmission, though current models are more nuanced. Dysregulation of dopamine is implicated in schizophrenia and Parkinson's disease.
