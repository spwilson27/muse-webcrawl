Organization theory

Organization theory (or organisational theory) is the interdisciplinary study of the structures, design and behaviour of organizations — firms, government agencies, non-profits and other collective enterprises. It draws on sociology, economics, psychology, political science and management studies to explain how organizations are formed, how they adapt or fail, and how their internal arrangements affect performance.

Classical perspectives

Early theory was dominated by Frederick Winslow Taylor's scientific management, Henri Fayol's administrative principles, and Max Weber's analysis of bureaucracy as a rational-legal form of authority characterised by hierarchy, written rules and specialisation. The Hawthorne studies of the 1920s–30s shifted attention to informal groups and social relations at work, giving rise to the human relations movement.

Modern perspectives

Contingency theory argues there is no single best structure: effectiveness depends on fit with environment, size and technology. Institutional theory emphasises legitimacy and conformity to social norms; resource dependence theory focuses on managing external dependencies; and transaction cost economics explains the boundaries of the firm. Later approaches study organizations as cultures, networks and complex adaptive systems.

Key questions

The field addresses organizational structure and design, leadership and decision-making, power and politics, organizational change, and the relationship between organizations and their environments.
