CinemaScope

CinemaScope is an anamorphic lens series used, from 1953 to 1967, and less often later, for shooting widescreen films that could be screened in theatres using existing equipment with only an adaptor lens. Created by 20th Century Fox president Spyros Skouras from Henri Chrétien's Hypergonar lens system, it marked the beginning of the modern anamorphic format in filmmaking, both for filming and for projection.

Origins

Fox acquired the rights to Chrétien's Hypergonar, an anamorphic lens patented in the 1920s, as the studios sought a spectacle that television could not match. The first film released in CinemaScope was the biblical epic The Robe (1953), followed shortly by How to Marry a Millionaire. The process squeezed a wide image horizontally onto standard 35 mm film and unsqueezed it in projection, yielding aspect ratios initially of 2.66:1 and later standardized at 2.39:1 (often called 2.40:1).

Technical characteristics

Early CinemaScope used four-track magnetic stereophonic sound recorded on the film itself. The lenses suffered from optical distortions — notably the "CinemaScope mumps," which widened faces in close-up — prompting the development of improved lenses by Panavision, whose system eventually displaced the original.

Legacy

By the late 1960s studios had largely shifted to Panavision lenses, but the CinemaScope name and its aspect ratio defined the look of the widescreen era; "shot in CinemaScope" remains shorthand for epic, panoramic filmmaking. The format was later revived occasionally, for example by filmmakers seeking a vintage anamorphic look.
