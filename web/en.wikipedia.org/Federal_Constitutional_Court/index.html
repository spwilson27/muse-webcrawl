Federal Constitutional Court

The Federal Constitutional Court (German: Bundesverfassungsgericht) is the supreme constitutional court of the Federal Republic of Germany. Established in 1951, it has its seat in Karlsruhe. The court is the guardian of the German constitution, the Basic Law, and has the power to declare legislation unconstitutional.

Organization

The court consists of two senates, each with eight justices, for a total of sixteen. Half of the justices are elected by the Bundestag and half by the Bundesrat, in each case by a two-thirds majority. Justices serve a single non-renewable term of twelve years and must retire at the age of 68. Each senate has a chamber system for filtering the large number of constitutional complaints.

Jurisdiction

The court's jurisdiction includes:

  • Constitutional complaints (Verfassungsbeschwerde), by which any person may claim that a public authority has violated their fundamental rights; these make up the great majority of the court's caseload, though only about 2% succeed.
  • Abstract and concrete judicial review of statutes for compatibility with the Basic Law.
  • Disputes between federal organs and between the federation and the Länder.
  • Prohibition of unconstitutional political parties, a power exercised in the 1950s against the Socialist Reich Party and the Communist Party of Germany.
  • Electoral review and impeachment proceedings against the Federal President or judges.

Influence

The court is among the most powerful constitutional courts in the world and enjoys high public trust in Germany. Landmark decisions include the Lüth judgment (1958), which established that fundamental rights form an objective order of values radiating through all law, and rulings on abortion, data privacy (the 1983 census judgment recognizing informational self-determination), and European integration. Its rulings bind all public authorities, and in some cases have the force of statute.
