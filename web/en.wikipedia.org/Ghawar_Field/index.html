Ghawar (Arabic: الغوار) is an oil field located in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia, about 100 km southwest of the port city of Dhahran. Measuring 280 by 30 km (174 by 19 mi), it is by far the largest conventional oil field in the world, and accounts for roughly a third or more of Saudi Arabia's cumulative oil production. Ghawar is entirely owned and operated by Saudi Aramco, the state oil company.

History

Ghawar was discovered in 1948 when the Ain Dar well struck oil, and production began in 1951. The field proved far larger than early estimates suggested: its five main producing areas — Ain Dar, Shedgum, Uthmaniyah, Hawiyah, and Haradh, from north to south — form a single giant anticline in the Jurassic Arab-D limestone reservoir. For decades Ghawar produced on the order of 5 million barrels per day, roughly half of Saudi output and some 6 percent of the world total; in 2019 Saudi Aramco disclosed that Ghawar's maximum sustainable capacity was about 3.8 million barrels per day, lower than many analysts had assumed.

Production and water injection

To maintain reservoir pressure, Saudi Aramco began peripheral water injection in the 1960s, eventually injecting millions of barrels per day of treated seawater pumped from the Gulf — one of the largest industrial water-handling operations on earth. Rising water cut (the fraction of water in produced fluids) and the maturity of the northern areas have prompted extensive use of horizontal and multilateral wells and "smart" completions to prolong the field's life. Cumulative production from Ghawar passed 60 billion barrels in the early 21st century, yet it is still believed to hold tens of billions of barrels of recoverable oil, more than the proved reserves of most oil-producing nations.

Significance

Because of its unmatched size, Ghawar is central to debates about Saudi spare capacity and global oil supply. In the peak oil debate, analysts such as Matthew Simmons (in his 2005 book Twilight in the Desert) questioned whether Ghawar could sustain its historic rates. Whatever its future rate, the field has produced more oil than any other single accumulation in history and remains the backbone of the Saudi petroleum industry.
