Derivative

In mathematics, the derivative measures the sensitivity of change of a function's output with respect to a change in its input. The derivative of a function of a single variable at a chosen input value, when it exists, is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of the function at that point. Derivatives are a fundamental tool of calculus: the process of finding a derivative is called differentiation.

The derivative at a point is the slope of the tangent line

Definition

For a real-valued function f of a real variable x, the derivative at a is the limit, if it exists, of the difference quotient [f(a+h) − f(a)]/h as h tends to zero. A function is differentiable at a point if this limit exists; differentiability implies continuity, but not conversely. Notation includes Leibniz's dy/dx and Lagrange's f′(x).

Interpretation

Geometrically the derivative is the slope of the tangent; physically it is an instantaneous rate of change — if the function gives position over time, its derivative is velocity, and the second derivative is acceleration. Derivatives also determine where functions increase or decrease, and their maxima and minima, making them central to optimization.

Rules of differentiation

Standard rules include the power rule, the product and quotient rules, and the chain rule for composite functions. Derivatives of elementary functions are tabulated (xⁿ → nxⁿ⁻¹; eˣ is its own derivative; sin → cos). The inverse operation, finding a function from its derivative, is integration; the two are linked by the fundamental theorem of calculus.

Generalizations

The concept extends to partial derivatives for functions of several variables, to derivatives of vector-valued and complex functions, and to abstract settings such as Fréchet derivatives in functional analysis.
