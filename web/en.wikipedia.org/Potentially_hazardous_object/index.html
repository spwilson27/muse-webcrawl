Potentially hazardous object

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
A potentially hazardous asteroid (artist's impression).

A potentially hazardous object (PHO) is a near-Earth object — either an asteroid (potentially hazardous asteroid, PHA) or a comet (PHC) — whose orbit brings it close enough to Earth, and which is large enough, that an impact could cause significant regional damage. By the conventional definition used by NASA and the Minor Planet Center, an object qualifies if its Earth minimum orbit intersection distance (MOID) is 0.05 astronomical units or less (about 7.5 million kilometres, or roughly 19.5 lunar distances) and its absolute magnitude (H) is 22 or brighter, corresponding to a diameter of roughly 140 metres or more.

Criteria: Earth MOID ≤ 0.05 AU and H ≤ 22

Approximate size: ≥ 140 m diameter

Known PHAs: More than 2,300 (as of the mid-2020s)

Monitoring and risk

Designation as a PHO does not mean an object will strike Earth, only that close approaches are possible and worth monitoring. Objects are tracked by surveys such as Pan-STARRS, the Catalina Sky Survey, and ATLAS, and their impact probabilities are computed and published, for instance in NASA's Sentry table. Impact danger is also communicated using the Torino scale and the technical Palermo scale. Famous PHAs include 99942 Apophis, which briefly reached an elevated Torino rating in 2004 before further observations ruled out impacts for at least a century, and 101955 Bennu, visited by NASA's OSIRIS-REx sample-return mission.

Planetary defence

The study of PHOs underpins planetary defence: strategies to deflect or disrupt a threatening object. These were tested in practice by NASA's DART mission, which in September 2022 successfully altered the orbit of the asteroid moonlet Dimorphos by kinetic impact. Proposed methods also include gravity tractors, laser ablation, and — as a last resort — nuclear disruption.
