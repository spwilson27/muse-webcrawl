Cast-iron cookware

Cast-iron skillet and Dutch oven

Cast-iron cookware is heavy-duty cookware made of cast iron, valued for its heat retention, durability, and ability to be used at very high temperatures. Common forms include skillets (frying pans), Dutch ovens, griddles, and kettles. Cast iron is prized for even heating once hot, excellent searing, and the ability to move from stovetop to oven; it is poor at heating quickly or uniformly at first because of its low thermal conductivity but retains heat exceptionally well owing to its mass and high volumetric heat capacity.

History

Cast-iron pots were among the earliest cast metal vessels; in China cast-iron cooking vessels date back many centuries, and in Europe cast-iron pots and kettles became common household items from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, American manufacturers such as Griswold and Wagner produced smooth, finely finished skillets. The rise of aluminium and stainless-steel cookware and, later, nonstick coatings reduced cast iron's market share, but it has enjoyed a revival among home cooks and manufacturers such as Lodge (founded 1896) and premium enamelled-iron makers such as Le Creuset and Staub.

Seasoning and care

Bare cast iron is protected and made moderately nonstick by "seasoning": a hard layer formed by heating thin coats of oil or fat on the surface until they polymerise. Seasoned cast iron should be protected from prolonged soaking and rust; enamelled cast iron needs no seasoning and resists acidic foods better. Small amounts of iron can leach into food cooked in cast iron, a minor dietary source of the element.

Types

  • Skillet / frying pan — the most common piece, used for searing, frying, and baking (e.g., cornbread).
  • Dutch oven — a heavy lidded pot for braises, stews, and bread.
  • Griddle — a flat plate for pancakes, flatbreads, and grilling.
  • Wok and specialty pieces — cast-iron versions also exist in many regional cuisines.
