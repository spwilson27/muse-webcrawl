Facial challenge

In United States constitutional law, a facial challenge is a challenge to a statute or regulation in which the plaintiff alleges that the legislation is always unconstitutional — that under no set of circumstances could it be validly applied — as opposed to an as-applied challenge, which contends that a law is unconstitutional as applied to the particular facts of the plaintiff's case.

Standards

Facial challenges are generally disfavoured by courts. In United States v. Salerno (1987), the Supreme Court stated that a facial challenge must establish "that no set of circumstances exists under which the Act would be valid". Courts have recognised exceptions and debated the scope of the Salerno standard, including in First Amendment cases, where the overbreadth doctrine permits a facial challenge when a statute prohibits a substantial amount of protected speech, and in challenges to abortion regulations and election laws, where different formulations have been applied.

Contrast with as-applied challenges

An as-applied challenge, by contrast, attacks a law only in the specific circumstances before the court, leaving the statute potentially enforceable in other situations. Judges often prefer as-applied challenges because they rest on concrete facts and avoid invalidating legislation more broadly than necessary, a principle associated with constitutional avoidance. Successful facial challenges, however, can strike down a law entirely, as occurred with the federal ban on partial-birth abortion's predecessor statutes and various campaign-finance provisions in modern First Amendment litigation.
