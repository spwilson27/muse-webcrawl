Ecosystem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rainforests are among the most productive and biodiverse ecosystems on Earth.

An ecosystem consists of all the organisms and the physical environment with which they interact. These biotic and abiotic components are linked together through nutrient cycles and energy flows. The term was coined in 1935 by the British ecologist Arthur Tansley. Energy enters the system through photosynthesis and is incorporated into plant tissue; animals feed on plants and on each other, and decomposers break down dead organic matter, recycling nutrients.

Processes

Ecosystem processes include primary production, decomposition, nutrient cycling and water cycling. Energy flows one way through food webs — from producers to herbivores to carnivores — losing roughly 90% at each trophic level as heat, whereas chemical elements are cycled repeatedly between organisms and the physical environment in biogeochemical cycles such as the carbon and nitrogen cycles.

Ecosystem services

Humans depend on ecosystem services: provisioning services such as food and fresh water; regulating services such as climate regulation, pollination and flood control; cultural services such as recreation; and supporting services such as soil formation. The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2005) found that a majority of these services were being degraded by human activity.

Disturbance and resilience

Ecosystems respond to disturbances such as fire, storms and human land use. Their capacity to absorb disturbance and reorganise while retaining essentially the same function and structure is known as ecological resilience; beyond thresholds, ecosystems can shift abruptly to alternative states.
