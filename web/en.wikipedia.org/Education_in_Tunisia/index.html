Education in Tunisia

Education in Tunisia is free and compulsory for children between the ages of 6 and 16. Since independence in 1956, Tunisia has devoted a large share of its state budget to education, achieving near-universal primary enrollment and among the highest literacy and school-attendance rates in Africa and the Arab world, including near-parity for girls. Instruction has historically been bilingual, with Arabic as the principal language and French widely used, especially for science and technical subjects in secondary and higher education.

History

Modern education began under the French protectorate (1881–1956), which maintained French schools alongside traditional Quranic schools and institutions such as Sadiki College (founded 1875). After independence, President Habib Bourguiba made mass education a centerpiece of nation-building: the 1958 education law unified the system, made primary schooling free and compulsory, and rapidly expanded enrollment. The 1991 law extended compulsory education to age 16 and reorganized basic education into two cycles.

Structure

Basic education lasts nine years — a six-year primary cycle followed by a three-year preparatory (lower secondary) cycle. Secondary education lasts four years in general lycées, with branches in letters, mathematics, experimental sciences, technology, economics, and other specialties, culminating in the national baccalaureate examination, which governs admission to higher education. Technical and vocational tracks are provided in parallel institutions.

Higher education

Higher education is provided by public universities — including Tunis El Manar University, the University of Carthage, the University of Sfax, and the older University of Ez-Zitouna (one of the world's oldest Islamic universities) — and by higher institutes of technological studies (ISET). Tunisia has one of the highest tertiary enrollment rates in the region, with women forming a majority of university students. Public higher education is tuition-free, though graduate unemployment has been a persistent social and political problem, contributing to the discontent behind the 2011 revolution.

Challenges

Despite strong access indicators, the system faces criticism over quality, rote learning, regional disparities between the coast and the interior, and the mismatch between graduates' skills and labor-market needs. International assessments have placed Tunisian pupils below international averages in mathematics and science.
