Bertrand's ballot theorem

Bertrand's ballot theorem is a result in combinatorics concerning the probability that, in an election where one candidate receives p votes and another receives q votes with p > q, the winning candidate stays strictly ahead in the count throughout the entire tallying process. The theorem is named after Joseph Bertrand, who published it in 1887.

Statement

Suppose two candidates A and B receive p and q votes respectively, with p > q. The probability that A is always strictly ahead of B during the count is (p − q)/(p + q). Equivalently, of the (p + q)!/(p! q!) possible orderings of the votes, the fraction in which A is always ahead is (p − q)/(p + q).

Proof

Several proofs exist. One elegant approach uses the reflection principle, also used in André's proof related to the reflection method for ballot sequences: sequences in which B at some point catches up with or overtakes A can be placed in bijection with sequences beginning with a vote for B, and counting these leads directly to the formula. Another proof proceeds by induction on p + q.

History

The problem was first posed and solved by Joseph Louis François Bertrand in 1887. A closely related result, the ballot theorem in its "weak" form (allowing ties), gives the probability that A is never behind as (p − q + 1)/(p + 1). The theorem is a precursor to results about random walks and Brownian motion, such as the arcsine law.
