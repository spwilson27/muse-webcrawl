Spanish Renaissance architecture

Spanish Renaissance architecture was that form of Renaissance architecture in the territories of the Spanish monarchy, principally Spain itself, in the late 15th and 16th centuries. Renaissance ideals arrived in Spain through trade and dynastic links with Italy, but they developed with strong local characteristics, mingling with Gothic and Mudéjar traditions before giving way to a more orthodox classicism and then to the austere Herrerian style.

Development

The transition from Gothic to Renaissance in Spain was gradual. An important early transitional style is Isabelline Gothic, named after Isabella I of Castile, which combined late Gothic structure with Renaissance and Mudéjar ornament. The early Renaissance proper is often identified with the Plateresque style (c. 1480–1560), whose name derives from platero (silversmith), reflecting its finely worked, silversmith-like surface decoration over essentially Gothic or early classical structures. Examples include the façade of the University of Salamanca and the Hospital de Santa Cruz in Toledo.

High Renaissance

In the second quarter of the 16th century, a purer Italianate classicism appeared, sometimes called the "princely style" or Purism. Landmark buildings include the Palace of Charles V in the Alhambra of Granada, begun in 1527 to designs by Pedro Machuca, with its remarkable circular courtyard; the cathedrals of Granada and Jaén, associated with Diego de Siloé and Andrés de Vandelvira; and the university buildings of Alcalá de Henares.

Herrerian style

From the 1560s the style became severe and unornamented under Juan Bautista de Toledo and Juan de Herrera, architect of the monastery-palace of El Escorial (1563–1584), built for Philip II. The Herrerian style is characterised by geometric rigour, slate spires, and a deliberate lack of decoration, and it dominated Spanish architecture into the 17th century before the exuberance of the Spanish Baroque.
