Capetian dynasty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Capetian dynasty (French: Capétiens) is a dynasty of Frankish origin and one of the largest and oldest European royal houses. Founded by Hugh Capet, who was elected King of the Franks in 987, the direct Capetian line ruled France continuously until 1328—the longest unbroken sequence of father-to-son successions in European history. Cadet branches, including the Houses of Valois and Bourbon, continued to rule France until 1848.

Origins and rise

The Capetians descended from the Robertians, a powerful Frankish family that had twice supplied kings (Odo and Robert I) in the 9th and 10th centuries. Hugh Capet's initial royal domain was small—essentially the Île-de-France—but his successors patiently expanded royal authority. Louis VI and his minister Abbot Suger consolidated the demesne; Philip II Augustus (r. 1180–1223) quadrupled royal territory, taking Normandy and other lands from the Angevin kings of England; Louis IX (Saint Louis) gave the monarchy immense prestige.

Succession and branches

When Charles IV died in 1328 without a son, the direct line ended, and succession passed to the Valois branch—an outcome contested by Edward III of England and a cause of the Hundred Years' War. Later branches included Valois-Orléans, Valois-Angoulême, Bourbon, Bourbon-Vendôme, and Orléans. Through the Bourbons, Capetian descendants came to rule Spain (from 1700), where King Felipe VI reigns today, making the dynasty still reigning in the 21st century. The Capetians also ruled, at various times, Naples, Sicily, Hungary, Poland, Portugal, Navarre, and the Latin Empire of Constantinople through branches such as Anjou and Évreux.

Legacy

With more than a thousand years of history counting its branches, the Capetian dynasty is among the most prolific royal houses in Europe. Its name derives from the nickname of Hugh Capet, supposedly from the cape (cappa) he wore as lay abbot of Saint Martin of Tours.
