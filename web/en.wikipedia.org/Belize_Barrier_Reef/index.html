Belize Barrier Reef

The Belize Barrier Reef is a series of coral reefs straddling the coast of Belize, roughly 300 metres (980 ft) offshore in the north and 40 kilometres (25 mi) in the south within the country limits. The Belize Barrier Reef is a 300-kilometre (190 mi) long section of the 900-kilometre (560 mi) Mesoamerican Barrier Reef System, which is continuous from Cancún on the north-eastern tip of the Yucatán Peninsula through the Riviera Maya and down to Honduras, making it the second largest coral reef system in the world after the Great Barrier Reef in Australia. It is Belize's top tourist destination, attracting almost half of its visitors, and is vital to its fishing industry.

Description

Charles Darwin described it in 1842 as "the most remarkable reef in the West Indies". The system includes fringing reefs, patch reefs, faros, three offshore atolls — Turneffe Atoll, Lighthouse Reef, and Glover's Reef — and some 450 sand and mangrove cayes. The Great Blue Hole, a giant marine sinkhole in Lighthouse Reef, is a world-famous diving site. The reef supports more than 500 species of fish, 70 species of hard coral, 36 species of soft coral, and hundreds of invertebrate species, plus endangered American manatees, sea turtles, and the American crocodile.

Conservation

A large portion of the reef is protected by the Belize Barrier Reef Reserve System, which includes seven marine reserves, including Hol Chan Marine Reserve and Bacalar Chico, as well as national parks and natural monuments. UNESCO inscribed the reserve system as a World Heritage Site in 1996. It was listed as endangered from 2009 until 2018, when it was removed after Belize imposed a moratorium on offshore oil exploration. Threats continue from coral bleaching, hurricanes, invasive lionfish, and coastal development.

Tourism

The reef is popular for scuba diving and snorkelling, particularly at Shark Ray Alley, Hol Chan Cut, and the Blue Hole. Tourism and fishing together support a large share of Belize's coastal population.
