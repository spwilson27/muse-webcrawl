Streamline Moderne is an international style of Art Deco architecture and design that emerged in the 1930s. It was inspired by aerodynamic design. Streamline architecture emphasized curving forms, long horizontal lines, and sometimes nautical elements. In industrial design, it was applied to appliances, railway locomotives, telephones, and other everyday objects to give them a sleek, modern, and efficient appearance.

Characteristics

Streamline buildings typically feature smooth stucco walls, rounded corners, horizontal bands of windows, glass block, flat roofs, and minimal ornament — a stripped-down machine aesthetic that contrasted with the lavish verticality of earlier Art Deco. The style expressed the speed and optimism of the machine age even as it coincided with the Great Depression.

Design and influence

Industrial designers such as Norman Bel Geddes, Raymond Loewy, Henry Dreyfuss, and Walter Dorwin Teague applied streamlining to products ranging from locomotives and automobiles to refrigerators and radios, arguing that aerodynamic forms suggested progress and efficiency. The style influenced the look of American diners, cinemas, bus stations, and ocean liners, and left a lasting mark on graphic and product design.
