Samuel Dashiell Hammett (27 May 1894 – 10 January 1961) was an American writer of hardboiled detective novels and short stories. He is widely regarded as having created the hard-boiled school of detective fiction. His best-known novels are Red Harvest (1929), The Maltese Falcon (1930), which introduced Sam Spade, and The Thin Man (1934), featuring Nick and Nora Charles.

Life

Hammett left school at 13 and worked a succession of jobs before becoming an operative for the Pinkerton National Detective Agency, an experience that supplied the authentic background of his fiction. After service in the First World War, during which he contracted tuberculosis, he began writing for pulp magazines, principally Black Mask, creating the Continental Op, a nameless, dogged agency detective. His writing career largely ended after 1934; he became a political activist, was jailed in 1951 for refusing to name contributors to a bail fund for convicted Communists, and was blacklisted during the McCarthy era. His long relationship with playwright Lillian Hellman lasted until his death.

Style and influence

Hammett stripped the detective story of the drawing-room puzzle conventions of the British Golden Age, replacing them with terse, behaviourist prose that records dialogue and action while withholding characters' thoughts. Raymond Chandler and generations of crime writers acknowledged his influence, and The Maltese Falcon's 1941 film adaptation, starring Humphrey Bogart, became a cornerstone of film noir.
