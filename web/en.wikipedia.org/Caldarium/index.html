Caldarium

The caldarium (also called the calidarium) was the hot room in the suite of bathing rooms in an ancient Roman bath complex (thermae). It was a very hot and steamy chamber, heated by a hypocaust system beneath a raised floor, with a hot-water pool (alveus) and a basin (labrum) of cold water for cooling.

Use in the bathing sequence

The typical bathing routine moved from the apodyterium (changing room) through the tepidarium (warm room) to the caldarium, where bathers sweated, scraped their skin with a strigil after applying oil, and soaked in hot water, before cooling down in the frigidarium (cold room). The caldarium was usually placed on the southwest side of the complex with large windows to take advantage of the afternoon sun.

Architecture

Temperatures in the caldarium could reach around 40–50 °C with high humidity. Floors rested on pillars (pilae stacks) above the hypocaust furnace chamber, and hollow box tiles (tubuli) in the walls carried hot air upward, heating the whole room envelope. Well-preserved examples survive at Pompeii and in the Baths of Caracalla in Rome.
