Classical education movement

The Classical education movement or renewal is a form of education based on the historic trivium in children's education, with a focus on primary sources at the secondary level and at the undergraduate level. It advocates a return to the liberal arts tradition of Western education — grammar, logic, and rhetoric (the trivium), followed by arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy (the quadrivium) — along with the study of Latin and Greek and of the great books of Western civilisation.

History

The modern movement is often traced to Dorothy Sayers's 1947 essay "The Lost Tools of Learning", which proposed matching the three stages of the trivium to children's developmental stages: the "poll-parrot" (grammar) stage, the "pert" (logic/dialectic) stage, and the "poetic" (rhetoric) stage. In the United States the movement grew from the 1980s through the Association of Classical Christian Schools (founded 1994), the Circe Institute, charter-school networks such as Great Hearts Academies and Ridgeview Classical Schools, and Hillsdale College's Barney Charter School Initiative. Classical approaches also became popular among homeschoolers, promoted by works such as The Well-Trained Mind by Susan Wise Bauer and Jessie Wise.

Principles

Classical schools typically teach Latin (and sometimes Greek), emphasise phonics and memorisation in early grades, Socratic discussion and formal logic in middle grades, and rhetoric, primary-source history, and the great books in the upper school. Many — though not all — classical schools are Christian; secular charter networks apply the same curriculum in a public setting.

Criticism

Critics question the developmental claims of the trivium model, the movement's Eurocentrism, and the extent to which classical charters function as publicly funded religiously inflected schooling. Supporters counter that the curriculum is content-rich, coherent, and demonstrably popular with parents.
