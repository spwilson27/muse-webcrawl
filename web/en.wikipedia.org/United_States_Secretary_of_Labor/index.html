United States Secretary of Labor

The United States Secretary of Labor is the head of the United States Department of Labor and a member of the Cabinet of the United States. The secretary oversees federal policy on the workforce, including workplace safety, wage and hour standards, unemployment insurance, and labour statistics, and is 11th in the presidential line of succession.

History

A Department of Labor existed as part of the Department of Commerce and Labor from 1903; in 1913 Congress split the two, creating a standalone Department of Labor. Its stated purpose is "to foster, promote, and develop the welfare of the wage earners of the United States, to improve their working conditions, and to advance their opportunities for profitable employment." William B. Wilson, a former coal miner and union official, was the first secretary, serving under President Woodrow Wilson.

Frances Perkins, appointed by Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1933, was the first woman to hold a cabinet post in the United States. She served for the whole of Roosevelt's presidency—the longest tenure of any secretary of labor—and was a central figure in the New Deal, including the Social Security Act and the Fair Labor Standards Act, which established a federal minimum wage and the 40-hour week.

Duties

The secretary directs the Department of Labor's agencies, including the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), the Wage and Hour Division, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Employment and Training Administration, and the Employee Benefits Security Administration. The secretary advises the president on labour policy, represents the department before Congress, and administers statutes such as the Fair Labor Standards Act and the Occupational Safety and Health Act.
