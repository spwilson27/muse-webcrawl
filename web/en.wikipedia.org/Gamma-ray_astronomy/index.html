Gamma-ray astronomy

Gamma-ray astronomy is the astronomical observation of gamma rays, the most energetic form of electromagnetic radiation, with photon energies above about 100 keV. Because the Earth's atmosphere blocks gamma rays, observations are made from satellites and high-altitude balloons, complemented by ground-based detectors that observe the air showers produced by very-high-energy gamma rays.

History

The field began in the 1960s with satellite detectors such as Explorer 11 and the Vela satellites, which unexpectedly discovered gamma-ray bursts in 1967. The SAS-2 and COS-B missions mapped diffuse Galactic emission and identified the first gamma-ray pulsars. The Compton Gamma Ray Observatory (1991–2000) transformed the field with its all-sky survey; later missions include INTEGRAL, Swift, and the Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope, launched in 2008, whose Large Area Telescope catalogued thousands of sources.

Sources

Gamma-ray sources include pulsars, supernova remnants, active galactic nuclei and blazars, gamma-ray bursts, and the Galactic centre. Diffuse emission traces cosmic-ray interactions with interstellar gas. Ground-based Cherenkov telescopes such as H.E.S.S., MAGIC and VERITAS detect TeV gamma rays indirectly via Cherenkov light from particle showers.

Detection

Space telescopes use scintillation detectors, spark chambers and pair-conversion trackers, since gamma rays cannot be focused by ordinary mirrors. At the highest energies, extensive air shower arrays and imaging atmospheric Cherenkov telescopes observe secondary particles instead.
