Shavuot

Shavuot (Hebrew: שָׁבוּעוֹת, Šāvūʿōṯ, "Weeks"), also called the Feast of Weeks, is a Jewish holiday occurring on the sixth day of the Hebrew month of Sivan (roughly late May to early June). It is one of the three pilgrimage festivals (Shalosh Regalim). In the Bible it marks the wheat harvest in the Land of Israel; in rabbinic tradition it also commemorates the anniversary of the giving of the Torah to the Israelites at Mount Sinai.

Biblical origins

Shavuot is the culmination of the seven-week Counting of the Omer that begins on Passover: "you shall count... seven full weeks" (Leviticus 23). In Exodus it is the "Feast of Harvest, of the first fruits of your labour", and in Numbers the "Day of First Fruits" (Yom ha-Bikkurim), when an offering of new grain was brought to the Temple in Jerusalem. In ancient Israel farmers brought their bikkurim — first-ripened fruits of the Seven Species — in processions to the Temple.

Customs

The best-known customs include the Tikkun Leil Shavuot, staying up all night studying Torah on the first night of the festival; reading the Book of Ruth, whose harvest setting and story of a convert's acceptance of the Torah suit the holiday; eating dairy foods such as cheesecake and blintzes (explanations range from the Torah being likened to milk, to the Israelites not yet having kosher meat dishes at Sinai); and decorating homes and synagogues with greenery and flowers. The Ten Commandments are read standing. In Reform and some other congregations, confirmation ceremonies for teenagers are held on Shavuot. Kibbutzim historically revived the first-fruits procession as a major agricultural pageant.

Duration and observance

Work is prohibited on Shavuot as on other festival days. In Israel it is observed for one day; outside Israel, Orthodox and some Conservative Jews observe two days. The festival is followed in the liturgical calendar by the summer fasts and the harvest festival of Sukkot in the autumn.
