British nationality law

British nationality law sets out who is a British national and the different classes of British nationality. The principal statute in force today is the British Nationality Act 1981, which took effect on 1 January 1983 and replaced the single status of Citizen of the United Kingdom and Colonies with several categories.

Classes of nationality

The main classes are:

  • British citizen — the principal class, carrying the right of abode in the United Kingdom.
  • British Overseas Territories citizen — connected with the British Overseas Territories; since 2002 most also hold British citizenship.
  • British Overseas citizen — a residual class, generally without the right of abode anywhere.
  • British subject — a residual status held mainly by certain people from Ireland and pre-1949 British subjects.
  • British National (Overseas) — created for Hong Kong residents before the 1997 handover; since 2021 holders have a route to settlement in the UK under the BN(O) visa scheme.
  • British protected person — a diminishing residual class from former protectorates.

Acquisition

Historically, British nationality followed jus soli: nearly everyone born on British soil was a subject. The 1981 Act ended automatic citizenship by birth alone; a child born in the UK is a British citizen if at least one parent is a British citizen or "settled" (holding indefinite leave to remain). Citizenship can also be acquired by descent, registration, adoption and naturalisation. Naturalisation for adults generally requires five years' residence (three if married to a British citizen), good character, sufficient knowledge of English (or Welsh or Scottish Gaelic) and of life in the UK, and an intention to live in the UK. The UK permits multiple citizenship. Issues in recent decades have included the Windrush scandal — in which long-resident Commonwealth citizens were wrongly treated as immigration offenders — and deprivation of citizenship on national-security grounds.
