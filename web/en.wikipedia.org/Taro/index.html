Taro

Taro (Colocasia esculenta) is a root vegetable cultivated for its starchy corms in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide. It is one of the oldest cultivated plants, domesticated in Southeast Asia and the Pacific, and a dietary staple in West Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and Oceania — in Hawaii, where it is called kalo, it holds central cultural significance. The corms, leaves, and stems are all eaten but must be cooked thoroughly to destroy irritating calcium oxalate crystals.

Cultivation

Taro is propagated vegetatively from cormels or stem cuttings and grown either in flooded paddies, like rice, or in upland plots. Wetland taro yields heavily but requires 6–12 months to mature. Global production exceeds 10 million tonnes annually, with Nigeria the largest producer, followed by China and Cameroon.

Culinary uses

In Hawaii taro is pounded into poi, a fermented paste. The corm is boiled, roasted, or made into chips and desserts across Asia; in the Philippines it features in laing (leaves in coconut milk) and sinigang. Taro-flavoured bubble tea and ice cream, recognizably purple, are popular worldwide, though the flesh itself ranges from white to pinkish. The large leaves, cooked as greens, are known as callaloo in parts of the Caribbean (where the plant is also called dasheen or eddoe).

Toxicity

Raw taro is acrid: needle-like calcium oxalate raphides and proteases cause intense irritation of the mouth and throat. Thorough cooking, soaking, or fermentation renders the plant safe. Leaf blight caused by Phytophthora colocasiae devastated Samoan production in the 1990s, a standing warning about the crop's genetic narrowness in the Pacific.
