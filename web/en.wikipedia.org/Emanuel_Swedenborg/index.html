Emanuel Swedenborg (born Emanuel Swedberg; 29 January 1688 – 29 March 1772) was a Swedish pluralist, scientist, philosopher, and mystic. He spent the first half of his career as a productive scientist and assessor on Sweden's Board of Mines, and the second half as a theologian who claimed that God had opened his spiritual senses, allowing him to converse freely with angels and spirits.

Scientific career

After studies at Uppsala University and travel in England, France, and the Netherlands, Swedenborg published works on metallurgy, anatomy, and cosmology. His three-volume Opera philosophica et mineralis (1734) proposed a nebular hypothesis of the Solar System's formation, and his studies of the brain anticipated later findings about the cerebral cortex and neurons.

Spiritual turning point

In 1744–45 Swedenborg underwent a series of vivid dreams and visions, which he recorded in his Journal of Dreams. He came to believe he had been appointed by God to reform Christianity by revealing the spiritual sense of Scripture. Over the following decades he published, mostly anonymously and in Latin, theological works including the eight-volume Arcana Coelestia (1749–56) and Heaven and Hell (1758).

Influence

Swedenborg's writings influenced figures including William Blake, Charles Baudelaire, Honoré de Balzac, and Ralph Waldo Emerson, and inspired the founding of the General Church of the New Jerusalem and other Swedenborgian churches. Immanuel Kant critically examined his claims in Dreams of a Spirit-Seer (1766).
