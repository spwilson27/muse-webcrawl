Coagulation

The coagulation cascade in vivo: the pathways converge on the activation of factor X and generation of thrombin

Coagulation, also known as clotting, is the process by which blood changes from a liquid to a gel, forming a blood clot. It potentially results in hemostasis, the cessation of blood loss from a damaged vessel, followed by repair. The mechanism of coagulation involves activation, adhesion and aggregation of platelets, as well as deposition and maturation of fibrin.

The coagulation cascade

Coagulation begins almost instantly after an injury to the vessel wall has damaged the endothelium lining the vessel. Exposure of blood to proteins such as tissue factor initiates changes in blood platelets and the plasma protein fibrinogen. The cascade is traditionally described as intrinsic (contact activation) and extrinsic (tissue factor) pathways that converge on the prothrombinase complex, which converts prothrombin to thrombin. Thrombin then converts soluble fibrinogen into strands of insoluble fibrin, and activates factor XIII, which cross-links the fibrin to form a stable clot.

Regulation and disorders

Coagulation is highly regulated: anticoagulant mechanisms involving protein C, protein S, antithrombin and tissue factor pathway inhibitor limit clot extension. Disorders of coagulation include bleeding disorders such as haemophilia (deficiency of factor VIII or IX) and von Willebrand disease, and hypercoagulable states that cause thrombosis, including deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. Laboratory tests of coagulation include the prothrombin time (PT/INR) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT).

Other senses

In chemistry, coagulation (flocculation) also refers to the aggregation of colloids — for example the curdling of milk by acids or rennet, exploited in cheese making, and the clumping step of water treatment. Protein denaturation by heat, as with the toxins of the ergot fungus Claviceps purpurea affecting vessels, belongs to related biochemical pathways.
