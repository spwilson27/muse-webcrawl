Parallax

Parallax Definition: Apparent displacement of an object due to change in observer position
Astronomical unit: Parsec = distance at which parallax is 1 arcsecond
First stellar measurement: Friedrich Bessel, 61 Cygni, 1838

Parallax is a displacement or difference in the apparent position of an object viewed along two different lines of sight, and is measured by the angle or half-angle of inclination between those two lines. Due to foreshortening, nearby objects show a larger parallax than farther objects when observed from different positions, so parallax can be used to determine distances.

Everyday and visual parallax

The term derives from the Greek parallaxis, "alteration." A simple example is looking at the dashboard of a vehicle: the needle of a speedometer may seem to read differently depending on the angle from which it is viewed, an error known as parallax error. In binocular vision, parallax between the two eyes contributes to depth perception, a principle exploited by stereoscopes and 3D displays.

Stellar parallax

Astronomers use the principle of parallax to measure distances to nearby stars. As the Earth orbits the Sun, a nearby star appears to shift position relative to the more distant background stars. The parallax angle is defined as half of the total angular shift observed over six months, from opposite sides of the Earth's orbit, giving a baseline of two astronomical units. The parsec (parallax arcsecond) is defined as the distance at which a star has a parallax of one arcsecond, equal to about 3.26 light-years or 3.086 × 1016 metres.

The apparent absence of stellar parallax was used for centuries as an argument against heliocentrism. Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel made the first successful measurement in 1838, determining a parallax of about 0.314 arcseconds for the star 61 Cygni. Modern space missions such as Hipparcos and Gaia have measured parallaxes for over a billion stars, building a precise three-dimensional map of the Milky Way.

Other forms

Lunar parallax, measured against the Earth's diameter, lets astronomers calculate the Moon's distance; historically it was also observed simultaneously from distant observatories to determine the scale of the solar system. Photographers encounter parallax in viewfinder cameras and in panoramic stitching, and video games and web design use "parallax scrolling" to simulate depth.
