Glossopteris

Glossopteris is a genus of extinct seed ferns (pteridosperms) known from fossils dating from the Permian period. It is the largest and best-known genus of the order Glossopteridales, a group of plants that dominated the floras of the southern supercontinent Gondwana. The name means "tongue fern", from the Greek glossa (tongue), referring to the tongue-shaped leaves.

Scientific classification
Kingdom: Plantae
Order: Glossopteridales
Family: Glossopteridaceae
Genus: Glossopteris Brongniart, 1828

Description

Glossopteris grew as a tree or shrub, in some species reaching heights of several metres, with a woody trunk (whose fossil wood is known as Araucarioxylon-like Vertebraria roots, an unusual form showing wedges of wood separated by gaps, possibly an adaptation to waterlogged soils). Its leaves were lance-shaped with a strong midrib and a distinctive net-like (reticulate) pattern of veins. The plants were deciduous, shedding their leaves in the cold, dark winters of the high southern latitudes where they lived. Reproductive organs were borne on or fused to the leaves, and the plants produced seeds rather than spores.

Distribution and significance

Fossils of Glossopteris are found across South America, Africa, Antarctica, India, Australia and New Zealand — all fragments of the former supercontinent Gondwana. Because the plant's large seeds could not have been carried across oceans by wind or water, its distribution on now widely separated southern landmasses became one of the earliest and most famous lines of evidence for continental drift, cited by Eduard Suess and later by Alfred Wegener. Robert Scott's ill-fated Terra Nova expedition collected Glossopteris fossils in Antarctica in 1912; they were found with the party's bodies and provided early proof that Antarctica had once been joined to the other southern continents and had a much warmer past.

Extinction

The glossopterids were devastated by the end-Permian mass extinction about 252 million years ago, the largest extinction event in Earth history, and the genus disappears from the fossil record at the Permian–Triassic boundary.
