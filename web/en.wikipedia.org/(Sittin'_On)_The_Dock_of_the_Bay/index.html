"(Sittin' On) The Dock of the Bay" is a song co-written by soul singer Otis Redding and guitarist Steve Cropper. Redding recorded it twice in 1967, including just three days before his death in a plane crash on December 10, 1967. It was released as a single on January 8, 1968, on Stax Records' Volt label, becoming the first posthumous single to top the charts in the United States. It reached number 3 on the UK Singles Chart.

Background and composition

Redding began writing the song in August 1967 while staying on a rented houseboat in Sausalito, California, looking across San Francisco Bay after performing at the Monterey Pop Festival. Inspired by the Beatles' Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band and determined to broaden his sound beyond traditional soul, he sketched the opening lines about watching the ships roll in. Cropper helped him complete the song in Memphis, adding the bridge. The famous whistled outro was not planned: Redding, who intended to fade with a vocal ad-lib, whistled a phrase during a run-through when he forgot the words, and Cropper and producer-engineer Tom Dowd kept it in the final mix.

Recording and release

The song was recorded at Stax Studios in Memphis in November 1967, with final overdubs in early December. Booker T. & the M.G.'s provided the backing, with Cropper on guitar and piano fills added by Isaac Hayes. Three days after the final session, Redding and most of his band, the Bar-Kays, were killed when their plane crashed into Lake Monona near Madison, Wisconsin. Cropper completed the mix, adding the sounds of seagulls and crashing waves that Redding had wanted. The single spent four weeks at number one on the Billboard Hot 100 in March 1968, and the album of the same name became a major posthumous collection of Redding's work.

Reception and legacy

"(Sittin' On) The Dock of the Bay" won two Grammy Awards in 1968: Best Rhythm & Blues Song and Best Rhythm & Blues Performance, Male. It has consistently been ranked among the greatest songs of all time, appearing on Rolling Stone's list of the 500 Greatest Songs of All Time and the magazine's list of the greatest singers' signature recordings. The song has been covered by numerous artists, including a 1987 version by Michael Bolton that reached number 11 on the Billboard Hot 100, and versions by Sammy Hagar, Pearl Jam, and Peggy Lee. In 1999 the single was inducted into the Grammy Hall of Fame, and it was selected for preservation in the National Recording Registry by the Library of Congress.
