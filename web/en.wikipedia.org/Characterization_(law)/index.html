Characterization (law)


Characterization in conflict of laws: assigning a legal category to a claim or issue.

Characterization, or classification, is a concept in the conflict of laws (private international law) describing the process by which a court assigns the facts of a case, or the legal question raised by them, to a recognised legal category — such as contract, tort, property, marriage or succession — in order to select the appropriate choice-of-law rule.

The problem

Connecting factors in choice-of-law rules (for example, the law of the place where a contract was made, or where immovable property is situated) depend on the category into which the issue falls. Because legal systems define their categories differently, a question characterised as one of "procedure" in the forum may be one of "substance" elsewhere, producing different outcomes. A court must therefore decide by which system's concepts the characterisation should be made: the lex fori (law of the forum), the lex causae (the law governing the substance), or an autonomous, comparative approach.

Approaches

The dominant common-law approach characterises according to the lex fori, but with an internationalist outlook — as in the English case Macmillan Inc v Bishopsgate Investment Trust plc (No 3), where the court characterised the issue (the priority of competing claims to shares) before applying the relevant choice-of-law rule. Scholars such as Bartin and Kahn, who identified the problem in the 1890s, proposed characterisation by the lex causae, while others favour an enlightened lex fori or autonomous concepts in instruments such as the Rome I and Rome II Regulations of the European Union.

Related concepts

Characterisation is the first stage of the conflict-of-laws process, followed by applying the choice-of-law rule and, if necessary, the doctrines of renvoi and the incidental question.
