Gardens of Versailles

Image placeholder: the Latona Fountain and Grand Canal

The Gardens of Versailles (French: Jardins du château de Versailles) occupy part of the Domaine national de Versailles in France. Situated to the west of the palace, the gardens cover some 800 hectares, much of it landscaped in the classic French formal garden style perfected by André Le Nôtre. They are among the most visited public sites in France and, together with the palace, are a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Creation

When Louis XIV built his palace on the site of his father's hunting lodge, he commissioned Le Nôtre to design gardens on an unprecedented scale, beginning in 1661. The works, which occupied thousands of labourers and took decades, involved terracing the natural slopes, creating the mile-long Grand Canal, and planting elaborate parterres, bosquets (formal groves), and an orangery. Fountains — eventually numbering around 1,400 jets fed by elaborate hydraulics, including the Machine de Marly pumping water from the Seine — displayed mythological scenes glorifying the Sun King.

Features

The garden's east–west axis runs from the palace's Hall of Mirrors through the Water Parterre, the Latona Fountain, and the Royal Avenue to the Apollo Fountain and the Grand Canal, where gondolas once carried courtiers. Among the bosquets are the Ballroom Grove, the Colonnade Grove designed by Jules Hardouin-Mansart, and the Enceladus Grove. The gardens also contain the Grand Trianon, Petit Trianon, and Marie Antoinette's Hamlet, a rustic retreat built in the 1780s.

Later history

The gardens were replanted several times, suffered during the Revolution, and were significantly damaged by storms in 1990 and December 1999, prompting extensive restoration that replanted whole groves following Le Nôtre's original plans. Today the fountains are operated for the "Musical Fountains Show" on weekends in season, using water recirculated from the Grand Canal.
