Computational fluid dynamics

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) is a branch of fluid mechanics that uses numerical analysis and data structures to analyse and solve problems involving fluid flows. Computers perform the calculations required to simulate the free-stream flow of a fluid and its interaction with surfaces defined by boundary conditions. With high-speed supercomputers, better solutions can be achieved, and CFD is now routinely used in aerospace, automotive design, weather forecasting, turbomachinery, and many other fields.

A CFD simulation of airflow over an aircraft.

Governing equations

The foundations of almost all CFD problems are the Navier–Stokes equations, which express conservation of momentum for a viscous fluid, together with conservation of mass and energy:

ρ(∂u/∂t + u · ∇u) = −∇p + μ∇²u + f

Because these equations are nonlinear partial differential equations, closed-form solutions exist only for simplified cases, so the flow domain is discretised into a mesh of small cells and the equations are solved approximately at each cell.

Methodology

A CFD analysis proceeds in three stages. Pre-processing defines the geometry, generates the mesh, and sets fluid properties and boundary conditions. The solver then computes the flow iteratively, using methods such as the finite volume method (the dominant approach in commercial codes), the finite element method, or the finite difference method, often with discretisation schemes like SIMPLE or PISO for pressure–velocity coupling. Post-processing extracts quantities of interest — lift, drag, pressure distributions — and produces visualisations such as contour plots and streamlines.

Turbulence modelling

Resolving every scale of turbulent motion directly (direct numerical simulation) is prohibitively expensive for most engineering flows. Practical CFD therefore uses models: Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) models such as k–ε and k–ω average the turbulence statistically, while large eddy simulation (LES) resolves the largest eddies and models only the smallest.

History and applications

Early CFD developed in the 1960s at national laboratories such as Los Alamos, where the particle-in-cell and marker-and-cell methods were pioneered. The growth of computing power made three-dimensional, unsteady simulations routine. Today CFD complements wind-tunnel testing in aircraft design, shapes Formula One cars, optimises pumps and turbines, predicts blood flow in medical research, and underpins numerical weather prediction.
