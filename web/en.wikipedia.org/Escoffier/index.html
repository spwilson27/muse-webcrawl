Georges Auguste Escoffier (28 October 1846 – 12 February 1935) was a French chef and culinary writer who popularized and updated traditional French cooking methods. Often called "the king of chefs and the chef of kings", he codified the recipes of haute cuisine in Le Guide Culinaire (1903) and reorganized the professional kitchen into the brigade de cuisine system still used today.

Career

Escoffier began his apprenticeship at thirteen in his uncle's restaurant in Nice and cooked in Paris and for the French army during the Franco-Prussian War. His fame was made in partnership with the hotelier César Ritz: from the 1890s he directed the kitchens of the Savoy Hotel in London, and then of the Ritz in Paris (1898) and the Carlton in London, cooking for royalty, statesmen, and stars of the stage such as Sarah Bernhardt and Nellie Melba.

Contributions

  • Le Guide Culinaire — some 5,000 recipes, rationalizing the grand cuisine of his predecessor Marie-Antoine Carême: lighter sauces built on stocks, simplified garnishes and menus, and dishes classified systematically. His five "mother sauces" became a teaching standard.
  • The brigade system — dividing the kitchen into stations (saucier, rôtisseur, garde-manger, pâtissier, and others) under a chef de cuisine, replacing chaotic shouting with hierarchy and specialization; he also insisted on cleanliness, sobriety, and quiet in the kitchen.
  • Signature dishes — Peach Melba and Melba toast for Nellie Melba, Fraises Sarah Bernhardt, and tournedos Rossini (following Carême's tradition).

Escoffier's simplified, codified French cuisine became the foundation of modern Western fine dining, and his books remain in print more than a century later.
