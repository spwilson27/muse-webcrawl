Communications satellite
Purpose: Relay of radio, TV, data, telephony
First active: Telstar 1 (1962)
Common orbit: Geostationary (35,786 km)
Key component: Transponders

A communications satellite is an artificial satellite placed in orbit to provide telecommunication links. It receives signals from a ground station through an uplink, amplifies and converts them using transponders, and retransmits them on a downlink to receiving stations within its footprint.

History

The concept was popularized by Arthur C. Clarke in 1945, who described geostationary relay stations. The first passive communications satellite was Echo 1 (1960), a reflective balloon; Telstar 1 (1962) was the first active satellite to relay transatlantic television. Syncom 3 (1964) demonstrated geostationary service, and Intelsat I ("Early Bird") began commercial operation in 1965.

Orbits

Most broadcast and fixed-service satellites operate in geostationary orbit, appearing fixed in the sky. Medium Earth orbit systems serve navigation-adjacent and data services, while low Earth orbit constellations — pioneered by Iridium for telephony and expanded by Starlink for broadband internet — use many satellites to minimize latency and provide global coverage.

Applications

Communications satellites carry television distribution, direct-to-home broadcasting, satellite radio, maritime and aeronautical connectivity, military communications, and broadband internet to remote areas. They remain essential where terrestrial infrastructure is impractical.
