Cat food

Cat food is food specifically formulated for consumption by domestic cats. As obligate carnivores, cats have nutritional requirements distinct from those of many other domestic animals: they need a high proportion of protein, require certain nutrients such as taurine and arachidonic acid that they cannot synthesise in sufficient quantities, and cannot thrive on a purely plant-based diet. Commercial cat food is produced in dry (kibble), wet (canned or pouched) and semi-moist forms, alongside raw and home-prepared diets.

Nutritional requirements

Cats require preformed vitamin A, are sensitive to deficiency in the amino acid taurine — essential for heart, eye and reproductive health — and need arginine at every meal. Regulatory bodies such as the Association of American Feed Control Officials (AAFCO) in the United States and FEDIAF in Europe publish nutrient profiles for growth, maintenance and reproduction life stages, and foods meeting them may be labelled "complete and balanced."

Types

Dry food, typically 6–10% moisture, is convenient and shelf-stable and may help reduce tartar. Wet food, around 75% moisture, supports hydration and urinary health but spoils quickly once opened. Therapeutic and veterinary diets are formulated for conditions such as chronic kidney disease, urinary crystals, obesity and food sensitivities. Kitten, adult and senior formulations differ in energy density and mineral content.

History and safety

Commercial cat food began in the nineteenth century with products such as Spratt's cat food in the 1860s and expanded greatly after the Second World War. The industry is periodically affected by safety incidents, most notably the 2007 pet food recalls involving melamine-contaminated ingredients, which prompted stricter testing and traceability.
