Risk-weighted asset

Risk-weighted assets (RWA) are a bank's assets and off-balance-sheet exposures, weighted according to their credit risk, used to determine the minimum amount of capital that a bank must hold under the Basel Accords. Each asset class is multiplied by a risk weight reflecting the creditworthiness of the counterparty and the nature of the exposure; the resulting total is the denominator of the capital adequacy ratio, which must exceed regulatory minima (8% total capital under Basel rules, with higher-quality Common Equity Tier 1 capital requirements beneath it).

Approaches under Basel II and III

Under the standardised approach, supervisors set fixed risk weights based on asset class and, where available, external credit ratings: for example, claims on highly rated sovereigns may attract a 0% weight, residential mortgages a reduced weight, and ordinary corporate loans 100%. Under the internal ratings-based (IRB) approaches, banks with supervisory approval use their own models of probability of default, loss given default, and exposure at default to compute weights. Basel III added capital buffers, a leverage ratio backstop (based on unweighted assets), and liquidity standards, partly in response to evidence that modeled risk weights had become implausibly low before the 2008 financial crisis.

RWA density and criticism

The ratio of RWA to total assets ("RWA density") varies widely between banks and fell for many large banks before 2008, as institutions optimized portfolios and models against the rules. Critics argue that risk weights invite regulatory arbitrage — for example, loading up on zero-weighted sovereign debt or securitizations that carried low weights but high actual risk, as with pre-crisis mortgage securities — and that opaque internal models undermine comparability. Basel III's output floor limits the benefit banks can obtain from internal models relative to the standardised approach.

Operational and market risk

Total RWA also includes amounts for market risk (in trading books) and operational risk, computed under their own standardized or modeled approaches, in addition to the dominant credit-risk component.
