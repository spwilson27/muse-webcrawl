Colony of Virginia

The Colony of Virginia was a British colony in North America chartered in 1606 and established with the founding of Jamestown in 1607, the first permanent English settlement in the Americas. After the Virginia Company of London was dissolved in 1624, Virginia became a royal colony governed by a crown-appointed governor and the elected House of Burgesses, the first representative legislative assembly in British America (established 1619). The colony became the largest and one of the wealthiest of the Thirteen Colonies and played a central role in the American Revolution.

Early years

Jamestown's first years were marked by disease, food shortages and conflict with the Powhatan Confederacy, including the "Starving Time" winter of 1609–10. The colony was saved economically by John Rolfe's cultivation of a marketable strain of tobacco, which became Virginia's staple export and drove demand for land and labour. In 1619 the first enslaved Africans arrived in Virginia, beginning the institution of slavery that would dominate the colony's plantation economy.

Government and society

The General Assembly, comprising the governor, his council and the House of Burgesses, met in Jamestown and, after 1699, in Williamsburg. Society was headed by a planter gentry whose wealth rested on tobacco and enslaved labour; below them were small farmers, indentured servants and a growing enslaved population. The frontier repeatedly erupted in conflict, most notably Bacon's Rebellion in 1676.

Revolution and statehood

Virginians such as George Washington, Thomas Jefferson, Patrick Henry and James Madison were leaders of the independence movement. In 1776 Virginia declared independence, adopted a declaration of rights and a state constitution, and in 1788 ratified the United States Constitution, becoming the tenth state.
