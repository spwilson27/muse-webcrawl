Pineal gland

The pineal gland (also known as the pineal body, conarium, or epiphysis cerebri) is a small endocrine gland in the brain of most vertebrates. It produces melatonin, a serotonin-derived hormone that modulates sleep patterns in both circadian and seasonal cycles. The shape of the gland resembles a pine cone, from which it derives its name. The pineal gland is located in the epithalamus, near the centre of the brain, between the two hemispheres, tucked in a groove where the two halves of the thalamus join.

Structure

The pineal gland is a reddish-gray body about the size of a grain of rice (5–8 mm) in humans. It is part of the epithalamus and lies between the laterally positioned thalamic bodies and behind the habenular commissure. The gland is primarily composed of pinealocytes, a type of highly modified photoreceptor cell, surrounded by neuroglial cells.

Unlike much of the rest of the mammalian brain, the pineal gland is not isolated from the body by the blood–brain barrier system; it has profuse blood flow, second only to the kidney, supplied by the choroidal branches of the posterior cerebral artery.

Function

The primary function of the pineal gland is to receive information about the state of the light–dark cycle from the environment and to convey this information through the production and secretion of the hormone melatonin. Melatonin is secreted at night and suppressed by light, playing a central role in regulating circadian rhythms such as the sleep–wake cycle.

In other vertebrates, the pineal organ can be directly photosensitive; in some species a parietal eye on top of the head is associated with the gland. In humans, light information reaches the pineal gland indirectly, via the retina, the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, and the sympathetic nervous system.

History and philosophy

The pineal gland was described by Galen in the 3rd century AD. René Descartes famously regarded it as the "seat of the soul" and the point where the mind interacts with the body, a view now considered historically interesting but scientifically obsolete.

Clinical significance

Tumours of the pineal gland, such as pinealomas and germinomas, can compress surrounding structures, causing hydrocephalus and Parinaud's syndrome. Calcification of the gland ("brain sand", or corpora arenacea) increases with age and is frequently used as a landmark in skull radiographs.
