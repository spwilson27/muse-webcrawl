Pituitary gland

LocationSella turcica, base of the brain
SizeAbout the size of a pea (c. 1 cm)
SystemEndocrine system
PrecursorRathke's pouch; infundibulum

The pituitary gland, or hypophysis, is a pea-sized endocrine gland that hangs from the base of the brain, beneath the hypothalamus, in a bony hollow called the sella turcica. Despite its small size, it is often called the "master gland" because its hormones regulate growth, metabolism, reproduction, stress responses and the activity of other endocrine glands including the thyroid, adrenal glands and gonads.

Structure and hormones

The gland has two main lobes of different embryological origin. The anterior pituitary (adenohypophysis) synthesises and releases growth hormone, prolactin, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinising hormone (LH), under the control of releasing hormones from the hypothalamus carried by a portal blood system. The posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis) stores and releases oxytocin and vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone), which are actually made in the hypothalamus and transported down nerve fibres.

Disorders

Pituitary tumours, most often benign adenomas, can overproduce hormones—causing acromegaly (excess growth hormone), Cushing's disease (excess ACTH) or prolactinoma—or press on the optic chiasm, producing characteristic visual field defects. Underactivity of the gland, hypopituitarism, may follow tumours, surgery, radiation or injury, and requires hormone replacement. Because the gland sits near vital structures, its surgery is a specialised branch of neurosurgery, often performed through the nose by the transsphenoidal route.
