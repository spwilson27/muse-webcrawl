ClassificationMeson, boson

A pion, also called a pi meson, is a subatomic particle belonging to the meson family, composed of one quark and one antiquark. There are three pions—positively charged, neutral and negatively charged—and they are the lightest of all mesons, a fact that makes them fundamental to understanding the strong nuclear force at low energies.

Role in nuclear forces

Hideki Yukawa proposed in 1935 that the force holding the atomic nucleus together is carried by particles of intermediate mass—between the electron and the proton—just as electromagnetism is carried by photons. Virtual pion exchange is now understood to describe the long-range part of the residual strong force between protons and neutrons. In modern quantum chromodynamics, pions are special because they are the pseudo-Nambu–Goldstone bosons arising from the spontaneous breaking of chiral symmetry, which explains why they are so much lighter than other hadrons.

Discovery and decays

Charged pions were identified in cosmic rays in 1947 by the Bristol group of Cecil Powell, with César Lattes and Giuseppe Occhialini; the neutral pion was found in accelerator experiments in 1950. A charged pion lives about 26 nanoseconds before decaying, almost always to a muon plus a neutrino—the discovery path that led to the muon neutrino—while the neutral pion decays within about 10⁻¹⁶ seconds into two photons. Pions produced in the upper atmosphere are the parents of most cosmic-ray muons reaching the ground.
