Robert Burns (25 January 1759 – 21 July 1796), also known familiarly as Rabbie Burns, was a Scottish poet and lyricist. He is widely regarded as the national poet of Scotland and is celebrated worldwide. He is the best known of the poets who wrote in the Scots language, although much of his writing is in a "light Scots dialect" of English that was accessible to audiences beyond Scotland. His poem (and song) "Auld Lang Syne" is often sung at Hogmanay (New Year), and "Scots Wha Hae" served for a long time as an unofficial national anthem of Scotland.

Life

Burns was born in Alloway, Ayrshire, the eldest son of a tenant farmer. He worked the land while composing verse, and the success of his first collection, Poems, Chiefly in the Scottish Dialect (the "Kilmarnock volume", 1786), made him famous across Scotland. After a triumphant winter in Edinburgh he combined farming at Ellisland with collecting and writing songs for The Scots Musical Museum and A Select Collection of Original Scottish Airs, preserving hundreds of traditional songs. From 1789 he worked as an excise officer in Dumfries, where he died at only 37, of a heart condition likely aggravated by his hard early life.

Works and themes

Burns wrote narrative poems such as "Tam o' Shanter", satires on the Kirk such as "Holy Willie's Prayer", love songs including "Ae Fond Kiss" and "A Red, Red Rose", and egalitarian anthems like "A Man's a Man for A' That". His sympathy for the poor and his use of vernacular speech influenced Romantic poets including Wordsworth and Coleridge, and later writers and political movements around the world.

Legacy

Burns suppers are held each year around his birthday, 25 January, featuring haggis, whisky and recitations. His birthday is a cultural institution of the Scottish diaspora, and statues of Burns stand in towns across Scotland, North America, Australia and New Zealand.
