Dualism in cosmology

The taijitu, symbol of complementary opposites in Chinese cosmology

Dualism in cosmology or dualistic cosmology is the moral or spiritual belief that reality is structured around two fundamental, often opposed, concepts or powers. Such systems teach that the universe, or important parts of it, can be explained by the interaction, conflict or balance of two principles — commonly light and darkness, good and evil, spirit and matter, or order and chaos.

Religious traditions

Zoroastrianism presents perhaps the most influential cosmological dualism: the wise lord Ahura Mazda and the destructive spirit Angra Mainyu (Ahriman) are locked in a cosmic struggle in which humanity participates through good thoughts, good words and good deeds. Manichaeism, founded by the prophet Mani in the 3rd century CE, radicalized this into two co-eternal kingdoms of Light and Darkness whose mixture constitutes the material world. Gnosticism in various forms opposed a transcendent, good God to a flawed creator (the Demiurge) responsible for the material universe.

Chinese cosmology

In Chinese thought, yin and yang describe complementary rather than antagonistic forces: dark and light, receptive and active, whose dynamic balance generates the "ten thousand things". Unlike Western religious dualisms, neither pole is morally evil, and each contains the seed of the other, as shown in the taijitu symbol.

Other forms

Pairs such as sky father and earth mother, the Dioscuri and similar divine twins in Indo-European myth, and the moieties of Australian Aboriginal cosmology reflect softer, complementary dualisms. Philosophers also discuss mind–body dualism, though that is usually classified under philosophy of mind rather than cosmology.

Criticism and interpretation

Scholars of religion caution that "dualism" covers a spectrum from radical, absolute dualism (two equal, eternal powers) to moderate or monotheistic dualism, in which the evil power is created and ultimately subordinate. The term has sometimes been applied loosely by outside observers to traditions that do not understand themselves in those terms.
