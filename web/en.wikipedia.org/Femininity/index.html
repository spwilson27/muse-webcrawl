Femininity

Femininity (also called womanliness) is a set of attributes, behaviors, and roles generally associated with women and girls. Femininity is socially constructed, but made up of both socially-defined and biologically-influenced factors, which makes it distinct from the simple definition of the biological female sex.

Characteristics

Traits traditionally cited as feminine vary across cultures and historical periods, but in many Western contexts they have included gentleness, empathy, sensitivity, nurturance, and an emphasis on appearance and grace. Norms of feminine dress and grooming — such as long hair, makeup, and dresses — likewise differ between societies and eras; in some periods or cultures, ornaments and cosmetics have been associated with men instead.

Scholarly perspectives

Feminist scholars such as Simone de Beauvoir argued that femininity is not innate but learned: in The Second Sex (1949), she wrote that "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." Judith Butler's theory of gender performativity similarly holds that gender is produced through repeated performances. Other researchers examine how biology and socialization interact, and cross-cultural studies document femininities that differ substantially from Western norms.
