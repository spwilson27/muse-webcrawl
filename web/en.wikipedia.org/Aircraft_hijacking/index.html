Aircraft hijacking

Aircraft hijacking (also known as skyjacking) is the unlawful seizure of an aircraft by an individual or group, typically to divert it to a destination not on its itinerary, to take hostages for political or financial demands, or — as in the September 11 attacks of 2001 — to use the aircraft itself as a weapon. Hijacking is a serious crime under international conventions agreed at Tokyo (1963), The Hague (1970), and Montreal (1971).

History

The first recorded hijacking occurred in 1931 in Peru. Between 1968 and 1972 more than 300 hijackings took place worldwide, many involving U.S. airliners diverted to Cuba. In 1970, the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine simultaneously hijacked several airliners and blew them up at Dawson's Field in Jordan. The 1976 hijacking of Air France Flight 139 ended with the Israeli raid on Entebbe Airport in Uganda. The introduction of universal passenger and baggage screening in the 1970s sharply reduced incidents in the West.

After September 11

The attacks of 11 September 2001, in which four U.S. airliners were seized and flown into the World Trade Center, the Pentagon, and a field in Pennsylvania, transformed aviation security: reinforced, locked cockpit doors became mandatory; passenger resistance replaced crew compliance as the assumed response; and new agencies such as the U.S. Transportation Security Administration were created. Successful hijackings have since become rare.

Notable cases

The 1971 case of "D. B. Cooper," who parachuted from a Northwest Orient 727 with ransom money and was never identified, remains the only unsolved air piracy case in U.S. history.
