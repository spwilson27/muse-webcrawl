Meteor Crater, also known as Barringer Crater, is a meteorite impact crater about 37 miles (60 km) east of Flagstaff and 18 miles (29 km) west of Winslow in the desert of northern Arizona, United States. It is about 3,900 feet (1,200 m) in diameter and 560 feet (170 m) deep, surrounded by a rim that rises 148 feet (45 m) above the surrounding plain. The crater was created about 50,000 years ago during the Pleistocene epoch, when the local climate was cooler and damper, by the impact of an iron meteorite roughly 160 feet (50 m) across travelling at several kilometres per second. The impact energy has been estimated at roughly 10 megatons.

Discovery and the Barringer controversy

European settlers learned of the crater in the nineteenth century. In 1903 the mining engineer and businessman Daniel Moreau Barringer became convinced that the crater was produced by the impact of a large iron meteorite, whose mass — potentially worth a fortune in iron and nickel — he believed was buried beneath the crater floor. He spent 26 years and a fortune mining for it, but the main mass had largely vaporised and dispersed on impact. Mainstream geologists of the day, notably G. K. Gilbert, favoured a volcanic steam explosion. Barringer's impact hypothesis was vindicated decisively in 1960 when Eugene Shoemaker's research demonstrated similarities between the crater and nuclear test craters, and identified the high-pressure silica mineral coesite — the discovery that effectively founded the science of impact cratering.

Geology and present day

The crater cuts through Permian and Triassic sedimentary rocks (Kaibab limestone and Moenkopi sandstone). Because the Arizona climate has remained dry, the crater has suffered little erosion and is the best-preserved impact crater on Earth. It is privately owned by the Barringer family through the Barringer Crater Company and operated as a tourist attraction with a visitor centre and museum. During the 1960s, NASA astronauts trained at the crater to prepare for the Apollo missions to the Moon. Designated a National Natural Landmark, it remains an active site for planetary science research and education.
