The greenhouse effect is the process by which greenhouse gases in a planet's atmosphere trap some of the outgoing thermal radiation from its surface, raising the surface temperature above what it would be without an atmosphere. On Earth, incoming shortwave solar radiation passes largely through the atmosphere and warms the surface; the surface re-radiates this energy as longwave infrared. Greenhouse gases — principally water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and ozone — absorb infrared at specific wavelengths and re-emit it in all directions, including back downward. The natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth's average surface temperature around 15 °C instead of the −18 °C it would otherwise be. Human increases in greenhouse gas concentrations since the Industrial Revolution have strengthened the effect, producing the observed global warming.

Mechanism

The effect does not work exactly like a glass greenhouse (which mainly warms by suppressing convection); rather, the atmosphere is selectively transparent: relatively clear to visible sunlight but partly opaque to infrared. Energy balance is restored when the planet warms enough that outgoing radiation at the top of the atmosphere again equals absorbed sunlight. Water vapour is the largest single contributor to the effect, but acts mainly as a feedback that amplifies changes initiated by other factors; carbon dioxide, though less abundant, is the principal long-lived "control knob" because it does not condense out of the atmosphere.

History

Joseph Fourier described the atmosphere's insulating role in the 1820s. Eunice Newton Foote demonstrated in 1856 that carbon dioxide and water vapour trap solar heat; John Tyndall measured the infrared absorption of gases from 1859; and Svante Arrhenius calculated in 1896 that halving or doubling atmospheric CO₂ would change global temperature by several degrees. Guy Stewart Callendar revived the idea in 1938, Charles David Keeling began precise CO₂ measurements at Mauna Loa in 1958, and by the 1980s–2000s attribution studies and IPCC assessments established that human activity dominates recent warming.

On other planets

Venus exhibits an extreme form: its dense CO₂ atmosphere produces a surface temperature of about 464 °C, often described as a runaway greenhouse effect. Mars has a weak greenhouse effect because its atmosphere is thin. Titan, Saturn's moon, has a greenhouse effect from hydrogen, nitrogen and methane, along with an "anti-greenhouse" haze layer.

Anthropogenic enhancement

Burning fossil fuels, deforestation, agriculture and industry have raised CO₂ concentrations from about 280 ppm pre-industrially to over 420 ppm, methane from about 700 ppb to over 1,900 ppb, with corresponding increases in other gases. This has produced an energy imbalance of roughly 0.5–1 W/m² and about 1.2 °C of warming since the late 19th century, with impacts including sea-level rise, shifting precipitation and more frequent heat extremes.
