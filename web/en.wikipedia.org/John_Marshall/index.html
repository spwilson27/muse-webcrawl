John Marshall (24 September 1755 – 6 July 1835) was an American statesman, lawyer, and Founding Father who served as the fourth Chief Justice of the United States from 1801 until his death in 1835. He remains the longest-serving Chief Justice and the third longest-serving justice in Supreme Court history.

Early life and career

Marshall was born in a log cabin in Fauquier County, Virginia, the eldest of fifteen children. He served in the Continental Army during the Revolutionary War, enduring the winter at Valley Forge, and afterwards read law and was admitted to the Virginia bar in 1780. He rose quickly in Virginia politics as a Federalist, defended the new Constitution at the Virginia ratifying convention of 1788, and served in the state legislature. In 1797 President John Adams appointed him to the XYZ mission to France; in 1799 he was elected to the US House of Representatives, and in 1800 Adams made him Secretary of State.

Chief Justice

Appointed Chief Justice in January 1801, Marshall transformed the Supreme Court into a co-equal branch of government. In Marbury v. Madison (1803) the Court asserted the power of judicial review. Under Marshall, the Court strengthened the federal government and the contract clause in cases including McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), which upheld the national bank and denied states the power to tax it; Dartmouth College v. Woodward (1819); Cohens v. Virginia (1821); and Gibbons v. Ogden (1824), which broadly defined Congress's commerce power. He also presided over the treason trial of Aaron Burr in 1807.

Legacy

Marshall's jurisprudence established the judiciary as the authoritative interpreter of the Constitution and laid foundations of American constitutional law. His five-volume biography of George Washington (1804–1807) was also widely read. He died in Philadelphia in 1835, having served on the Court for over 34 years.
