The Frankfurt Constitution (German: Frankfurter Verfassung), also known as the Paulskirchenverfassung after St. Paul's Church where it was debated, was the constitution drafted by the Frankfurt National Assembly during the German revolutions of 1848–49. Adopted on 28 March 1849, it was the first democratic constitution for a united Germany, but it never came into force because the princes of the larger German states refused to accept it.

Background

Following the March Revolution of 1848, elections were held across the German Confederation for a National Assembly, which convened in Frankfurt's St. Paul's Church on 18 May 1848. The assembly's task was to create a unified German nation-state with a constitutional order guaranteeing civil rights.

Content

The constitution provided for a federal empire headed by a hereditary emperor, a bicameral legislature (the Reichstag) with a popularly elected lower house (Volkshaus) chosen by universal male suffrage, and responsible government. Its catalogue of fundamental rights (Grundrechte) — freedom of the press, speech, assembly, and religion, equality before the law, and abolition of noble privilege — was remarkably progressive for its time and later served as a model for the Weimar Constitution of 1919 and the Basic Law of 1949.

Failure

The assembly offered the imperial crown to Frederick William IV of Prussia, who refused it, dismissing a crown offered by an elected assembly. Other major states also withheld recognition, the assembly's authority collapsed amid armed resistance and counter-revolution, and its remnants were dispersed in Stuttgart in June 1849.

Legacy

Although a political failure, the Frankfurt Constitution became a foundational document of German democratic tradition. Its fundamental-rights catalogue influenced later German constitutions, and the Basic Law of the Federal Republic deliberately continued the Paulskirche tradition a century later.
