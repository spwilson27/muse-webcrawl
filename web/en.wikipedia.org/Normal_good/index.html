Normal good

In economics, a normal good is a type of good for which demand increases as consumer income rises. This is the opposite of an inferior good, for which demand falls as income rises. A good is normal when its income elasticity of demand is positive.

[Image placeholder: Engel curve showing demand for a normal good rising with income]

Analysis

When income increases, a consumer's budget constraint shifts outward, and for a normal good the quantity purchased at any given price increases — the demand curve shifts to the right. Most goods are normal in most circumstances: examples commonly given include restaurant meals, clothing, and consumer electronics, though whether a good is normal or inferior depends on the income range and the consumer considered.

Within normal goods a further distinction is sometimes made between necessities, whose income elasticity is positive but less than one (so their budget share falls as income grows), and luxuries, whose income elasticity exceeds one. The relationship between income and demand for a normal good is illustrated by an upward-sloping Engel curve.
