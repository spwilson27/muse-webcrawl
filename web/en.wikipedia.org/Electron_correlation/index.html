Electronic correlation

Electronic correlation (or electron correlation) is the interaction between electrons in a quantum system beyond what is captured by a mean-field description. In the Hartree–Fock method, each electron moves in the average field of the others; the difference between the exact non-relativistic energy of a system and its Hartree–Fock limit is defined as the correlation energy (a definition due to Per-Olov Löwdin). Because electrons repel instantaneously, their motions are correlated — each electron keeps away from the others more than the average field suggests.

Types of correlation

Dynamic correlation arises from the instantaneous avoidance of electrons, especially those of opposite spin sharing a region of space. Static (nondynamic) correlation arises when a single determinant is a poor starting point — for example in bond dissociation or near-degenerate states — and several configurations have comparable importance. Fermi correlation between same-spin electrons is already contained in Hartree–Fock through the exchange interaction and the Pauli principle, which create a "Fermi hole" around each electron; the remaining "Coulomb hole" is what correlation methods must recover.

Methods

Post-Hartree–Fock methods recover correlation energy with increasing sophistication: configuration interaction (CI), Møller–Plesset perturbation theory (MP2 and higher), coupled cluster theory (CCSD, CCSD(T) — often called the "gold standard"), and multi-configurational self-consistent field (MCSCF/CASSCF) for static correlation. In condensed-matter physics, strongly correlated systems — where electron-electron interactions dominate — include Mott insulators, heavy fermion compounds, and high-temperature superconductors, and are treated with methods such as dynamical mean-field theory.
