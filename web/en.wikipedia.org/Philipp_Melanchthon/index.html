Philipp Melanchthon, Luther's closest collaborator.

Philipp Melanchthon (born Philipp Schwartzerdt; 16 February 1497 – 19 April 1560) was a German Lutheran reformer, collaborator with Martin Luther, the first systematic theologian of the Protestant Reformation, intellectual leader of the Lutheran Reformation, and an influential designer of educational systems. He stands next to Luther and John Calvin as a reformer, theologian, and molder of Protestantism.

Early life and Wittenberg

A child prodigy and grand-nephew of the humanist Johann Reuchlin — who gave him the Greek name Melanchthon ("black earth," a translation of Schwartzerdt) — he studied at Heidelberg and Tübingen. In 1518, aged 21, he became professor of Greek at the University of Wittenberg, where his inaugural address on educational reform impressed Luther. The two became lifelong colleagues, Luther the charismatic prophet, Melanchthon the careful systematizer.

Theological works

His Loci communes (1521) was the first systematic treatment of Lutheran theology. Most famously, he drafted the Augsburg Confession (1530), the primary confession of faith of the Lutheran Church, presented to Emperor Charles V at the Diet of Augsburg, and its Apology (1531). Melanchthon participated in nearly every major religious colloquy of the era, seeking — to the suspicion of stricter Lutherans — formulas that might reconcile Protestants with Catholics and with each other.

Educator

Called Praeceptor Germaniae ("Teacher of Germany"), Melanchthon reformed curricula at universities and Latin schools across Germany, writing widely used textbooks on Greek and Latin grammar, rhetoric, dialectics, ethics and physics. His students spread his educational model throughout Protestant Europe.

Death and legacy

Melanchthon died in Wittenberg in 1560 and is buried beside Luther in the Castle Church. After his death, disputes between his followers (Philippists) and strict Lutherans (Gnesio-Lutherians) shaped Lutheran orthodoxy until the Formula of Concord (1577).
