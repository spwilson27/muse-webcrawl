The Reformation, also known as the Protestant Reformation, was a major movement within Western Christianity in 16th-century Europe that posed a religious and political challenge to the Catholic Church and papal authority. It is usually considered to have begun with Martin Luther's publication of the Ninety-five Theses in 1517, and to have ended with the Peace of Westphalia in 1648. The Reformation gave rise to Protestantism and the Lutheran, Reformed (Calvinist), Anglican and Anabaptist traditions, permanently dividing the Western Church.

Background

Calls for reform long predated Luther: John Wycliffe in England and Jan Hus in Bohemia attacked clerical corruption and were condemned, and Renaissance humanism, with its call to return to the sources (ad fontes), supplied tools for biblical criticism. By 1500 many resented the fiscal demands of the papal court, the sale of indulgences, simony and the worldly lives of some clergy. The printing press, spreading through Europe after c. 1450, ensured that criticism — and Luther's writings — could circulate faster than authorities could suppress them.

Luther and the German Reformation

Luther's protest against indulgences escalated between 1517 and 1521 into a theological programme: justification by faith alone (sola fide), Scripture alone as doctrinal authority (sola scriptura), and the priesthood of all believers. Excommunicated and outlawed at Worms, he was protected by the Elector of Saxony, and his reforms were adopted by many German princes and free cities. In 1529 the evangelical estates' formal "Protestation" at the Diet of Speyer gave "Protestant" its name; in 1530 Philipp Melanchthon presented the Augsburg Confession at Augsburg. The religious division of the Empire was provisionally settled by the Peace of Augsburg (1555) under the principle cuius regio, eius religio.

Other Reformations

In Switzerland, Huldrych Zwingli led a reform in Zürich from 1519 that diverged from Luther's over the Lord's Supper, while John Calvin's Geneva — organised around the Institutes of the Christian Religion (1536) — became the engine of the international Reformed tradition, carried to France, the Netherlands and Scotland, where John Knox led the Scottish Reformation of 1560. The Radical Reformation produced Anabaptists, who baptised adult believers and often rejected the state church altogether, sometimes violently as at Münster (1534–35). In England, Henry VIII's break with Rome (1534) created the Church of England, which took a more fully Protestant form under Edward VI and was settled under Elizabeth I.

Counter-Reformation and wars of religion

The Catholic Church responded with its own reform: the Council of Trent (1545–63) clarified doctrine and disciplined abuses, new orders such as the Jesuits drove education and missions, and Catholic states pressed back Protestant advances. Religious conflict fuelled a century of warfare — the French Wars of Religion, the Dutch Revolt, and the Thirty Years' War (1618–48), whose settlement at Westphalia confirmed the confessional map of Europe.

Consequences

The Reformation reshaped European life: vernacular Bibles and catechisms promoted literacy and standardised languages; church lands were secularised; state power over religion grew; and new confessional identities hardened. Its long-term effects on capitalism, individual conscience, science and political thought remain debated — from Max Weber's "Protestant ethic" thesis onward. Commemorations of the 500th anniversary in 2017, held jointly by Lutherans and Catholics in a new ecumenical spirit, marked how far interpretation of the Reformation has moved.
