The Augsburg Confession, also known as the Augustan Confession or Confessio Augustana, is the primary confession of faith of the Lutheran Church and one of the most important documents of the Protestant Reformation. It was presented by Lutheran princes and cities of the Holy Roman Empire to Emperor Charles V at the Diet of Augsburg on June 25, 1530, in an attempt to demonstrate that the Lutheran reforms restored, rather than broke with, the faith of the historic Catholic Church.

Background and authorship

Charles V summoned the Diet of Augsburg in 1530 hoping to settle the Empire's religious divisions so as to face the Ottoman threat. The Lutheran estates were required to present an account of their faith and of the reforms introduced in their territories. The confession was drafted principally by Philipp Melanchthon, the leading theologian of Wittenberg after Martin Luther, who, as an outlaw, could not attend Augsburg and remained at Coburg Castle, approving Melanchthon's draft in correspondence. The jurist Christian Beyer read the German text aloud to the emperor; the reading reportedly took two hours.

Contents

The Confession contains twenty-eight articles. The first twenty-one set out doctrines: the Trinity (I), original sin (II), Christ (III), and — centrally for Lutherans — justification by faith (IV), teaching that human beings "cannot be justified before God by their own strength, merits, or works, but are freely justified for Christ's sake, through faith." Further articles treat the ministry, the church, baptism, the Lord's Supper (affirming Christ's real presence), confession, and civil government. The last seven articles address disputed abuses: communion in both kinds, clerical marriage, the Mass, compulsory confession, monastic vows, fasting traditions and the power of bishops — arguing that these reforms were required by Scripture.

Response and aftermath

Roman Catholic theologians replied with the Confutatio Augustana, read at the Diet in August 1530; Melanchthon answered with the Apology of the Augsburg Confession (1531), an extended defence later included among the Lutheran confessions. Charles V rejected the Confession, but it quickly became the defining statement of Lutheran identity, subscribed by Lutheran churches and included in the Book of Concord (1580). Melanchthon's own later revised version, the Variata (1540), softened Article X on the Eucharist and became a source of controversy within Lutheranism.

Legacy

The Augsburg Confession remains the doctrinal standard of Lutheran churches worldwide; pastors in many Lutheran bodies vow fidelity to it at ordination. June 25 is commemorated in Lutheran calendars as the Presentation of the Augsburg Confession. Its irenic tone and method — stating agreement with the ancient church before addressing disputes — have also made it a reference point in modern ecumenical dialogue between Lutherans and Roman Catholics.
