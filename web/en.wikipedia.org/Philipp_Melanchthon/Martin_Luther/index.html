Martin Luther (November 10, 1483 – February 18, 1546) was a German priest, theologian, author, hymnwriter, professor and Augustinian friar. As the seminal figure of the Protestant Reformation, his teachings — above all that salvation is received by faith alone through God's grace alone, on the authority of Scripture alone — inspired the Lutheran churches and profoundly reshaped Western Christianity, German culture and the German language.

Early life and monastic career

Luther was born in Eisleben to Hans Luder, a leaseholder of copper mines and smelters, and his wife Margarethe. Educated at Magdeburg and Eisenach, he enrolled at the University of Erfurt in 1501, earning a master's degree in 1505. Against his father's wish that he study law, he entered the Augustinian friary at Erfurt after vowing to St. Anne during a thunderstorm. He was ordained in 1507 and, after a formative trip to Rome, transferred to Wittenberg, where he earned a doctorate in theology in 1512 and became professor of biblical studies at the university. His lectures on the Psalms, Romans and Galatians developed the theological insights — forged in his own "tower experience" of finding a merciful God in Romans 1:17 — that led to the Reformation.

The Ninety-five Theses and the breach with Rome

On October 31, 1517, Luther sent his Disputation on the Power and Efficacy of Indulgences — the Ninety-five Theses — to Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz, protesting the sale of indulgences preached by Johann Tetzel. Tradition holds that he also posted them on the door of Wittenberg's Castle Church. Printed and translated, the theses spread rapidly. Years of controversy followed: debates at Heidelberg (1518) and Leipzig (1519), where Johann Eck drove Luther to deny the infallibility of popes and councils; the papal bull Exsurge Domine (1520), which Luther burned publicly; and his defiant refusal to recant at the Diet of Worms in 1521 ("Here I stand, I can do no other"). Declared an outlaw, he was hidden by Elector Frederick the Wise in the Wartburg Castle, where he translated the New Testament into German.

Builder of a church

Luther returned to Wittenberg in 1522 to steer reform against radicals such as Andreas Karlstadt and the prophets of Zwickau, and he opposed the Peasants' War of 1525 in harsh terms. The same year he published On the Bondage of the Will against Erasmus, and married Katharina von Bora, a former nun, establishing a model of Protestant clerical family life. He organised the Saxon churches through visitations, wrote the Large and Small Catechisms (1529), hymns including "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God," and championed universal education. In 1530 his colleague Philipp Melanchthon presented the Augsburg Confession setting out the Lutheran faith. Luther's complete Bible translation appeared in 1534; its language helped standardise written German.

Later years and death

Luther's last decades were marked by ceaseless writing, preaching and teaching, deteriorating health, and polemics of increasing harshness — notoriously including the anti-Jewish tract On the Jews and Their Lies (1543), whose vitriol later anti-Semites exploited and which Lutheran churches have formally repudiated. He died in Eisleben, his birthplace, in February 1546 while mediating a dispute between the counts of Mansfeld, and was buried in Wittenberg's Castle Church.

Legacy

Lutheranism spread through Germany and Scandinavia and became one of the major traditions of Christianity; the Reformation he opened transformed European religion, politics, education and print culture. Luther is commemorated on February 18 in Lutheran and Anglican calendars, and his influence on music (through the chorale tradition taken up by Bach), language and ideas of conscience remains a subject of extensive scholarship.
