St Kilda (Scottish Gaelic: Hiort) is a remote archipelago situated 64 kilometres (40 mi) west-northwest of North Uist in the North Atlantic Ocean. It contains the westernmost islands of the Outer Hebrides of Scotland. The largest island is Hirta, whose sea cliffs are the highest in the United Kingdom. Three other islands — Dùn, Soay, and Boreray — were also used for grazing and seabird hunting. The islands are administratively part of the Comhairle nan Eilean Siar local authority area.

History

St Kilda was continuously inhabited for at least two millennia, probably from the Bronze Age. The islanders lived by exploiting the archipelago's enormous seabird colonies — gannets, fulmars, and puffins — climbing the cliffs to harvest birds, eggs, and feathers, and keeping Soay sheep. The population, never much above 200, was sustained by a communal, strongly religious society, but during the 19th and early 20th centuries emigration, disease, the First World War, and declining self-sufficiency eroded numbers. On 29 August 1930 the remaining 36 residents were evacuated to the mainland at their own request, ending the permanent habitation of the islands.

Natural history and conservation

St Kilda is a breeding ground for many important seabird species, including the world's largest colony of northern gannets (on Boreray and the stacs) and up to 30,000 puffins. The endemic St Kilda field mouse and St Kilda wren survive there, as does the primitive Soay sheep. The archipelago was bequeathed to the National Trust for Scotland in 1957 and designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1986 for its natural heritage; the listing was extended in 2004 to include cultural heritage, making it one of the few "mixed" sites worldwide. A small military radar tracking station on Hirta, staffed by contractors, gives the islands a small seasonal population.
