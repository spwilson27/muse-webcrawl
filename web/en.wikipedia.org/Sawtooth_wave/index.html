Sawtooth wave

The sawtooth wave (or saw wave) is a kind of non-sinusoidal waveform named for its resemblance to the teeth of a plain-toothed saw with a zero rake angle. It ramps linearly upward (or downward) and then drops sharply back, repeating periodically.

Harmonic content

A sawtooth wave contains all integer harmonics — both odd and even — of the fundamental frequency, with amplitudes falling off as 1/n. This makes it the richest of the common synthesis waveforms and a staple of subtractive synthesis for imitating brass and string sounds. It can be constructed by additive synthesis by summing sine waves at every harmonic.

Applications

Sawtooth waveforms are used in music synthesis, in the deflection circuits of older CRT displays and oscilloscopes (where the ramp sweeps the beam and the sharp drop is the retrace), in phase-locked loops, and as test signals. A reversed sawtooth is sometimes called a ramp wave.
