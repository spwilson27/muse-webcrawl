Plunderphonics builds new music from conspicuously "plundered" existing recordings.

Plunderphonics is a music genre and compositional approach in which new works are constructed entirely from samples of existing recordings, typically left clearly recognisable. The term was coined by Canadian composer John Oswald in his 1985 essay "Plunderphonics, or Audio Piracy as a Compositional Prerogative", arguing that once a recording is published, listeners' transformation of it is a legitimate creative act.

Practice

Oswald's works manipulate famous songs by slowing, accelerating, slicing and layering them: his track "Dab", from Michael Jackson's "Bad", progressively dissects the recording, and the 1993 Plexure compressed fragments of a thousand pop songs into brief, dense collages. His 1989 album Plunderphonics, distributed free to radio stations and libraries, was withdrawn and its remaining copies destroyed after legal threats from the Canadian Recording Industry Association over the cover art and the Michael Jackson track — an episode that made the work a landmark in debates about copyright and sampling.

Influence

Plunderphonics anticipated and influenced sample-based genres: the mashup, the Avalanches' Since I Left You (built from an estimated 3,500 samples), vaporwave's slowed pop collages, and much hip-hop production practice. Oswald distinguished plunderphonics from generic sampling by insisting the source remain audible — the listener should recognise the plundered original — making the technique a form of commentary as much as composition. Debates it raised about ownership, fair use and derivative works continue in copyright scholarship.
