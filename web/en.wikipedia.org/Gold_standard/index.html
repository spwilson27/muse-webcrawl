The gold standard is a monetary system in which the standard unit of currency is defined as a fixed quantity of gold, and notes and deposits are freely convertible into gold at that rate. Under a full international gold standard, exchange rates between participating currencies are fixed by their respective gold parities.

History

Britain moved onto gold de facto when Isaac Newton's mint price of 1717 overvalued gold relative to silver, and de jure in the nineteenth century. By the 1870s most major economies — Germany, France, the United States and others — had joined, creating the "classical gold standard" that coincided with the first great era of globalisation. Its self-adjusting mechanism, described by David Hume's price–specie flow model, balanced trade through gold movements.

The First World War suspended convertibility. Interwar attempts to restore gold, notably Britain's return in 1925 at the pre-war parity, contributed to deflation and were abandoned during the Great Depression — Britain in 1931, the United States (for domestic purposes) in 1933.

Bretton Woods and collapse

The Bretton Woods system of 1944 created a gold-exchange standard: currencies were pegged to the US dollar, itself convertible into gold at $35 per troy ounce for foreign governments. Mounting deficits led President Nixon to suspend convertibility in 1971 (the "Nixon shock"), and by 1973 the world had moved to floating fiat currencies. No country uses a gold standard today, though economists continue to debate its merits in limiting inflation versus its deflationary rigidity.
