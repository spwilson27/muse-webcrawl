Station wagon

A station wagon (also called an estate car in British English) is a car body style with a two-box design: a passenger cabin that extends to the rear of the vehicle, a large cargo area behind the seats, and an upright rear hatch or tailgate instead of a separate boot (trunk). Station wagons are built on ordinary passenger-car platforms and share their sedan counterparts' handling and fuel economy while offering van-like carrying capacity.

History

The name derives from depot hacks — vehicles that carried passengers and luggage between railway stations and hotels. Early station wagons of the 1920s–1940s had bodies framed in wood ("woodies"), built by coachbuilders on car chassis. After the Second World War all-steel wagons such as the Jeep Wagoneer and the big American family wagons of the 1950s–70s made the type the quintessential suburban family car in the United States, often with rear-facing third-row seats and simulated wood panelling.

Decline and survival

From the 1980s the minivan and later the SUV and crossover displaced the wagon in North America, where the style nearly vanished. In Europe, by contrast, estates from manufacturers such as Volvo, Audi, BMW, Mercedes-Benz and Škoda remained a major segment, prized for combining load space with car-like driving dynamics. Recent years have seen niche revivals in the form of raised "all-road" and performance wagons.

Characteristics

Defining features include a roof that extends at full height to the rear, split folding rear seats, a tailgate that may open upward or in two sections, and often roof rails. Shooting brake models are a sportier, lower-roofed relative of the wagon.
