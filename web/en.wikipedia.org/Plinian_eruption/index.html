Plinian eruptions (or Vesuvian eruptions) are volcanic eruptions characterised by enormous, sustained columns of gas and ash rising tens of kilometres into the stratosphere. They are named for Pliny the Younger, whose letters describe the AD 79 eruption of Mount Vesuvius that destroyed Pompeii and Herculaneum; he compared the column to a Mediterranean pine tree.

Characteristics

Plinian eruptions involve viscous, gas-rich magma — usually rhyolite or dacite — that fragments violently as it ascends. The eruption column, driven by thermal buoyancy, may rise 20 to 45 kilometres; where it collapses, it generates devastating pyroclastic flows and surges. Ash falls can blanket thousands of square kilometres, and sulphate aerosols injected into the stratosphere can cool the global climate. Eruptions of this type typically rate VEI 4 to 6; the largest, called ultra-Plinian, reach VEI 7–8 and may empty the magma chamber enough to form a caldera.

Examples

Historical Plinian eruptions include Vesuvius in AD 79, Mount St. Helens in 1980 (VEI 5), El Chichón in 1982, Mount Pinatubo in 1991 (VEI 6, which measurably cooled the planet), and Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai in 2022. Prehistoric ultra-Plinian events include the eruptions of Tambora (1815, VEI 7, the largest in recorded history) and the supervolcano eruptions of Yellowstone and Toba.

Hazards

Principal hazards are ash fall (collapsing roofs, destroying crops, disrupting aviation), pyroclastic density currents, lahars, volcanic gases and, for coastal volcanoes, tsunamis. Because Plinian eruptions are relatively infrequent but catastrophic, they are a central concern of volcano monitoring and civil protection.
