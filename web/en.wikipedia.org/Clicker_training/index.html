Clicker training

Clicker training
Method: Operant conditioning with a marker signal
Key figures: B. F. Skinner (theory); Keller and Marian Breland; Karen Pryor (popularized)
Applies to: Dogs, dolphins, horses, zoo animals

Clicker training is a method of animal training that uses positive reinforcement together with a distinctive sound — typically the click of a small handheld device called a clicker — to mark the exact moment an animal performs a desired behaviour. The click acts as a conditioned reinforcer (a "bridge" or marker signal) because it has been repeatedly paired with a primary reward such as food; it tells the animal precisely which action earned the reward, even if the reward follows a moment later.

History

The method derives from B. F. Skinner's work on operant conditioning and was first applied professionally by Keller and Marian Breland, former students of Skinner, who trained many species for commercial work from the 1940s. Whistle markers were long used in dolphin training. Behaviourist and author Karen Pryor popularized the clicker for dog training with her 1985 book Don't Shoot the Dog! and subsequent works; the practice spread widely among pet owners, assistance-dog programs, zoos, and marine mammal trainers.

Method

Training begins by "charging" or "loading" the clicker — clicking and immediately offering a treat until the animal associates the sound with reward. The trainer then clicks at the instant the animal performs the target behaviour and follows with the reward. Techniques include capturing (waiting for the behaviour), luring, and shaping (reinforcing successive approximations). Once a behaviour is reliable, a verbal cue is added, and the clicker and treats are gradually faded. Because it relies on reinforcement rather than punishment, clicker training is considered a humane, force-free approach.
