Manorialism

Manorialism, also known as seigneurialism, was the organizing principle of rural society and economy in medieval Europe. It described the system by which peasants were dependent on their lord and his manor — a large estate with its own jurisdiction — rendering labour, dues and services in return for land and protection. Manorialism is sometimes described as an element of feudalism, though feudalism properly concerns relations among the military aristocracy, whereas manorialism governed relations between lords and peasants.

Structure of the manor

A manor typically comprised the lord's demesne, worked directly for his household; peasant holdings, some held by free tenants and others by unfree villeins or serfs; and common resources such as pasture, woodland and mills. Peasants owed week-work on the demesne, extra services at harvest ("boon work"), rents in kind or money, and payments such as heriot and merchet. The lord's manorial court settled disputes and regulated agriculture.

Origins and spread

Manorial arrangements developed from the late Roman villa system and the conditions of the early Middle Ages, spreading widely in France, England and western Germany between the 10th and 13th centuries. East of the Elbe, a harsher "second serfdom" arose later, in the early modern period.

Decline

From the 14th century, population collapse after the Black Death, the commutation of labour services into money rents, and the growth of markets eroded manorialism in Western Europe. Peasant revolts such as the English Rising of 1381 and the German Peasants' War of 1524–25 challenged its burdens. Seigneurial dues survived longest in France, where they were abolished during the Revolution in 1789.
