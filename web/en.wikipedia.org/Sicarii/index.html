The Sicarii were a group of Jewish assassins who were active in Judaea in the decades preceding the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE. They were an extremist splinter group of the Zealots and are sometimes described as among the earliest known organised assassins. Their name derives from the Latin sica, a small curved dagger, which they concealed under their cloaks.

The main source for the Sicarii is the historian Josephus, whose Jewish War and Antiquities of the Jews describe their assassinations, their role in the revolt against Rome, and their final stand at Masada in 73 or 74 CE.

Methods

The Sicarii murdered their enemies — Roman officials and Jews they considered collaborators or apostates — in broad daylight, preferably during festivals, when crowds in Jerusalem allowed them to mingle unnoticed, stab their victims with short daggers hidden in their garments, and then join in the cries of indignation of the onlookers to escape detection. Josephus reports that the high priest Jonathan was among their victims, and that the daily murders spread terror through Jerusalem in the 50s CE. They also kidnapped officials for ransom and destroyed the property of opponents.

Role in the revolt

At the outbreak of the First Jewish–Roman War in 66 CE, the Sicarii under Menahem ben Yehuda seized the fortress of Masada, massacring its Roman garrison, and entered Jerusalem. Menahem was soon killed by rival rebels, and his followers withdrew to Masada under his relative Eleazar ben Ya'ir. From Masada they raided surrounding settlements, including Ein Gedi, where Josephus accuses them of massacring the inhabitants.

Masada

After the fall of Jerusalem in 70 CE, Masada remained the last rebel stronghold. In 73/74 CE the Roman legate Flavius Silva besieged it with Legio X Fretensis and built an enormous assault ramp. According to Josephus, when the Romans breached the wall they found that the 960 defenders had killed themselves — the men killing their families and one another, with the last man falling on his sword — rather than submit to capture. Only two women and five children survived by hiding. The historicity of Josephus's account, especially the mass suicide narrative, is debated among scholars, but Masada became a powerful symbol in modern Israeli national memory.
