Centriole

Cellular component
Found inMost eukaryotic cells (animal cells)
StructureCylinder of nine triplet microtubules
Dimensions≈0.5 μm long, ≈0.2 μm diameter
Part ofCentrosome

In cell biology, a centriole is a cylindrical organelle composed mainly of a protein called tubulin. Centrioles are found in most eukaryotic cells, though they are absent from higher plants and most fungi. A bound pair of centrioles, surrounded by a shapeless mass of dense material called the pericentriolar material (PCM), forms a structure called a centrosome. The walls of each centriole are typically made of nine triplet microtubules arranged in a characteristic "pinwheel" pattern, a structure first described by Édouard Van Beneden and Theodor Boveri in the 1880s.

Function

Centrioles are involved in organizing the mitotic spindle and in completing cytokinesis during cell division. They also act as basal bodies, templating the formation of cilia and flagella: the centriole that gives rise to a cilium is called the mother centriole. Defects in centriole number and structure are linked to developmental disorders of the brain (such as microcephaly) and to ciliopathies, and abnormal centriole amplification is a common feature of cancer cells.

Duplication

Each daughter cell formed by mitosis inherits one centrosome containing a pair of centrioles. New centrioles form in the S phase of the cell cycle, growing perpendicular to each existing centriole, so that each pair contains one older "mother" and one newer "daughter" centriole.
