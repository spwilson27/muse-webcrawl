Saccade

A saccade is a quick, simultaneous movement of both eyes between two phases of fixation in the same direction. Saccades are the fastest movements produced by the human body, used to rapidly redirect the fovea — the high-acuity centre of the retina — onto objects of interest.

Characteristics

Human saccades can reach peak angular velocities of up to about 900 degrees per second and typically last between 20 and 200 milliseconds depending on amplitude. Vision is effectively suppressed during the movement (saccadic suppression), so the world appears stable despite the eye jumping several times per second. The relationship between a saccade's amplitude, duration and peak velocity is stereotyped and is known as the "main sequence".

Function

Saccades underpin reading, visual search and scene perception. While reading, the eyes advance in saccades of roughly seven to nine letter spaces, pausing in fixations of about 200–250 milliseconds to extract information. Saccades may be reflexive, triggered by a new stimulus in the visual periphery, or voluntary, directed to a remembered or intended target; "express" saccades have latencies as short as 80–100 milliseconds.

Neural control

Saccades are generated by burst neurons in the brainstem — the paramedian pontine reticular formation for horizontal movements and the rostral interstitial nucleus of the medial longitudinal fasciculus for vertical ones — under control of the superior colliculus, frontal eye fields and basal ganglia. The cerebellum calibrates their accuracy. Disorders of saccades occur in conditions such as progressive supranuclear palsy, Huntington's disease and various ataxias, and saccadic eye movements are widely measured in neuroscience, psychology, marketing and human–computer interaction research.

Etymology

The word comes from the French saccade, meaning a jerk or sharp pull on a horse's reins; the ophthalmologist Émile Javal is credited with early studies of the discontinuous eye movements of reading in the late nineteenth century.
