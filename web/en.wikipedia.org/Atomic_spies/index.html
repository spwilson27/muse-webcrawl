Atomic spies

Atomic spies is the popular name for people in the United States, Great Britain and Canada who are known to have illicitly passed information about the Manhattan Project — the Allied programme to build the first atomic bombs — to the Soviet Union during and shortly after the Second World War. Their work gave Soviet scientists valuable information about bomb design (particularly the implosion-type plutonium weapon) and probably accelerated the Soviet atomic bomb project, which produced its first test, RDS-1, in August 1949, earlier than Western intelligence had expected.

Principal figures

The most consequential spy was Klaus Fuchs, a German-born British physicist who worked at Los Alamos and passed detailed information on the plutonium bomb and early hydrogen-bomb work to Soviet couriers from 1942 onwards; arrested in 1950 after the Anglo-American Venona decrypts identified him, he served nine years in prison. Others included Theodore Hall, a young Harvard-trained physicist at Los Alamos who volunteered information to the Soviets in 1944 and was identified by Venona but never prosecuted; David Greenglass, an Army machinist at Los Alamos whose sketches reached the USSR through his brother-in-law's network; his sister Ethel Rosenberg and her husband Julius Rosenberg, who ran a wider Soviet spy ring and were convicted of conspiracy to commit espionage in 1951 and executed in June 1953 — the only American civilians executed for espionage during the Cold War. In Canada, the defection of cipher clerk Igor Gouzenko in 1945 exposed a Soviet ring that included the physicist Alan Nunn May, who had passed uranium samples and information from the Montreal Laboratory. The courier Harry Gold linked several of these cases in his confessions.

Assessment

Historians debate how much the espionage shortened the Soviet programme — estimates range from a few months to two years — and whether the Rosenbergs' sentence was proportionate (documents released later confirmed Julius's guilt while suggesting Ethel's role was marginal). The spy cases fuelled the anti-communist climate of McCarthyism and were a central episode in early Cold War history. Declassification of the Venona intercepts in the 1990s confirmed the broad outlines of the networks while revealing additional agents, such as the Los Alamos machinist and scientist George Koval, publicly identified only in 2007.
