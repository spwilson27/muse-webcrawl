In Greek mythology, Nyx (Ancient Greek: Νύξ, "Night") is the goddess and personification of the night. A shadowy figure, Nyx stood at or near the beginning of creation and was one of the primordial deities (protogenoi). She is the offspring of Chaos, and, with her brother Erebus (Darkness), she produced Aether (Brightness) and Hemera (Day).

Nyx was a figure of exceptional power and beauty, feared even by Zeus: in Homer's Iliad, Zeus refrains from angering her when Hypnos (Sleep) flees to her protection.

Offspring

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Nyx produced a brood of dark and abstract powers, including Moros (Doom), Thanatos (Death), Hypnos (Sleep), the Oneiroi (Dreams), Momus (Blame), Oizys (Pain), the Hesperides, the Moirai (Fates), the Keres, Nemesis (Retribution), Apate (Deceit), Philotes (Friendship), Geras (Old Age), and Eris (Strife). Many of these she produced alone, without a partner.

Dwelling and role

Nyx dwelt in a cave or mansion beyond Oceanus or at the edge of the world near Tartarus, where she and her daughter Hemera (Day) alternated: as one left the house, the other entered, so that night and day never occupied the earth at the same time. In Orphic tradition Nyx held an even more central position, as a prophetic goddess from whom even Zeus sought counsel.

Worship and legacy

Nyx had few temples in Greece, though she appears in art as a winged goddess or charioteer. Her Roman equivalent is Nox. The name Nyx has been given to a moon of Pluto (Nix, spelled with a variant) and to other objects, and she appears widely in modern literature and games.
