Erga omnes

Erga omnes is a Latin phrase meaning "towards all" or "towards everyone". In international law, obligations erga omnes are owed by a state to the international community as a whole, so that any state (not only a directly injured one) has an interest in, and may invoke responsibility for, their breach.

The Barcelona Traction case

The concept was famously articulated by the International Court of Justice in the Barcelona Traction case (1970), where the Court distinguished obligations owed to the international community as a whole from those owed to particular states. Examples given included the prohibitions of aggression and genocide, and the principles and rules concerning basic human rights, including protection from slavery and racial discrimination.

Erga omnes partes

A related concept, erga omnes partes, describes obligations under a treaty that are owed to all the other parties to that treaty, so that any party may invoke responsibility for breach — for example under the Convention against Torture or the Genocide Convention. The ICJ applied this reasoning in The Gambia v. Myanmar concerning the Rohingya.

Related uses

In private law the phrase describes rights effective against the whole world, such as property rights, in contrast to rights in personam effective only against a specific party. Peremptory norms (jus cogens) overlap with, but are conceptually distinct from, erga omnes obligations.
