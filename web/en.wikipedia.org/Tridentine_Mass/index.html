The Tridentine Mass, also known as the Traditional Latin Mass or the Traditional Rite, is the form of the Roman Rite Mass which was codified in the Roman Missal promulgated by Pope Pius V in 1570, following the Council of Trent, from which it takes its name. It remained the standard form of the Latin Church's Eucharistic liturgy until it was replaced by the Mass of Paul VI introduced in 1969–1970 after the Second Vatican Council.

History

Before 1570, local variants of the Roman Rite existed across Europe. Pius V's bull Quo primum standardised the rite, while permitting uses more than two centuries old (such as the Ambrosian and Mozarabic rites, and those of some religious orders) to continue. Successive popes issued revised typical editions of the Missal, the last being that of John XXIII in 1962.

Characteristics

The Tridentine Mass is celebrated almost entirely in Latin. The priest generally faces the altar in the same direction as the congregation (ad orientem), much of the Canon is recited silently, and Communion is traditionally received kneeling and on the tongue. The liturgy distinguishes between Low Mass, Sung Mass (Missa cantata) and Solemn High Mass, with elaborate ceremonial and Gregorian chant.

After Vatican II

After the introduction of the new Missal, celebration of the older form continued under various permissions. Pope Benedict XVI's 2007 motu proprio Summorum Pontificum broadened its availability as the "Extraordinary Form" of the Roman Rite; Pope Francis's 2021 motu proprio Traditionis custodes subsequently restricted its use. It remains the liturgy of traditionalist communities such as the Priestly Fraternity of St Peter.
