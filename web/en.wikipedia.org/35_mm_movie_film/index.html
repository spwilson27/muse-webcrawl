35 mm movie film is a film stock 35 millimetres (1.4 in) wide used in motion picture photography and projection. Developed in the early 1890s by William Kennedy Dickson in Thomas Edison's laboratory, it became the dominant gauge for theatrical cinema for more than a century, and its frame dimensions underlie many digital cinema standards today.

A strip of 35 mm color negative film

History

Dickson, working for Edison, settled in 1892 on film slit from 70 mm roll stock to about 35 mm wide, with four pairs of rectangular perforations per image, for the Kinetoscope peep-hole viewer and the Kinetograph camera. The format was adopted internationally: in 1909 the international congress of film producers in Paris recognized 35 mm with Edison-style perforations as the standard gauge, ending competition with other widths. With the coming of synchronized sound in the late 1920s, the industry added an optical soundtrack along one edge of the film, reducing the usable picture width; the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences standardized the resulting frame in 1932 as the Academy ratio of 1.37:1.

From the 1950s, widescreen systems reshaped the 35 mm frame: CinemaScope used anamorphic lenses to squeeze a 2.39:1 image onto the film, and formats such as VistaVision ran the film horizontally with an eight-perforation pulldown for a larger negative. Television and cost pressures encouraged 3-perf and 2-perf pulldowns, while Super 35 reclaimed the full width of the frame for photography. Since the 2000s, digital cameras and digital projection have displaced 35 mm in most production and exhibition, though some directors continue to shoot on the format for its characteristic image texture.

Technical characteristics

Standard 35 mm cine film has rounded-corner rectangular perforations along both edges. In the common 4-perf pulldown, each frame advances four perforation pairs, giving a frame height of 19 mm; at 24 frames per second the film travels at 90 feet (27.4 m) per minute, so a 1,000 ft reel holds about 11 minutes. Camera negative stocks are supplied in lengths up to 1,000 ft, and projection prints historically shipped on 2,000 ft reels of roughly 20 minutes, requiring changeovers or plattering.

The film base was originally highly flammable cellulose nitrate (used until about 1951), replaced by safety acetate and later by stable polyester for prints. Color processes associated with the gauge include Technicolor and the integral tripack Eastmancolor stocks introduced from 1950. Sound is carried on the same strip as an optical track — variable-density or variable-area — or, in later practice, as digital data blocks printed between the perforations (Dolby Digital, SDDS) alongside a timecode for DTS discs.
