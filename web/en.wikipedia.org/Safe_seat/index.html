Safe seat

In safe seats, the decisive contest is often the party's nomination rather than the general election.

A safe seat is an electoral district (constituency) in a legislative body which is regarded as fully secure for either a certain political party or the incumbent representative personally. Safe seats are so reliably won that the party holding them faces little or no realistic prospect of defeat, whether because of the district's demographics, partisan composition, or redistricting (gerrymandering).

Consequences

In first-past-the-post systems such as those of the United States House of Representatives and the British House of Commons, a large proportion of seats are considered safe. Where a seat is safe for a party, the effective contest shifts to the primary or selection process, which can pull representatives toward their party's base and away from median voters. Safe seats also shape campaign strategy: parties concentrate money and attention on marginal (swing) seats, while voters in safe seats may feel their votes matter less. The predictive ratings published by organizations such as the Cook Political Report classify U.S. House races as "solid," "likely," "lean," or "toss-up."

Contrast

The opposite of a safe seat is a marginal seat, where small swings decide the outcome. In parliamentary terminology a safe seat may be contrasted with the personal vote of an incumbent and with "bellwether" districts that mirror national results. Electoral reforms such as proportional representation are sometimes advocated on the ground that they eliminate safe seats.
