The Hooker telescope is a 100-inch (2.54 m) reflecting telescope at the Mount Wilson Observatory in California. Funded largely by Los Angeles businessman John D. Hooker and championed by astronomer George Ellery Hale, it was the largest telescope in the world from its completion in 1917 until the 200-inch Hale Telescope opened in 1949.

Construction

The mirror blank was cast in Saint-Gobain, France, from a massive pour of glass; grinding and figuring took years, delayed by the First World War. The telescope uses a fork equatorial mounting and weighs about 100 tons, floating on mercury bearings.

Discoveries

With the Hooker telescope, Edwin Hubble identified Cepheid variables in the Andromeda Nebula in 1923–24, proving that spiral nebulae are separate galaxies beyond the Milky Way. Hubble later used it with Milton Humason to measure galactic redshifts, establishing the expansion of the universe. Michelson and Pease used an interferometer mounted on the telescope in 1920 to make the first direct measurement of a star's diameter (Betelgeuse).

Later use

Light pollution from Los Angeles ended its main research role in 1986, but it was refitted with adaptive optics and returned to scientific use; it remains one of the most historically important telescopes ever built.
