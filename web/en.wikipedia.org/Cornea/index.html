The cornea is the transparent front part of the eye that covers the iris, pupil, and anterior chamber. Together with the lens, it refracts light onto the retina; the cornea provides roughly two-thirds (about 43 dioptres) of the eye's total optical power. It is the eye's clearest window and its first line of defence, and it is the most densely innervated tissue in the human body, making corneal injuries exquisitely painful.

The cornea is the transparent dome at the front of the eye.

Structure

The cornea consists of five layers. The outer epithelium regenerates rapidly; beneath it lie Bowman's layer, the thick stroma of precisely ordered collagen lamellae whose regular arrangement is essential to transparency, Descemet's membrane, and the single-cell endothelium, whose pump keeps the stroma deturgesced and clear. The cornea has no blood vessels; it receives oxygen directly from the air and nutrients from tears and aqueous humour. At its rim, the limbus contains stem cells that renew the epithelium.

Function and disorders

Because the cornea's curvature does most of the eye's focusing, irregularities cause refractive errors: astigmatism from uneven curvature, and keratoconus, in which the cornea thins and bulges into a cone. Infections (keratitis), dystrophies such as Fuchs' dystrophy, and scarring from injury can cloud the cornea and blind the eye.

Treatment

Refractive surgery (LASIK, PRK) reshapes the cornea to correct vision. Clouded corneas can be replaced by corneal transplantation (keratoplasty) — full-thickness penetrating grafts or lamellar procedures such as DMEK replacing only the diseased layers; corneal grafting is the most common and one of the most successful transplant operations, aided by the cornea's relative immune privilege. Contact lenses rest on the tear film over the cornea.
