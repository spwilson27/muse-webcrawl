Jataka tales

The Jataka tales are a voluminous body of literature native to the Indian subcontinent, mainly concerning the previous births of Gautama Buddha in both human and animal form. The Jataka stories "represent the attempts of ordinary people in the earliest landscape of South Asia" to incorporate Buddhist ideals of moral conduct into familiar folk narrative. According to tradition, the Buddha, during his sermons, would recount these stories of his past lives to illustrate moral lessons and the working of karma.

The collection

The Pali Jataka, part of the Khuddaka Nikaya of the Sutta Pitaka, contains 547 stories in verse, each framed by prose commentary that retells the story in full. The verses alone are canonical; the prose commentary is attributed to the fifth-century tradition associated with Buddhaghosa. Sanskrit and other recensions exist, including the Jatakamala of Aryashura. The tales vary from short fables to long, elaborate romances.

Themes

In each tale the future Buddha (the Bodhisatta) is born as a king, merchant, ascetic, or animal — a deer, monkey, elephant, bird, or fish — and displays one of the perfections (paramitas), above all generosity, morality, patience, and wisdom. Well-known tales include the Sasa Jataka (the hare who offers his own body as food, and whose image is stamped on the moon), the Vessantara Jataka (the prince who gives away everything, even his children), and the Sibi Jataka (the king who sacrifices his flesh to save a dove).

Influence and art

Jataka scenes are among the earliest subjects of Buddhist art, carved in the reliefs of Bharhut and Sanchi (from around the second century BCE) and painted in the Ajanta caves. The stories spread with Buddhism across Asia and influenced world literature: through Persian and Arabic intermediaries they fed into collections such as the Panchatantra tradition, Kalila wa Dimna, and ultimately European fable literature; some tales parallel those of Aesop.
