In biochemistry, allosteric regulation is the regulation of an enzyme or other protein by an effector molecule binding to a site other than the protein's active site — the allosteric site (from Greek allos, "other", and stereos, "solid/shape"). Binding changes the protein's conformation and hence its activity, allowing cells to fine-tune metabolic pathways, often through feedback inhibition in which a pathway's end product switches off an early enzyme.

Schematic of allosteric regulation: an effector binds a site distinct from the active site and changes the protein's shape.

Mechanism

Allosteric proteins typically exist in an equilibrium between a tense (T, low-activity) state and a relaxed (R, high-activity) state. An allosteric activator stabilises the R state, while an inhibitor stabilises the T state. Because many allosteric proteins are oligomers with several subunits, binding at one subunit can alter the others — the basis of cooperativity, in which binding of a substrate molecule increases the protein's affinity for further substrate molecules and produces a sigmoidal (S-shaped) response curve instead of a hyperbolic one.

Models

Two classic models describe allostery. The concerted Monod–Wyman–Changeux (MWC) model (1965) proposes that all subunits switch between T and R states simultaneously, with effectors shifting the equilibrium. The sequential Koshland–Némethy–Filmer (KNF) model proposes that each subunit changes conformation individually upon binding ("induced fit"), with interactions between subunits modifying subsequent binding. Modern views combine both ideas and emphasise shifts in populations of pre-existing conformations.

Examples

The textbook example is haemoglobin: oxygen binding at one haem group increases the affinity of the remaining subunits, while 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate, hydrogen ions (the Bohr effect) and carbon dioxide act as allosteric inhibitors promoting oxygen release in the tissues. In metabolism, the bacterial enzyme aspartate carbamoyltransferase is activated by ATP and feedback-inhibited by CTP, and mammalian phosphofructokinase-1 — a key control point of glycolysis — is activated by AMP and fructose 2,6-bisphosphate and inhibited by ATP and citrate.

Allosteric drugs

Many medicines act at allosteric sites rather than active sites, including benzodiazepines, which enhance the effect of GABA at GABAA receptors, and cinacalcet, which modulates the calcium-sensing receptor. Allosteric modulators can offer greater selectivity and a ceiling on effect, making them an active area of drug design.
