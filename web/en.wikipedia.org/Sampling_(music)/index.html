Sampling (music)

An Akai MPC, an influential sampling workstation.

Sampling is a technique in music production in which a portion of one sound recording (a "sample") is reused and incorporated into a different song or piece. Samples may comprise elements such as rhythm, melody, speech, or sound effects, and are typically manipulated by equipment such as samplers or digital audio workstations — sped up, slowed down, looped, reversed, or otherwise transformed.

History

Sampling has roots in the 1940s musique concrète of Pierre Schaeffer, who manipulated recorded sounds on tape, and in the Mellotron keyboard of the 1960s, which played back tape loops of instruments. Digital samplers such as the Fairlight CMI (1979) and the E-mu SP-1200 and Akai MPC series made sampling central to hip hop production in the 1980s. The "Amen break", a drum solo from the Winstons' 1969 recording "Amen, Brother", became the most sampled passage in popular music and a foundation of jungle and drum and bass.

Techniques

Producers "chop" samples into fragments, rearrange them, and layer them with synthesised or newly recorded material. Sampling underlies genres including hip hop, house, plunderphonics, and much contemporary pop.

Legal issues

Using samples without permission can infringe copyright in both the composition and the sound recording. Landmark cases include Grand Upright Music, Ltd. v. Warner Bros. Records Inc. (1991), which ended the era of uncleared sampling in commercial hip hop, and litigation over Kraftwerk's "Metall auf Metall" in Germany and the European Union. Artists including De La Soul and Biz Markie reshaped their practices in response, and a sample-clearance industry emerged. Some uses may qualify as fair use, but courts have been inconsistent, notably on whether de minimis copying of sound recordings is permissible.
