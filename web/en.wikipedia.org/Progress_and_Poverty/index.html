Progress and Poverty

Progress and Poverty: An Inquiry into the Cause of Industrial Depressions and of Increase of Want with Increase of Wealth: The Remedy is a book by social theorist and economist Henry George, published in 1879. It became one of the best-selling books of the 19th century, selling several million copies worldwide, and launched the Georgist movement and the campaign for a "single tax" on land values.

Argument

George posed the paradox named in his title: why does poverty deepen amid advancing wealth and technology? He argued that the private appropriation of land rent is the fundamental cause. As society progresses, the value of land — which no individual created — rises, and landowners capture an increasing share of production as rent, depressing wages and interest and periodically producing speculative land booms and depressions.

His remedy was a land value tax: a tax on the unimproved value of land that would replace all other taxes (the "single tax"), leaving the products of labour and capital untaxed. George argued this would discourage speculation, make land available to those who would use it, and fund public services, while being impossible to evade or shift onto others. He did not advocate confiscation or nationalisation of land titles, but the taxation of its rental value for the community that creates it.

Reception and influence

The book made George internationally famous; he lectured across the English-speaking world and nearly won the New York City mayoral election of 1886. Progress and Poverty influenced figures as diverse as Leo Tolstoy, Sun Yat-sen, Winston Churchill, and the founders of the British welfare state, and Georgist ideas shaped land policies in places such as Taiwan, Denmark, and parts of Australia. Economists have debated George's theory ever since; Milton Friedman called the land value tax "the least bad tax," and Joseph Stiglitz formalised a version of George's argument as the "Henry George theorem."

The work also advanced George's theory of the business cycle, attributing depressions to land speculation, and his account of technological progress displacing labour when land is monopolised.
