Human migration

Human migration
The movement of people from one place to another with the intention of settling, permanently or temporarily, at a new location.

Human migration is the movement of people from one place to another, often across political or geographic boundaries, with the intention of settling at the destination. Migration may be voluntary or forced, internal or international, and permanent or temporary; people who migrate are called migrants, or, when leaving a place, emigrants, and when arriving, immigrants.

Early human migration

Anatomically modern humans originated in Africa and dispersed across the globe beginning roughly 70,000–100,000 years ago in the "Out of Africa" expansion. Migrants reached Australia by about 65,000 years ago, Europe by 45,000 years ago, and the Americas — via Beringia — by at least 15,000 years ago. Genetic and archaeological evidence traces these movements, which populated every continent except Antarctica and produced the global pattern of human biological variation.

Theories and causes

Scholars describe migration in terms of "push" and "pull" factors: war, persecution, famine and unemployment push people from a place, while safety, jobs and family ties pull them toward another. Ravenstein's "laws of migration" (1880s) noted that most migrants move short distances and that migration proceeds step by step. Forced migration includes the transatlantic slave trade, which carried some 12.5 million Africans to the Americas, as well as deportations and refugee flight.

Modern migration

The International Organization for Migration estimated about 281 million international migrants in 2020, roughly 3.6% of the world population. Major contemporary flows include labour migration to the Gulf states, movement within the European Union, and migration from Latin America to the United States. Remittances sent home by migrants exceed foreign aid flows to low- and middle-income countries.
