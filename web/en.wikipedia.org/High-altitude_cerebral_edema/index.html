High-altitude cerebral edema

SpecialtyEmergency medicine, neurology
SymptomsAtaxia, confusion, altered consciousness, severe headache
CausesRapid ascent to high altitude, hypoxia
TreatmentImmediate descent, oxygen, dexamethasone

High-altitude cerebral edema (HACE) is a medical condition in which the brain swells as a result of the physiological effects of traveling to a high altitude. It is a severe, life-threatening form of altitude sickness, generally occurring after 2 to 5 days above 4,000 metres (13,000 ft), though it can occur at lower altitudes in susceptible individuals.

Signs and symptoms

HACE typically develops in people who already show symptoms of acute mountain sickness (AMS) and continue ascending. Its hallmark features are a change in mental status — confusion, drowsiness, irrational behaviour — and truncal ataxia, an unsteady, staggering gait. Other symptoms include severe headache unresponsive to analgesics, vomiting, hallucinations, and, in late stages, seizures and coma.

Mechanism

The condition is thought to result from vasodilation of cerebral blood vessels in response to hypoxia, increasing cerebral blood flow and capillary pressure. In susceptible individuals this produces vasogenic edema — leakage of fluid across the blood–brain barrier — raising intracranial pressure. Because the skull is rigid, swelling compresses brain tissue and can lead to herniation and death.

Treatment and prevention

Immediate descent of at least 1,000 metres is the definitive treatment, supplemented by supplemental oxygen or a portable hyperbaric chamber when descent is impossible. Dexamethasone is used to reduce cerebral edema. Prevention relies on gradual ascent, acclimatization days, and prophylactic acetazolamide. Untreated, HACE can progress to coma and death within 24 hours; with prompt descent, full recovery is common.
