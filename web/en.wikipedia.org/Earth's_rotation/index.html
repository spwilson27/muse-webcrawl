Earth's rotation or Earth's spin is the rotation of planet Earth around its own axis, as well as changes in the orientation of the rotation axis in space. Earth rotates eastward, in prograde motion. As viewed from the northern polar star Polaris, Earth turns counterclockwise.

The North Pole, also known as the Geographic North Pole or Terrestrial North Pole, is the point in the Northern Hemisphere where Earth's axis of rotation meets its surface. Earth rotates once in about 24 hours with respect to the Sun, but once every 23 hours, 56 minutes and 4 seconds with respect to other distant stars — a sidereal day.

History of measurement

Ancient Greek astronomers understood that the apparent daily motion of the heavens could be explained by Earth's rotation, though the idea was long rejected in favor of a rotating celestial sphere. Foucault's pendulum (1851) gave a direct demonstration of the rotation. Modern measurements by the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service track tiny variations in rotation speed and polar motion.

Effects

Rotation produces the cycle of day and night, the apparent motion of the Sun and stars across the sky, and — through the Coriolis effect — the deflection of winds and ocean currents. Centrifugal acceleration slightly reduces effective gravity at the equator and causes Earth's equatorial bulge.

Long-term changes

Earth's rotation is slowing gradually because of tidal friction with the Moon: the day lengthens by roughly 1.7 milliseconds per century, and leap seconds are occasionally added to Coordinated Universal Time to keep civil time aligned with solar time. Superimposed on this trend are short-term variations caused by earthquakes, ocean currents, and atmospheric winds.
