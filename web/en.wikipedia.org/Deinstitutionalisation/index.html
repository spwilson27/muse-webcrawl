Deinstitutionalisation

Deinstitutionalisation (or deinstitutionalization) is the policy and process of replacing long-stay psychiatric hospitals and other large institutions with less isolated community-based services for people diagnosed with mental disorders or developmental disabilities.

History

The movement gathered force from the 1950s, driven by the arrival of antipsychotic medication, exposés of poor conditions in asylums, civil-liberties litigation, and the hope that community care would be both more humane and cheaper. In the United States, the state psychiatric hospital population fell from a peak of about 559,000 in 1955 to a small fraction of that figure within a few decades; similar closures occurred across Britain, Italy — where the 1978 Basaglia Law mandated closure of asylums — and other countries.

Outcomes and debate

Where well funded, community services have enabled many people to live fuller lives outside institutions. Critics argue that in many places hospitals closed faster than community provision grew, contributing to homelessness and the "transinstitutionalisation" of people with severe mental illness into prisons and nursing homes. The balance between liberty, treatment access and public safety remains contested.
