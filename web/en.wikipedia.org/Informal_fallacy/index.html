Informal fallacy

An informal fallacy is an error in reasoning that occurs in natural-language argumentation because of the content or context of an argument, rather than its logical form. Unlike formal fallacies, which violate rules of deductive logic, informal fallacies depend on the meaning of the premises, the relevance of evidence, or ambiguities of language.

Classification

Philosophers have proposed many classifications of informal fallacies. A common scheme distinguishes:

  • Fallacies of relevance — premises that are logically irrelevant to the conclusion, such as appeals to emotion, ad hominem attacks, and appeals to authority or popularity.
  • Fallacies of defective induction — weak inductive reasoning, including hasty generalisation, false cause, and slippery slope arguments.
  • Fallacies of presumption — arguments resting on unjustified assumptions, such as begging the question, false dilemmas, and loaded questions.
  • Fallacies of ambiguity — exploiting shifts in meaning of words or phrases, including equivocation and the fallacy of composition or division.

Examples

Well-known informal fallacies include the straw man (misrepresenting an opponent's position), red herring (introducing an irrelevant topic), appeal to ignorance, no true Scotsman, and the genetic fallacy. Many informal fallacies can be reasonable argumentative moves in some contexts — an appeal to authority, for instance, may be legitimate when the authority is a genuine expert in the relevant domain — which is why their evaluation depends on context rather than form alone.

History

The study of fallacies goes back to Aristotle's Sophistical Refutations, which catalogued thirteen fallacies, most of them informal. Medieval logicians expanded the taxonomy, and in the twentieth century C. L. Hamblin's Fallacies (1970) reinvigorated scholarly interest, criticising the "standard treatment" of fallacies in textbooks and inspiring pragmatic and dialectical approaches to argument analysis.
