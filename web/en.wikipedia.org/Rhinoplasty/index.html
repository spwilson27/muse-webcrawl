Rhinoplasty


Nasal structures modified in rhinoplasty

Rhinoplasty (from Greek rhis, nose, and plassein, to shape), commonly known as a nose job, is a plastic surgery procedure for altering and reconstructing the nose. It is performed either to improve the appearance of the nose (cosmetic rhinoplasty) or to correct breathing problems and congenital or traumatic defects (functional or reconstructive rhinoplasty). It is among the most commonly performed cosmetic surgical procedures worldwide.

History

Nasal reconstruction is ancient: the Indian physician Sushruta described forehead-flap reconstruction of amputated noses in the Sushruta Samhita, composed around the 6th century BCE, a technique that remained in use in India for millennia. In 16th-century Italy, Gaspare Tagliacozzi published a method of rebuilding the nose using a flap of skin from the upper arm. Modern aesthetic rhinoplasty developed in the late 19th and 20th centuries through the work of surgeons such as John Orlando Roe and Jacques Joseph, and was refined throughout the 20th century.

Procedure

Rhinoplasty is usually performed under general anaesthesia as an outpatient procedure. The surgeon reshapes the nasal bones and cartilages — removing or adding tissue, narrowing the nostrils, changing the angle between the nose and upper lip, or straightening a deviated septum (often combined with septoplasty). Two approaches are used: the closed approach, with all incisions inside the nostrils, and the open approach, which adds a small incision across the columella for better access in complex cases.

Recovery and risks

Patients typically wear a splint for about a week; swelling and bruising subside over weeks, though subtle swelling can persist for a year or more before the final result is apparent. Risks include infection, bleeding, adverse reaction to anaesthesia, breathing difficulties, septal perforation, and dissatisfaction with the aesthetic result; a minority of patients undergo revision surgery.

Non-surgical rhinoplasty

Minor contour irregularities can be temporarily corrected by injecting dermal fillers ("liquid rhinoplasty"), a non-surgical alternative whose results last months rather than years and which carries its own risks, including rare vascular complications.
