Paleomagnetism

Paleomagnetism (or palaeomagnetism in the United Kingdom) is the study of magnetic fields recorded in rocks, sediment, or other geological materials. Because magnetic minerals such as magnetite and hematite record the direction and intensity of Earth's magnetic field at the time of their formation, paleomagnetic data preserve a record of the behaviour of the geomagnetic field through time and of the past positions of tectonic plates.

Remanent magnetization

Rocks acquire natural remanent magnetization in several ways. Igneous rocks gain thermoremanent magnetization as they cool through the Curie temperature of their magnetic minerals. Sediments gain detrital remanent magnetization as magnetic grains align with the field while settling. Chemical changes during diagenesis can add a chemical remanent magnetization. Laboratory stepwise demagnetisation isolates the primary component acquired at formation.

Applications

Paleomagnetism provided key evidence for continental drift and plate tectonics: apparent polar wander paths for different continents could only be reconciled if the continents had moved. The pattern of magnetic reversals recorded in oceanic crust (magnetic striping) confirmed seafloor spreading and underpins the geomagnetic polarity timescale used to date rocks. Paleomagnetic inclination also yields the paleolatitude at which a rock formed, aiding reconstructions of ancient geography and climate.

History

The field developed in the 1950s through the work of researchers including P. M. S. Blackett, Keith Runcorn, and Edward Irving. Studies of reversal stratigraphy by Allan Cox, Richard Doell, and Brent Dalrymple in the 1960s, together with the Vine–Matthews–Morley hypothesis, helped settle the debate over continental drift.
