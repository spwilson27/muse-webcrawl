Miletus

LocationNear Balat, Aydın Province, Turkey
RegionIonia, Anatolia
FoundedBronze Age settlement; Greek city from c. 10th century BC
Abandoned15th century AD
Known forMilesian school of philosophy; Hippodamian grid plan; theatre

Miletus was an ancient Greek city on the western coast of Anatolia, near the mouth of the Maeander River in Ionia. In the Archaic period it was among the greatest and wealthiest Greek cities, founding dozens of colonies around the Black Sea and Mediterranean, and it was the home of Thales, Anaximander, and Anaximenes — the Milesian philosophers who inaugurated Greek natural philosophy. Its ruins, including a vast theatre, lie near the village of Balat in present-day Turkey.

History

The site was inhabited in the Bronze Age and figures in Hittite records as Millawanda, a foothold of Mycenaean Greeks. After Ionian settlement, Miletus flourished on maritime trade and wool production, resisted Lydia, and suffered destruction by Persia after the Ionian Revolt (494 BC). Rebuilt on a regular grid plan associated with Hippodamus of Miletus, it prospered under Alexander the Great and Rome, before silting of its harbors by the Maeander gradually strangled its commerce.

Philosophy and science

The Milesians sought a single material principle (arche) underlying nature — water for Thales, the apeiron for Anaximander, air for Anaximenes — marking the beginnings of rational cosmology. Miletus also produced the geographer and historian Hecataeus and the architect Isidore of Miletus, co-designer of Hagia Sophia.

Archaeology

Excavations, principally German since 1899, have uncovered the Delphinion sanctuary, bouleuterion, agora, baths of Faustina, and a theatre seating some 15,000. The Market Gate of Miletus was reconstructed in Berlin's Pergamon Museum. Because the coastline has advanced, the ancient port city now lies well inland among marshes.
