Oath

Traditionally an oath is either a statement of fact or a promise taken by someone that invokes a sacred being, deity, or revered text as a witness to the binding nature of the statement. A person taking an oath indicates that what they are saying is subject to a divine or solemn sanction of truth, and that breaking the oath carries serious consequences. The modern legal equivalent, which does not invoke a deity, is an affirmation.

Types

Common oaths include the oath of office taken by officials before assuming public duties, the oath of allegiance to a state or sovereign, the oath of citizenship in naturalization, and oath of testimony in court, in which a witness swears to tell the truth under penalty of perjury. The Hippocratic Oath is a famous professional oath in medicine.

Historical development

Oaths appear in many ancient cultures, often sworn on sacred objects or before gods; Roman soldiers swore the sacramentum to their commanders and later to the emperor. With the growth of secular legal systems, affirmations were introduced so that non-believers and members of faiths that prohibit oaths could make equally binding statements.
