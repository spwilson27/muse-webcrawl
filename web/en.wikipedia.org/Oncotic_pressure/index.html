Oncotic pressure

Oncotic pressure, also called colloid osmotic pressure, is the component of osmotic pressure exerted by large molecules — chiefly plasma proteins, above all albumin — that cannot freely cross the capillary endothelium. It is the force that draws water from the interstitial space back into the blood plasma, opposing the hydrostatic pressure that pushes fluid out of capillaries.

Physiology

In humans, plasma oncotic pressure is about 25–28 mmHg, of which albumin contributes roughly 70–80 percent; globulins and fibrinogen make up most of the remainder. Fluid exchange across capillary walls is described by the Starling equation, which balances hydrostatic and oncotic pressures in the capillary and interstitium, together with the membrane's filtration coefficient and reflection coefficient. Along a typical capillary, falling hydrostatic pressure and relatively constant oncotic pressure shift the balance from filtration near the arterial end toward reabsorption near the venous end — though modern (glycocalyx-aware) revisions of the model suggest reabsorption is more limited than the classic picture.

Clinical significance

When plasma protein levels fall — as in nephrotic syndrome, liver failure, severe malnutrition (kwashiorkor) or major burns — oncotic pressure drops and fluid accumulates in tissues, producing oedema. Clinicians measure or calculate colloid osmotic pressure in critical care, and infused colloid solutions (albumin, starches, gelatins) are used partly for their oncotic effect, though their advantage over crystalloids remains debated.
