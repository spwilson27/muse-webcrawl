Atmospheric refraction
Field: Atmospheric optics, astronomy
Standard value at horizon: approx. 34 arcminutes
Effects: delayed sunset, mirages, green flash, Novaya Zemlya effect

Atmospheric refraction is the deviation of light or other electromagnetic waves from a straight line as they pass through the atmosphere, owing to the variation in air density as a function of height. Because density decreases with altitude, rays passing through the atmosphere are bent toward the denser layers, so celestial objects appear higher in the sky than they really are.

Astronomical refraction

Refraction is negligible near the zenith but grows toward the horizon, where it reaches about 34 arcminutes under standard conditions—slightly more than the apparent diameter of the Sun. Consequently the Sun is visible when its true position is entirely below the horizon, lengthening the day by several minutes, and the setting Sun appears flattened because its lower limb is lifted more than its upper limb. Astronomers must correct measured positions for refraction, and strong temperature gradients can produce anomalous refraction and shimmering ("seeing").

Terrestrial effects

The same physics produces mirages: over hot surfaces, steep temperature gradients bend light upward, creating the appearance of water on roads; over cold surfaces, looming and superior mirages lift distant objects above the horizon. Extreme refraction across polar temperature inversions can produce the Novaya Zemlya effect, in which the Sun appears well before its geometric sunrise.
