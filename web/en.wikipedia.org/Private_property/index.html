Private property

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Privately owned land and housing

Private property is a legal designation for the ownership of property by non-governmental legal entities. Private property is distinguishable from public property, which is owned by a state entity, and from collective or cooperative property, which is owned by one or more non-governmental entities. Private property is foundational to capitalism, with most contemporary definitions describing it as the legal right of private entities to fruits and resources.

Private property is a social relationship between the owner and persons represented by the state that prohibits the latter from using the property unless the owner allows. Private property may be used as a term both for real property (land and buildings) and, in political philosophy, for the means of production. Private property as a legal concept also covers intellectual property, such as patents and copyrights.

History

The concept of private property emerged with settled agriculture and the rise of states, replacing earlier communal arrangements among hunter-gatherer groups. In Roman law, dominium described absolute ownership. John Locke argued in his Second Treatise of Government (1689) that individuals acquire property by mixing their labour with natural resources, a theory that became central to liberal thought. Adam Smith, Karl Marx and later economists debated the economic and moral consequences of private ownership: Smith emphasised its role in efficient markets, while Marx argued that private ownership of the means of production enabled exploitation of labour.

Types

Private property includes tangible assets such as land, buildings, vehicles and personal possessions, as well as intangible assets such as shares, bonds and intellectual property. Ownership may be held by individuals, families, partnerships, corporations or other legal persons. Rights associated with ownership typically include the rights to use, exclude others from, and transfer the property, subject to limits imposed by law, such as zoning rules, taxation and eminent domain.
