Medea (Ancient Greek: Μήδεια, Mēdeia) is an ancient Greek tragedy written by Euripides. It is based on the myth of Medea and Jason and was first produced in 431 BC as part of a trilogy, the other plays of which have not survived. The plot centres on the actions of Medea, a former princess of the kingdom of Colchis and wife of Jason; she finds her position in the Greek world threatened as Jason leaves her for a Greek princess of Corinth. Medea takes vengeance on Jason by murdering his new wife as well as her own two sons, after which she escapes to Athens.

The play is widely regarded as one of the great tragedies of world theatre. Euripides was seen as controversial in his own time: Medea won only third prize at the Dionysia of 431 BC, but the play's sympathetic yet horrifying portrait of a wronged foreign woman, and its rhetorically sophisticated treatment of passion, made it a classic of the repertoire from the Hellenistic period onwards and a touchstone for later dramatists and feminist criticism.

Plot

In Corinth, Medea laments Jason's betrayal: he has divorced her to marry Glauce, daughter of King Creon. Creon banishes Medea, fearing her cleverness and her threats; she secures one day's delay. She wins a promise of refuge from Aegeus, king of Athens. Then she sends Glauce a poisoned robe and coronet, which kill both the princess and Creon. Though she wavers, Medea kills her two sons to complete her revenge, and escapes in the dragon-drawn chariot of her grandfather, the sun god Helios, leaving Jason to confront his ruin.
