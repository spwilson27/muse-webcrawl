Griddle

TypeCooking appliance / cookware
SurfaceFlat, smooth, usually cast iron or steel
UsesPancakes, eggs, burgers, flatbreads, teppanyaki
RelatedFrying pan, plancha, comal, tawa

A griddle is a cooking surface consisting of a broad, usually flat plate of metal — traditionally cast iron, today also steel, aluminium or non-stick coated — heated from below. Unlike a frying pan, a griddle has no raised sides (or only a slight lip), giving unobstructed access for spatulas and room to cook several items at once.

Types

Domestic griddles include stovetop plates spanning one or two burners, reversible plates with a ribbed grill side, and countertop electric griddles with thermostatic control. In Scotland a heavy round griddle is traditionally called a girdle and used for scones, bannocks and oatcakes. Commercial griddles — the flat tops of diners and short-order kitchens — are large steel plates capable of cooking dozens of burgers, eggs and pancakes simultaneously, and are central to Japanese teppanyaki and Mongolian barbecue cooking.

Cooking

The large even surface suits foods cooked in batches: pancakes and crêpes, French toast, bacon and eggs, hash browns, grilled sandwiches, smash burgers and seared vegetables. Flatbreads worldwide are griddle-cooked, from tortillas on a Mexican comal to dosas and chapatis on an Indian tawa and injera on an Ethiopian mitad. Proper use involves preheating, light oiling, and — for cast iron — maintaining a seasoned polymerised-oil surface that resists sticking and rust.

History

Flat heated stones are among humanity's oldest cooking implements. Iron griddles hung over hearth fires were staples of early modern European and American kitchens, and the nineteenth-century cast-iron stove made the integrated griddle plate a standard feature of the American cooktop — the direct ancestor of today's diner flat top.
