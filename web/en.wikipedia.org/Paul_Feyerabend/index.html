Paul Karl Feyerabend (January 13, 1924 – February 11, 1994) was an Austrian-born philosopher of science, best known for his provocative critique of universal scientific method. His 1975 book Against Method argued that there is no single method that all successful science follows, and that methodological rules are routinely and productively violated. His slogan "anything goes" summarised this epistemological anarchism—though Feyerabend insisted it was the "terrified exclamation" of the rationalist, not his own doctrine. He spent most of his career at the University of California, Berkeley, and later also taught at ETH Zürich.

Life

Feyerabend was born in Vienna and served as an officer in the German army during the Second World War, receiving a bullet wound that left him with lasting disabilities. After the war he studied history, physics, mathematics, and astronomy at the University of Vienna, where he joined the circle around Viktor Kraft, and encountered Karl Popper's philosophy. He studied in London under Popper in 1952, then taught at the University of Bristol before moving to Berkeley in 1958. His friendship and sharp disagreements with Imre Lakatos shaped much of his work; Against Method was originally planned as half of a joint volume with Lakatos.

Philosophy

Feyerabend argued that the history of science—especially his case study of Galileo—shows that progress often required "counterinduction": proceeding against well-confirmed theories and accepted rules of evidence. With Thomas Kuhn, he developed the idea of the incommensurability of rival theories, which lack a common measure for comparison. In later works such as Science in a Free Society (1978) and Farewell to Reason (1987), he extended the argument politically, defending a pluralism in which science is one tradition among others and should be subject to democratic oversight. His final, unfinished work, published posthumously as Conquest of Abundance (1999), explored how abstraction impoverishes experience.

Feyerabend remains one of the most debated philosophers of science: criticised by some as an enemy of reason, defended by others as a humane pluralist who championed intellectual freedom.
