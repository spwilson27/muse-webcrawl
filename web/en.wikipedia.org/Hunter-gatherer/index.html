Hunter-gatherer

Summary

Subsistence: Hunting wild animals, gathering wild plants

Era: Dominant until c. 10,000 BCE

Social unit: Small bands, typically under 100 people

Examples: San people, Hadza, Ainu, many Aboriginal Australian groups

A hunter-gatherer is a human living in a society in which most or all food is obtained by foraging — hunting wild animals and gathering wild plants — rather than by farming. Hunting and gathering was humanity's original and only subsistence strategy from the emergence of Homo sapiens until the development of agriculture beginning around 10,000 BCE.

Way of life

Hunter-gatherer groups are typically small, mobile bands organised around kinship, with relatively egalitarian social structures and a division of labour in which hunting large game is often (though not universally) done by men and gathering plant foods by women — gathered foods frequently supplying the majority of calories. Because they rely on naturally available resources, population densities remain low, and groups move seasonally to exploit different food sources.

Transition to agriculture

Following the Neolithic Revolution, farming and pastoral societies spread and displaced or absorbed foraging peoples, a process that continued into the modern era. Today only a small number of societies, such as the Hadza of Tanzania, the San of the Kalahari, and some groups in the Arctic and Amazonia, maintain substantially hunter-gatherer lifestyles, often under pressure from encroaching states and development.

Study

Anthropologists study hunter-gatherers both to understand their contemporary cultures in their own right and to model aspects of early human society. Marshall Sahlins famously described some foraging peoples as the "original affluent society," arguing that their needs were met with relatively few hours of labour, though this characterisation remains debated.
