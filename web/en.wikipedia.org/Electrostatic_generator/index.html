Electrostatic generator

An electrostatic generator, or electrostatic machine, is an electromechanical device that produces static electricity — a high voltage at very low current — using mechanical work to transport electric charge against electrostatic forces. Unlike electromagnetic generators, which exploit magnetic induction, electrostatic generators use friction (triboelectric effect), electrostatic induction, or moving charged belts and plates.

History

Early friction machines of the 17th and 18th centuries — Otto von Guericke's rotating sulphur ball (c. 1663) and the glass-globe machines of Francis Hauksbee and others — supplied the charge for experiments with Leyden jars and underpinned early electrical science. Influence machines of the 19th century, such as the Holtz machine and the Wimshurst machine (1880s), used induction to multiply charge and became standard laboratory equipment.

Modern types

The best-known modern design is the Van de Graaff generator (1929), in which a moving insulating belt carries charge into a hollow metal terminal, reaching millions of volts; such machines powered early particle accelerators and remain common in physics education. Pelletrons use a chain of metal pellets for greater stability in tandem accelerators. Related devices include the Kelvin water dropper, which uses falling water and induction.

Applications

Beyond accelerators and demonstrations, electrostatic principles are used in photocopiers and laser printers, electrostatic precipitators, spray painting, and some air purification systems — though these usually employ electronic high-voltage supplies rather than mechanical generators.
