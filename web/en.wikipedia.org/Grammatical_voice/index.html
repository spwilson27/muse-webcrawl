Voice (grammar)

Active and passive voice express the same event with different subjects

In grammar, the voice (also called diathesis) of a verb describes the relationship between the action (or state) that the verb expresses and the participants identified by its arguments (subject, object, etc.). Voice is a central feature of clause structure in many languages and affects which participant is presented as the subject of the sentence.

Active and passive voice

In the active voice, the subject performs the action expressed by the verb: "The dog chased the cat." In the passive voice, the subject is the patient or target of the action: "The cat was chased by the dog." In English, the passive is formed with the auxiliary verb be and the past participle of the main verb; the agent may be omitted entirely, as in "Mistakes were made."

Other voices

Many languages possess additional voices. The middle voice, found in Ancient Greek and Sanskrit, indicates that the subject acts on or for itself. The antipassive voice, common in ergative languages, demotes or omits the object. Some languages, such as those of the Philippines, have rich voice systems (sometimes called focus systems) in which several participants — agent, patient, location, beneficiary — can be promoted to subject. Even English has limited middle constructions, as in "This book reads easily."

Function

Voice alternations allow speakers to manage information structure: topical participants are placed in subject position, agents can be backgrounded or omitted, and patients can be foregrounded. Style guides often advise preferring the active voice for clarity, but the passive is indispensable in scientific writing and whenever the agent is unknown, irrelevant, or obvious.
