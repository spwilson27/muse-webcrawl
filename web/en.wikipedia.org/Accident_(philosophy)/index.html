Accident (philosophy)

An accident (Greek: συμβεβηκός, symbebēkós; Latin: accidens) in metaphysics and philosophy is a property or quality of a thing that is not essential to it — it may be present or absent without destroying the thing's nature — in contrast to an essence or substance. The doctrine originates in Aristotle's philosophy and was elaborated throughout medieval scholasticism.

Aristotle

In Aristotle's Categories and Metaphysics, an accident is one of the nine categories of accidents (quality, quantity, relation, place, time, posture, having, action, and passion) that inhere in a substance. A substance, such as a human being, can gain or lose accidents — becoming tan, sitting down, or growing taller — while remaining the same substance. Aristotle also used the notion in his treatment of chance events: an accidental cause is one that is incidental to the outcome, as when a man digging a hole for a plant happens to discover treasure.

Scholastic development

Medieval philosophers, notably Thomas Aquinas, developed the distinction in the context of theology. In the doctrine of transubstantiation, the substance of the bread and wine is held to change into the body and blood of Christ while the accidents — colour, taste, texture — remain. Aquinas distinguished accidents that can exist only in a subject from the special case, in the Eucharist, in which accidents were held to subsist by divine power without their natural substance.

Early modern philosophy

For John Locke, qualities of objects were divided into primary qualities (solidity, extension, figure, motion) which resemble ideas in the mind, and secondary qualities (colours, sounds, tastes) which do not — a distinction descended from the scholastic contrast between essential and accidental attributes. In contemporary analytic metaphysics, related territory is covered by distinctions between essential and accidental properties, and by discussions of contingent versus necessary attributes of objects.
