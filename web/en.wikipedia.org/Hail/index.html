Hail is a form of solid precipitation consisting of balls or irregular lumps of ice, each of which is called a hailstone. Unlike ice pellets, which are smaller and translucent, hailstones measure 5 mm or more in diameter and form inside thunderstorm updrafts, principally in cumulonimbus clouds.

Formation

Hail forms when supercooled water droplets freeze onto condensation nuclei — often small ice pellets or graupel — carried aloft by strong updrafts. As the growing stone is lifted through regions of varying temperature and liquid-water content, it accretes alternating layers of clear and opaque (rime) ice; a stone cut in half shows these concentric layers much like an onion. When the updraft can no longer support the stone's weight, it falls to the ground. The strongest updrafts, in supercell thunderstorms, can produce stones the size of golf balls, baseballs or larger.

Distribution and hazards

Hail occurs most frequently in mid-latitude continental interiors, notably the Great Plains of North America ("Hail Alley" in Colorado, Nebraska and Wyoming), parts of central Europe, China, Argentina and Australia. It causes roughly a billion dollars or more in damage annually in the United States alone, destroying crops, denting vehicles and breaking roofs and windows. Very large hail can injure or kill people and livestock caught outdoors. The deadliest recorded hailstorm struck Moradabad, India, in 1888, killing some 246 people.

Detection and mitigation

Meteorologists detect hail with weather radar — high reflectivity and specific polarimetric signatures indicate hail aloft — and issue severe thunderstorm warnings when stones of 1 inch (2.5 cm) or larger are expected. Cloud seeding with silver iodide has been used in hail-suppression programmes in several countries, though its effectiveness remains scientifically contested.
