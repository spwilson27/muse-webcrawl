Biennale

In the art context, a biennale (pronounced bee-en-NAH-leh) is a large-scale international exhibition of contemporary art that takes place every two years. The word is Italian for "biennial". The concept derives from the Venice Biennale, founded in 1895, which became the model for hundreds of similar recurring exhibitions held around the world.

The Venice model

The Venice Biennale organizes its exhibition around national pavilions in the Giardini and venues across the city, each country presenting selected artists, alongside a central curated international exhibition directed by an appointed artistic director. Prizes such as the Golden Lion are awarded to the best national participation and the best artist.

Proliferation

Following Venice's example, major biennales were founded in São Paulo (1951), Kassel (documenta, held every five years), Sydney, Istanbul, Gwangju, Lyon, Berlin, Sharjah, and many other cities. From the 1990s onward the number of biennials expanded dramatically across Asia, Africa, and Latin America, a phenomenon sometimes called "biennialization". These exhibitions became key platforms for curators, for the global circulation of contemporary art, and for host cities' cultural tourism and urban branding.

Criticism

Biennales have been criticized for homogenizing contemporary art into a global "biennial style", for their dependence on tourism and state or corporate sponsorship, and for environmental costs associated with international art travel. Defenders argue they democratize access to contemporary art and give visibility to artists outside the traditional Western art centres.
