Structural linguistics

Structural linguistics, or structuralism in linguistics, means collecting a corpus of utterances and then attempting to classify all of the elements of the corpus at their different linguistic levels: phonemes, morphemes, lexical categories, noun phrases, verb phrases, and sentence types. It is considered the precursor of modern linguistics.

The approach was originated by the Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure (1857–1913), whose posthumous Course in General Linguistics (1916) founded the study of language as a system (langue) distinct from speech (parole), analyzed synchronically, in which signs are arbitrary and derive their value from differences from other signs — ideas summarized by the signifier/signified relation and by paradigmatic and syntagmatic relations among elements.

Schools and development

The Prague Linguistic Circle (Roman Jakobson, Nikolai Trubetzkoy) developed structural phonology; in Denmark, Louis Hjelmslev formalized glossematics; in the United States, Leonard Bloomfield and followers such as Zellig Harris pursued a behaviorist, distribution-based "post-Bloomfieldian" structuralism that influenced mid-20th-century linguistics. Structuralist ideas spread beyond linguistics into anthropology (Claude Lévi-Strauss), literary theory, and philosophy, forming the wider structuralist movement.

Noam Chomsky's generative grammar grew out of — and partly reacted against — American structuralism, shifting attention from classifying corpora to modeling speakers' internal, rule-based competence.
