Graphic novel

A graphic novel is a book made up of comics content. Although the word "novel" normally refers to long fictional works, the term is applied broadly to include fiction, non-fiction and anthologized short works, presented in a comics format and published as a book with a spine, rather than as a periodical magazine.

History of the term

The term gained popularity in the 1970s and 1980s. Will Eisner's A Contract with God (1978), marketed as a "graphic novel", is often credited with popularizing the label in the English-speaking world. In the 1980s works such as Art Spiegelman's Maus, Alan Moore and Dave Gibbons' Watchmen and Frank Miller's The Dark Knight Returns brought graphic novels wide critical attention; Maus won a special Pulitzer Prize in 1992.

Formats and markets

Graphic novels include original book-length stories, collected editions of serialized comic books (often called trade paperbacks), and translated works such as Japanese manga volumes and Franco-Belgian albums. The format is used for memoirs (such as Persepolis by Marjane Satrapi), journalism, history and educational works, as well as superhero and literary fiction.

Criticism of the term

Some cartoonists and critics regard "graphic novel" as a marketing term intended to give comics respectability rather than a distinct artistic category. Others use it to distinguish long-form, self-contained comics from periodical comic books.
