The politics of France take place within the framework of the Fifth Republic, a unitary semi-presidential system established by the Constitution of 4 October 1958, championed by Charles de Gaulle. Executive power is shared between the President of the Republic, elected directly for a five-year term, and a government headed by a Prime Minister appointed by the President but responsible to Parliament. This "dual executive" produces periods of cohabitation when the presidency and the parliamentary majority are held by opposing camps.

Institutions

The President is head of state, commander-in-chief, appoints the Prime Minister, may dissolve the National Assembly, call referendums and wield emergency powers under Article 16. The Parliament consists of the National Assembly (577 deputies, directly elected by two-round voting) and the Senate (348 senators, indirectly elected). The Constitutional Council reviews laws' conformity with the Constitution; the Council of State is the supreme administrative court and legal adviser to the government; the Court of Cassation heads the ordinary judiciary.

Party system

For decades French politics was structured by competition between Gaullist/right-wing parties (successively the UMP and Les Républicains) and the Socialist Party, with the far-right National Front (now National Rally) of the Le Pen family, the centre, and the far left as additional poles. Emmanuel Macron's victory in 2017 with his centrist movement La République En Marche (now Renaissance) shattered this order, and politics since has been organised largely between "Macronist" centrists, Marine Le Pen's nationalist right and Jean-Luc Mélenchon's La France Insoumise on the left. France elects its president by a two-round system, in 2002 producing the shock qualification of Jean-Marie Le Pen for the run-off.

Territorial organisation

France is a decentralised unitary state divided into regions, departments and communes; it also administers overseas departments, regions and collectivities. French politics is marked by a strong tradition of protest, strikes and street demonstration, and by the principle of laïcité (secularism) in public life.
