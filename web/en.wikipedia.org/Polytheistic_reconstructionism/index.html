Polytheistic reconstructionism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Reconstructionist practice aims to revive historical polytheistic religions

Polytheistic reconstructionism (or simply reconstructionism) is an approach to modern Paganism that seeks to revive the religions of ancient polytheistic cultures by reconstructing their practices from historical, archaeological and literary evidence. Reconstructionists distinguish their methodology from more eclectic forms of Neopaganism by emphasising fidelity to attested sources.

Approach

Adherents study the primary sources of a chosen culture — its myths, liturgies, languages and material remains — and adapt historical rites, festivals, ethics and social customs for contemporary life. Reconstructionism is not a single religion but a method: each tradition (sometimes called a "recon" path) is specific to one culture.

Traditions

Examples include Heathenry (Germanic, including Norse, traditions, in its reconstructionist forms), Hellenismos (Greek), Religio Romana (Roman), Kemetism (Egyptian), Celtic reconstructionism, and Baltic and Slavic native faiths such as Romuva and Rodnovery. Communities debate how literally ancient practices — including animal sacrifice or socially hierarchical customs — should be adapted, and how to handle gaps in the evidence.

History

The approach emerged in the 1970s–1990s as some Pagans reacted against the eclecticism of Wicca-influenced Neopaganism, looking instead to scholarship and revived ethnic traditions; some Baltic and other European movements have older roots in romantic nationalism and, in a few places, claimed unbroken folk survivals. The growth of the internet greatly aided source-sharing and community organisation.
