A public inquiry, or commission of inquiry, is an official review of events or actions ordered by a government body. It is a common means of investigating disasters, scandals and matters of grave public concern, establishing the facts, apportioning responsibility where appropriate, and recommending changes to prevent recurrence. In the United Kingdom, statutory inquiries may be convened under the Inquiries Act 2005; equivalents exist across Commonwealth countries and elsewhere, including commissions of investigation in Ireland, royal commissions in Australia and Canada, and presidential or congressional commissions in the United States.

Procedure

An inquiry is chaired by an independent figure — frequently a serving or retired judge — assisted by counsel to the inquiry. Terms of reference define its scope. Hearings are generally held in public; witnesses may be compelled to give evidence under oath in a statutory inquiry. The inquiry publishes a report with findings and recommendations, which the government is not usually bound to accept. Inquiries differ from trials: they cannot determine civil or criminal liability, though their findings may inform subsequent proceedings.

Notable inquiries

  • The Taylor Report (1990) into the Hillsborough disaster, which transformed football stadium safety in England.
  • The Saville Inquiry (1998–2010) into Bloody Sunday in Derry, which reversed the conclusions of the earlier Widgery Tribunal.
  • The Leveson Inquiry (2011–2012) into the culture, practices and ethics of the British press.
  • The Iraq Inquiry (Chilcot, 2009–2016) into the United Kingdom's role in the Iraq War.
  • The Grenfell Tower Inquiry (2017–2024) into the 2017 fire in London.

Criticism

Public inquiries are criticised for their cost and duration, for the risk that recommendations are ignored, and sometimes for serving to defer political accountability. Supporters argue they provide a thorough, transparent fact-finding process and a form of catharsis for victims and families.
