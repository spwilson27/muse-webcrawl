Rose hip

Ripe hips of the dog rose (Rosa canina)

The rose hip or rosehip, also called a rose haw or rose hep, is the accessory fruit of the various species of rose plant. It is typically red to orange in colour, though some species produce dark purple or black hips. Rose hips form after successful pollination of the flowers in spring or early summer, and ripen in late summer through autumn.

Structure

Strictly speaking, the fleshy hip is the enlarged floral cup (hypanthium) of the rose flower. The true fruits are the dry, single-seeded achenes enclosed within it, embedded among fine, irritating hairs. The hips of the dog rose (Rosa canina) and rugosa rose (Rosa rugosa) are among those most commonly harvested; rugosa hips are especially large and tomato-like.

Uses

Rose hips are notably rich in vitamin C—among the richest plant sources—and also contain carotenoids such as lycopene, and various polyphenols. They are used to make herbal tea, jam, jelly, syrup, marmalade (such as the Swedish nyponsoppa, a rose hip soup), wine and liqueurs. During the Second World War, Britain organised national rose hip collection campaigns to produce syrup as a vitamin C supplement when citrus imports were scarce.

Rose hip seed oil, pressed from the achenes, is used in cosmetics and skin care products. The hips must be processed to remove the irritating internal hairs, which are also the source of a traditional itching powder used in pranks. Powdered rose hip preparations are marketed as dietary supplements, and some clinical studies have investigated their effects on osteoarthritis symptoms.

Ecology

Hips are an important autumn and winter food for birds such as thrushes and waxwings, which disperse the seeds in their droppings. The bright colour advertises the fruit to dispersers once the achenes have matured.
