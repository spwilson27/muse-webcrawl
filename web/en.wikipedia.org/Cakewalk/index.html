Cakewalk

The cakewalk was a dance developed from the "prize walks" held in the late 19th century, generally at get-togethers on Black slave plantations before and after emancipation in the Southern United States. The dance, a showy strut in which couples promenaded in a square or circle with high steps, kicks, and bows, was originally a satire by enslaved people of the formal ballroom dances of white slaveholders. The couple judged to have performed the most elegant or inventive walk won a prize, traditionally a cake — hence the expression "takes the cake".

History

The cakewalk became a staple of minstrel shows in the 1870s and 1880s and then a national and international craze in the 1890s and 1900s, performed in vaudeville, ballrooms, and social dance halls. Cakewalk music, written in 2/4 time with syncopated rhythms, helped lay the groundwork for ragtime; composers such as Scott Joplin were influenced by its rhythms. Famous performers included Bert Williams and George Walker.

Legacy

The idioms "cakewalk" (meaning something easily accomplished) and "piece of cake" derive from the dance. The cakewalk remained a popular fairground entertainment in Britain, where a mechanical fairground ride was also named after it. Elements of the dance fed into later African-American social dances such as the Charleston and the Lindy Hop.
