A bouleuterion (Greek: βουλευτήριον, bouleutērion) was a building in ancient Greece which housed the council of citizens (boulē) of a democratic city-state. The boule, elected representatives charged with supervising the day-to-day affairs of the city and preparing business for the assembly (ekklēsia), met here daily.

Architecture

Bouleuteria were typically square or rectangular halls, often built like small roofed theatres with rising tiers of seats around three sides facing a speaker's area. Roof spans limited their size, so interior columns supported the ceiling. The Old Bouleuterion of Athens (c. 508 BCE) stood on the west side of the Agora; it was replaced around 415 BCE by a New Bouleuterion, where the Council of Five Hundred set the agenda for the assembly.

Examples

Well-preserved bouleuteria survive at Priene (about 200 seats), Miletus (much larger, with an altar court), Olympia, and Aphrodisias. Their Roman successors were the curia buildings, such as the Curia Julia in Rome.
