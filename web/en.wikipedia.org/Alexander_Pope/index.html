Alexander Pope

Born: 21 May 1688, London, England
Died: 30 May 1744, Twickenham, England
Occupation: Poet, satirist, translator
Notable works: The Rape of the Lock, An Essay on Man, The Dunciad

Alexander Pope (21 May 1688 – 30 May 1744) was an English poet, translator, and satirist of the Enlightenment era, considered one of the greatest English poets. He is best known for his satirical and discursive poetry, including The Rape of the Lock, The Dunciad, and An Essay on Criticism, and for his translation of Homer.

Life

Pope was born in London to a Catholic linen merchant's family; penal laws against Catholics shaped his education, much of it self-directed. Tuberculosis of the bone (Pott's disease) left him severely deformed and only about 4 feet 6 inches tall. He published his Pastorals in 1709 and achieved fame with An Essay on Criticism (1711) and the mock-heroic The Rape of the Lock (1712, expanded 1714). His subscription translations of the Iliad (1715–1720) and Odyssey made him the first English poet to live handsomely by his pen, and funded his villa and famous grotto at Twickenham.

Major works

With his Scriblerus Club friends — Jonathan Swift, John Gay, and John Arbuthnot — Pope championed Tory satire against political and literary folly. The Dunciad (1728; expanded 1743) crowned an age of dunces; An Essay on Man (1733–34), addressed to Bolingbroke, distilled his philosophical optimism into heroic couplets ("Whatever is, is right"). His Imitations of Horace and Moral Essays are masterpieces of the couplet form.

Legacy

Pope is the second-most quoted writer in The Oxford Dictionary of Quotations, after Shakespeare. Lines such as "To err is human; to forgive, divine", "A little learning is a dang'rous thing", and "Fools rush in where angels fear to tread" remain proverbial. His mastery fixed the heroic couplet as the dominant verse form of the Augustan age.
