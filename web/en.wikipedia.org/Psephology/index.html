Psephology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Psephology (/sɪˈfɒlədʒi/; from Greek ψῆφος, psephos, 'pebble' — used as a ballot by the ancient Greeks — + -λογία, -logia, 'study') is the study of elections and voting, a branch of political science and electoral sociology. Psephology deals with the statistical analysis of elections, voting behavior, opinion polls, electoral geography, and the relationships between demographics and election outcomes. A practitioner or expert is known as a psephologist.

History

The term was coined in 1948 in the United Kingdom by W. F. R. Hardie (1902–1990), reportedly after a friend suggested it to Ronald McCallum, the first academic analyst of British elections. The Nuffield Election Studies, beginning with the 1945 British general election, established the tradition of systematic post-election analysis in Britain. In the United States, the development of survey research and exit polling — and figures such as George Gallup — built a parallel science of election forecasting, while in Australia psephology is closely associated with analysts such as Antony Green.

Methods

Psephologists use opinion polling, exit polls, historical results at fine geographic levels, and demographic data to explain and predict outcomes. Core techniques include:

  • Swing analysis: measuring uniform or local shifts in party vote shares between elections.
  • Seat projections: translating vote shares into seat counts under electoral systems such as first-past-the-post, often via models like the uniform national swing or multilevel regression and poststratification.
  • Electoral geography: mapping results to identify regional patterns, marginal seats, and demographic sorting.
  • Forecasting models: combining polls with "fundamentals" such as economic growth and incumbency.

Psephology also examines turnout, ticket splitting, the effects of electoral systems, boundary delimitation, and long-term realignments of party support.
