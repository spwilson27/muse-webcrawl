Rapid prototyping is a group of techniques used to quickly fabricate a scale model or functional part of a physical product using three-dimensional computer-aided design (CAD) data. Construction of the part is usually done with 3D printing or other additive layer manufacturing technology, in which the object is built up layer upon layer from the digital model. The first methods for rapid prototyping became available in the late 1980s and were used to produce models and prototype parts; today they are used for a wide range of applications, including manufacturing production-quality parts in small numbers ("rapid manufacturing").

History

The roots lie in 1960s–70s research on photopolymers and computer-controlled machining. In 1984 Charles Hull invented stereolithography (SLA) — curing liquid photopolymer resin with a UV laser — co-founded 3D Systems, and shipped the first commercial machine, the SLA-1, in 1988. Carl Deckard developed selective laser sintering (SLS), fusing powdered material with a laser, at the University of Texas (patented 1989), while Scott Crump invented fused deposition modeling (FDM), extruding molten thermoplastic filament, and co-founded Stratasys (1989). The STL file format became the standard interface between CAD models and the machines, which slice the model into thin layers and build them sequentially.

Process and applications

The workflow is: create a CAD model, convert it to STL or a similar mesh format, slice it into layers, print, and post-process (removing supports, curing, sanding, painting). Prototypes serve successive purposes in product development — proof-of-concept models, form-and-fit checks, functional testing, and design verification — letting engineers catch errors cheaply before committing to tooling. Industries using rapid prototyping include automotive, aerospace, medical and dental (hearing-aid shells and surgical guides became mass applications), architecture, and consumer products. Related practice includes rapid tooling (making moulds and dies from printed patterns) and, in software engineering, an analogous concept of quickly building working models of applications.

Advantages and limitations

Rapid prototyping compresses development cycles from months to days, enables shapes impossible to machine (internal channels, lattices, consolidated assemblies), and makes one-off customisation economical. Limitations include restricted material properties compared with injection-moulded or machined parts, surface finish and layer lines, build-size constraints, and anisotropy — parts are weaker across layers than along them. As machines and materials improved, the boundary between prototyping and production blurred, and "additive manufacturing" became the broader industry term.
