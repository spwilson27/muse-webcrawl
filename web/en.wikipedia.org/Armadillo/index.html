Armadillo

Armadillos (Spanish for "little armoured ones") are New World placental mammals in the order Cingulata, characterised by a leathery armour shell of bony plates (osteoderms) in the skin. There are around 21 extant species, ranging from the tiny pink fairy armadillo (about 12 cm long) to the giant armadillo (up to 1.5 m). They are native to the Americas, from Argentina to the southern and central United States.

Biology

Armadillos have poor eyesight but a keen sense of smell, strong legs, and sharp claws used for digging burrows and digging up insects and other invertebrates, their main food. Most species cannot roll into a complete ball — contrary to popular belief, only the three-banded armadillos can — but they can leap vertically into the air when startled. The nine-banded armadillo, common in the United States, always gives birth to identical quadruplets from a single fertilised egg, a unique form of polyembryony.

Relation to humans

Armadillos are used in research on leprosy, since they are among the few animals that can contract the disease systemically and their low body temperature suits the bacterium; wild nine-banded armadillos in the southern United States can carry Mycobacterium leprae. Some species are hunted for food or their shells, and several are threatened by habitat loss. Their extinct relatives include the huge glyptodonts, which survived until the end of the last ice age.
