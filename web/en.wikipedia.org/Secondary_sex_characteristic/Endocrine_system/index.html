Endocrine system

The endocrine system is a messenger system comprising glands and organs that secrete hormones directly into the circulatory system to regulate distant target organs and tissues. It works alongside the nervous system to coordinate metabolism, growth and development, tissue function, sleep, mood, and sexual and reproductive processes.

Major endocrine glands: pineal, pituitary, thyroid, thymus, adrenals, pancreas, ovaries, and testes.

Major glands and hormones

The hypothalamus and pituitary gland form the master control axis of the system, releasing regulatory hormones that direct other glands. The thyroid gland produces thyroxine, governing metabolic rate; the parathyroids regulate calcium. The adrenal glands produce cortisol, aldosterone, and adrenaline. The pancreas secretes insulin and glucagon, controlling blood sugar. The gonads—testes and ovaries—produce the sex steroids (testosterone and estrogens) that drive puberty and the development of secondary sex characteristics. Other hormone sources include the pineal gland (melatonin), the thymus, and organs such as the heart, kidneys, and gut.

Signalling and feedback

Hormones act through specific receptors; steroid hormones typically bind intracellular receptors that alter gene expression, while peptide hormones bind cell-surface receptors and trigger second-messenger cascades. Most axes are regulated by negative feedback loops, such as the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. Disorders include diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism, Addison's disease, and Cushing's syndrome. The branch of medicine concerned with the system is endocrinology.
