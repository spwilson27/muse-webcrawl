Dihydrotestosterone (DHT)

Dihydrotestosterone (DHT, 5α-dihydrotestosterone, 5α-DHT, androstanolone or stanolone) is an endogenous androgen sex steroid and hormone. The enzyme 5α-reductase catalyzes the formation of DHT from testosterone in certain tissues including the prostate gland, seminal vesicles, epididymides, skin, hair follicles, liver, and brain. This enzyme mediates reduction of the C4-5 double bond of testosterone.

Relative to testosterone, DHT is considerably more potent as an agonist of the androgen receptor. DHT is essential for the development of male external genitalia in utero, growth of the prostate, and the development of certain secondary sex characteristics at puberty.

Biological activity

DHT binds to the androgen receptor with greater affinity than testosterone and dissociates more slowly, making it a more potent androgen on a molar basis. Unlike testosterone, DHT cannot be aromatized to estradiol, so its effects are purely androgenic.

Role in development

During fetal development, DHT mediates the differentiation of the external genitalia, including formation of the penis and scrotum and fusion of the labioscrotal folds. Individuals with 5α-reductase deficiency are born with undervirilized genitalia and typically virilize at puberty, when testosterone levels rise.

Clinical significance

DHT is implicated in benign prostatic hyperplasia and prostate cancer growth, and in androgenetic alopecia (pattern hair loss), in which genetically susceptible hair follicles miniaturize under DHT exposure. Drugs that inhibit 5α-reductase, such as finasteride and dutasteride, are used to treat these conditions. DHT itself, as androstanolone, has been used medically as an androgen and anabolic steroid.

Biosynthesis

DHT is produced from testosterone by the enzyme steroid 5α-reductase, of which three isozymes exist. Its synthesis is part of the broader pathway of steroidogenesis.
