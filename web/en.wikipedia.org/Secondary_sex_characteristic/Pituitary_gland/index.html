Pituitary gland


The pituitary gland sits in the sella turcica below the hypothalamus

The pituitary gland or hypophysis is an endocrine gland in vertebrates. In humans, the pituitary gland is of the size of a lentil and weighs about 0.5 grams (0.018 oz) on average. It is a protrusion off the bottom of the hypothalamus at the base of the brain. The hypophysis rests upon the hypophysial fossa of the sphenoid bone, in a depression known as the sella turcica.

The pituitary gland is often called the "master gland" because its hormones regulate many other endocrine glands and bodily processes, under the control of the hypothalamus.

Structure

The pituitary is divided into an anterior lobe (adenohypophysis), which develops from oral ectoderm and secretes hormones under the control of hypothalamic releasing factors, and a posterior lobe (neurohypophysis), which stores and releases oxytocin and vasopressin produced in the hypothalamus.

Hormones secreted

The anterior pituitary secretes growth hormone (GH), prolactin, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH, which drives cortisol production), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). LH and FSH regulate the gonads and are central to puberty, reproduction, and the menstrual cycle; their deficiency causes central hypogonadism.

Clinical significance

Pituitary adenomas are the most common pituitary disorders and may cause hormone excess (such as acromegaly from GH, Cushing's disease from ACTH, or hyperprolactinemia from prolactin) or hypopituitarism from compression of normal tissue. Diagnosis and management fall within endocrinology and neurosurgery.
