Steroid hormone

A steroid hormone is a steroid that acts as a hormone. Steroid hormones share the characteristic four-ring (gonane) carbon skeleton derived from cholesterol and are lipid-soluble, allowing them to pass through cell membranes and bind to intracellular receptors that regulate gene expression.

The steroidogenesis pathway from cholesterol to the five classes of steroid hormones.

Classes

Vertebrate steroid hormones are grouped into five classes by the receptors they bind: glucocorticoids (e.g., cortisol, regulating metabolism and immune response), mineralocorticoids (e.g., aldosterone, regulating salt and water balance), androgens (e.g., testosterone), estrogens (e.g., estradiol), and progestogens (e.g., progesterone). The corticosteroids are made in the adrenal cortex and the sex steroids in the gonads and placenta, all under regulation by the endocrine system.

Synthesis and mechanism

All steroid hormones are synthesized from cholesterol, largely in the mitochondria and smooth endoplasmic reticulum of steroidogenic tissues, through a cascade of cytochrome P450 and hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase enzymes (steroidogenesis). Because they cannot be stored in vesicles, they are released as made. In the blood they travel bound to carrier proteins. Their receptors are transcription factors: on binding, the hormone–receptor complex enters the nucleus and alters the expression of target genes, which is why steroid effects develop over hours rather than seconds. Sex steroids drive puberty and the appearance of secondary sex characteristics. Synthetic steroids are widely used as medicines and include anti-inflammatory corticosteroids, contraceptives, and anabolic steroids.
