Primary sex characteristic


The primary sex organs are directly involved in reproduction

Primary sex characteristics are the anatomical structures directly involved in reproduction. In humans and other mammals, these are the gonads (testes in males, ovaries in females) and the internal and external reproductive organs, including the penis and scrotum in males and the vagina, uterus, and fallopian tubes in females.

Unlike secondary sex characteristics — such as facial hair, breast development, and voice deepening, which emerge at puberty — primary sex characteristics are present from birth, having developed during embryonic and fetal life.

Development

Sexual differentiation of the primary sex organs occurs in utero under the influence of the SRY gene on the Y chromosome, which directs testis formation. Testicular hormones — testosterone and anti-Müllerian hormone — then drive development of the male internal ducts and external genitalia, with dihydrotestosterone essential for the external structures. In the absence of these signals, development proceeds along the female pathway.

Function at puberty

Although present from birth, the primary sex organs mature functionally at puberty, when rising gonadotropins from the pituitary gland activate gamete production: spermatogenesis in the testes and the ovarian cycle culminating in ovulation and the menstrual cycle in females. Disorders of gonadal function produce hypogonadism.
