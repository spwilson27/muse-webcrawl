Menarche


Secular decline in the average age of menarche

Menarche (from Ancient Greek μήν (mēn, 'month') and ἀρχή (arkhē, 'beginning')) is the first menstrual cycle, or first menstrual bleeding, in female humans. From both social and medical perspectives, it is often considered the central event of female puberty, as it signals the possibility of fertility.

Menarche typically occurs between the ages of 10 and 16, with the average age around 12 to 13 years in most developed countries. It is a late event in the sequence of pubertal development, occurring after the development of breast buds, pubic hair, and the adolescent growth spurt, typically corresponding to stage IV on the Tanner scale.

Physiology

Menarche results from maturation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis. Pulsatile release of gonadotropin-releasing hormone from the hypothalamus stimulates the pituitary gland to secrete LH and FSH, which drive ovarian estrogen production and endometrial growth; the first shedding of the endometrium marks menarche and the beginning of the menstrual cycle. Early cycles are frequently anovulatory and irregular.

Timing

The timing of menarche is influenced by genetics, nutrition, body composition, and general health. There is a well-documented secular trend: average age at menarche fell from around 16–17 years in the mid-nineteenth century to around 12–13 years by the late twentieth century in industrialized countries, a change attributed mainly to improved nutrition and childhood health. Earlier or later menarche outside the normal range may prompt evaluation for precocious puberty or delayed puberty.

Cultural aspects

In many cultures, menarche is marked by rites of passage or celebrations, reflecting its social significance as a transition to womanhood. In other contexts it is surrounded by taboos and restrictions. Educational preparation for menarche is an important part of puberty education.
