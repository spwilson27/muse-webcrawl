Hormone


Epinephrine (adrenaline), a classic hormone

A hormone (from the Greek participle ὁρμῶν, "setting in motion") is a class of signaling molecules in multicellular organisms that are sent to distant organs or tissues by complex biological processes to regulate physiology and behavior. Hormones are required for the correct development of animals, plants, and fungi. Due to the broad definition of a hormone (as a signaling molecule that exerts its effects far from its site of production), numerous kinds of molecules can be classified as hormones. Among the substances that can be considered hormones are eicosanoids, steroids, amino acid derivatives, peptides, and proteins.

The cells that secrete hormones comprise the endocrine system. Examples of hormones include insulin, which regulates blood glucose; cortisol, the stress hormone; and the sex hormones such as testosterone and progesterone.

Overview

Hormonal signaling involves biosynthesis of a particular hormone in a particular tissue, storage and secretion of the hormone, transport to the target cell(s), recognition by an associated hormone receptor protein, relay and amplification of the signal, and breakdown of the hormone itself. Hormones are typically released from endocrine glands into the bloodstream, although some act locally on neighboring cells (paracrine signaling) or on the secreting cell itself (autocrine signaling).

Classes

Hormones are chemically classified as peptides (such as insulin and growth hormone), steroids (such as cortisol, testosterone, and estradiol, produced through steroidogenesis), and amino-acid derivatives (such as epinephrine and thyroxine). Steroid hormones are lipid-soluble and pass through cell membranes to bind intracellular receptors, while peptide hormones bind receptors on the cell surface.

Regulation

Hormone secretion is typically governed by feedback loops, most notably negative feedback, in which rising levels of a hormone suppress its own further release. The hypothalamus and pituitary gland form a master control axis for much of the endocrine system, the subject of endocrinology.

Effects

Hormones drive development, including puberty and the appearance of secondary sex characteristics; regulate metabolism, growth, appetite, and fluid balance; modulate immune function and mood; and coordinate reproduction, including the menstrual cycle.

Discovery

The concept of internal secretion was developed in the nineteenth century. In 1902, William Bayliss and Ernest Starling identified secretin, the first substance to be described as a hormone; Starling introduced the word "hormone" in 1905.
