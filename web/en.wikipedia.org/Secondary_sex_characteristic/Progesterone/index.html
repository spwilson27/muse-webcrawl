Progesterone


Progesterone, the principal progestogen

Progesterone (P4) is an endogenous steroid and progestogen sex hormone involved in the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and embryogenesis of humans and other species. It belongs to a group of steroid hormones called the progestogens and is the major progestogen in the body.

Progesterone has a variety of important functions in the body. It is also a crucial metabolic intermediate in the production of other endogenous steroids, including the sex hormones and the corticosteroids, and plays an important role in brain function as a neurosteroid.

Biological functions

Progesterone is produced principally by the corpus luteum of the ovary after ovulation, and by the placenta during pregnancy. Its chief function is to convert the endometrium to a secretory state receptive to implantation and to maintain pregnancy by suppressing uterine contractility. It also raises basal body temperature and prepares the mammary glands for lactation.

Biosynthesis

Progesterone is synthesized from pregnenolone, itself derived from cholesterol, as part of steroidogenesis. It in turn serves as a precursor of aldosterone, cortisol, and the androgens and estrogens.

Medical use

Progesterone and synthetic progestins are used in hormonal contraception, hormone replacement therapy, prevention of preterm birth in selected pregnancies, and treatment of certain gynecological disorders. Progesterone levels are also measured in the evaluation of ovulation and fertility.

Levels across the cycle

Progesterone is low during the follicular phase of the menstrual cycle and rises markedly after ovulation; failure of this rise suggests anovulation. The onset of cycling at menarche is often anovulatory, so progesterone peaks become regular only later in adolescence.
