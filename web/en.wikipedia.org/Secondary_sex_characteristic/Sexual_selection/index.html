Sexual selection and secondary sex characteristics

Sexual selection
Proposed byCharles Darwin (1859, 1871)
MechanismsMate choice (intersexual); contest competition (intrasexual)
Traits affectedOrnaments, weapons, displays, vocalizations
Classic exampleThe peacock's train

Sexual selection is a mode of natural selection in which members of one sex choose mates of the other sex (intersexual selection) or compete with members of the same sex for access to mates (intrasexual selection). It is the principal explanation for the evolution of secondary sex characteristics — traits that distinguish the sexes but are not directly part of the reproductive system, such as the peacock's train, the stag's antlers, the lion's mane, and the bright plumage of many male birds.

Darwin's theory

Charles Darwin introduced sexual selection in On the Origin of Species (1859) and developed it fully in The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex (1871). He proposed it to explain extravagant traits that seemed to hinder survival and therefore could not be products of ordinary natural selection. Alfred Russel Wallace remained sceptical, preferring camouflage-based explanations, and the mechanism of female choice in particular was long neglected before being revived in the 20th century.

Mechanisms

Under intersexual selection, preferences of the choosing sex — usually females — drive the evolution of ornaments in the chosen sex. Fisherian runaway selection models a self-reinforcing loop between preference and trait, while "good genes" or indicator models propose that costly ornaments honestly signal health and genetic quality, as in Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle. Under intrasexual selection, competition — combat, intimidation, or sperm competition — favours weapons, large body size, and other armaments.

Secondary sex characteristics

Secondary sex characteristics affected by sexual selection include antlers and horns used in contests, elaborate courtship displays and songs, bright coloration, and in humans, traits such as facial hair and voice pitch that develop at puberty under hormonal control. Because sexually selected traits are costly, they often trade off against survival, producing the conspicuous sexual dimorphism seen across the animal kingdom.
