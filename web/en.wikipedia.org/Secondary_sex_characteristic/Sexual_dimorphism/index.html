Sexual dimorphism


The peacock's train is a classic example of sexual dimorphism

Sexual dimorphism is the condition where sexes of the same species exhibit different morphological characteristics, particularly characteristics not directly involved in reproduction. The condition occurs in most dioecious species, which are most animals and some plants. Differences may include secondary sex characteristics, size, weight, color, markings, or behavioral or cognitive traits.

These differences may be subtle or exaggerated and may be subject to sexual selection and natural selection. The opposite of dimorphism is monomorphism, when both biological sexes are phenotypically indistinguishable from each other.

Examples

Ornamentation and coloration are common: male birds are frequently more brightly colored than females, and male deer bear antlers. Extreme size dimorphism occurs in elephant seals, where males may be several times heavier than females, and in many insects and spiders, where females are the larger sex. In anglerfish, tiny males fuse to the female.

Humans

Humans show moderate sexual dimorphism. Men are on average taller and heavier, with greater upper-body muscle mass, more body and facial hair, and deeper voices; women have wider pelvises, greater subcutaneous fat deposition, and developed mammary glands. These traits develop under the influence of sex hormones — androgens and estrogens — during fetal life and puberty, and are quantified in developmental studies such as the Tanner scale.

Evolutionary causes

Charles Darwin proposed sexual selection in The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex (1871) to explain dimorphic traits that seemed costly for survival: traits may evolve because they aid competition for mates (intrasexual selection) or increase attractiveness to the opposite sex (intersexual selection).
