Secondary sex characteristic

FieldBiology, human development
OnsetPuberty
Regulated bySex hormones (oestrogens, androgens)
ContrastPrimary sex characteristics (reproductive organs)

Secondary sex characteristics are features that appear during puberty in humans and at sexual maturity in other animals. They distinguish the two sexes of a species but, unlike primary sex characteristics, are not directly part of the reproductive system. Examples include the development of breasts and widening of hips in females, facial hair and deepening of the voice in males, and in other species such features as the peacock's tail or the lion's mane.

In humans

In females, oestrogens drive breast development, the widening of the pelvis, and changes in body fat distribution. In males, testosterone causes growth of facial and body hair, increased muscle mass, a deepened voice due to enlargement of the larynx, and broadening of the shoulders. In both sexes, pubic and underarm hair develop, and sweat glands become more active.

Evolutionary significance

Charles Darwin proposed that many secondary sex characteristics evolved through sexual selection: traits that make an individual more attractive to mates, or more formidable to rivals, can spread even when they carry survival costs. The handicap principle suggests that extravagant displays honestly signal genetic fitness precisely because they are costly to maintain.
