Steroid


The steroid skeleton: three cyclohexane rings and one cyclopentane ring

A steroid is an organic compound with four fused rings arranged in a specific molecular configuration. Steroids have two principal biological functions: as important components of cell membranes that alter membrane fluidity, and as signaling molecules. Hundreds of steroids are found in plants, animals, and fungi. All steroids are manufactured in cells from the sterols lanosterol (opisthokonts) or cycloartenol (plants).

The steroid nucleus (gonane) consists of three cyclohexane rings and one cyclopentane ring joined in a characteristic arrangement. Variation in attached functional groups gives rise to the many steroid classes.

Classes

Important classes of steroid hormones include the corticosteroids (glucocorticoids such as cortisol, and mineralocorticoids such as aldosterone) and the sex steroids: androgens, estrogens, and progestogens such as progesterone. Cholesterol is the most abundant sterol and the precursor of all steroid hormones.

Biosynthesis

Steroid hormones are synthesized from cholesterol in the gonads, adrenal glands, and placenta through steroidogenesis, a network of enzymatic steps including side-chain cleavage, hydroxylations, and aromatization.

Medical uses

Steroid drugs are widely used: corticosteroids as anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive agents, anabolic steroids for muscle wasting and hypogonadism, and steroid hormones in contraception and hormone replacement therapy. In common usage, "steroids" often refers either to corticosteroids or to anabolic steroids depending on context.
