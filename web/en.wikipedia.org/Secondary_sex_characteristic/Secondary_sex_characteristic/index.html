Secondary sex characteristic

Secondary sex characteristics are features that appear during puberty in humans and at sexual maturity in other animals, distinguishing the sexes of a species but not directly part of the reproductive system. In contrast, primary sex characteristics are the sex organs themselves, present from birth.

Secondary sex characteristics vary widely across species, from manes and antlers to human facial hair and breasts.

In humans

Secondary sex characteristics are driven by sex hormones secreted by the gonads under control of the endocrine system. In females, estrogens promote breast development, widening of the hips, and a higher proportion of body fat. In males, testosterone promotes growth of facial and body hair, deepening of the voice as the larynx enlarges, increased muscle mass and bone density, and broadening of the shoulders. In both sexes, pubic and axillary hair develop under the influence of adrenal and gonadal androgens, and the apocrine sweat glands become active.

In other animals

Across the animal kingdom, secondary sex characteristics include the mane of the male lion, the antlers of male deer, bright breeding plumage in many birds, enlarged claws or tusks, and size differences between the sexes (sexual dimorphism). Charles Darwin proposed that many such traits evolved through sexual selection, either by competition within a sex or by mate choice, even when the traits carry survival costs.
