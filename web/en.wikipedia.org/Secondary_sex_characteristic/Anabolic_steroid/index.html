Anabolic steroid


Testosterone, the prototypical anabolic steroid

Anabolic steroids, also known more properly as anabolic–androgenic steroids (AAS), are steroidal androgens that include natural androgens like testosterone as well as synthetic androgens that are structurally related and have similar effects to testosterone. They increase protein within cells, especially in skeletal muscles, and also have varying degrees of androgenic and virilizing effects, including induction of the development and maintenance of masculine secondary sexual characteristics such as the growth of facial and body hair and deepening of the voice.

Anabolic steroids were first isolated in the 1930s, and are now used therapeutically in medicine to stimulate muscle growth and appetite, induce male puberty, and treat chronic wasting conditions such as cancer and AIDS. The American College of Sports Medicine acknowledges that AAS, in the presence of adequate diet, can contribute to increases in body weight, often as lean mass increases, and that gains in muscle strength achieved through high-intensity exercise and proper diet can additionally occur.

Medical uses

Since the discovery and synthesis of testosterone in the 1930s, AAS have been used by physicians for many purposes, with varying degrees of success. These include treatment of hypogonadism, delayed puberty, certain types of impotence, wasting in HIV-positive individuals, osteoporosis in older men, aplastic anemia, endometriosis, and hereditary angioedema.

Non-medical uses

Most steroid users are not competitive athletes. In the United States, between 1 million and 3 million people (1% of the population) are thought to have used AAS. The majority of users are male, though female use has been on the rise. Non-medical use is common in bodybuilding, powerlifting, and other sports where muscle mass and strength confer an advantage. Their use is prohibited by the rules of most international sports organizations, and detection methods include urine and hair testing.

Adverse effects

Known possible adverse effects of AAS include cardiovascular disease, liver dysfunction, behavioral changes such as increased aggression, suppression of natural testosterone production, gynecomastia, acne, and — in adolescents — premature closure of the growth plates. In women, virilizing effects such as deepening of the voice and increased body hair may be irreversible.

Pharmacology

Anabolic steroids act by binding to and activating the androgen receptor, which regulates gene expression and increases the production of proteins involved in muscle growth. They may also act by competitive antagonism at the glucocorticoid receptor, inhibiting protein breakdown. AAS are typically administered orally or by intramuscular injection.

History

The development of anabolic steroids dates to the 1930s, when testosterone was first isolated and synthesized. The first known mention of anabolic steroids in a United States weightlifting magazine occurred in 1938. During the 1950s, the Soviet Union's weightlifting successes were attributed to testosterone use, prompting American sports physicians to develop anabolic steroids such as methandrostenolone (Dianabol), introduced in 1958.
