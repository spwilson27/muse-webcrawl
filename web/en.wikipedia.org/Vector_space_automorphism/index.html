Vector space automorphism

In linear algebra, an automorphism of a vector space is an invertible linear transformation of the space to itself: a bijective linear map T : V → V. Automorphisms are the symmetries of a vector space that preserve its linear structure — addition and scalar multiplication — and they form a group under composition.

The general linear group

The set of all automorphisms of a vector space V forms the general linear group GL(V). For an n-dimensional space over a field F, choosing a basis identifies GL(V) with GL(n, F), the group of invertible n × n matrices over F. A linear map is an automorphism if and only if its matrix in any basis has nonzero determinant:

T ∈ GL(V) ⟺ det(T) ≠ 0

Structure and examples

Rotations, reflections, dilations, and shears of the plane (with nonzero scaling) are automorphisms of ℝ2. Every automorphism maps bases to bases, preserves linear independence, dimension, and the lattice of subspaces, though it may change lengths and angles unless it is also an isometry. In infinite-dimensional spaces an injective linear self-map need not be surjective, so the definition of automorphism requires bijectivity outright.

Role in mathematics

Automorphism groups encode the symmetries of mathematical objects; for vector spaces, GL(V) is the archetypal Lie group and the starting point for the theory of linear algebraic groups and representations. Change of basis in linear algebra is precisely the action of GL(V) on the set of bases of V.
