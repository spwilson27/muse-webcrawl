Nitrogen

Nitrogen is a chemical element; it has symbol N and atomic number 7. It is a nonmetal and the lightest member of group 15 of the periodic table, often called the pnictogens. It is a common element in the universe, estimated at seventh in total abundance in the Milky Way and the Solar System.

Properties

At standard temperature and pressure, two atoms of the element bond to form N2, a colourless and odourless diatomic gas. Dinitrogen forms about 78% of Earth's atmosphere, making it the most abundant uncombined element in air. The strong triple bond in N2 makes the gas largely inert at room temperature.

History

Nitrogen was formally discovered by Scottish physician Daniel Rutherford in 1772, who called it noxious air. Antoine Lavoisier later named it azote, from the Greek for "no life". The English name derives from the Greek nitron and genes, referring to its presence in nitre and nitric acid.

Compounds and applications

Nitrogen compounds are central to life: nitrogen is a component of amino acids, proteins and nucleic acids. Fixation of atmospheric nitrogen by the Haber process produces ammonia for fertilisers on an enormous industrial scale. Other important compounds include nitrous oxide, nitric acid, nitrates and cyanides. Liquid nitrogen is widely used as a cryogenic coolant, and nitrogen gas is used as an inert atmosphere for food packaging and electronics manufacture.

Biological role

The nitrogen cycle describes the movement of the element through the atmosphere, terrestrial and marine ecosystems. Certain bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen into biologically usable forms, while denitrifying bacteria return it to the air.
