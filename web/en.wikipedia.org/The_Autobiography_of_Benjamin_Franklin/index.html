The Autobiography of Benjamin Franklin

AuthorBenjamin Franklin
LanguageEnglish (first published in French)
GenreAutobiography

The Autobiography of Benjamin Franklin is the traditional name for the unfinished record of Benjamin Franklin's own life, written between 1771 and 1790. Franklin addressed the work to his son William, initially as a letter, and composed it in four parts across nearly two decades — in England in 1771, in France in 1784, and in Philadelphia in 1788–1790. It breaks off in 1757, well before the American Revolution and Franklin's diplomatic career, yet it became one of the most influential works of American literature and the model of the "self-made man" narrative.

Contents

The narrative follows Franklin from his Boston childhood and apprenticeship to his elder brother James, through his runaway journey to Philadelphia, his rise as a printer and publisher of Poor Richard's Almanack and the Pennsylvania Gazette, and his civic projects — the Library Company, the fire company, the academy that became the University of Pennsylvania. A famous section describes his "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection", a scheme of thirteen virtues (temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquillity, chastity, humility) tracked in a homemade chart of daily faults.

Publication and legacy

Franklin never published the work; a French translation appeared in Paris in 1791, and the first English edition followed in London in 1793. The complete text was not published from the original manuscript until John Bigelow's edition of 1868. Critics have praised its lucid, witty style and its democratic voice, while noting its self-fashioning and omissions. It remains a foundational text of American autobiography and the source of Franklin's canonical image as printer, scientist, and practical philosopher.
