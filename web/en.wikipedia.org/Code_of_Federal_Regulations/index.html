Code of Federal Regulations

The Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) is the codification of the general and permanent rules and regulations (sometimes called administrative law) published in the Federal Register by the executive departments and agencies of the federal government of the United States. The CFR is divided into 50 titles that represent broad areas subject to federal regulation. It is published by the Office of the Federal Register, part of the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA), and is updated on a staggered annual schedule.

History

The CFR was authorised by the Federal Register Act of 1935, the same statute that created the daily Federal Register. Before systematic codification, federal rules were scattered through individual publications, making the law difficult to find and follow. The first edition of the CFR, covering regulations in force as of June 1, 1938, was published beginning in 1938 and completed in 1939. A crisis in 1934 — when the Supreme Court decided cases in which the government had difficulty producing the text of regulations it sought to enforce — had demonstrated the need for an authoritative, organised publication. The Administrative Procedure Act of 1946 later standardised the rulemaking process that feeds material into the CFR.

The United States Government Publishing Office (GPO) prints and distributes the paper edition, while the Government Publishing Office and NARA jointly maintain the online eCFR, which is updated daily and is widely used, although the official legal edition remains the printed annual revision together with the Federal Register.

Structure

The CFR is organised into 50 titles, each covering a broad subject area, such as Title 7 (Agriculture), Title 21 (Food and Drugs), Title 29 (Labor), Title 40 (Protection of Environment), and Title 49 (Transportation). Titles are subdivided into chapters, usually bearing the name of the issuing agency, then parts and sections. A citation such as "21 CFR 1308.11" refers to Title 21, Part 1308, Section 11. Some titles preserve the structure of their authorising statutes; others reflect agency organisation.

Rules enter the CFR through the rulemaking process. Agencies publish proposed rules and final rules in the Federal Register; final rules with general applicability and legal effect are then incorporated into the CFR at their next revision. Because only final, effective rules appear in the CFR, a complete picture of current law on a topic may require consulting the Federal Register for recent amendments as well — a task aided by the "List of CFR Sections Affected" (LSA), published monthly.

Revision schedule

The CFR is revised annually, but the 50 titles are updated on a quarterly rotation: Titles 1–16 are revised as of January 1, Titles 17–27 as of April 1, Titles 28–41 as of July 1, and Titles 42–50 as of October 1. Each annual edition uses a different cover colour so that users can tell at a glance whether a volume is current. The eCFR, by contrast, incorporates amendments on a near-daily basis and is the most common way practitioners consult the code, though it carries a disclaimer that it is an unofficial editorial compilation.

Significance

Along with the United States Code, which codifies statutes enacted by Congress, the CFR forms the backbone of published federal law. Its rules govern matters ranging from food labelling and drug approval to workplace safety, environmental emissions, aviation, and immigration. Courts generally treat duly promulgated CFR regulations as having the force of law. Under the doctrine associated with the Supreme Court's decision in Chevron U.S.A. v. NRDC (1984), courts for decades deferred to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes, a framework overturned in 2024 in Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, which reshaped how much authority agency rules codified in the CFR carry in judicial review.
