Alexandrine

The alexandrine was the dominant metre of French classical tragedy.

An alexandrine is a line of poetic metre comprising twelve syllables — in English and German verse generally counted as iambic hexameter. It is the most important line in French poetry, where it dominated classical drama and epic for centuries, and it is widely used in Dutch, German, and other European verse traditions.

Origin of the name

The name derives from the Roman d'Alexandre, a 12th-century cycle of Old French romances about Alexander the Great written in twelve-syllable lines. The metre rose to pre-eminence in France in the 16th century with the poets of the Pléiade, and it became obligatory for tragedy and serious verse in the age of Corneille and Racine.

Structure

In its classical French form, the alexandrine consists of two hemistichs of six syllables each, divided by a fixed pause, the caesura. Rhymes typically alternate between masculine and feminine couplets (rimes plates) in classical tragedy. Strict rules governed the treatment of mute e, hiatus, and enjambment; Romantic poets such as Victor Hugo famously loosened these rules, displacing the caesura to produce the alexandrin ternaire with three measures instead of two.

In English verse

English alexandrines are lines of iambic hexameter (twelve syllables, six iambic feet). They appear most famously as the closing line of the Spenserian stanza in Edmund Spenser's The Faerie Queene, and occasionally in the heroic couplets of Dryden and Pope. Alexander Pope mocked the form's lumbering effect in An Essay on Criticism:

A needless Alexandrine ends the song,
That, like a wounded snake, drags its slow length along.

The second line of the couplet is itself an alexandrine, enacting the fault it describes. George Gordon, Lord Byron, likewise joked in Don Juan about versifying "in a fit of Alexandrine".

Other traditions

The alexandrine is the principal line of Dutch Golden Age drama (Joost van den Vondel) and of Polish epic poetry: the national epic Pan Tadeusz by Adam Mickiewicz is written in thirteen-syllable lines often counted as the Polish alexandrine. In German, the Alexandriner was a standard metre of Baroque poetry.
