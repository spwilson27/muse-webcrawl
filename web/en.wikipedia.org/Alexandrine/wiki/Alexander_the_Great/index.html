Alexander the Great

Alexander III of Macedon (20/21 July 356 BC – 10/11 June 323 BC), commonly known as Alexander the Great, was a king of the ancient Greek kingdom of Macedon. He succeeded his father Philip II to the throne in 336 BC at the age of 20 and spent most of his ruling years conducting a lengthy military campaign throughout Western Asia, Central Asia, parts of South Asia, and Egypt. By the age of thirty, he had created one of the largest empires in history, stretching from Greece to northwestern India.

Conquests

Alexander was undefeated in battle and is widely considered to be one of history's greatest and most successful military commanders. He broke the power of the Achaemenid Persian Empire at the battles of Issus (333 BC) and Gaugamela (331 BC), conquered Egypt — where he founded Alexandria — and advanced through Mesopotamia and Persia into Bactria and the Punjab, defeating King Porus at the Hydaspes in 326 BC. His army's refusal to march further east forced a return journey through the Gedrosian desert.

Death and legacy

Alexander died in Babylon in 323 BC, aged 32, without a clear heir; his empire was divided among his generals, the Diadochi, inaugurating the Hellenistic period. His campaigns spread Greek culture, language, and cities across the Near East, and his legend permeated the literature of many cultures for two millennia.
