Alexander Pope

Alexander Pope (21 May 1688 – 30 May 1744) was an English poet, translator and satirist of the Enlightenment era, regarded as one of the greatest English poets. He is best known for his satirical and discursive poetry, his translations of Homer, and his mastery of the heroic couplet.

Born21 May 1688, London, England
Died30 May 1744, Twickenham, England
OccupationPoet, translator, satirist
Notable worksThe Rape of the Lock, The Dunciad, An Essay on Criticism

Life

Pope was born to a Catholic family in London at a time when anti-Catholic laws restricted his education; he was largely self-taught. A childhood illness (Pott's disease) left him deformed and only about 4 feet 6 inches tall, but he rose quickly to literary fame with the Pastorals (1709) and An Essay on Criticism (1711).

Works

The Rape of the Lock (1712–1714) turned a trivial social quarrel into a mock-heroic masterpiece. His subscription translations of the Iliad (1715–1720) and Odyssey (1725–1726) made him financially independent. Later satires include The Dunciad (1728–1743), An Essay on Man (1733–34) and the Imitations of Horace. At his villa in Twickenham he built a famous garden and grotto.

Legacy

Pope is the second most quoted writer in the Oxford Dictionary of Quotations, after Shakespeare. Lines such as "To err is human; to forgive, divine" and "A little learning is a dang'rous thing" entered common speech, and his polished couplets defined Augustan verse.
