Metre (poetry)

In poetry, metre (Commonwealth spelling) or meter (American spelling; see difference) is the basic rhythmic structure of a verse or verse line. Many traditional verse forms prescribe a specific verse metre, or a certain set of metres alternating in a particular order. The study and the actual use of metres and forms of versification are both known as prosody.

Types of metre

Metres are classified by the pattern that repeats in each line. In the quantitative metre of classical Greek and Latin poetry, the pattern is of long and short syllables, as in the dactylic hexameter of epic. In the accentual-syllabic metre of most English verse, lines are measured by both the number of syllables and the pattern of stresses, organized into feet: iambs (da-DUM), trochees (DUM-da), dactyls, anapaests, and spondees, counted as dimeter, trimeter, tetrameter, pentameter, and so on. Syllabic verse, as in French, counts syllables alone — the alexandrine has twelve; accentual verse, as in Old English, counts stresses alone.

Features

Within the line, the caesura marks a customary pause; enjambment runs the syntax past a line ending, creating tension with the metre. Poets deviate from strict patterns through substitution, inversion, and extra or missing syllables, and critics analyse these effects as part of the poem's expressive form. Free verse abandons regular metre while retaining lineation.
