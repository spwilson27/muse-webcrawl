Blank verse

Blank verse is poetry written with a regular meter — in English, almost always unrhymed iambic pentameter — but with no rhyme scheme. It is the verse form closest to natural speech rhythms in English and has been called the principal metre of English dramatic and epic poetry.

MeterIambic pentameter (typically)
RhymeNone
First used in EnglishHenry Howard, Earl of Surrey (c. 1540s)

History

The term derives from the French vers blanc. Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey introduced blank verse into English in his translation of books of Virgil's Aeneid, adapting Italian versi sciolti. Christopher Marlowe brought it to the stage, and Shakespeare made it the dominant medium of English drama, varying it with enjambment and metrical substitutions.

Major practitioners

Milton defended his choice of blank verse for Paradise Lost (1667), calling rhyme "the invention of a barbarous age." Wordsworth used it for The Prelude, Tennyson for Idylls of the King and dramatic monologues, and Robert Frost for much of his narrative poetry. It remains common in modern verse drama and long poems.

Characteristics

Because sentences can run across lines without the closure imposed by rhyme, blank verse readily accommodates extended syntax, rhetorical argument and dramatic speech, while its steady beat supplies form and momentum.
