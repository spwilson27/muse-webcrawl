La Pléiade

La Pléiade was a group of 16th-century French Renaissance poets, named after the ancient Alexandrian Pléiade of seven tragic poets and the seven stars of the Pleiades. The core members were Pierre de Ronsard, Joachim du Bellay, and Jean-Antoine de Baïf, with Pontus de Tyard, Étienne Jodelle, Rémi Belleau, and Jean Dorat (their teacher) commonly counted among the seven.

Programme

The group's manifesto, Du Bellay's Défense et illustration de la langue française (1549), argued that French — then considered a "barbarous" vernacular — could rival Greek and Latin as a literary language if enriched by coinage, borrowing, and the imitation of classical and Italian models. The Pléiade championed the alexandrine, the ode, and the sonnet, and sought to create a French epic to match Virgil's Aeneid; Ronsard's unfinished Franciade was the result.

Achievements and influence

Ronsard, hailed as "prince of poets," produced the Odes (1550) and the love sequences Les Amours; Du Bellay wrote the sonnet cycles L'Olive and, after a disillusioning stay in Rome, Les Regrets (1558). The Pléiade's efforts permanently elevated the status of French as a literary language and set the course for French classical poetry, even though the following century's classicism reacted against some of their exuberance.
