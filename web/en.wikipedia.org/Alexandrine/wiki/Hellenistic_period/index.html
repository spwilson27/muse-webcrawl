Hellenistic period

The Hellenistic period covers the history of the Mediterranean world and western Asia between the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC and the emergence of the Roman Empire, conventionally dated to the Battle of Actium in 31 BC and Rome's annexation of Ptolemaic Egypt in 30 BC. Greek culture spread widely and fused with local cultures across Alexander's former empire.

Wars of the Diadochi

After Alexander's death, his generals (the Diadochi) fought decades of wars that partitioned the empire. By about 281 BC three great dynasties had stabilized: the Ptolemies in Egypt, the Seleucids in Asia, and the Antigonids in Macedon, alongside smaller kingdoms such as Pergamon, Bactria and Pontus.

Culture and science

Hellenistic cities such as Alexandria, Antioch and Pergamon were centres of learning and art. The period produced Euclid's geometry, Archimedes' mathematics and engineering, Eratosthenes' measurement of the Earth, and Aristarchus's heliocentric hypothesis; philosophy flourished in the schools of Epicureanism and Stoicism, and sculpture and literature took on more emotional and cosmopolitan forms.

Roman expansion

Rome progressively defeated the Hellenistic kingdoms — Macedon in 168 BC, the Seleucids by the 60s BC, and finally Ptolemaic Egypt in 30 BC — while Hellenistic Greek culture deeply shaped the Roman world that absorbed it.
