Heroic couplet

A heroic couplet is a traditional form for English poetry, commonly used in epic and narrative poetry, consisting of a pair of rhyming lines in iambic pentameter. The form was pioneered by Geoffrey Chaucer in the Legend of Good Women and the Canterbury Tales, and was later perfected by John Dryden and Alexander Pope in the Restoration and Augustan periods.

Characteristics

The name derives from the form's use in English translations and imitations of classical epic "heroic" poetry. In its Augustan perfection the couplet is typically "closed": each pair of lines forms a complete syntactic and rhetorical unit, often balanced by parallelism and antithesis, with the rhyme chiming at the end of a completed thought. Pope's An Essay on Criticism and The Rape of the Lock exemplify the form's wit and compression.

Variations and decline

Poets from Dryden onward sometimes used triplets and alexandrines for variety. The Romantic poets largely abandoned the closed heroic couplet in favour of blank verse and looser forms, though Byron revived it for satire. The term also covers the French alexandrine couplet used in classical tragedy, divided by a caesura into two hemistichs.
