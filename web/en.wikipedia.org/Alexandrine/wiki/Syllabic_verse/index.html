Syllabic verse

Syllabic verse is a poetic form regulated by the number of syllables in each line rather than by a pattern of stresses. It is the basis of verse in languages such as French, Japanese and Polish, where syllable count is perceptually salient; in English, whose native tradition is accentual and accentual-syllabic, syllabics are a deliberate, artificial experiment.

PrincipleFixed syllable count per line
Typical traditionsFrench alexandrine (12 syllables), Japanese haiku (5-7-5)
English practitionersMarianne Moore, Dylan Thomas, W. H. Auden

In French and Japanese

Classical French verse counts syllables: the alexandrine has twelve, with a caesura after the sixth. Japanese forms such as haiku and tanka are organized by counts of sound units (on) in patterns of 5-7-5 and 5-7-5-7-7.

In English

Because the English ear hears stress more than syllable count, purely syllabic poems can appear almost free to listeners. Marianne Moore built intricate stanzas of identical syllable counts; Dylan Thomas and Auden also used syllabic stanzas, and Thom Gunn wrote notable syllabics.

Related concepts

Syllabic verse contrasts with accentual verse (counting stresses only), accentual-syllabic verse (counting both, as in most traditional English meters), quantitative verse (based on syllable length, as in Greek and Latin), and free verse.
