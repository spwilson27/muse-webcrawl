Alexandrine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Alexandrine

Type: Syllabic verse line

Length: 12 syllables (French); iambic hexameter in English

Structure: Two hemistichs of six syllables, divided by a caesura

Named after: The medieval Roman d'Alexandre

Alexandrine is a name used for several distinct types of verse line with related metrical structures, of which the most common is the twelve-syllable French alexandrine. The form takes its name from its use in the Roman d'Alexandre, a collection of Old French romances about the life of Alexander the Great composed in the twelfth century. In French poetry the alexandrine became the pre-eminent line for epic, tragedy, and elevated verse generally, occupying a position comparable to that of iambic pentameter in English or the dactylic hexameter of classical epic.

In its classical French form, the alexandrine consists of twelve syllables divided by a caesura (a pause in the middle of the line) into two hemistichs of six syllables each. Because French metre is syllabic rather than accentual, what matters is the count of syllables rather than the pattern of stresses. In English, where metre is accentual-syllabic, the term "alexandrine" refers instead to a line of iambic hexameter — six iambic feet, also totalling twelve syllables — which is used chiefly as a variation line, most famously as the closing line of the Spenserian stanza.

French alexandrine

The French alexandrine is governed by rules that were codified during the classical period of the seventeenth century. The line must contain twelve syllables, counting mute e according to strict conventions: a mute e counts as a syllable except when it is elided before a vowel or stands at the end of the line. A caesura falls after the sixth syllable, dividing the line into two equal hemistichs, and the syllables adjacent to the caesura and at the end of the line bear a metrical accent. In classical practice the caesura must coincide with the end of a word, and enjambment across the caesura or the line ending was tightly constrained.

Alexandrines normally occur in rhyming couplets or other regular rhyme schemes. Classical rules further required the alternation of masculine and feminine rhymes — rhymes ending, respectively, in a sounded consonant or vowel, and in a mute e. The paired line, or distique, is the basic unit of classical French dramatic verse.

Structure and rules

A well-known example of the classical alexandrine is the opening of Racine's Phèdre:

Le dessein en est pris : je pars, cher Théramène,
Et quitte le séjour de l'aimable Trézène.

Each line has twelve syllables with a caesura after the sixth. Later poets experimented with loosening the form. The Romantics of the nineteenth century, led by Victor Hugo, famously displaced the caesura or weakened it, producing the alexandrin ternaire, a line of three measures of four syllables each. Hugo boasted of having "dislocated" the alexandrine, and his verse drama Hernani (1830) became a landmark in the relaxation of classical rules. Symbolist and modern poets such as Paul Verlaine and Arthur Rimbaud explored lines of uneven syllable counts, while the alexandrine itself continued in use throughout the twentieth century.

History

Twelve-syllable lines appear in medieval French poetry well before the modern period; the name derives from the Roman d'Alexandre, begun around 1170 and expanded over the following decades, in which the narrative was told in laisses of monorhymed twelve-syllable lines. The form fell from favour in the late Middle Ages but was revived by the poets of the Pléiade in the sixteenth century, notably Pierre de Ronsard, who used it for epic and for his Sonnets pour Hélène in a shortened sonnet scheme.

In the seventeenth century the alexandrine became the obligatory vehicle of French tragedy and comedy in verse. Pierre Corneille and Jean Racine brought the form to its classical perfection in plays such as Le Cid, Andromaque, and Phèdre, while Molière used it for his great verse comedies, including Tartuffe and Le Misanthrope. Nicolas Boileau's Art poétique (1674) codified the rules. For French readers the alexandrine remains the sound of high poetic seriousness, and it is still used by modern poets, songwriters, and even rappers working in French.

English alexandrine

In English prosody, an alexandrine is a line of iambic hexameter. It rarely sustains whole poems but appears as a variation: Michael Drayton's Poly-Olbion (1612–1622) is written entirely in alexandrine couplets, an exceptional case. The best-known use is the ninth and final line of the Spenserian stanza, where a longer alexandrine closes eight lines of iambic pentameter, as in Edmund Spenser's The Faerie Queene and, later, in Byron's Childe Harold's Pilgrimage and Keats's The Eve of St. Agnes.

Alexander Pope famously mocked the line's potential monotony in his Essay on Criticism, with a self-demonstrating couplet:

A needless Alexandrine ends the song,
That, like a wounded snake, drags its slow length along.

The second line is itself an alexandrine, deliberately elongated to illustrate the fault it describes.

Other languages

Analogous twelve-syllable lines exist in other European traditions. The Spanish alejandrino, used in the medieval mester de clerecía poems such as the Libro de Alexandre (itself an Alexander romance, reinforcing the name), consists of fourteen syllables in Spanish counting conventions, divided into two hemistichs of seven. German poetry adopted the alexandrine in the seventeenth century under French influence, in the accentual form of iambic hexameter with a medial caesura; it was the standard line of German Baroque drama and was championed by Martin Opitz. Dutch and Polish literatures also developed their own alexandrine traditions, the Polish thirteen-syllable line being a close relative used in Adam Mickiewicz's epic Pan Tadeusz.
