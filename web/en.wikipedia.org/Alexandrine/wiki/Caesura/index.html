Caesura

A caesura (also spelled cæsura and cesura; plural caesuras or caesurae; from Latin caesura, "a cutting") is a metrical pause or break in a verse where one phrase ends and another begins. It may be a comma, a tick, or two slashes (//) and can occur anywhere within a line of verse, though its position is often conventionalized by metre and tradition.

In classical and modern verse

In Greek and Latin epic verse, the caesura is a structural feature: the dactylic hexameter normally has a principal caesura in the third foot, classed as masculine (after the long element) or feminine (after the first short element). In French verse, the classical alexandrine requires a caesura after the sixth syllable, dividing the line into two hemistichs. In Old English alliterative verse, the caesura divides each line into two half-lines bound by alliteration.

Poets manipulate caesuras for rhythm, emphasis, and enjambment effects; a caesura near the start of a line is sometimes called initial, near the end, terminal. In music notation, a caesura (often marked //, nicknamed "railroad tracks") indicates a complete pause in the music.
