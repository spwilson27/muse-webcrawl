Jonathan Swift

Jonathan Swift (30 November 1667 – 19 October 1745) was an Anglo-Irish satirist, essayist, political pamphleteer, poet, and Anglican cleric who became Dean of St Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin — hence his common nickname, "Dean Swift". He is remembered above all for Gulliver's Travels (1726), A Modest Proposal (1729), A Tale of a Tub (1704), and the Drapier's Letters, and is regarded as the foremost prose satirist in the English language.

Life

Swift was born in Dublin to English parents and educated at Kilkenny College and Trinity College Dublin. He worked as secretary to Sir William Temple in England, where he wrote A Tale of a Tub and The Battle of the Books. Returning to Ireland, he was ordained in the Church of Ireland and in 1713 became Dean of St Patrick's. His Drapier's Letters (1724) mobilised Irish opinion against a debased English coinage scheme and made him a national hero in Ireland.

Major works

Gulliver's Travels, published in 1726, satirises human nature and the traveller's-tale genre through voyages to Lilliput, Brobdingnag, Laputa, and the land of the Houyhnhnms. A Modest Proposal (1729) ironically suggests that impoverished Irish parents sell their children as food to the rich, in a savage attack on English policy and heartless economic reasoning.

Legacy

Swift's name is given to the adjective "Swiftian", and his epitaph in St Patrick's, written by himself in Latin, speaks of his "savage indignation" — saeva indignatio. He left much of his fortune to found St Patrick's Hospital for the mentally ill in Dublin.
