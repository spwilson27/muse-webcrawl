Alexandria

Alexandria is the second largest city in Egypt and a major Mediterranean port, founded by Alexander the Great in 331 BC. In antiquity it was the capital of Hellenistic, Roman and Byzantine Egypt, famed for the Lighthouse of Alexandria (Pharos) and the Great Library.

Ancient Alexandria

Planned by the architect Dinocrates, the city was laid out on a grid between the sea and Lake Mareotis, connected by a causeway (the Heptastadion) to Pharos island, where the great lighthouse — one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World — was built under the early Ptolemies. Under the Ptolemaic dynasty Alexandria became the intellectual capital of the Mediterranean world: its Mouseion and Library attracted scholars such as Euclid and Eratosthenes, and the city had a large Jewish community and a famous medical school.

Roman and later history

Rome annexed Egypt in 30 BC after the death of Cleopatra. Alexandria remained a great commercial and scholarly centre, an early hub of Christianity and home to theologians such as Origen and Athanasius. It was conquered by the Arabs in AD 641–642 and declined after the founding of Cairo, reviving under Muhammad Ali in the 19th century as Egypt's principal port and a cosmopolitan trading city.

Modern city

Today Alexandria is Egypt's main seaport and an industrial centre. Landmarks include the Citadel of Qaitbay, built on the site of the Pharos, the modern Bibliotheca Alexandrina (opened 2002), and the catacombs of Kom el-Shoqafa.
