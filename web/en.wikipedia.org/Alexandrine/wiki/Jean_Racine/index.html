Jean Racine

Jean-Baptiste Racine (22 December 1639 – 21 April 1699) was a French dramatist, one of the three great playwrights of 17th-century France, along with Molière and Corneille. He was primarily a tragedian, producing such "examples of neoclassical perfection" as Phèdre, Andromaque, and Athalie. He wrote one comedy, Les Plaideurs (1668), and a muted tragedy, Esther, for the schoolgirls of Saint-Cyr.

Life and works

Orphaned young, Racine was educated at the Jansenist schools of Port-Royal, whose rigorous moral theology left a lasting mark on his tragedies of passion and guilt. His breakthrough came with Andromaque (1667), followed by Britannicus (1669), Bérénice (1670), Bajazet (1672), Mithridate (1673), Iphigénie (1674), and Phèdre (1677), generally considered his masterpiece. His plays, written in rhymed alexandrine couplets of extraordinary economy — his complete tragedies use a vocabulary of only a few thousand words — concentrate on a single crisis in which love collides with duty, honour, or divine law.

Later years

After the cabal against Phèdre, Racine withdrew from the commercial theatre, married, and became royal historiographer to Louis XIV. At the request of Madame de Maintenon he returned to drama with the biblical plays Esther (1689) and Athalie (1691), written for the girls of Saint-Cyr. He died in Paris in 1699 and, at his request, was buried at Port-Royal.
