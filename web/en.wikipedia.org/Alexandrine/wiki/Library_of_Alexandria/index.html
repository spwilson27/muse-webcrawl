Library of Alexandria

The Great Library of Alexandria in Egypt was one of the largest and most significant libraries of the ancient world. Part of the larger research institution known as the Mouseion, it flourished under the patronage of the Ptolemaic dynasty from the third century BC and became the intellectual capital of the Hellenistic world, attracting scholars such as Euclid, Eratosthenes, and Aristarchus of Samos.

History

The library was probably established under Ptolemy I Soter or his son Ptolemy II Philadelphus. Its librarians — including Zenodotus, Callimachus, Eratosthenes, and Aristophanes of Byzantium — edited the standard texts of Homer and other Greek authors, and Callimachus compiled the Pinakes, a vast bibliographic catalogue. Estimates of its holdings range from 40,000 to several hundred thousand scrolls.

Decline and destruction

Contrary to the popular image of a single catastrophic fire, the library declined gradually over centuries: scholars were expelled under Ptolemy VIII, a fire during Julius Caesar's siege of Alexandria in 48 BC damaged book stores, and the Mouseion faded under Roman rule. Its final fate is uncertain, though the destruction of the Serapeum — a "daughter library" — in AD 391 is often cited as an end point.

Legacy

The library endures as a symbol of universal knowledge and of its fragility. The modern Bibliotheca Alexandrina, opened in 2002 near the ancient site, was built as a commemoration of the original.
