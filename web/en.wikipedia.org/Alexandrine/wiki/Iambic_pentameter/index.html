Iambic pentameter

Iambic pentameter is a type of metric line used in traditional English poetry and verse drama. The term describes a rhythm, or metre, in which each line consists of five metrical feet, each foot being an iamb: an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed syllable (da-DUM). It is the predominant metre of English dramatic verse, used by William Shakespeare, and of major narrative and lyric forms including blank verse, the heroic couplet, and the sonnet.

History

Geoffrey Chaucer introduced the ten-syllable line into English in the 14th century, adapting French and Italian models. In the 16th century the Earl of Surrey regularized it as blank verse, and Christopher Marlowe and Shakespeare made it the standard medium of English drama, varying it with inversions, feminine endings, and caesuras to imitate living speech. John Milton's Paradise Lost demonstrated its epic capacity in blank verse.

Variations

Skilled poets rarely keep the pattern mechanically: substitutions such as a trochee at the line's start, pyrrhic and spondaic feet, and mid-line pauses (caesuras) create expressive tension against the underlying beat. Lines may end with an extra unstressed syllable (a feminine ending), as in Hamlet's "To be, or not to be, that is the question."
