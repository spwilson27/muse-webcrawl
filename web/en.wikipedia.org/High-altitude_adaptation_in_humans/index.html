High-altitude adaptation in humans

High-altitude adaptation in humans is an instance of evolutionary modification in certain human populations, including those of Tibet, the Andes, and Ethiopia, who have acquired the unique capacity to survive at extremely high altitudes. While more than 140 million people worldwide live at altitudes above 2,500 metres (8,200 ft), only selected populations show genetic adaptations to the chronic hypoxia (low oxygen) found there; most lowlanders instead rely on short-term physiological acclimatization, which is less efficient and can lead to altitude sickness.

Physiological responses

When a lowlander ascends to high altitude, the body responds within hours by increasing the breathing rate (hyperventilation) and heart rate. Over days to weeks, acclimatization includes increased production of red blood cells under the influence of the hormone erythropoietin, changes in blood chemistry that help unload oxygen in the tissues, and increased capillarization.

Genetic adaptations

Genomic studies have identified different adaptive strategies in the three great high-altitude populations:

  • Tibetans show strong selection on EPAS1 (encoding HIF-2α) and EGLN1; the adaptive EPAS1 haplotype appears to have been inherited from Denisovans. Tibetans maintain relatively modest haemoglobin levels but have increased blood flow and nitric oxide levels.
  • Andeans tend to have elevated haemoglobin concentrations, larger lung volumes and chest dimensions, reflecting a different evolutionary path.
  • Ethiopian highlanders (such as the Amhara) show yet another pattern, with near-sea-level haemoglobin and oxygen saturation, and candidate genes including THRB and ARNT2.

These populations are a frequently cited example of convergent evolution in humans.
