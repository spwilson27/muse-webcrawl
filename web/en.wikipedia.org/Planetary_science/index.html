Planetary science (or planetology) is the scientific study of planets, moons, asteroids, comets, and planetary systems — principally the Solar System, and increasingly the exoplanets around other stars. It is an interdisciplinary field drawing on astronomy, geology, geophysics, atmospheric science, and chemistry, united by the comparative approach: understanding worlds by comparing them with each other and with Earth.

Scope and subfields

Planetary geology (or astrogeology) maps and interprets surfaces — craters, volcanoes, canyons, dunes — on bodies from Mercury to Titan. Planetary atmospheres research models climates and weather, from the runaway greenhouse of Venus to the thin CO₂ air of Mars. Studies of interiors use gravity fields, seismology (as on the Moon and Mars), and magnetic fields to infer structure. Astrobiology searches for habitable conditions and biosignatures, while the study of planetesimals and meteorites reconstructs the Solar System's origin.

History

The field began with telescopic astronomy — Galileo's observations of Jupiter's moons and the phases of Venus — and matured in the Space Age. Flybys, orbiters, landers, and rovers have now visited every planet: the twin Voyager probes toured the outer Solar System, orbiters have mapped Venus, Mars, and Mercury in detail, landers have sampled Mars and Titan, and missions have returned samples from the Moon, asteroids (Hayabusa2, OSIRIS-REx), and comets. Since the first confirmed exoplanets in the 1990s — thousands are now known — planetary science has extended to comparing whole planetary systems.

Methods

Planetary scientists combine remote sensing from Earth and spacecraft, in-situ measurements, laboratory analysis of meteorites and returned samples, fieldwork in terrestrial analogues (Iceland, Antarctica, Atacama), and computer simulations of accretion, impacts, and climate. Phenomena such as planetary nebulae belong to stellar astronomy and are unrelated to planets despite the similar name.
