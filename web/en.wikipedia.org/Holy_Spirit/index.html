Holy Spirit

In Christianity, the third person of the Trinity; in Judaism, the divine force and spirit of God.

In Christian theology, the Holy Spirit, also known as the Holy Ghost, is the third person of the Trinity, understood as God, coequal and coeternal with God the Father and God the Son (Jesus Christ). Christians believe the Spirit proceeds from the Father (and, in the Western tradition defined by the filioque, from the Son as well) and is active in creation, revelation, the inspiration of scripture, the conception and anointing of Jesus, and the sanctification of believers. The Spirit's descent on the apostles at Pentecost, described in Acts 2, is traditionally regarded as the birth of the Church.

In Judaism

In the Hebrew Bible and rabbinic literature, the ruach ha-kodesh ("holy spirit") is the spirit or breath of God—a divine force, quality and influence, rather than a distinct person—associated with prophecy, wisdom and creation, as in Genesis 1:2, where the spirit of God hovers over the waters.

Christian doctrine

The New Testament presents the Spirit descending on Jesus at his baptism in the form of a dove, guiding his ministry, and being promised by him as the Paraclete (Advocate or Comforter) in the Gospel of John. Paul's letters describe the "fruits of the Spirit" and the charismatic "gifts of the Spirit", including prophecy and speaking in tongues—gifts emphasised in modern Pentecostal and charismatic movements, which stress baptism in the Holy Spirit. The doctrine of the Trinity took shape over the first centuries; the Council of Constantinople (381) affirmed the Spirit's full divinity, and the Western addition of the filioque clause ("and the Son") to the Nicene Creed became a major cause of the East–West Schism of 1054.

Symbols

The Holy Spirit is traditionally represented by a dove, by tongues or flames of fire (from Pentecost), by wind and breath, and by water, oil and light. In art, the Spirit almost always appears as a dove in scenes of the Annunciation, the Baptism of Christ and Pentecost.

Other traditions

Islam recognises a "holy spirit" (ruh al-qudus), often identified with the angel Gabriel, who conveys revelation. Baháʼí, Latter-day Saint and other traditions hold their own distinct understandings, and nontrinitarian Christians deny the Spirit's personhood or full deity.
