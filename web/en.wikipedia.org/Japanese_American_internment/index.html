Japanese American internment

The Japanese American internment, also known as the internment of Japanese Americans, was the forced relocation and incarceration of about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, most of whom lived on the Pacific Coast, in concentration camps in the western interior of the United States during World War II. About two-thirds of the internees were United States citizens. These actions were initiated by President Franklin D. Roosevelt via Executive Order 9066 on February 19, 1942, following the Imperial Japan attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941.

Background

Anti-Japanese sentiment in the western United States long predated the war. After Pearl Harbor, fears of espionage and sabotage, fanned by military officials, politicians, and newspapers, produced pressure for the removal of all persons of Japanese ancestry from the West Coast, even though government intelligence assessments found little evidence of disloyalty.

The camps

Families were given days to dispose of their homes and businesses and were sent first to temporary assembly centers, then to ten camps administered by the War Relocation Authority in remote areas of California, Arizona, Wyoming, Colorado, Utah, and Arkansas. The camps were surrounded by barbed wire and guard towers. Major sites included Manzanar and Tule Lake in California, Heart Mountain in Wyoming, Minidoka in Idaho, Topaz in Utah, Poston and Gila River in Arizona, Granada in Colorado, and Jerome and Rohwer in Arkansas.

Legal challenges and military service

The Supreme Court upheld the curfew and exclusion orders in cases such as Hirabayashi v. United States (1943) and Korematsu v. United States (1944), though it ruled in Ex parte Endo (1944) that loyal citizens could not be detained indefinitely. Despite their treatment, many Nisei (second-generation) men served with distinction in the U.S. Army, notably in the 442nd Regimental Combat Team in Europe.

Redress

The last camp closed in 1946. In 1983, the Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians concluded that the policy had been driven by "race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership." The Civil Liberties Act of 1988 granted a formal apology and $20,000 in reparations to each surviving internee.
