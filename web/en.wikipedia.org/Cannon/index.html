Cannon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cannon is a large-caliber gun classified as a type of artillery, which usually launches a projectile using explosive chemical propellant. Gunpowder ("black powder") was the primary propellant before the invention of smokeless powder during the late 19th century. Cannons were invented in China as early as the 12th century and were among the earliest forms of gunpowder artillery, replacing earlier mechanical artillery such as the trebuchet.

History

The earliest known depiction of a cannon appears in a 12th-century Sichuan sculpture, and the oldest surviving cannon, from China, dates to the late 13th century. Cannon technology spread westward through the Islamic world into Europe by the early 1300s. Early European cannon were used at engagements such as the Battle of Crécy (1346), and by the 15th century large bombards—such as those used by Mehmed II at the siege of Constantinople in 1453—were breaching medieval walls, transforming siege warfare.

By the early modern period, standardized muzzle-loading smoothbore cannon classified by the weight of their shot (e.g., 12-pounder, 24-pounder) armed both fortresses and warships. The 19th century brought rifled barrels, breech-loading mechanisms, explosive shells, and recoil systems. During the First World War heavy artillery dominated the battlefield; in the modern era the term "cannon" most often refers to autocannons and aircraft or vehicle armament of 20 mm or larger.

Construction and operation

A cannon consists of a barrel—historically cast from bronze or iron, later forged steel—mounted on a carriage or turret. Muzzle velocity, range, and destructive effect depend on bore diameter (caliber), barrel length, and propellant charge. Historical cannon fired solid shot, explosive shells, grapeshot, and canister; modern cannon fire a wide range of specialized projectiles.
