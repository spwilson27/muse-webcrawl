Peel Commission

The Peel Commission, formally the Palestine Royal Commission, was a British Royal Commission of Inquiry headed by Lord Peel, appointed in 1936 to investigate the causes of unrest in Mandatory Palestine. Its report, published in July 1937, was the first official proposal to partition Palestine into separate Arab and Jewish states.

Peel Commission
Appointed: 1936
Report published: 7 July 1937
Chairman: Earl Peel

Background

The commission was sent after the outbreak of the Arab revolt in 1936. It heard evidence in Palestine and London, and concluded that the Mandate's dual obligations—to foster a Jewish national home and to protect Arab rights—had become unworkable.

Recommendations

The report proposed ending the Mandate and partitioning the territory: a small Jewish state in the Galilee and coastal plain, an Arab state in the hill country and south merged with Transjordan, and a continued British enclave around Jerusalem and a corridor to the sea. It also suggested a population transfer to reduce minorities within each state.

Reception

The Zionist leadership debated and conditionally engaged with the principle of partition, while Arab leaders rejected it outright. The British government endorsed the report, then retreated after the Woodhead Commission judged the plan impractical; the 1939 White Paper abandoned partition.
