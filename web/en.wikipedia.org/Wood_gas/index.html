Wood gas

Wood gas is a fuel gas produced by the partial combustion of wood or charcoal in a gasifier. Composed mainly of nitrogen, carbon monoxide, hydrogen, and small amounts of methane, it can power internal combustion engines or be burned for heat. Wood-gas vehicles were used by the hundreds of thousands in Europe during World War II fuel shortages.

Main componentsN₂ (~50%), CO (~20%), H₂ (~18%), CO₂, CH₄
Energy contentAbout 5–6 MJ/m³ (roughly one-tenth of natural gas per unit volume)
Produced byPyrolysis and partial oxidation in a gasifier

Production

In a gasifier, wood chips or charcoal pass through zones of drying, pyrolysis, combustion, and reduction. In the reduction zone, carbon dioxide and steam react with glowing charcoal to form combustible carbon monoxide and hydrogen. The gas must be filtered and cooled to remove tars and ash before entering an engine. Georges Imbert's downdraft gasifier (1920s) became the standard vehicle design.

Historical use

Related "producer gas" powered early stationary engines and town gas came from coal. During World War II, petroleum rationing put an estimated 500,000 to one million wood-gas vehicles on the road in Germany, Sweden, Finland, and other countries, running everything from cars to tractors and buses. After the war, cheap oil made the bulky, labor-intensive units obsolete, though interest revives during energy crises; North Korea and some rural and hobbyist communities still use wood gas, and modern biomass gasification is studied for renewable heat and power.

Drawbacks

Wood gas has low energy density, so engines lose substantial power; startup takes minutes; and the gas contains toxic carbon monoxide. Gasifier tars foul engines if the gas is not properly cleaned.
