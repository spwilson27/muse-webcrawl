Avidin

Organism sourceEgg white of birds, reptiles, amphibians
StructureTetramer, about 66–69 kDa
LigandBiotin (vitamin B7)
Biotin affinityKd ≈ 10−15 M — among the strongest non-covalent bindings known

Avidin is a tetrameric biotin-binding protein produced in the oviducts of birds, reptiles and amphibians and deposited in the whites of their eggs. In chicken egg white, avidin makes up approximately 0.05% of total protein. Its extraordinarily tight binding to the vitamin biotin is thought to serve as a defence against bacterial growth in the egg, starving bacteria of biotin.

Properties

Each of the four identical subunits binds one biotin molecule. The avidin–biotin complex is among the strongest known non-covalent interactions, with a dissociation constant around 10−15 M, comparable in strength to a covalent bond. Avidin is highly stable to heat and denaturants, though cooking destroys much of its biotin-binding in consumed eggs; eating large amounts of raw egg white can cause biotin deficiency.

Applications

The avidin–biotin system is a workhorse of laboratory biotechnology, used to link biotin-tagged molecules (antibodies, probes, enzymes) to avidin- or streptavidin-coated surfaces and labels in assays such as ELISAs, blots, and affinity purification. Streptavidin, a bacterial analogue from Streptomyces avidinii, is often preferred because of its near-neutral charge and lower non-specific binding.
