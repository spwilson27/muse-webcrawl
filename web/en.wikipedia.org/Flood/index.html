Flood

Flooding inundating a residential area.

A flood is an overflow of water (or rarely other fluids) that submerges land that is usually dry. In the sense of "flowing water", the word may also be applied to the inflow of the tide. Floods are of significant concern in agriculture, civil engineering and public health. Human changes to the environment often increase the intensity and frequency of flooding, for example land-use changes such as deforestation and removal of wetlands, changes in waterway course or flood controls such as with levees, and larger environmental issues such as climate change and sea level rise.

Floods can occur as an overflow of water from water bodies, such as a river, lake or ocean, in which the water overtops or breaks levees, resulting in some of that water escaping its usual boundaries. They may also occur due to an accumulation of rainwater on saturated ground in an areal flood. Flooding may also result from the volume of water within a body of water, such as a river or lake, exceeding its usual capacity, or from storm surge and high tides coinciding.

Types

  • Riverine (fluvial) floods — occur when a river or stream exceeds the capacity of its channel, typically after prolonged or heavy rainfall, snowmelt, or ice jams.
  • Flash floods — rapid flooding caused by intense rainfall over a short period, often in steep terrain or urban areas; among the deadliest flood types because of their sudden onset.
  • Coastal floods — inundation of coastal land by seawater, driven by storm surge, high tides, tsunamis, and long-term sea level rise.
  • Pluvial (surface water) floods — flooding from rainfall overwhelming drainage systems, independent of a water body overflowing.
  • Groundwater floods — occur when the water table rises above the ground surface after prolonged wet periods.

Effects

Flooding is among the most damaging of natural disasters. Primary effects include loss of life, damage to buildings and infrastructure, destruction of crops, and loss of livestock. Secondary effects include waterborne disease, contamination of drinking water, disruption of transport and power supplies, and long-term economic and psychological harm to affected communities. Floods also bring benefits in some contexts: they deposit nutrient-rich sediment on floodplains, recharge groundwater, and sustain wetland ecosystems.

Control and management

Methods of flood control include dams and reservoirs, levees and flood walls, channelisation, floodplain zoning, early-warning systems, and — increasingly — "soft" approaches such as restoring wetlands and floodplains to absorb excess water ("room for the river"). Flood risk is commonly mapped using recurrence intervals such as the "100-year flood", an event with a 1% probability of being equalled or exceeded in any given year.

Notable floods

Among history's deadliest floods are the 1931 China floods, the 1887 Yellow River flood, and the 1970 Bhola cyclone's storm surge. The Mississippi River floods of 1927 and 1993, the 1953 North Sea flood, and the 2005 flooding of New Orleans after Hurricane Katrina are landmark events in modern flood management.
