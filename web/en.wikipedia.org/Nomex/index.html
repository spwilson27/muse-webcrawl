Nomex

TypeMeta-aramid fibre
ManufacturerDuPont
Introduced1960s
PropertiesFlame-resistant, heat-resistant; does not melt or drip

Nomex is a flame-resistant meta-aramid material developed in the early 1960s by DuPont and first marketed in 1967. Chemically it is poly(m-phenylene isophthalamide), a relative of the para-aramid Kevlar. Nomex does not melt, drip or support combustion in air under normal conditions; when exposed to intense heat, its fibres thicken and carbonise, forming a protective barrier.

Properties

Nomex combines inherent thermal stability with good textile properties: it retains its strength at high temperatures, resists many chemicals and solvents, and is electrically insulating. It is produced as filament yarns, staple fibre, paper and honeycomb sheet.

Applications

Nomex is best known for protective clothing: firefighters' turnout gear, wildland firefighting uniforms, military flight suits, and racing drivers' suits, gloves and balaclavas, where it is credited with greatly improving survival in cockpit fires. In paper form it is used as electrical insulation in transformers and motors. Nomex honeycomb — a lightweight, stiff core material — is widely used in aircraft panels, helicopter rotor blades, boat hulls and motorsport chassis.

History

Nomex was developed by a DuPont team led by chemist Wilfred Sweeny, whose work on high-temperature-resistant polymers in the early 1960s led to commercial production later that decade, alongside Stephanie Kwolek's contemporaneous invention of Kevlar.
