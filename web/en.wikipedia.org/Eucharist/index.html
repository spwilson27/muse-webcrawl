Eucharist

The Eucharist (from Greek eukharistía, "thanksgiving"), also known as Holy Communion, the Mass, and the Lord's Supper, is a Christian rite that is considered a sacrament in most churches, and as an ordinance in others. According to the New Testament, the rite was instituted by Jesus Christ during the Last Supper, the night before his crucifixion, when he gave his disciples bread and wine, saying "this is my body" and "this is my blood".

Biblical basis

The institution narrative appears in the Synoptic Gospels (Matthew 26, Mark 14, Luke 22) and in Paul's First Letter to the Corinthians (11:23–25), the earliest account. The Gospel of John's discourse on the "Bread of Life" (John 6) is also traditionally associated with the rite. The elements are bread (or a wafer) and wine (or grape juice), consumed in remembrance of Christ's sacrifice.

Theological interpretations

Christians differ over how Christ is present in the Eucharist. Roman Catholic teaching holds to transubstantiation: the substance of the bread and wine is converted into the body and blood of Christ while the appearances remain. Eastern Orthodox teaching affirms a real change without defining it by that term. Lutherans teach sacramental union; Reformed traditions teach a real spiritual presence; many Baptists and others hold a memorialist view, treating the rite as a symbolic remembrance and proclamation.

Practice

The Eucharist is the central act of worship in Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, and Lutheran traditions, and is celebrated regularly in most other churches. Churches differ on who may receive communion ("closed" vs "open" communion), on the frequency of celebration, and on admission of baptised children. The ecumenical movement has produced substantial convergence documents, notably the 1982 Lima text Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry.
