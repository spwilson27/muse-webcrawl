3:2 pulldown

3:2 pulldown, also called telecine judder when its artefacts are visible, is a process for converting motion picture film, shot at 24 frames per second, into video at the 29.97 (approximately 30) frames per second used by the NTSC television standard. It works by distributing four film frames across five video frames: successive film frames are shown for alternately three and two video fields, producing ten fields (five video frames) from every four film frames.

How it works

Because interlaced video displays each frame as two fields, the conversion is done at the field level. The first film frame is written to three fields, the second to two fields, the third to three, and the fourth to two, after which the pattern repeats. The film is also slowed slightly, from 24 to 23.976 frames per second, so that the resulting field rate matches the 59.94 fields per second of NTSC colour television.

Judder and inverse telecine

The uneven 3-2-3-2 cadence means frames are displayed for unequal durations, which can produce a slight stuttering, called judder, most noticeable in slow camera pans. For progressive displays, an inverse telecine process detects the cadence and reconstructs the original 24 progressive frames; displays with a 120 Hz refresh rate can show each film frame exactly five times, eliminating judder. PAL regions traditionally avoided the problem by simply running film 4% faster at 25 frames per second.
