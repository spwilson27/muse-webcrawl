Geomagnetism is the study of the Earth's magnetic field, its origin in the planet's interior, its variation in space and time, and its interaction with the solar wind. The field at the surface is approximately that of a magnetic dipole tilted about 9–11 degrees from the rotation axis, with a strength of roughly 25 to 65 microtesla.

Origin

The field is generated by the geodynamo: convective motion of electrically conducting liquid iron in the outer core, organised by the Earth's rotation, sustains electric currents that produce the magnetic field. Numerical dynamo models reproduce many observed features, including spontaneous reversals of polarity.

Variations and reversals

The field changes continuously (secular variation): the magnetic poles wander, and the dipole moment has decreased by roughly 9% over the past two centuries. Over geological time the field has reversed polarity hundreds of times, most recently the Brunhes–Matuyama reversal about 780,000 years ago. The record of past fields preserved in rocks and sediments is the subject of paleomagnetism, which provided key evidence for seafloor spreading and plate tectonics. External variations, driven by solar activity, include magnetic storms and substorms associated with auroras and disturbances to power grids and satellites.

Measurement and uses

Geomagnetic observatories, survey satellites such as the ESA Swarm mission, and reference models like the International Geomagnetic Reference Field (IGRF) support navigation, directional drilling, geological surveying, and space-weather forecasting. The magnetosphere formed by the field shields the surface from much of the solar wind and cosmic radiation.
