Old-time radio

Old-time radio, also called the Golden Age of Radio, refers to the era of radio programming in the United States from the birth of radio broadcasting in the early 1920s through roughly 1962, when television displaced radio as the dominant home entertainment medium. During this period the major networks — NBC, CBS, ABC, and Mutual — filled their schedules with comedy, drama, mystery, westerns, soap operas, quiz shows, variety, and live music.

Programming

Signature programmes included The Jack Benny Program, Fibber McGee and Molly, The Shadow, Suspense, Gunsmoke, Amos 'n' Andy, and Orson Welles's Mercury Theatre broadcasts, whose 1938 adaptation of The War of the Worlds became legendary. Radio drama depended on dialogue, music, and sound effects to conjure scenes in the listener's imagination — the "theatre of the mind".

Preservation

Because much programming was broadcast live and recorded only on fragile transcription discs, a large share of the era's output is lost. Tens of thousands of episodes nonetheless survive and circulate among collectors; broadcasts that entered the public domain form the backbone of the old-time radio hobby, and the genre has enjoyed revivals through reissue labels, podcasts, and streaming archives.
