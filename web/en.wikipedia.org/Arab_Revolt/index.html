Arab Revolt

The Arab Revolt (1916–1918) was an uprising of Arab forces against the Ottoman Empire during the First World War, initiated by Hussein bin Ali, Sharif of Mecca, in alliance with Britain and France. Its goal was an independent Arab state stretching from Syria to Yemen, as suggested in the McMahon–Hussein Correspondence of 1915–16.

Course of the revolt

The revolt began in June 1916 with attacks on Ottoman garrisons in the Hejaz. Arab forces under Hussein's sons, notably Faisal, captured Mecca, Jeddah and Taif, then moved north with British support. The capture of Aqaba in July 1917 opened the way into Palestine and Syria. Arab irregulars, advised by officers including T. E. Lawrence ("Lawrence of Arabia"), waged a guerrilla campaign against the Hejaz railway, tying down Ottoman troops while General Allenby's Egyptian Expeditionary Force advanced. Damascus fell in October 1918 shortly before the Armistice of Mudros ended Ottoman participation in the war.

Aftermath

Arab hopes of a unified independent kingdom were frustrated by the Sykes–Picot Agreement and the post-war mandate system, which divided the region between Britain and France. Faisal briefly ruled Syria before French forces expelled him in 1920; he later became king of Iraq, while his brother Abdullah became emir (later king) of Transjordan. The revolt remains a foundational episode in modern Arab nationalism.
