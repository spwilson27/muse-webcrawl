Portland cement

Portland cement is the most common type of cement in general use around the world as a basic ingredient of concrete, mortar, stucco, and non-speciality grout. It was developed from other kinds of hydraulic lime in England in the early 19th century by Joseph Aspdin, and is usually made from limestone. It is a fine powder, produced by heating limestone and clay minerals in a kiln to form clinker, and then grinding the clinker with the addition of a small quantity of gypsum.

History

Joseph Aspdin, a Leeds bricklayer and builder, patented "Portland cement" in 1824, naming it for its resemblance in colour to Portland stone from the Isle of Portland in Dorset. His son William Aspdin developed the modern product in the 1840s by burning the raw materials at higher temperatures to produce clinker rich in alite. Portland cement rapidly displaced earlier cements and became the foundation of the modern concrete industry.

Composition and chemistry

Clinker consists mainly of four phases: alite (tricalcium silicate), belite (dicalcium silicate), aluminate (tricalcium aluminate), and ferrite (tetracalcium aluminoferrite). When mixed with water, the cement hydrates in an exothermic reaction, forming calcium silicate hydrate gel that binds aggregates into concrete and gains strength over weeks and months. Standards classify Portland cements into types according to properties such as early strength, sulphate resistance, and heat of hydration.

Environmental impact

Cement manufacturing accounts for roughly 8% of global carbon dioxide emissions, from both the calcination of limestone, which releases CO2 chemically, and the fuel burned to heat kilns to about 1,450 °C. Research and industry efforts to reduce this footprint include blended cements using fly ash, slag, and calcined clays, alternative fuels, and carbon capture.
