Occupational stress

Occupational stress is stress related to one's job. It often stems from responsibilities and pressures that do not align with a person's knowledge, skills, or expectations, inhibiting their ability to cope. Occupational stress can increase when workers do not feel supported by supervisors or colleagues, or feel as if they have little control over work processes.

Causes

Major sources of occupational stress include excessive workload, long hours, job insecurity, unclear or conflicting roles, low decision latitude, interpersonal conflict and workplace bullying, and poor work–life balance. Influential models include the demand–control model of Robert Karasek, which predicts the greatest strain where demands are high and control is low, and the effort–reward imbalance model.

Health effects

Chronic occupational stress is associated with cardiovascular disease, musculoskeletal disorders, depression, anxiety and burnout. It also contributes to absenteeism, presenteeism, staff turnover and reduced productivity, imposing substantial costs on employers and economies.

Prevention and management

Organisational interventions include redesigning jobs to increase autonomy, clarifying roles, improving communication, and addressing bullying and excessive demands. Individual-level approaches include stress management training, cognitive behavioural techniques, relaxation and exercise, though evidence favours tackling sources of stress at the organisational level.
