John Edensor Littlewood

Born: 9 June 1885, Rochester, Kent, England
Died: 6 September 1977 (aged 92), Cambridge, England
Alma mater: Trinity College, Cambridge
Known for: Hardy–Littlewood collaborations; analytic number theory; Littlewood's three principles
Awards: Royal Medal (1929); Sylvester Medal (1943); Copley Medal (1958); Fellow of the Royal Society

John Edensor Littlewood (9 June 1885 – 6 September 1977) was a British mathematician. He worked on topics relating to analysis, number theory, and differential equations, and had lengthy collaborations with G. H. Hardy and Srinivasa Ramanujan. Along with Hardy, he is regarded as one of the creators of the modern British school of mathematical analysis.

Life and work

Littlewood was born in Rochester, Kent, the eldest son of Edward Thornton Littlewood and Sylvia Maud (née Ackland). In 1892 his father accepted the headmastership of a school in Wynberg, Cape Town, South Africa, and took his family there. Littlewood returned to England in 1900 to attend St Paul's School in London, studying under the notable teacher and mathematician Francis Sowerby Macaulay. In 1903 he entered Trinity College, Cambridge, and in 1906 he was Senior Wrangler in the Mathematical Tripos, ahead of his contemporary Hardy being examined the previous generation.

From 1911 Littlewood collaborated with Hardy on the mathematical work of mathematical analysis and analytic number theory. This led (in part) to the Hardy–Littlewood conjectures on the distribution of prime numbers and to one of the most famous of all mathematical collaborations, lasting over 35 years. During the First World War he served as a second lieutenant in the Royal Garrison Artillery, working on ballistics.

Hardy–Littlewood collaboration

The collaboration between Hardy and Littlewood is one of the most celebrated in mathematical history. Working mostly by correspondence and in intense meetings at Oxford and Cambridge, the two developed deep results on the Riemann zeta function, partitions of integers, and the Hardy–Littlewood circle method. Their joint papers number around one hundred. Harald Bohr quipped that the theorems were attributed to Hardy and Littlewood, with Hardy taking priority of discovery and Littlewood of writing — a jest that both men treated as a compliment.

Littlewood also proved results on the distribution of prime numbers, showing that the difference π(x) − li(x) changes sign infinitely often, demolishing the conjecture that π(x) < li(x) always. He formulated "Littlewood's three principles" of real analysis, widely taught to graduate students, and his supremum norm conjecture and work on Diophantine approximation remain influential.

Later life

Littlewood continued publishing research into his eighties. He remained at Trinity College, Cambridge for most of his life, becoming a legendary figure for his wit and his miscellany of anecdotes, later published as Littlewood's Miscellany (edited by Béla Bollobás). He died in Cambridge in 1977.
