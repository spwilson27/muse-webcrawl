Anna Freud

Anna Freud (3 December 1895 – 9 October 1982) was a British psychoanalyst of Austrian-Jewish origin. She was born in Vienna, the sixth and youngest child of Sigmund Freud and Martha Bernays. She followed the path of her father and contributed to the field of psychoanalysis. Alongside Hermine Hug-Hellmuth and Melanie Klein, she may be considered the founder of psychoanalytic child psychology.

Early life

Anna Freud was born in Vienna on 3 December 1895. She was the youngest daughter of Sigmund Freud. She had an unhappy childhood, in which, according to her biographer, she felt "cast aside" in favour of her older sister Sophie. She attended the Cottage Lyceum in Vienna, but her schooling left much to be desired and she gained most of her knowledge from the lessons of her father's associates and from his guests. She finished her education in 1912 and spent time in Italy under the supervision of a chaperone. From 1914 to 1920 she worked as an elementary school teacher and began translating her father's works into German.

Psychoanalytic career

Anna Freud began her psychoanalytic practice with children in 1923 in Vienna and from 1925 until 1934 she was the Secretary of the International Psychoanalytical Association. In 1927 she chaired a symposium on child analysis. She became a director of the Vienna Psychoanalytical Training Institute in 1935. The next year she published her influential study of the "ways and means by which the ego wards off depression and anxiety", The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936), which laid the groundwork for ego psychology and established her reputation as a pioneering theoretician.

London years

After the Anschluss of Austria by Nazi Germany in 1938, the Freud family emigrated to London. During and after the Second World War, Anna Freud ran the Hampstead War Nurseries for children orphaned or displaced by the Blitz, work that informed her studies of child development and separation. In 1947 she founded the Hampstead Child Therapy Course and Clinic, which she directed until her death. Her later writings, including Normality and Pathology in Childhood (1965), developed the concept of developmental lines.

Legacy

The Anna Freud Centre in London, a children's mental health charity, continues her work. Her collected writings were published in eight volumes. She was appointed a Commander of the Order of the British Empire (CBE) in 1967.
