Dualism

Dualism is the idea that reality, or some domain of it, consists of two fundamentally different and irreducible kinds of things, principles, or powers. The term is used in several distinct but related senses in philosophy and religion.

Mind–body dualism

In philosophy of mind, dualism holds that mental phenomena are, in some respects, non-physical, or that the mind and body are distinct substances. Its most famous form is René Descartes' substance dualism, which divided reality into res cogitans (thinking substance) and res extensa (extended substance). Dualists face the interaction problem — explaining how an immaterial mind can affect a material body — which Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia pressed on Descartes himself. Later variants include property dualism, which accepts one substance but two kinds of properties, and epiphenomenalism. Dualism contrasts with monistic views such as physicalism and idealism.

Religious and cosmological dualism

In religion, dualism describes systems in which two opposed powers — good and evil, light and darkness — contend in the cosmos. Zoroastrianism presents the conflict of Ahura Mazda and Angra Mainyu; Manichaeism, founded by Mani in the 3rd century, taught a stark dualism of light and darkness that influenced Gnostic movements and medieval groups such as the Cathars. Chinese thought expresses a complementary dualism in yin and yang, which are interdependent rather than antagonistic.

Other senses

Dualism also names doctrines in ethics, epistemology, and political theory, and in Hindu philosophy the Dvaita Vedanta school asserts a real distinction between the individual soul and God, in contrast to Advaita non-dualism.
