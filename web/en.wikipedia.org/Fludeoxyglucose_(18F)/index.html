Fludeoxyglucose (18F)

Fludeoxyglucose (18F), abbreviated 18F-FDG, is a radiopharmaceutical used in positron emission tomography (PET) imaging. It is an analogue of glucose in which the normal hydroxyl group at the 2-position is replaced by the positron-emitting isotope fluorine-18. Because cells take it up like glucose but cannot metabolise it further, FDG accumulates in tissues with high glucose consumption — making it the workhorse tracer for detecting cancers and imaging brain and heart metabolism.

Mechanism

After intravenous injection, FDG is carried into cells by glucose transporters and phosphorylated by hexokinase to FDG-6-phosphate. Unlike glucose-6-phosphate, it cannot proceed through glycolysis and, in most tissues, is dephosphorylated only slowly, so it remains trapped. The positron emitted by fluorine-18 annihilates with an electron, producing two gamma photons detected in coincidence by the PET scanner to map tracer concentration. Many tumours show markedly increased glucose uptake (the Warburg effect), so they appear as hot spots.

Production and logistics

Fluorine-18 is produced in a cyclotron, usually by proton bombardment of oxygen-18-enriched water, and incorporated into FDG by automated synthesis modules. Its 109.8-minute half-life is long enough for regional distribution from central radiopharmacies to hospitals, but short enough that production, quality control, transport and scanning must be tightly scheduled each day.

Clinical use

FDG PET, usually combined with CT as PET/CT, is used to stage and monitor many cancers, including lung cancer, lymphoma, melanoma and colorectal cancer, and to assess treatment response. In neurology it maps cerebral glucose metabolism in dementia and epilepsy; in cardiology it identifies viable but hibernating heart muscle. Interpretation must account for normal uptake in brain, heart, kidneys and bladder, and for inflammatory tissue, which can mimic tumours.
