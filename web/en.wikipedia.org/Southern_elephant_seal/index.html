Southern elephant seal


A male southern elephant seal (Mirounga leonina)

The southern elephant seal (Mirounga leonina) is one of two species of elephant seals. It is the largest member of the clade Pinnipedia and the largest extant marine mammal that is not a cetacean. It gets its name from the large proboscis of the adult male, reminiscent of an elephant's trunk, which is used to make extraordinarily loud roaring sounds, especially during the mating season.

Description

The southern elephant seal is sexually dimorphic to an extreme degree, among the most pronounced of any mammal. Males are much larger and heavier than females: adult males typically measure 4.2 to 5.8 metres in length and weigh 2,200 to 4,000 kilograms, while females are 2.6 to 3 metres long and weigh 400 to 900 kilograms. The male's proboscis is an inflatable, trunk-like structure overhanging the mouth.

Distribution and behaviour

The species breeds on subantarctic and Antarctic coastal islands and mainland shores, with major populations at South Georgia, Macquarie Island, Heard Island, the Kerguelen Islands, and the Antarctic Peninsula. It spends most of its life at sea, feeding on squid and fish, and is a remarkable diver: recorded dives exceed 2,000 metres in depth and can last more than two hours.

During the breeding season, dominant males ("beachmasters") defend harems of females on breeding beaches. At sea the seals alternate between long foraging trips and periods hauled out to moult. The species was heavily hunted for its blubber oil in the 19th century, bringing it close to extinction, but populations have since recovered to an estimated 650,000 animals.
