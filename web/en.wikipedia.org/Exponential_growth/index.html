Exponential growth

An exponential growth curve.

Exponential growth occurs when the growth rate of a quantity is proportional to its current size, so that the quantity grows ever faster in absolute terms as it becomes larger. Mathematically, dx/dt = kx, with solution x(t) = x0ekt. In discrete steps, growth by a fixed percentage per period—such as compound interest—produces the same pattern.

Doubling time

Exponential processes are characterized by a constant doubling time: the time needed for the quantity to double. For a growth rate of r percent per period, the doubling time is approximately 70/r periods (the "rule of 70"). Because each doubling adds more than all previous growth combined, exponential processes can appear deceptively slow before rising abruptly—a property illustrated by the classic wheat and chessboard problem.

Examples

Early-stage population growth of bacteria, viruses, and invasive species is often exponential, as is the early spread of an epidemic with a reproduction number above one. Compound interest, chain reactions, and—over the period described by Moore's law—the density of transistors on integrated circuits also follow exponential patterns. In practice, resource limits eventually bend exponential growth into logistic growth toward a carrying capacity.

Malthusian growth

Thomas Robert Malthus argued in 1798 that population grows geometrically (exponentially) while food supply grows only arithmetically, a mismatch he predicted would produce famine and restraint—an argument that shaped later thought on population and resources.
