1080 usage in New Zealand

1080, the brand name given to the synthetic version of sodium fluoroacetate, is used in New Zealand in efforts to control introduced mammalian pests, principally the common brushtail possum, as well as rats, stoats and other predators that threaten native wildlife and spread bovine tuberculosis. New Zealand is the world's largest user of 1080, applying most of it by aerial distribution over conservation land and farmland.

Background

New Zealand's native birds and invertebrates evolved in the absence of mammalian predators. The introduction of possums, rats, stoats and ferrets caused catastrophic declines in native species. Sodium fluoroacetate occurs naturally in some plants in Australia, Africa and Brazil, and was synthesised in the 1940s. It has been used in New Zealand since 1954.

Application

1080 is delivered in cereal-based or carrot baits, by ground laying or by helicopter over rugged terrain. Large-scale aerial operations, coordinated by the Department of Conservation and OSPRI under programmes such as Predator Free 2050, target pest eruptions following beech mast (heavy seeding) events that fuel rat and stoat plagues.

Effects

1080 breaks down in water and soil and does not bioaccumulate. Monitoring shows dramatic recoveries in some native bird populations after operations, though accidental by-kill of some native birds and of dogs and deer has occurred. Reviews by the Parliamentary Commissioner for the Environment (2011) and the Environmental Protection Authority concluded that the benefits of use outweigh the risks, while recommending ongoing research into alternatives.

Controversy

The use of 1080 is publicly contentious. Opponents cite animal welfare concerns, potential contamination of water supplies, risks to hunting dogs and deer herds, and call for greater investment in trapping and alternative methods. Protests and threats against Department of Conservation staff have accompanied major operations.
