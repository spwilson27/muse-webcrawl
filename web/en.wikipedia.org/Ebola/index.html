Ebola

Ebola, also known as Ebola virus disease (EVD), is a viral haemorrhagic fever of humans and other primates caused by viruses of the genus Ebolavirus. It was first identified in 1976 in outbreaks near the Ebola River in what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and in South Sudan.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically begin two days to three weeks after infection with fever, sore throat, muscle pain, and headaches, followed by vomiting, diarrhoea, and rash, with decreased function of the liver and kidneys. Some patients bleed both internally and externally. Case fatality rates have averaged around 50 percent, though they have varied widely between outbreaks.

Transmission and outbreaks

The virus spreads through direct contact with the blood or body fluids of an infected person or animal, and with contaminated surfaces. Fruit bats are believed to be the natural reservoir. The largest outbreak on record, the West African epidemic of 2013–2016, caused more than 28,000 cases and 11,000 deaths, mainly in Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone. Vaccines against the Zaire ebolavirus have since been licensed and used to control later outbreaks in the Congo.
