Institutional Revolutionary Party

The Institutional Revolutionary Party (Spanish: Partido Revolucionario Institucional, PRI) is a political party in Mexico. Founded in 1929 by former president Plutarco Elías Calles to stabilise the country after the Mexican Revolution, it held uninterrupted power in Mexico for 71 years, from 1929 until 2000 — one of the longest continuous party governments of the twentieth century — before returning to the presidency under Enrique Peña Nieto from 2012 to 2018.

The PRI emblem uses the green, white and red of the Mexican flag.

History

Calles created the National Revolutionary Party (PNR) in 1929 to end the cycle of caudillo rebellion and give the revolutionary factions a single institutional home. Lázaro Cárdenas reorganised it in 1938 as the Party of the Mexican Revolution (PRM), structured by corporatist sectors — labour, peasants, the military and the "popular" sector. In 1946 it took its present name. Presidents served single six-year terms (sexenios), and the outgoing president traditionally selected his successor (the dedazo), producing orderly transfers of power within a one-party-dominant system that the writer Mario Vargas Llosa famously called "the perfect dictatorship".

Dominance and decline

PRI governments delivered decades of political stability and rapid growth (the "Mexican miracle"), but also authoritarianism, corruption and repression, including the Tlatelolco massacre of students in 1968. The disputed 1988 election, which the party narrowly won against Cuauhtémoc Cárdenas, accelerated demands for reform. The PRI lost its congressional majority in 1997 and the presidency in 2000 to Vicente Fox of the PAN. It returned to power with Peña Nieto in 2012, but corruption scandals and the rise of Andrés Manuel López Obrador's MORENA party, which won the presidency in 2018, reduced the PRI to a secondary force, though it remained strong in several state governorships.
