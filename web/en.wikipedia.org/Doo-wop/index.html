Doo-wop

Doo-wop (also spelled doo wop or doo-wopp) is a genre of vocal-group harmony music, a form of rhythm and blues that developed in African-American communities of New York, Philadelphia, Chicago, Baltimore, Newark, Pittsburgh, and other eastern and midwestern cities in the 1940s and achieved mainstream popularity in the 1950s and early 1960s. It features vocal group harmony carrying an engaging melody over simple chord progressions, nonsense syllables, a simple beat, and sometimes little or no instrumentation.

Characteristics

A typical doo-wop group consists of four or five singers: a lead tenor, a falsetto, a baritone, and a deep bass voice providing the rhythmic foundation. Songs frequently use the "ice cream changes" (I–vi–IV–V) chord progression and nonsense syllables — "doo wop", "sh-boom", "doo-lang" — from which the genre eventually took its name. The term "doo-wop" itself was not in common use until the genre's revival as an oldies category in the 1970s; in its heyday the music was marketed simply as rhythm and blues or rock and roll.

History

The style grew out of the close-harmony singing of groups such as the Ink Spots and the Mills Brothers in the 1930s and 1940s, combined with gospel quartet traditions. Young singers rehearsed on street corners, in schoolyards, and in subway stations, where tiled walls provided natural echo. Formative hits included the Orioles' "It's Too Soon to Know" (1948) and the Penguins' "Earth Angel" (1954). The Five Satins' "In the Still of the Night" (1956), recorded in a church basement, became one of the genre's standards.

At its commercial peak, doo-wop produced major hits such as "Sh-Boom" by the Chords (1954), "Why Do Fools Fall in Love" by Frankie Lymon and the Teenagers (1956), "Blue Moon" by the Marcels (1961), and "The Lion Sleeps Tonight" by the Tokens (1961). Italian-American groups such as Dion and the Belmonts and Jewish and mixed groups also became prominent, making doo-wop one of the era's most integrated popular styles.

Decline and revival

The genre declined after about 1963 with the British Invasion and changing tastes, though its harmonies influenced Motown, soul, and later artists such as Billy Joel and Boyz II Men. A nostalgic revival beginning with concerts in the late 1960s established doo-wop as a mainstay of oldies radio.
