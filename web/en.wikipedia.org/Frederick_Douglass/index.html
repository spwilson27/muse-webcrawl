Frederick Douglass
Born: Frederick Augustus Washington Bailey, c. February 1818, Cordova, Maryland, U.S.
Died: February 20, 1895 (aged 77), Washington, D.C., U.S.
Occupations: Abolitionist, writer, orator, statesman
Known for: Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave (1845)

Frederick Douglass (born Frederick Augustus Washington Bailey, c. February 1818 – February 20, 1895) was an American social reformer, abolitionist, orator, writer, and statesman. He became the most important leader of the movement for African-American civil rights in the 19th century. After escaping from slavery in Maryland in 1838, Douglass rose to national prominence as a writer and speaker in Massachusetts and New York, and his 1845 autobiography, Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave, became a best-seller that fueled the abolitionist cause.

Douglass believed in dialogue and in making alliances across racial and ideological divides, and he argued that the United States Constitution was fundamentally an anti-slavery document. He also supported women's suffrage, attending the Seneca Falls Convention in 1848, and held several public offices late in life, including U.S. Minister to Haiti.

Early life and escape

Douglass was born into slavery on the Eastern Shore of Maryland. Separated from his mother as an infant, he was taught the alphabet by Sophia Auld, the wife of a Baltimore slaveholder, before her husband forbade the lessons. Douglass continued learning to read in secret, an experience he later described as the pathway from slavery to freedom. In September 1838 he escaped to New York City disguised as a sailor, and settled in New Bedford, Massachusetts, where he adopted the surname Douglass, suggested by Nathan Johnson after a character in Walter Scott's poem The Lady of the Lake.

Abolitionist career

At an 1841 anti-slavery convention in Nantucket, Douglass was invited to speak about his experiences, and his eloquence launched his career as a lecturer for the Massachusetts Anti-Slavery Society. He published three autobiographies: the Narrative (1845), My Bondage and My Freedom (1855), and Life and Times of Frederick Douglass (1881, revised 1892). To avoid recapture after the Narrative named his enslavers, he toured Britain and Ireland, where supporters raised funds to purchase his legal freedom in 1846.

Douglass founded and edited several abolitionist newspapers, beginning with The North Star in Rochester, New York, in 1847. During the American Civil War he recruited African-American soldiers for the Union Army, including the famed 54th Massachusetts Infantry Regiment, and pressed President Abraham Lincoln to make emancipation a central war aim.

Later life and legacy

After the Civil War, Douglass served in several federal posts: president of the Freedman's Savings Bank, Marshal of the District of Columbia, Recorder of Deeds, and Minister Resident and Consul General to Haiti (1889–1891). He died of a heart attack in Washington, D.C., in 1895, hours after attending a meeting of the National Council of Women. Douglass remains one of the most celebrated figures of American letters and civil rights history; his famous Fourth of July address of 1852, "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?", is still widely read.
