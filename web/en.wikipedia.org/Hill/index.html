Hill

A hill is a landform that extends above the surrounding terrain. It often has a distinct summit and is generally considered smaller and less steep than a mountain, though the distinction is a matter of local convention rather than any universally accepted definition.

Terminology

There is no precise international standard separating hills from mountains. In the United Kingdom, geographers historically regarded a summit above 2,000 feet (610 m) as a mountain, and although the government no longer applies this rule, it persists in popular usage; in the United States, various thresholds around 1,000–2,000 feet of local relief have been used. Hills may carry specific names by shape or origin: a butte is an isolated steep-sided hill, a mesa a flat-topped one, a drumlin a glacially formed elongated hill, and a tor a rocky outcrop on a hilltop.

Formation

Hills form by many processes: uplift and faulting, volcanic activity, deposition of glacial debris (moraines and drumlins), and the erosion of surrounding land, which can leave resistant rock standing as a residual hill. Artificial hills, or mounds, include burial mounds, motte-and-bailey castle earthworks, and spoil heaps.

Significance

Hills have long been chosen for settlement and defence: hillforts crowned many British and European hills in the Iron Age, and cities such as Rome (the Seven Hills) and Edinburgh grew around defensible heights. Hills offer advantages in military strategy, drainage, and observation, but are obstacles to road and rail construction, leading to cuttings, tunnels, and switchback roads.
