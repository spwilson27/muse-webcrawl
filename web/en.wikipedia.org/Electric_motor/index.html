Electric motor

An electric motor is an electrical machine that converts electrical energy into mechanical energy. Most electric motors operate through the interaction between the motor's magnetic field and electric current in a wire winding to generate force in the form of torque applied on the motor's shaft.

Electric motors are found in applications as diverse as fans, pumps, machine tools, household appliances, and electric vehicles.

Principle of operation

When a current-carrying conductor is placed in a magnetic field, it experiences a force (the Lorentz force). In a motor, windings on the rotor (or stator) are energized in sequence so that the resulting torque turns the shaft continuously. The reverse process, converting mechanical energy to electrical energy, is performed by a generator.

Types

Motors are classified by power source as DC motors and AC motors. AC motors include induction (asynchronous) motors, which dominate industrial use, and synchronous motors. DC motors include brushed motors with mechanical commutators and brushless DC motors with electronic commutation. Other types include stepper motors and servo motors, used where precise position control is required.

Efficiency and applications

Electric motors consume a large fraction of the world's electricity — roughly 40–45% of global consumption is attributed to motor-driven systems. Efficiencies of large industrial motors can exceed 95%. Electric motors are typically far more efficient and simpler to maintain than internal combustion engines, and they are central to electrified transport.

History

Early electromagnetic devices were demonstrated in the 1820s by Michael Faraday and others; the first practical DC motors appeared in the 1830s. Nikola Tesla's induction motor patent of 1888 helped establish AC power systems and made the modern industrial motor possible.
