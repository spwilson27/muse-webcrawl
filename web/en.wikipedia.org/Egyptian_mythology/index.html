Egyptian mythology is the collection of myths from ancient Egypt, which describe the actions of the Egyptian gods as a means of understanding the world around them. The beliefs that these myths express are an important part of ancient Egyptian religion. Myths appear frequently in Egyptian writings and art, particularly in short stories and in religious material such as hymns, ritual texts, funerary texts, and temple decoration.

Creation myths

Several creation traditions coexisted. At Heliopolis, the god Atum emerged from the primordial waters of Nun and generated Shu and Tefnut, whose descendants formed the Ennead of nine gods. At Memphis, the god Ptah created the world through the thought of his heart and the speech of his tongue. These myths were not seen as contradictory but as complementary perspectives.

The Osiris myth

The central myth cycle concerns Osiris, murdered and dismembered by his brother Set, reassembled and revived by his wife Isis, and succeeded by his son Horus, who battles Set for the throne. The myth underpinned Egyptian ideas of kingship, death and resurrection: the dead pharaoh became Osiris, and his successor was Horus.

The solar cycle

The sun god Ra was believed to sail across the sky by day and through the underworld (Duat) by night, fighting the serpent Apep, embodiment of chaos. This daily rebirth expressed the Egyptian conviction that order (maat) must continually overcome chaos (isfet).

Afterlife

Egyptians believed the deceased would be judged in the Hall of Maat, where the heart was weighed against the feather of truth; the virtuous entered the Field of Reeds. Texts such as the Book of the Dead guided the soul through the Duat.
