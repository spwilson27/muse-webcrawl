Linen

Linen is a textile made from the fibres of the flax plant, Linum usitatissimum. Linen is very strong and absorbent and dries faster than cotton; garments made of linen are valued for their exceptional coolness and freshness in hot and humid weather. It is one of the oldest textiles in the world: dyed flax fibres found in a Georgian cave date back more than 30,000 years, and linen was widely used in ancient Egypt for clothing and for wrapping mummies.

Production

Making linen is labour-intensive. Flax stems are harvested, and the fibres are separated from the stalk by retting (soaking or dew exposure), then scutching and hackling to remove woody matter and align the long line fibres. The fibres are spun into yarn and woven. Linen is produced on most continents, with notable production in Western Europe, China and the Indian subcontinent. Typical products include bed sheets ("linens", retaining the name even when made of other fibres), tablecloths, towels, clothing, and canvas.

Properties

Linen fibres are two to three times stronger than cotton, are naturally resistant to dirt and stains, and soften with washing, though they have poor elasticity and wrinkle readily. Flax seeds also yield linseed oil, used in paints and finishes.
