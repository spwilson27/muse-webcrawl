Stuffing

Stuffing, also known as filling, or dressing in some regions of the United States, is a mixture of food typically composed of a starchy base (usually bread), vegetables, herbs, and sometimes meat or eggs, used to fill a cavity in another food item during cooking. Stuffing is most famously associated with roast poultry, especially the Thanksgiving turkey in North America.

History

Stuffing animal carcasses dates to antiquity: the Roman cookbook Apicius (attributed to the era of the Roman Empire) contains recipes for stuffed chicken, dormouse, hare, and pig. In medieval Europe, stuffings — then often called forcemeat or farce, from the Latin farcire, "to stuff" — were used for poultry, game, and vegetables. The first known documented stuffing recipes in English appear in works such as The Forme of Cury (c. 1390).

Types

Bread-based stuffing is the classic North American style, seasoned with celery, onion, sage, thyme, and stock. Regional variants include Southern cornbread dressing, New England oyster stuffing, and Pennsylvania Dutch potato filling. Around the world, stuffings are made with rice (as in Middle Eastern and Greek cooking), chestnuts, sausage meat (British sage-and-onion stuffing), couscous, and fruit such as prunes and apples (common in northern European pork dishes). Vegetables — peppers, tomatoes, zucchini, cabbage leaves (stuffed cabbage), and grape leaves (dolma) — are also stuffed in many cuisines.

Food safety

Cooking stuffing inside poultry poses food-safety risks, since the stuffing may not reach a temperature sufficient to kill bacteria such as Salmonella. Food-safety authorities recommend cooking stuffing separately, or ensuring the centre of in-bird stuffing reaches at least 74 °C (165 °F).
