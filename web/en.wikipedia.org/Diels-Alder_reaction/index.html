Diels–Alder reaction

The Diels–Alder reaction is a reaction in organic chemistry between a conjugated diene and a substituted alkene, commonly termed the dienophile, to form a substituted cyclohexene derivative. It is the prototype of a [4+2] cycloaddition, a concerted pericyclic reaction in which two new carbon–carbon bonds form simultaneously.

Overview

The reaction was first described by Otto Diels and Kurt Alder in 1928, work for which they received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1950. It proceeds fastest with electron-rich dienes and electron-poor dienophiles ("normal demand"), though inverse-electron-demand variants exist. The reaction is stereospecific: the geometry of the dienophile is retained in the product, and the endo product is usually favored kinetically through secondary orbital interactions.

Variants and applications

Hetero-Diels–Alder reactions use carbonyls or imines as dienophiles to give oxygen- or nitrogen-containing rings, and the aza- and oxa-variants are widely used in synthesis. The reaction is a cornerstone of complex molecule synthesis, featuring in total syntheses of steroids, alkaloids, and many pharmaceuticals, and its reversibility (the retro-Diels–Alder reaction) is exploited in self-healing materials and click chemistry.
