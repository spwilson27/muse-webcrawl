Scheme (mathematics)

In mathematics, a scheme is a mathematical structure that enlarges the notion of an algebraic variety so that arithmetic (working over rings rather than fields) and geometry can be treated in a unified way. Schemes were introduced by Alexander Grothendieck around 1960 and are now the basic objects of modern algebraic geometry.

Definition

An affine scheme is the prime spectrum Spec(R) of a commutative ring R — the set of its prime ideals, equipped with the Zariski topology and a sheaf of rings (the structure sheaf) whose sections are locally given by fractions of elements of R. A general scheme is a locally ringed space that is covered by open sets isomorphic to affine schemes. Morphisms of schemes are morphisms of locally ringed spaces.

Motivation and examples

The scheme viewpoint has several advantages over classical varieties. Points of a scheme include "generic points" for irreducible subvarieties; nilpotent elements in the structure sheaf encode infinitesimal information such as tangent directions and multiple roots; and varieties over any base — for example over the integers — can be studied, linking geometry to number theory. Spec(ℤ) functions as "the integers as a geometric object", and arithmetic surfaces such as schemes over Spec(ℤ) underlie proofs in Diophantine geometry, including the approach to Fermat's Last Theorem through modularity of elliptic curves.

Development

Grothendieck and Jean Dieudonné set out the theory in the Éléments de géométrie algébrique (1960s). Schemes have since been extended to stacks and to derived algebraic geometry. They underpin much of modern number theory, including the Langlands program's geometric side and the theory of motives.
