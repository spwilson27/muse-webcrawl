Duodenum

The duodenum is the first section of the small intestine in most higher vertebrates, including mammals, reptiles, and birds. In fish, the divisions of the small intestine are not as clear, and the terms anterior intestine or proximal intestine may be used instead of duodenum.

The duodenum is a hollow jointed tube about 25–38 centimetres long connecting the stomach to the jejunum. It begins with the duodenal bulb and ends at the suspensory muscle of the duodenum (ligament of Treitz). The duodenum is largely responsible for the breakdown of food in the small intestine, using enzymes, and also regulates the rate of gastric emptying via hormonal pathways.

Structure

The duodenum is divided into four parts: the superior (bulb), descending, horizontal and ascending parts. It curves in a "C" shape around the head of the pancreas. In the descending part, the major duodenal papilla (ampulla of Vater) receives the common bile duct and pancreatic duct, delivering bile and pancreatic enzymes. Brunner's glands in the duodenal submucosa secrete alkaline mucus that protects the mucosa from acidic gastric contents.

Function

Chyme entering from the stomach is mixed with bile (emulsifying fats) and pancreatic juice (supplying enzymes and bicarbonate). Hormones such as secretin and cholecystokinin coordinate these secretions. Most iron absorption and much calcium absorption occur in the duodenum.

Clinical significance

Duodenal ulcers are a common form of peptic ulcer disease, often associated with Helicobacter pylori infection or NSAID use. Other conditions include duodenitis, duodenal atresia in newborns, and coeliac disease, whose characteristic villous atrophy is biopsied in the duodenum.

History

The name derives from the Latin duodenum digitorum, "twelve finger-widths", referring to its length of roughly twelve finger breadths, a description attributed to Herophilus of Chalcedon in the 3rd century BC.
