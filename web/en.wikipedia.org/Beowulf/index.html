Beowulf is an Old English epic poem in the tradition of Germanic heroic legend, consisting of 3,182 alliterative lines. It is one of the most important and most often translated works of Old English literature. The date of its composition is a matter of much debate among scholars; the surviving manuscript was written between about 975 and 1025 AD. The poem is set in pagan Scandinavia in the 6th century, though it was composed in Christian England.

Plot

Beowulf, a hero of the Geats, comes to the aid of Hrothgar, king of the Danes, whose great hall Heorot has been terrorized for twelve years by the monster Grendel. Beowulf kills Grendel with his bare hands, then defeats Grendel's mother in her underwater lair with a giant's sword found there. Returning home in triumph, he eventually becomes king of the Geats. Fifty years later, an aged Beowulf fights a dragon that has been provoked by the theft of a cup from its hoard; with the help of the young warrior Wiglaf he kills the dragon, but is himself mortally wounded. The poem ends with his funeral and a lament for his passing.

Manuscript and scholarship

The poem survives in a single manuscript, the Nowell Codex, which was damaged in the Ashburnham House fire of 1731; transcripts made before and after the fire preserve letters since lost. Interest in the poem grew in the 19th century, and J. R. R. Tolkien's 1936 lecture "Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics" transformed scholarship by defending the poem as a unified work of art rather than a quarry of historical details.

Themes and legacy

The poem interweaves heroic values — loyalty, kinship, generosity, fame — with a somber sense of inevitable decline, and mixes pagan subject matter with Christian reflection. Beowulf has been translated many times, notably by Seamus Heaney (1999), and has influenced modern fantasy, including Tolkien's own fiction.
