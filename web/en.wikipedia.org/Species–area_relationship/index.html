Species–area relationship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A species–area curve on log–log axes

The species–area relationship or species–area curve describes the relationship between the area of a habitat, or of part of a habitat, and the number of species found within that area. Larger areas tend to contain larger numbers of species, and empirically the relationship is often well described by a power law:

S = cAz

where S is the number of species, A is the area, c is a constant depending on the taxon and region, and z is the slope of the relationship in log–log space, typically between 0.1 and 0.4 for islands.

History

The pattern was noted as early as the natural-history voyages of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and was formalised by Olof Arrhenius in 1921 and Henry Gleason in 1922. Robert MacArthur and E. O. Wilson incorporated the species–area relationship into their equilibrium theory of island biogeography (1963, 1967), in which the number of species on an island reflects a balance between immigration and extinction.

Implications

Species–area curves are widely used in conservation biology to estimate extinction rates following habitat loss: if habitat area is reduced to a fraction of its original extent, the power law predicts the proportion of species committed to extinction. The relationship also underlies the design of nature reserves and the study of habitat fragmentation.
