Poisson's ratio

Common symbolsν (nu)
SI unitdimensionless
Named afterSiméon Denis Poisson
Typical range−1.0 to 0.5 (isotropic materials)

Poisson's ratio is a measure of the Poisson effect, the deformation of a material in directions perpendicular to the specific direction of loading. When a material is compressed along one axis, it tends to expand in the other two directions perpendicular to the direction of compression. Conversely, when a sample of material is stretched in one direction, it tends to contract in the other two directions.

The ratio is named after the French mathematician and physicist Siméon Denis Poisson (1781–1840).

Definition

Poisson's ratio ν is defined as the negative of the ratio of transverse strain to axial strain: ν = − εtrans / εaxial, where strain is the change in length divided by the original length. For most common materials, ν lies between 0.0 and 0.5. A perfectly incompressible isotropic material deformed elastically would have a Poisson's ratio of exactly 0.5.

Typical values

Most steels and rigid polymers have values around 0.3. Rubber has a ratio near 0.5, while cork is close to 0, which is why cork makes a good bottle stopper: it does not expand sideways when pushed into a bottle neck. Materials with negative Poisson's ratios, which expand transversely when stretched, are called auxetic materials.

Applications

Poisson's ratio is a fundamental parameter in solid mechanics, used in the design of pressure vessels, pipelines, aircraft structures, and in geophysics for analysing seismic waves in the Earth's crust.
