The Odeon of Pericles was a concert hall in ancient Athens, built around 442 BC under the statesman Pericles on the south-east slope of the Acropolis, next to the Theatre of Dionysus. It is generally regarded as the earliest known odeon in the Greek world.

Description

According to Plutarch, the building was square in plan, with many seats and many columns, and its pyramidal roof was said to imitate the great tent of the Persian king Xerxes. Excavations uncovered a nearly square hall, about 62 by 68 metres, whose roof was carried on dozens of internal columns arranged in rows; it could hold perhaps 3,000 people. The odeon hosted the musical contests of the Panathenaic festival, rehearsals and preliminary auditions, and at times served as a law court, a lecture hall and even a grain store.

History

The odeon was burned during Sulla's sack of Athens in 86 BC — according to Appian, it was set alight so its timbers could not be used against the Romans. It was rebuilt by Ariobarzanes II, king of Cappadocia, in the mid-first century BC. Only scant foundation remains survive today.
