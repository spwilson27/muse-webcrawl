Mesha Stele

Mesha Stele

Created: c. 840 BC

Discovered: 1868, Dhiban, Jordan

Material: Basalt

Current location: Louvre, Paris

The Mesha Stele, also known as the Moabite Stone, is a basalt inscription from about 840 BC commissioned by King Mesha of Moab (in modern Jordan). It is one of the most important direct accounts of biblical history outside the Hebrew Bible.

Discovery

The stele was discovered intact in 1868 at Dhiban by the missionary F. A. Klein. Before it could be secured by European scholars, local tribesmen, in a dispute, heated the stone and poured cold water on it, shattering it. A paper squeeze taken beforehand allowed most of the text to be reconstructed; the fragments are now held by the Louvre Museum.

Content

The 34-line inscription, written in the Moabite language closely related to Hebrew, recounts how Chemosh, the god of Moab, was angry with his people and allowed Israel under King Omri to oppress them, and how Mesha then revolted and reconquered the lost territory. It is famous for containing one of the earliest extra-biblical references to the Israelite God (YHWH) and to the "House of David" in a much-debated line.
