Cultural relativism

Cultural relativism is the principle that a person's beliefs and practices should be understood in terms of that individual's own culture, rather than judged by the standards of another. Developed as a methodological cornerstone of anthropology by Franz Boas and his students — including Ruth Benedict and Margaret Mead — it arose in opposition to 19th-century theories of unilineal cultural evolution that ranked societies on a single scale of progress.

Methodological and moral relativism

Scholars distinguish methodological relativism — the working rule that fieldworkers should set aside their own categories to grasp the internal logic of another culture — from moral relativism, the ethical claim that no culture's practices can be judged by external standards. Most anthropologists endorse the former; the latter is more contested.

History

Boas argued from the early 1900s that cultures develop through their particular histories (historical particularism) and must not be measured against Western civilization. His student Ruth Benedict's Patterns of Culture (1934) popularized the idea that cultures are integrated configurations. In 1947, as the United Nations drafted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the American Anthropological Association issued a statement warning that rights framed in one culture's terms might not apply universally — a position the Association later moderated.

Criticism and debates

Critics argue that strong cultural relativism can excuse practices such as female genital cutting, honor killings, or the denial of rights to women and minorities, and can conflict with universal human rights. Defenders reply that relativism need not forbid moral judgment, only ethnocentric judgment; Clifford Geertz defended it as an antidote to ethnocentrism rather than a doctrine of "anything goes." The concept remains central to debates in anthropology, philosophy, and international politics.
