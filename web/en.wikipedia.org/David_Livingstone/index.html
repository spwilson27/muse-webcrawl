David Livingstone (19 March 1813 – 1 May 1873) was a Scottish physician, Congregationalist missionary with the London Missionary Society, and explorer of Africa. One of the most famous Europeans of the Victorian era, he traversed much of the southern African interior, was the first European to document Victoria Falls, and campaigned passionately against the East African slave trade, which he called "the open sore of the world".

Early life

Livingstone was born in the mill town of Blantyre, Scotland, and from age ten worked long shifts in a cotton mill, educating himself in Latin and science at night and in the works of missionaries and explorers. He qualified as a physician in Glasgow, joined the London Missionary Society — inspired by Robert Moffat's accounts of the African interior — and sailed for the Cape in 1840.

Exploration

Based first at Kuruman and then at Kolobeng in Bechuanaland, Livingstone pushed northward beyond any European, reaching Lake Ngami in 1849 and the upper Zambezi in 1851. Determined to open a route to the interior for "Christianity, commerce and civilisation", he crossed the continent from the Zambezi to Luanda on the Atlantic in 1854 and back to the Indian Ocean coast at Quelimane in 1856 — the first recorded transcontinental crossing of southern Africa. En route in November 1855 he became the first European to see the great waterfall the Lozi called Mosi-oa-Tunya ("the smoke that thunders"), which he renamed Victoria Falls for Queen Victoria.

His official Zambezi Expedition (1858–1864) ended in recall after navigating the Ruoha and Shire rivers and reaching Lake Malawi, but he returned in 1866 to search for the source of the Nile. Years without reliable contact at home produced the celebrated expedition of journalist Henry Morton Stanley, who "found" him at Ujiji on Lake Tanganyika in November 1871 — the occasion of the famous (and probably embellished) greeting "Dr Livingstone, I presume?"

Death and legacy

Refusing to leave Africa, Livingstone died of malaria and dysentery at Chitambo's village in 1873. His attendants Susi and Chuma carried his body — his heart buried under a tree at the site — over a thousand miles to the coast, whence it was returned to Britain; he was buried in Westminster Abbey in 1874. His journals and Stanley's publicity galvanised the campaign that eventually suppressed the East African slave trade, but his vision of river-borne commerce also helped prepare the way for the European "Scramble for Africa", an irony much debated in his later reputation. Cities (Livingstone, Zambia; Blantyre, Malawi) and institutions across the region bear his name, and he remains honoured in Zambia, Zimbabwe and Malawi as an opponent of slavery.
