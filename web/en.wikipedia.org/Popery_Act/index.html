Popery Act

The Popery Act (An Act to prevent the further Growth of Popery) was an Act of the Parliament of Ireland, passed in 1704 (2 Anne c. 6) and amended in 1709. It formed a central part of the Penal Laws applied to Roman Catholics in Ireland, and is often regarded as the most severe of that body of legislation.

Provisions

The Act's most consequential provision concerned the inheritance of land. When a Catholic landowner died, his estate was to be divided equally among all his sons (the practice known as gavelkind), rather than passing intact to the eldest son by primogeniture. This rule was intended, over generations, to break up large Catholic estates into uneconomic fragments. However, if the eldest son converted to the established Church of Ireland, he could inherit the whole estate, providing a strong incentive to conformity.

Other provisions prohibited Catholics from purchasing or taking long leases of land, from receiving property by inheritance from Protestants, and from acting as guardians to Catholic children. Catholic clergy were required to register, and provisions were directed against the entry of further priests and members of religious orders into the kingdom.

Effects and repeal

Although enforcement varied, the Act greatly reduced Catholic landownership in Ireland over the eighteenth century. Its inheritance rules were relaxed by the Roman Catholic Relief Acts of 1778 and 1782, and most of the remaining penal legislation was dismantled during the later eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, culminating in Catholic emancipation in 1829.
