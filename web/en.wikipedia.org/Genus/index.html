Genus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genus (plural genera) is a taxonomic rank used in the biological classification of living and fossil organisms as well as viruses. In the hierarchy of biological classification, genus comes above species and below family. In binomial nomenclature, the genus name forms the first part of the binomial species name for each species within the genus, e.g. Panthera leo (lion) and Panthera tigris (tiger) are two species within the genus Panthera. A genus is composed of one or more species; a genus containing only a single species is termed monotypic.

Nomenclature

The scientific name of a genus is a Latin or latinised word treated as a noun in the nominative singular, always written with an initial capital letter and in italics (or underlined). Generic names are governed by the international codes of nomenclature for zoology, botany, bacteria and viruses; within each code a name may be used only once, and the name is anchored to a type species. The author who first publishes the name is cited, e.g. Homo Linnaeus, 1758.

Criteria

There is no single objective rule for delimiting genera: taxonomists weigh morphological similarity, monophyly (a genus should comprise all descendants of a common ancestor), ecological coherence and practical stability of names. Genetic studies have led to many reclassifications, splitting or merging genera — for example, the reassignment of species among frog and lizard genera as DNA phylogenies replaced older arrangements. The number of recognised genera is very large: animals alone include well over 100,000 named genera, and new ones are described every year in taxonomy journals.

History

The concept in its modern sense was developed by Joseph Pitton de Tournefort and standardised by Carl Linnaeus in Species Plantarum (1753) and the 10th edition of Systema Naturae (1758). Linnaeus grouped species into genera largely by reproductive structures in plants and by teeth and other characters in animals.
