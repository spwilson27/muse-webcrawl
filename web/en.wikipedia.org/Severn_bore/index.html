Severn bore

Surfers and spectators gather for large Severn bores

The Severn bore is a tidal bore seen on the tidal reaches of the River Severn in southwestern England. It is formed when the rising tide from the Bristol Channel forces its way into the narrowing, shallow estuary of the Severn; the incoming water piles up into a wave that travels upstream against the river's flow for some 25 miles (40 km), as far as Gloucester and, at the highest tides, beyond.

Formation

The Severn Estuary has the second highest tidal range in the world, up to about 15 metres (49 feet) at spring tides. The funnel shape and shallowing bed of the estuary compress the incoming tide until it forms a distinct breaking wave front, whose height can reach two metres or more on the largest tides. Bores occur on dozens of days each year, with the biggest typically around the spring and autumn equinoxes.

Ratings and recreation

The bore is conventionally rated on a scale of one to five stars according to its predicted height. The largest bores attract crowds of spectators at vantage points such as Minsterworth, Stonebench and Over Bridge, and surfers and kayakers who attempt to ride the wave for as long as possible; the record rides on the Severn bore cover several miles. River users are warned that the bore arrives with a sudden rise in water level and strong currents.

History

The bore has been a feature of life along the Severn for centuries, historically posing a hazard to shipping navigating to Gloucester, whose timing of voyages took account of the tide. It remains one of the best-known tidal bores in the world, comparable to those on the Qiantang River in China and the Amazon.
