Long-term potentiation

In neuroscience, long-term potentiation (LTP) is a persistent strengthening of synapses based on recent patterns of activity. These are patterns of synaptic activity that produce a long-lasting increase in signal transmission between two neurons. The opposite of LTP is long-term depression (LTD), which produces a long-lasting decrease in synaptic strength. LTP is widely considered one of the major cellular mechanisms underlying learning and memory, and provides experimental support for Hebb's postulate of 1949: "cells that fire together wire together."

LTP increases the number and conductance of postsynaptic AMPA receptors

Discovery

LTP was first observed by Terje Lømo in 1966 in the anaesthetised rabbit hippocampus and described in detail by Lømo and Timothy Bliss in 1973: brief high-frequency stimulation of the perforant path produced an enhancement of responses in the dentate gyrus that lasted for hours. Subsequent work established LTP in the hippocampal CA1 region, neocortex, amygdala and other structures.

Mechanisms

The canonical form at CA3–CA1 synapses requires NMDA receptors, which act as coincidence detectors: they open only when presynaptic glutamate release coincides with strong postsynaptic depolarisation, relieving their magnesium block. The resulting calcium influx activates CaMKII and other kinases, driving insertion of additional AMPA receptors into the postsynaptic density (early LTP, lasting 1–3 hours). Late-phase LTP, lasting many hours, requires new gene expression and protein synthesis involving CREB, and is associated with structural changes such as enlargement of dendritic spines. Key properties are input specificity, cooperativity, and associativity.

Relation to learning and memory

Pharmacological blockade of NMDA receptors impairs both LTP and spatial learning in rodents; genetic manipulations that enhance LTP can enhance memory, and learning itself can occlude subsequent LTP induction — evidence that LTP is a genuine memory mechanism. The synaptic plasticity and memory (SPM) hypothesis formalises the criteria for this claim. Impaired LTP is also a feature of Alzheimer's disease models.
