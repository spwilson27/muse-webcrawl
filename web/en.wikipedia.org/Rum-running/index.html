Rum-running

Rum-running, or bootlegging, is the illegal business of transporting (smuggling) alcoholic beverages where such transportation is forbidden by law. The term rum-running is most often applied to smuggling by sea during the Prohibition era in the United States (1920–1933), when ships carried rum and other liquor from Caribbean and Canadian ports to "Rum Row", a line of vessels anchored just outside U.S. territorial waters off the East Coast.

Rum-runners used fast boats to ferry liquor from offshore ships to shore under cover of darkness.

History

Smuggling of spirits long predates Prohibition; rum was smuggled past British customs in colonial America, and the Whisky Rebellion of the 1790s protested federal liquor taxes. Rum-running in its famous form began in 1920 when the Eighteenth Amendment and the Volstead Act banned the manufacture and sale of alcohol in the United States. Entrepreneurs such as William "Bill" McCoy — whose genuine, undiluted liquor gave rise to the phrase "the real McCoy" — sailed cargoes of rum from Bimini in the Bahamas to the Florida coast, while Canadian whisky crossed the Great Lakes and the Detroit River, and French ships from Saint Pierre and Miquelon supplied the Northeast.

Rum Row and enforcement

Large mother ships lay at anchor outside the three-mile (later twelve-mile) limit, beyond the jurisdiction of the U.S. Coast Guard, selling to fast contact boats that ran the cargo ashore. The Coast Guard responded with faster cutters, seized rum-runners' vessels when they crossed the limit, and eventually gained authority under treaties with supplier nations to board ships further out. Notorious clashes, seizures, and high-speed chases made the rum-runners folk heroes of the era. After the repeal of Prohibition in 1933, large-scale rum-running collapsed, though the term survives as a general word for liquor smuggling.
