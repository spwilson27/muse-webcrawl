Hydrogen peroxide

Hydrogen peroxide
Chemical formula: H2O2
A pale blue liquid, slightly more viscous than water; a versatile oxidiser, bleach and antiseptic.

Hydrogen peroxide is a chemical compound with the formula H2O2. In its pure form it is a very pale blue liquid that is slightly more viscous than water. It is used as an oxidiser, bleaching agent, and antiseptic, usually as a dilute solution (3–6% by weight) in water for consumer use and in higher concentrations for industrial use.

Properties and production

The molecule has a non-planar, skewed structure with an O–O single bond that breaks readily, making hydrogen peroxide a powerful though often slow oxidising agent and a source of the reactive hydroxyl radical. Nearly all commercial hydrogen peroxide is manufactured by the anthraquinone process, in which hydrogen and oxygen are indirectly combined using an anthraquinone working solution. It decomposes to water and oxygen, a reaction catalysed by light, heat, metal ions and the enzyme catalase.

Uses

The largest use is in bleaching pulp and paper and in detergents (often via percarbonates). Dilute solutions disinfect wounds and act as a mouth rinse, though medical guidance now favours gentler alternatives. Concentrated "high-test" peroxide has served as a rocket propellant and torpedo oxidiser. In biology, peroxide is generated as a by-product of metabolism and functions in cell signalling at low concentrations.

History

Hydrogen peroxide was first described by Louis Jacques Thénard in 1818, who produced it by reacting barium peroxide with nitric acid and named it eau oxygénée ("oxygenated water").
