Nucleon

Nucleon
TypesProton, neutron
ClassificationBaryons (fermions, spin-½)

MassProton 938.27 MeV/c²; neutron 939.57 MeV/c²

In physics and chemistry, a nucleon is either a proton or a neutron, considered in its role as a component of an atomic nucleus. The number of nucleons in a nucleus defines the atom's mass number (nucleon number). Protons and neutrons are so similar in mass and behaviour inside the nucleus that they are treated as two states of a single particle, distinguished by the quantum number of isospin.

Properties

Both nucleons are baryons, composed of three quarks: the proton of two up quarks and one down quark (uud, charge +1), the neutron of one up and two down quarks (udd, charge 0). Outside the nucleus the proton is stable, while a free neutron decays by beta decay with a mean lifetime of about 880 seconds (roughly 15 minutes). Inside stable nuclei, neutrons are stabilised by the nuclear binding energy.

Role in the nucleus

Nucleons are bound together by the residual strong interaction, often described as the exchange of pions between them. The count of protons (Z) fixes the chemical element; the count of neutrons (N) fixes the isotope; and their sum (A = Z + N) is the mass number. Nucleon structure — the distribution of charge, magnetism and spin among their quarks and gluons — is an active subject of study at particle accelerators, where deep inelastic scattering first revealed the nucleons' internal constituents.

Terminology

The word "nucleon" was proposed by the Danish physicist Christian Møller around 1941, from the Latin nucleus, as a collective term for the particles making up the nucleus. Until the 1960s, nucleons were regarded as elementary particles; they are now understood to be composite states of quarks and gluons described by quantum chromodynamics.
