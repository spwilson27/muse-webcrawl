Presbyterian polity is a method of church governance typified by the rule of assemblies of presbyters, or elders. It stands in contrast to episcopal polity, governed by bishops, and congregational polity, in which each congregation is autonomous. Presbyterian polity is characteristic of the Reformed and Presbyterian churches that arose from the Protestant Reformation in Switzerland, France, the Netherlands and Scotland.

Structure

Governance proceeds through graded "courts". The local congregation is governed by a session (kirk session) of elders elected by the congregation, moderated by the minister. Congregations are grouped into a presbytery of ministers and representative elders, which ordains and oversees ministers and congregations. Presbyteries may be grouped into synods, and the highest court is the general assembly, which meets periodically and is chaired by an elected moderator who serves typically for one year. Decisions are made by majority vote of the assembled elders, teaching (ministers) and ruling elders having parity.

Offices

Presbyterian polity recognizes the offices of minister (teaching elder), ruling elder and deacon. Ruling elders share in government and oversight; deacons traditionally administer mercy ministry and the church's practical affairs. The parity of ministers — none exercising permanent jurisdiction over others — is a hallmark of the system.

Biblical and historical basis

Presbyterians ground their polity in the New Testament use of presbyteros (elder) and in passages describing councils such as the Jerusalem council of Acts 15. The system was articulated by John Calvin in Geneva and given national form by John Knox's Church of Scotland (Book of Discipline, 1560), whence it spread through Reformed churches worldwide, influencing some secular representative institutions.
