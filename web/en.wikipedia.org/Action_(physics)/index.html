Action (physics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The action is evaluated along a path between two points in configuration space.

In physics, action is a scalar quantity that describes how a physical system changes over time. It is a key quantity in modern physics and is especially important in Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics. Given the structure of the physical system, an experimenter can determine the path that a system will take by minimising (or, more generally, making stationary) its action over the possible paths. This rule, called the principle of least action or principle of stationary action, gives rise to the equations of motion.

Definition

Classically, the action is the integral of the Lagrangian over time:

S = ∫t₁t₂ L dt

where L = T − V is the difference between the kinetic and potential energies of the system. Action has the dimensions of energy × time, the same as angular momentum; its SI unit is the joule-second (J·s). Planck's constant, h, is sometimes described as the "quantum of action".

History

The idea emerged from optics: Pierre de Fermat's principle that light travels along the path of least time, generalised by Pierre Louis Maupertuis in the 1740s into a principle of least action for mechanics. Leonhard Euler and Joseph-Louis Lagrange developed the rigorous variational formulation in the 18th century, leading to the Euler–Lagrange equations. William Rowan Hamilton reformulated mechanics around the action in the 1830s, producing Hamilton's principle and Hamiltonian mechanics.

Significance

The action formulation is prized because it expresses the laws of physics in terms of a single scalar function and automatically respects symmetries: by Noether's theorem, every continuous symmetry of the action corresponds to a conservation law. In the 20th century, the action principle was extended to fields, general relativity (the Einstein–Hilbert action), and quantum mechanics. In Feynman's path integral formulation, the probability amplitude for a process is obtained by summing contributions eiS/ħ over all possible paths, weighted by their action — recovering the classical principle of stationary action in the limit where S is much larger than ħ.

Examples

  • For a free particle, L = ½mv², and the stationary path is uniform motion in a straight line.
  • For a particle in a potential, varying the action yields Newton's second law.
  • In general relativity, extremising the Einstein–Hilbert action with respect to the metric yields Einstein's field equations.
