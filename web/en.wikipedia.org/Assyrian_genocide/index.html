Assyrian genocide

The Assyrian genocide, known in Syriac as the Sayfo ("the Sword"), was the mass murder and deportation of Assyrian Christians in the Ottoman Empire and neighbouring Persia during World War I, principally between 1914 and 1920. It occurred alongside the Armenian genocide and the Greek genocide.

Background and events

Assyrians — adherents of the Church of the East, Chaldean Catholic and Syriac Orthodox churches — lived mainly in Hakkari, Tur Abdin and the Urmia region. During the war, Ottoman forces and allied Kurdish tribes massacred Assyrian villages, beginning with attacks in Hakkari in 1915 and spreading to Tur Abdin, where communities resisted at places such as Azakh. In Urmia, the withdrawal of Russian forces in 1918 was followed by large-scale massacres and a refugee exodus toward British lines in Iraq.

Death toll and recognition

Estimates of deaths commonly range from about 150,000 to 300,000, amounting to a large fraction of the pre-war Assyrian population. Several states and parliaments, including Sweden (2010), Armenia (2015) and Germany (2016), have recognised the Sayfo as genocide, and it is commemorated by the Assyrian diaspora, notably on August 7, Assyrian Martyrs Day. The events devastated the Assyrian homeland and drove the formation of a worldwide diaspora, later compounded by the Simele massacre of 1933 in Iraq.
