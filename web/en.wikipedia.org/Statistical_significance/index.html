Statistical significance

In statistical hypothesis testing, a result has statistical significance when a result at least as "extreme" as the observed sample result would be very unlikely to occur if the null hypothesis were true. More precisely, a study's defined significance level, denoted α, is the probability of the study rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true. The p-value of a result is the probability of obtaining results at least as extreme as those observed, assuming the null hypothesis is correct. A result is statistically significant if its p-value is less than or equal to α.

History

The concept emerged from the work of statisticians including Ronald Fisher, who popularized the 0.05 threshold in the 1920s, and Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson, who developed the framework of significance levels and hypothesis tests. The conventional threshold of α = 0.05 (5%) corresponds to roughly two standard deviations in a normal distribution and has become a widely used — and widely criticized — convention across the sciences.

Interpretation and criticism

Statistical significance does not imply practical importance: with a large enough sample, trivially small effects can be statistically significant. Common misinterpretations include treating the p-value as the probability that the null hypothesis is true. Concerns about p-hacking, publication bias, and the replication crisis have led the American Statistical Association to issue guidance (2016, 2019) urging researchers to report effect sizes and uncertainty, avoid dichotomous "significant / not significant" thinking, and in some proposals to lower the default threshold to 0.005.
