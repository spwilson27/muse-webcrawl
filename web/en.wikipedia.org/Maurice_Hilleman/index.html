Maurice Hilleman

Maurice Ralph Hilleman (August 30, 1919 – April 11, 2005) was an American microbiologist who specialised in vaccinology and developed over 40 vaccines, an unparalleled record of productivity. According to one estimate, his vaccines save nearly 8 million lives each year. He has been described as one of the most influential vaccinologists of all time and as the father of modern vaccines.

Of the 14 vaccines routinely recommended in American vaccine schedules, Hilleman developed eight: those for measles, mumps, hepatitis A, hepatitis B, chickenpox, Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Haemophilus influenzae bacteria. During the 1957–1958 influenza pandemic, he also developed the vaccine that is believed to have saved hundreds of thousands of lives. He played a role in the discovery of the cold-producing adenoviruses, the blood-borne hepatitis viruses, and the potentially cancer-causing virus SV40.

Early life and education

Hilleman was born on a farm near Miles City, Montana. His mother and twin sister died when he was two days old. He credited his work with chickens growing up for his later successes with egg-based vaccines. He graduated from Montana State University and earned his PhD in microbiology from the University of Chicago in 1944, where his doctoral work concerned chlamydia infections.

Career at Merck

After work at E. R. Squibb and the Walter Reed Army Institute of Research, Hilleman joined the Merck Institute for Therapeutic Research in 1957. There he developed the measles vaccine (after isolating a new strain from a sick child in 1963), the mumps vaccine (developed from a strain collected from his own daughter Jeryl Lynn in 1967), combined them into the MMR vaccine in 1971, and later produced the first licensed hepatitis B vaccine (1981) and the recombinant version (1986). In 1957 he recognised the pandemic potential of a new influenza strain in Hong Kong, organised the production of 40 million vaccine doses, and is credited with blunting the 1957 Asian flu pandemic.

Recognition

Hilleman received the National Medal of Science in 1988, the Prince Mahidol Award in 2002, and was elected to the National Academy of Sciences. He remained largely unknown to the general public despite his enormous impact on public health.
