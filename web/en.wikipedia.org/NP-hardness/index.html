NP-hardness

NP-hardness (non-deterministic polynomial-time hardness) is the defining property of a class of problems that are informally "at least as hard as the hardest problems in NP". A problem H is NP-hard when every problem L in NP can be reduced in polynomial time to H; equivalently, when an NP-complete problem can be reduced to H.

Definition and consequences

Formally, a problem is NP-hard if there exists an NP-complete problem that is polynomial-time (Turing or many-one) reducible to it. If an NP-hard problem were solvable in polynomial time, then every problem in NP would be as well, so NP-hard problems are widely believed to be intractable. Unlike NP-complete problems, NP-hard problems need not be decision problems, nor need their solutions be verifiable in polynomial time — they may even be undecidable, as the halting problem is NP-hard under Turing reductions.

Examples

NP-hard problems that are not in NP include the optimisation versions of the travelling salesman problem (find the shortest tour), integer programming, the longest path problem in general graphs' complement form, and functional and counting problems associated with NP-complete decision problems.

History

The terminology developed after Stephen Cook's 1971 theorem and Richard Karp's 1972 list of 21 NP-complete problems; Donald Knuth helped popularise the notation "NP-hard" and "NP-complete" following a poll of computer scientists.
