Pungency

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pungency ( /ˈpʌndʒənsi/ PUN-jən-see) is the taste or sensation of a food commonly referred to as spiciness, hotness or heat, found in foods such as chili peppers. Although strictly a chemesthetic sensation rather than a taste — it is detected by pain receptors (nociceptors) rather than taste buds — pungency is usually classified as a flavour.

Highly pungent scents may also be experienced as sharp or acrid smells. Substances that produce pungency act on the TRPV1 and TRPA1 ion channels in sensory neurons, the same receptors that respond to heat and certain chemicals, which is why chili "burns".

Common pungent compounds

  • Capsaicin — the active component of chili peppers, measured on the Scoville scale.
  • Piperine — responsible for the pungency of black pepper.
  • Allyl isothiocyanate — the pungent compound in mustard, horseradish and wasabi.
  • Gingerol and shogaol — in fresh and dried ginger respectively.
  • Hydroxy alpha sanshool — in Sichuan pepper, which also produces a tingling, numbing sensation.
  • Allicin — in raw garlic.

Measurement

The Scoville scale, devised by Wilbur Scoville in 1912, rates the pungency of chili peppers in Scoville Heat Units (SHU), from 0 for bell peppers to over two million for the hottest cultivars and 16 million for pure capsaicin. Modern measurement uses high-performance liquid chromatography to quantify capsaicinoid content directly.

Effects

Pungent foods provoke sweating, salivation and tearing; capsaicin also stimulates endorphin release, which some researchers link to the pleasure many people take in spicy food. Capsaicin is used medicinally in topical analgesic creams and defensively in pepper spray.
