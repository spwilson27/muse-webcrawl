Art colony

An art colony is a community of artists living and working together, usually in a rural village or small town valued for its scenery, low costs or distinctive light and character. Art colonies flourished across Europe and North America from the mid-19th century into the early 20th century, when improved rail transport let urban artists reach picturesque countryside, and many colonies became identified with particular styles of painting.

European colonies

Famous European colonies include the Barbizon school near the Forest of Fontainebleau in France, where painters such as Théodore Rousseau pioneered plein air landscape painting; Giverny, which grew around Claude Monet's home; the Skagen Painters in Denmark; and colonies at St Ives and Newlyn in Cornwall, Worpswede in Germany, and Abramtsevo in Russia. Colonies often attracted international memberships and summer schools, and their inns and studios became social as much as artistic centres.

American colonies

In the United States, colonies formed at Woodstock and the Byrdcliffe Colony in New York, at Old Lyme in Connecticut (a centre of American Impressionism), in Provincetown and at the Cornish Art Colony in New Hampshire, and later at Taos and Santa Fe in New Mexico, where desert light and Pueblo culture drew modernist painters. Some colonies endure today as art schools, residencies or historic sites.
