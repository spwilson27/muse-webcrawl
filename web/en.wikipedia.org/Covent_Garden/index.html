Covent Garden is a district in London, on the eastern fringes of the West End, between St Martin's Lane and Drury Lane. It is associated with the Royal Opera House, which is often called simply "Covent Garden", and with the former fruit and vegetable market that occupied its central piazza, now a major shopping and tourist destination.

History

The name derives from the "convent garden" of Westminster Abbey, which supplied produce in the Middle Ages. In the 1630s the Earl of Bedford commissioned Inigo Jones to lay out London's first formal square, with the church of St Paul's. A market grew in the square, and by the nineteenth century the wholesale flower, fruit, and vegetable market had become London's principal one, served by the market buildings designed from 1828 by Charles Fowler. The wholesale market moved to Nine Elms in 1974, and the central building reopened in 1980 as a shopping centre.

Culture

The district is dense with theatres, including the Royal Opera House, the Theatre Royal Drury Lane, and the London Coliseum nearby. The piazza is famous for street performers, a tradition associated with the area since the seventeenth century — Samuel Pepys recorded seeing a Punch and Judy show there in 1662. Covent Garden features prominently in works such as Shaw's Pygmalion and Hitchcock's Frenzy.

Transport

Covent Garden station on the Piccadilly line is famous for its short distance from Leicester Square station — the shortest on the Underground network — and for having only lifts and a spiral staircase to the platforms.
