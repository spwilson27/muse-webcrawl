Homeostasis

Homeostasis is the state of steady internal, physical and chemical conditions maintained by living systems. It is the condition of optimal functioning for the organism and involves keeping variables such as body temperature, blood pH, fluid balance and blood sugar within narrow, pre-set limits (homeostatic ranges), despite changes in the external environment.

Mechanism

Homeostatic control typically works by negative feedback: a sensor (receptor) detects deviation of a variable from its set point, a control centre — often in the brain — compares it with the desired value, and an effector acts to restore it. When the body overheats, for example, temperature receptors signal the hypothalamus, which triggers sweating and dilation of blood vessels in the skin. Positive feedback, in which a change is amplified, is rarer and occurs in processes such as blood clotting and childbirth.

Examples

  • Thermoregulation: humans maintain a core temperature near 37 °C by shivering, sweating and adjusting blood flow.
  • Blood glucose: the hormones insulin and glucagon, secreted by the pancreas, lower and raise blood sugar respectively.
  • Osmoregulation: the kidneys, directed by antidiuretic hormone, regulate water and salt balance.
  • Calcium and pH: parathyroid hormone regulates blood calcium; breathing rate and kidney function stabilise blood pH around 7.4.

History

The idea descends from Claude Bernard's concept of the milieu intérieur (1865) — the internal environment whose constancy is "the condition of free and independent life". Walter Cannon coined the term homeostasis in 1926, from the Greek homoios (similar) and stasis (standing still), and popularised it in The Wisdom of the Body (1932). Later thinkers, including W. Ross Ashby in cybernetics, generalised the principle to machines and organisations; the related concept of allostasis describes regulation through changing set points.
