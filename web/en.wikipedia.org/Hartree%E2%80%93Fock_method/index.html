Hartree–Fock method

The Hartree–Fock method is a method of approximation in quantum mechanics for determining the wavefunction and energy of a many-body system in a stationary state. It approximates the true wavefunction as a single Slater determinant of one-particle orbitals, with each particle moving in the mean field created by the others. It is foundational to computational chemistry and condensed-matter physics.

Hartree–Fock method
Field: Quantum chemistry, computational physics
Developed by: Douglas Hartree; Vladimir Fock (1930)
Approximation: Single Slater determinant, mean field

Method

Starting from an initial guess for the orbitals, one solves the Fock equations — an eigenvalue problem in which the Fock operator depends on its own solutions — and iterates until self-consistency is reached. For molecules, the expansion of orbitals in a finite basis set leads to the Roothaan–Hall equations, a matrix problem solved on computers.

Limitations

Hartree–Fock neglects electron correlation beyond the exchange interaction required by antisymmetry, so it typically recovers about 99% of the total energy but misses the chemically crucial remainder. Post-Hartree–Fock methods such as Møller–Plesset perturbation theory, configuration interaction, and coupled cluster build corrections on the Hartree–Fock reference.
