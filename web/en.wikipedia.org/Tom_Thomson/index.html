Thomas John Thomson (August 5, 1877 – July 8, 1917) was a Canadian artist active in the early 20th century. Directly influenced by the Art Nouveau movement, he is remembered for his vivid landscape paintings of the Canadian Shield, particularly of Algonquin Provincial Park in Ontario, and is considered a foundational figure in Canadian art. His approach strongly influenced the painters who later formed the Group of Seven.

Early life

Thomson was born in Claremont, Ontario, and raised near Owen Sound. In 1899 he apprenticed at a machine shop and later worked as a draftsman. He spent several years in Seattle, Washington, working as a commercial artist and engraver, before returning to Toronto in 1905. In Toronto he worked for design firms including Grip Ltd., where he met several of the artists who would later form the Group of Seven.

Artistic career

Thomson began painting in earnest after 1911. Encouraged by the collector Dr. James MacCallum, he made his first extended trip to Algonquin Provincial Park in 1912 and thereafter spent most springs, summers and autumns sketching in the park, working as a guide and fire ranger to support himself, while winters were spent painting large canvases in a Toronto studio shack behind the Studio Building.

Working mainly in oil on small wood panels and canvas-board sketch supports, Thomson developed a bold, direct style characterized by thick paint, strong colour and simplified forms. His mature works of 1915–17, including Northern River, The Pointers, The West Wind and The Jack Pine, are among the most celebrated images of the Canadian landscape.

Death

Thomson disappeared during a canoe trip on Canoe Lake in Algonquin Park on July 8, 1917. His upturned canoe was found that afternoon, and his body was recovered eight days later. The official cause of death was drowning, but the circumstances have been the subject of enduring speculation and debate in Canadian popular culture. He was 39 years old.

Legacy

Thomson's reputation grew rapidly after his death, assisted by exhibitions organized by his friends in the Group of Seven, who regarded him as their inspiration and spiritual precursor. His works are held by the National Gallery of Canada, the Art Gallery of Ontario and the McMichael Canadian Art Collection, and the Tom Thomson Art Gallery in Owen Sound bears his name.
