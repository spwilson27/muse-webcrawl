Mannerism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Parmigianino, Madonna with the Long Neck (c. 1535–40)]

Mannerism (from Italian maniera, "style") is a style of European art that emerged in the later years of the Italian High Renaissance around 1520 and lasted until about 1600, when it gave way to the Baroque. It spread from Florence and Rome to the courts of Northern Italy, France (School of Fontainebleau), Spain and Prague.

Characteristics

Mannerists deliberately departed from Renaissance harmony and naturalism, favouring elongated proportions, exaggerated musculature, crowded or ambiguous space, artificial poses (the figura serpentinata), and acid or clashing colour. Key works include Michelangelo's Laurentian Library vestibule, Giulio Romano's Palazzo Te frescoes, Pontormo's Deposition, Parmigianino's Madonna with the Long Neck, and Bronzino's court portraits.

Reception

Giorgio Vasari praised maniera, but later critics from the 17th to the 19th century used "mannered" pejoratively, seeing the style as decadent affectation. Twentieth-century scholarship rehabilitated Mannerism as a self-conscious, intellectual style responding to the Sack of Rome (1527) and religious upheaval.

Architecture and sculpture

Mannerist architecture played with classical rules—blind windows, rustication and inverted orders—while sculptors such as Giambologna and Benvenuto Cellini produced intricate multi-viewpoint figures in bronze and marble.
