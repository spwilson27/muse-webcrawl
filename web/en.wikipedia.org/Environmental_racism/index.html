Environmental racism

Environmental racism

Field
Environmental justice, sociology
Related concepts
Redlining, sacrifice zones, environmental inequality

Environmental racism, or ecological racism, is the disproportionate exposure of ethnic or racial minority groups to harmful environmental conditions, including proximity to toxic waste sites, industrial pollution, and other hazards. The term is used to describe the way environmental burdens and benefits are distributed unevenly along racial lines, whether or not the inequality results from deliberate intent.

History

The concept gained prominence in the United States during the environmental justice movement of the early 1980s. A landmark moment was the 1982 protest in Warren County, North Carolina, where residents of a predominantly Black county opposed the siting of a landfill for PCB-contaminated soil. In 1983, a study by the U.S. General Accounting Office found that three of four major hazardous waste landfills in the southeastern United States were located in predominantly African American communities. A 1987 report by the United Church of Christ Commission for Racial Justice, Toxic Wastes and Race in the United States, concluded that race was the most significant variable in predicting the location of commercial hazardous waste facilities.

Causes

Researchers attribute environmental racism to a combination of historical housing discrimination such as redlining, lower land values in minority neighbourhoods, limited political power to resist unwanted land uses, and zoning and permitting practices that concentrate industry away from affluent areas.

Examples

Frequently cited cases include the petrochemical corridor in Louisiana known as "Cancer Alley", the water crisis in Flint, Michigan beginning in 2014, and the siting of waste facilities near Indigenous reservations. Similar patterns have been documented in other countries, affecting Roma communities in Europe, Aboriginal Australians, and marginalised castes in India.

Response and policy

Responses have included executive orders requiring federal agencies to consider environmental justice, community litigation, and changes to permitting rules. Critics of the concept argue that income and land prices, rather than race itself, drive facility siting, while supporters point to studies showing disparities persist even after controlling for income.
