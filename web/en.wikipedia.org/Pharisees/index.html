Pharisees

The Pharisees (Hebrew: פְּרוּשִׁים, Pərūšīm, "the separated ones") were a Jewish social movement and school of thought in the Levant during the time of Second Temple Judaism. At the core of their understanding of the Torah was the belief that God gave the Israelites both the Written and the Oral Torah. After the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, Pharisaic beliefs became the foundational, liturgical and ritualistic basis for Rabbinic Judaism.

History

The Pharisees emerged by the 2nd century BC during the Hasmonean period, in tension with the priestly, aristocratic Sadducees and distinct from other groups such as the Essenes and the Zealots. Under John Hyrcanus and Alexander Jannaeus their fortunes swung between influence and persecution; under Queen Salome Alexandra (76–67 BC) they held considerable power. Josephus, our principal source alongside the New Testament and later rabbinic literature, numbers them at about 6,000 in the time of Herod.

Figures such as Hillel and Shammai, whose houses debated questions of law around the turn of the era, became archetypal rabbinic sages. After 70 CE, teachers in the Pharisaic tradition, gathered around Yohanan ben Zakkai at Yavneh, reshaped Judaism around synagogue, study and prayer.

Beliefs

Pharisees affirmed divine providence alongside human free will, the resurrection of the dead, angels and spirits, and a coming judgment — positions Josephus contrasts with Sadducean denials. Above all they upheld the Oral Torah: traditions of interpretation, attributed to Moses at Sinai, that governed how the written commandments were observed in purity, tithing, Sabbath and festival practice. Their meticulous observance extended Temple purity rules into daily life and the home.

Conflict and portrayal

The Gospels frequently depict Pharisees in controversy with Jesus over Sabbath healing, purity and tradition, and the word "pharisaical" entered European languages as a synonym for hypocritical self-righteousness — a portrayal scholars note reflects later polemics and the diversity within the movement, which also included figures sympathetic to Jesus, such as Nicodemus and Gamaliel (the latter also Paul's teacher). Rabbinic literature preserves a more ambivalent, self-critical picture, distinguishing sincere Pharisees from those motivated by fear or show.

Legacy

Through the Mishnah, Talmud and the synagogue tradition, the Pharisees' interpretive approach shaped normative Judaism to the present day.
