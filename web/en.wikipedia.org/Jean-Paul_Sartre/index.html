Jean-Paul Sartre

Jean-Paul Charles Aymard Sartre (21 June 1905 – 15 April 1980) was a French philosopher, playwright, novelist, screenwriter and political activist, and a leading figure of existentialism. His philosophy centred on radical human freedom, summed up in the formula "existence precedes essence". In 1964 he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature, which he declined.

Life

Sartre was born in Paris; his father, a naval officer, died when he was an infant, and he was raised in the household of his grandfather, the Alsatian scholar Charles Schweitzer, uncle of Albert Schweitzer. He studied philosophy at the École Normale Supérieure, where he met Simone de Beauvoir, his lifelong partner, and taught in lycées before serving in the French army in the Second World War. Captured in 1940, he was released in 1941 and joined the Resistance.

Philosophy and literature

His novel Nausea (1938) and the philosophical treatise Being and Nothingness (1943) made his name; plays such as No Exit (1944), with its famous line "Hell is other people", brought existentialism to a wide public. The 1945 lecture Existentialism Is a Humanism became a popular manifesto. Later works, including the Critique of Dialectical Reason (1960), sought to reconcile existentialism with Marxism.

Political engagement and death

Sartre was one of the most prominent public intellectuals of the postwar era, campaigning against the Algerian and Vietnam wars and supporting the student movement of May 1968. He founded the newspaper Libération in 1973. He died in Paris in 1980; some 50,000 people followed his funeral procession to Montparnasse Cemetery.
