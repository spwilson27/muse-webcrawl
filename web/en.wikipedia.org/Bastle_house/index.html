A bastle house is a type of fortified farmhouse built along the Anglo-Scottish border during the troubled period of the Border Reivers in the late 16th and early 17th centuries. The name is thought to derive from the French bastille, a fortification.

Design

Bastles are two-storey stone buildings with walls up to about a metre thick. The ground floor, entered by a single low door, served as a byre or stable for livestock, with only slit windows for light and ventilation. The family's living quarters occupied the floor above, reached originally by a removable ladder and later by an external stone stair; the upper floor had a fireplace, somewhat larger windows, and sometimes a stone-slabbed or barrel-vaulted ceiling to resist fire from above or below.

History

Hundreds of bastles were built in the decades around 1600, when cross-border raiding and feuding made defensible homes essential even for ordinary farming families. After the Union of the Crowns in 1603 pacified the border, many bastles were converted into ordinary farmhouses or barns; their massive walls and distinctive form survive across Northumberland, Cumbria, and the Scottish Borders, and well-preserved examples are protected as listed buildings and scheduled monuments.
