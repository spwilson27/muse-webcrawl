Familial adenomatous polyposis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) is an autosomal dominant inherited condition in which numerous adenomatous polyps form mainly in the epithelium of the large intestine. While these polyps start out benign, malignant transformation into colon cancer occurs if they are left untreated. FAP is caused by mutations in the APC tumour suppressor gene on chromosome 5q22, and affects roughly 1 in 8,000–18,000 people.

Presentation

In classic FAP, hundreds to thousands of polyps develop during adolescence; by age 35, almost all patients have polyps, and without treatment colorectal cancer is virtually inevitable, typically by around age 40. A milder form, attenuated FAP, produces fewer polyps (10–100) and a later onset of cancer. Extra-colonic manifestations include polyps of the stomach and duodenum (with a significant risk of duodenal cancer), desmoid tumours, osteomas of the jaw, congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium (CHRPE), and dental abnormalities. The association of FAP with extra-colonic tumours was historically called Gardner's syndrome, now understood to be the same condition.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis combines colonoscopic findings with genetic testing for APC mutations; a related condition, MUTYH-associated polyposis, is inherited recessively. At-risk family members are offered genetic counselling and regular endoscopic surveillance beginning in early adolescence. Because of the near-certain cancer risk, prophylactic surgery — total colectomy or proctocolectomy, often with an ileal pouch–anal anastomosis — is usually recommended, followed by lifelong surveillance of the rectum or pouch and upper gastrointestinal tract. Chemoprevention with NSAIDs such as sulindac can shrink polyps but does not replace surgery.
