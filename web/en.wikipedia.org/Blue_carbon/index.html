Blue carbon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Blue carbon is the term for carbon captured by the world's ocean and coastal ecosystems. The carbon is captured by living organisms in oceans and stored mainly in coastal sediments. Unlike terrestrial forests, which store carbon largely in biomass and release much of it within decades, blue carbon ecosystems — mangroves, tidal marshes, and seagrass meadows — bury carbon in waterlogged, anaerobic soils where it can remain locked away for millennia.

Ecosystems

The three principal vegetated coastal ecosystems recognised as blue carbon habitats are:

  • Mangrove forests, which cover roughly 14 million hectares in the tropics and subtropics;
  • Tidal (salt) marshes, extensive in temperate estuaries;
  • Seagrass meadows, found on every continent except Antarctica.

These ecosystems occupy less than 2% of the ocean's area but are estimated to bury carbon at a rate many times higher per unit area than tropical forests; coastal sediments may account for a large share of all carbon burial in ocean sediments.

Climate significance

When mangroves, marshes, and seagrasses are degraded or converted — for aquaculture, agriculture, or coastal development — stored carbon is released as carbon dioxide, making habitat loss a significant source of emissions. Conservation and restoration of blue carbon ecosystems are promoted by initiatives such as the Blue Carbon Initiative (UNESCO, IUCN, Conservation International) as nature-based climate solutions that also protect coasts, support fisheries, and sustain biodiversity. Blue carbon credits have entered voluntary carbon markets, though questions of measurement, permanence, and additionality remain active areas of research. Some researchers also discuss macroalgae (kelp and seaweeds) and oceanic processes in an extended blue carbon framework, though their long-term sequestration pathways are less certain.
