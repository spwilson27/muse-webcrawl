Capital requirement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A capital requirement (also known as regulatory capital or capital adequacy) is the amount of capital a bank or other financial institution has to have as required by its financial regulator. It is usually expressed as a capital adequacy ratio of equity as a percentage of risk-weighted assets. These requirements are intended to ensure that financial institutions do not take on excess leverage and become insolvent, so that they can absorb a reasonable amount of loss and honour depositors' claims.

Basel framework

International standards are set by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision. Under Basel I (1988), banks were required to hold capital of at least 8% of risk-weighted assets. Basel II refined risk measurement, allowing internal models and adding pillars for supervisory review and market discipline. After the 2008 financial crisis, Basel III raised both the quantity and quality of required capital, emphasizing Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1), adding capital conservation and countercyclical buffers, a leverage ratio, and liquidity requirements (the Liquidity Coverage Ratio and Net Stable Funding Ratio).

Types of capital

Tier 1 capital is the bank's core capital, primarily common equity and retained earnings, which can absorb losses while the bank remains a going concern. Additional Tier 1 includes instruments such as contingent convertible bonds. Tier 2 capital includes subordinated debt and other instruments that absorb losses in liquidation. Systemically important banks are subject to additional surcharges and total loss-absorbing capacity (TLAC) requirements.

Criticism and debate

Economists debate whether capital requirements are set at optimal levels: higher requirements improve financial stability by giving banks more "skin in the game," while industry argues they constrain lending. The interplay between capital rules and risk-weight manipulation has prompted complementary measures such as stress testing and simple leverage limits.
