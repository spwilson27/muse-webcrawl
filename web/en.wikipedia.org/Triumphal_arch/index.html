Triumphal arch

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A triumphal arch is a free-standing monumental structure, usually built to commemorate a military victory or another significant public event. The form was developed by the Romans, who erected arches across their empire with a large central arched passage flanked by classical columns and an attic bearing a dedicatory inscription.

Roman arches

The earliest Roman triumphal arches were temporary wooden structures raised for triumphal processions. Later, permanent stone arches celebrated victories and imperial deeds: the Arch of Titus (c. AD 81) commemorates the sack of Jerusalem, the Arch of Septimius Severus (AD 203) stands in the Roman Forum, and the Arch of Constantine (AD 315) is the largest surviving example, decorated extensively with reused reliefs from earlier monuments.

Later arches

The form was revived in the Renaissance and again by early modern states. Napoleon commissioned the Arc de Triomphe in Paris (begun 1806) to honour the Grande Armée; it remains the most famous modern example. Other notable arches include the Marble Arch and Wellington Arch in London, the Arch of Triumph in Pyongyang, and the Washington Square Arch in New York.
