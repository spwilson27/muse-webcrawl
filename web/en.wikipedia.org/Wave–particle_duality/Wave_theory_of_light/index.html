Wave theory of light

Huygens' principle: every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets.

The wave theory of light holds that light travels as a wave, analogous to ripples on water or vibrations in a medium. It was first systematically proposed by the Dutch physicist Christiaan Huygens in his Traité de la Lumière (Treatise on Light, 1690), in opposition to Isaac Newton's corpuscular theory, which held that light consists of streams of tiny particles.

Huygens' principle

Huygens proposed that light propagates through an all-pervading medium, the luminiferous aether, as a series of waves. Every point reached by a wavefront becomes itself the source of a new secondary wavelet, and the envelope of all these wavelets forms the new wavefront. This principle elegantly explained reflection and refraction, including the observation that light travels more slowly in denser media such as water — a prediction opposite to Newton's theory and later confirmed by Léon Foucault in 1850.

Young and Fresnel

In 1801 Thomas Young presented his double-slit experiment, demonstrating interference of light, a phenomenon explainable only if light is a wave. Augustin-Jean Fresnel extended the theory mathematically in the 1810s and 1820s, successfully explaining diffraction and polarization. The celebrated "Poisson spot" — a bright point predicted by Fresnel's equations at the center of a circular obstacle's shadow — was observed experimentally, providing striking confirmation.

Electromagnetic theory

In 1865 James Clerk Maxwell showed that light is an electromagnetic wave, propagating at a speed matching the measured speed of light. The wave theory reached its classical culmination in Maxwell's equations, though Einstein's 1905 work on the photoelectric effect revealed that light also behaves as particles (photons), leading to the modern concept of wave-particle duality.
