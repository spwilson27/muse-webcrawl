Wave–particle duality

Wave–particle duality is the concept in quantum mechanics that every particle or quantum entity can be described as either a particle or a wave. It expresses the inability of the classical concepts "particle" or "wave" to fully describe the behaviour of quantum-scale objects. Depending on the experimental arrangement, entities such as photons and electrons manifest wave phenomena (interference, diffraction) or particle phenomena (localised detection, quantised energy exchange), but never both aspects simultaneously in a single measurement.

Historical development

The debate began with the opposing theories of Newton (light as corpuscles) and Huygens (light as waves). Interference experiments by Young (1801) and Fresnel established the wave theory, crowned by Maxwell's identification of light with electromagnetic waves. Planck's quantisation (1900) and Einstein's photon hypothesis for the photoelectric effect (1905) restored a particle aspect. In 1924 de Broglie extended the duality to matter, proposing that electrons have wavelengths λ = h/p; electron diffraction experiments by Davisson and Germer and by G. P. Thomson (1927) confirmed matter waves, and duality has since been demonstrated for atoms and large molecules.

Complementarity

Niels Bohr's principle of complementarity (1927) holds that wave and particle behaviour are complementary facets of a single reality, disclosed by mutually exclusive experimental setups. The uncertainty principle of Werner Heisenberg, a colleague in Copenhagen, expresses a related limit: the more precisely a particle's position is known, the less precisely its momentum can be known. Modern treatments use the wavefunction, a probability amplitude that evolves wave-like and yields particle-like outcomes on measurement, and quantitative measures of duality relate fringe visibility to path information.

Demonstrations

Delayed-choice and quantum-eraser variants of the double-slit experiment show that the observed behaviour depends on the complete experimental arrangement, even when the choice of measurement is made after the photon has passed the slits. Interference has been observed with objects as large as molecules of hundreds of atoms, while attempts to determine the path taken always reduce or destroy the interference, in line with duality relations derived by Englert and others.
