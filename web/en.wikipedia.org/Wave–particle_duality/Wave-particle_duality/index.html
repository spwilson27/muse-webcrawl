Wave-particle duality

The double-slit experiment demonstrates that matter and light exhibit both wave and particle properties.

Wave-particle duality is the concept in quantum mechanics that fundamental entities of the universe, like photons and electrons, exhibit particle or wave properties according to the experimental circumstances. It expresses the inability of the classical concepts such as particle or wave to fully describe the behavior of quantum objects.

During the 19th century it became accepted that light is a wave. Indeed, in 1801, Thomas Young observed the interference of light in his double-slit experiment. In this experiment, light from an external source was passed through two closely spaced slits. If light were composed of particles, then only two bands of illuminated light (aligned with the slits) would appear on the screen placed behind the slits. Instead, an interference pattern of bright and dark bands was observed, which could only be explained by the wave nature of light.

History

Democritus (5th century BC), the originator of the atomic theory of matter, argued that all things in the universe, including light, are composed of indivisible sub-components. Euclid (4th–3rd century BC) gives emission theory of vision, and Lucretius (1st century BC) continued the atomic ideas. In the early 11th century, the Arabic scientist Ibn al-Haytham gave the first comprehensive description of optics in his Book of Optics, rejecting the emission theory in favor of the idea that light rays entering the eyes come from the objects seen.

At the beginning of the 17th century, Johannes Kepler proposed that space is filled with light rays emitted from a source at a finite speed. Isaac Newton (1642–1726) held that light was composed of tiny particles (corpuscles), while his contemporary Christiaan Huygens proposed that light consists of waves. Robert Hooke supported the wave theory too. The wave theory was finally established by the work of Thomas Young and Augustin-Jean Fresnel in the early 19th century.

From particles to waves

Albert Einstein's 1905 explanation of the photoelectric effect reintroduced the particle picture: light arrives in discrete quanta, later called photons, each carrying energy E = hν. In 1924, Louis de Broglie hypothesized that matter also has wave properties, with wavelength λ = h/p. Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer confirmed electron diffraction in 1927, showing that electrons — particles with mass — produce interference patterns like waves.

Complementarity

Niels Bohr articulated the principle of complementarity: objects have complementary properties which cannot all be observed or measured simultaneously, and wave and particle descriptions are two complementary faces of the same reality. Modern quantum field theory resolves the duality by describing both particles and waves as excitations of underlying quantum fields.
