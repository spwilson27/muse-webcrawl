Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier (21 March 1768 – 16 May 1830) was a French mathematician and physicist best known for initiating the investigation of Fourier series and their applications to problems of heat transfer and vibrations. The Fourier transform and Fourier's law of conduction are also named in his honour, and he is credited with the earliest scientific description of the greenhouse effect.

Life

Fourier was born in Auxerre, the son of a tailor, and orphaned young. Educated by Benedictine monks, he became a teacher of mathematics and an active supporter of the French Revolution, narrowly escaping the guillotine during the Terror. In 1798 he accompanied Napoleon's expedition to Egypt as a scientific adviser and administrator, helping found the Institut d'Égypte. Returning to France, he served as prefect of Isère, where he oversaw road-building and drainage while doing his great work on heat.

The Analytical Theory of Heat

In his Théorie analytique de la chaleur (1822), developing a prize essay of 1807–1811, Fourier showed that the conduction of heat in solids could be modelled by a partial differential equation, and that arbitrary functions could be represented as sums of sines and cosines — trigonometric (Fourier) series. Initially controversial, notably with Lagrange, the idea transformed mathematics and became fundamental to signal processing, optics, quantum mechanics and much of modern analysis. His memoir of 1824 on the temperature of the Earth noted that the atmosphere warms the planet — the first account of the greenhouse effect.

Honours and death

Fourier was elected to the Académie des Sciences in 1817 and the Académie française in 1826, and became perpetual secretary of the Academy of Sciences. His health, attributed by him to the heat of Egypt, declined in Paris, and he died in 1830. His name is one of the 72 names inscribed on the Eiffel Tower.
