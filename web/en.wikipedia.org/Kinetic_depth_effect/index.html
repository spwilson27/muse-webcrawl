Kinetic depth effect

In visual perception, the kinetic depth effect is the phenomenon in which the three-dimensional structure of an object can be perceived from its motion alone. A flat shadow or silhouette of a rotating object, unrecognisable when still, is immediately seen as a solid form in depth once it moves — first demonstrated with shadow projections by Wallach and O'Connell in 1953.

Explanation

The visual system infers rigid three-dimensional structure from the changing two-dimensional pattern of motion (structure from motion). Point-light displays — dots on a walker's joints — similarly reveal a human figure at once when moving. The effect underlies motion-capture animation and much research on how the brain recovers depth without stereo vision.
