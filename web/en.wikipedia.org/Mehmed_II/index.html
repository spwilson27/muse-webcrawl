Mehmed II (Ottoman Turkish: محمد ثانى, Meḥmed-i sānī; Turkish: II. Mehmed; 30 March 1432 – 3 May 1481), commonly known as Mehmed the Conqueror (Turkish: Fatih Sultan Mehmed), was an Ottoman sultan who ruled from August 1444 to September 1446 and then later from February 1451 to May 1481. In Mehmed II's first reign, he defeated the crusade led by John Hunyadi after the Hungarian incursions into his country broke the conditions of the Treaty of Szeged. When Mehmed II ascended the throne again in 1451, he strengthened the Ottoman Navy and made preparations to attack Constantinople.

At the age of 21, he conquered Constantinople (now Istanbul) in 1453, bringing an end to the Byzantine Empire. After the conquest Mehmed claimed the title Caesar of the Romans (Kayser-i Rûm), recognising himself as the heir to the Roman Empire. He went on to annex Serbia, Bosnia, Morea, Albania (after the death of Skanderbeg), the Crimean Khanate's coast, Trebizond, and territories in Anatolia, extending Ottoman rule deep into Europe and Asia.

Reforms and legacy

Mehmed reorganised the Ottoman state, codifying law in his Kanunname, centralising the administration, founding Istanbul's great mosque complexes and the Fatih complex, inviting artists and scholars — including the Venetian painter Gentile Bellini — to his court, and granting communal autonomy to his non-Muslim subjects through their religious leaderships. He died in 1481 while preparing a new campaign, and was succeeded by his son Bayezid II.
