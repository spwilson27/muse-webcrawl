Article One of the United States Constitution establishes the legislative branch of the federal government, the United States Congress. It is the longest article of the Constitution and reflects the framers' view of the legislature as the first branch of government. Under the principle of separation of powers, Congress — not the executive or judiciary — holds the lawmaking authority of the United States.

Structure

  • Section 1 (Vesting Clause): "All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress of the United States, which shall consist of a Senate and House of Representatives."
  • Section 2: the House of Representatives — two-year terms, apportionment by population, qualifications, and the power of impeachment.
  • Section 3: the Senate — six-year terms, originally chosen by state legislatures (changed by the Seventeenth Amendment), trial of impeachments.
  • Sections 4–7: elections, sessions, rules, compensation, and the lawmaking process, including presidential veto and override by two-thirds of both houses.

Enumerated powers (Section 8)

Section 8 grants Congress its enumerated powers: to levy taxes, borrow money, regulate commerce with foreign nations and among the states (the Commerce Clause), coin money, establish post offices, declare war, raise and support armies and a navy, and to make all laws "necessary and proper" for executing the foregoing powers (the Necessary and Proper Clause). These clauses have grounded much of the expansion of federal authority and many landmark Supreme Court cases, from McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) onward.

Limits (Sections 9–10)

Section 9 restricts Congress — prohibiting bills of attainder, ex post facto laws, and suspension of habeas corpus except in rebellion or invasion — while Section 10 imposes limits on the states, barring them from coining money, entering treaties, or impairing the obligation of contracts.
