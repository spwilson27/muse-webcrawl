Golden spike

The golden spike (also known as the "last spike") is a ceremonial 17.6-karat gold spike driven by Leland Stanford to join the rails of the First Transcontinental Railroad across the United States, connecting the Central Pacific Railroad from Sacramento and the Union Pacific Railroad from Omaha, at Promontory Summit, Utah Territory, on May 10, 1869.

The ceremony

The spike, commissioned by David Hewes, was engraved with the names of railroad officers and the prayer "May God continue the unity of our Country as this Railroad unites the two great Oceans of the world." A silver hammer was used, and the spike and laurel tie were wired to a telegraph line so that the hammer strokes signalled the moment across the nation; the single word "DONE" was flashed when the connection was made. The gold spike was removed after the ceremony and is now held by the Cantor Arts Center at Stanford University, along with a second gold spike cast at the same time.

Legacy

The completion of the transcontinental railroad cut cross-country travel from months to about a week and symbolised American industrial unification after the Civil War. The site is preserved as Golden Spike National Historical Park. "Golden spike" has since become a general term for ceremonial completion spikes and, in geology, gives its name (in translation) to the boundary points defining the geologic time scale.
