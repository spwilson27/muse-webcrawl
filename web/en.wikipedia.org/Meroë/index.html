Meroë

Meroë
Location: Shendi area, Sudan
Period: c. 800 BC – c. AD 350
UNESCO World Heritage Site (2011, Island of Meroe)

Meroë (also spelled Meroe) was an ancient city on the east bank of the Nile, about 230 km northeast of Khartoum, Sudan. It was the southern capital of the Kingdom of Kush from roughly 800 BC until the kingdom's decline in the fourth century AD, succeeding Napata as the royal centre.

History

Kushite kings who once ruled Egypt as its Twenty-fifth Dynasty governed from Napata and later Meroë. The city's wealth rested on agriculture in the fertile "Island of Meroe" between the Nile and Atbara rivers, on trade in gold, ivory, ebony and exotic goods, and on extensive iron smelting — slag heaps at the site led early scholars to call it the "Birmingham of ancient Africa." Meroë's rulers included powerful queens, the Kandakes (Candaces) known to Greek and Roman writers. Relations with Ptolemaic and Roman Egypt mixed diplomacy, trade and war; a Kushite embassy met Augustus and secured a favourable border settlement.

Pyramids and monuments

More pyramids were built at Meroë than in all of Egypt: some two hundred steep-sided Nubian pyramids in three cemeteries (Begrawiya) served as royal tombs. The city contains temples — notably a Temple of Amun and a "Roman kiosk" — palaces, baths and reservoirs (hafirs). In 1821 the Italian treasure-hunter Giuseppe Ferlini destroyed several pyramids searching for gold, damaging the site badly.

Script and legacy

Meroë developed its own writing, the Meroitic script — hieroglyphic and cursive forms — whose sounds can be read but whose language remains largely undeciphered. Meroë, Napata and other Kushite sites were inscribed as the UNESCO World Heritage Site "Island of Meroe" in 2011.
