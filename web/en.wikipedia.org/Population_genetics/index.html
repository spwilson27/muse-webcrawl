Population genetics

Population genetics is a subfield of genetics that deals with genetic differences within and among populations, and is a part of evolutionary biology. Studies in this branch of biology examine such phenomena as adaptation, speciation, and population structure. Population genetics was a vital ingredient in the emergence of the modern evolutionary synthesis.

History

The discipline was founded by R. A. Fisher, J. B. S. Haldane, and Sewall Wright in the early 20th century, who reconciled Mendelian inheritance with Darwinian natural selection by mathematical modelling. Fisher's 1930 work The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection is a landmark of the field.

Fundamental forces

Four basic processes shape genetic variation in populations: natural selection, genetic drift, mutation, and gene flow. The Hardy–Weinberg principle describes the null condition in which allele frequencies remain constant in the absence of these forces.

Linkage and recombination

Genes located close together on a chromosome tend to be inherited together, a phenomenon called genetic linkage. Recombination breaks up these associations over generations, and the rate of decay of linkage disequilibrium allows geneticists to map genes and estimate population history.

Applications

Population genetics informs conservation biology, forensic science, medicine (through the study of disease-associated alleles), and our understanding of human origins and migration through the analysis of genetic markers in modern and ancient DNA.
