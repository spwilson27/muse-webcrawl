Tower of the Winds

The Tower of the Winds, or Horologion of Andronikos Kyrrhestes, is an octagonal Pentelic marble clocktower in the Roman Agora of Athens. It is considered the world's first meteorological station: it combined a water clock (clepsydra) inside with sundials on its faces and a wind vane on its roof, and each of its eight sides carries a frieze depicting the personified wind god (an Anemoi) for that direction.

History

The tower was built by the astronomer Andronikos of Kyrrhos, probably in the second or first century BC, around the time the Roman Agora was laid out. It stands about 12 metres high and just under 8 metres across. Inside, a water clock was fed by a stream from the Acropolis. In early Christian times the tower was used as a church belfry; under Ottoman rule it became a tekke used by dervishes. It was partly buried until excavations in the nineteenth century cleared it.

The eight winds

The eight relief figures represent Boreas (north), Kaikias (northeast), Apeliotes (east), Euros (southeast), Notos (south), Lips (southwest), Zephyrus (west), and Skiron (northwest), each shown with attributes suggesting the weather it brings — Boreas blowing a conch and dressed for cold, Zephyrus scattering flowers of spring. Lines for sundials were incised beneath the figures, and a bronze Triton weathervane on the conical roof pointed to the prevailing wind.

Legacy

The tower inspired later buildings, including the Radcliffe Observatory in Oxford, whose top section copies it, and it gave its name to the "Tower of the Winds" on other observatories. It remains one of the best-preserved monuments of ancient Greek science.
