Rape culture

Rape culture is a concept used in feminist theory and sociology to describe a setting, studied since the 1970s, in which rape is pervasive and normalised due to cultural attitudes about gender and sexuality. Behaviours commonly associated with the concept include victim blaming, sexual objectification, trivialising rape, denying the prevalence of sexual violence, and refusing to acknowledge the harm it causes.

Origins

The term is generally credited to second-wave feminists in the United States in the 1970s and was popularised by the 1975 documentary film Rape Culture. Susan Brownmiller's Against Our Will (1975) argued that rape functions as a means of social control. Later writers, notably Emilie Buchwald and co-editors of Transforming a Rape Culture (1993), developed the idea that societies can instead teach consent and bystander intervention.

Criticism

The concept is contested. Some critics argue that in Western societies rape is widely condemned and severely punished, so talk of a "culture" endorsing it mislabels problems such as poor institutional responses or individual criminality. Others question whether the concept, framed around Western contexts, travels well to other cultures. Supporters respond that the term refers to toleration of harassment and coercive norms rather than explicit approval of rape.

Responses

Public discussion of rape culture intensified with movements such as SlutWalk (from 2011), which protested victim-blaming remarks by a Toronto police officer, and the Me Too movement from 2017, which focused attention on sexual harassment and assault in workplaces and institutions.
