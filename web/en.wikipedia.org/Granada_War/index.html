The Granada War (Spanish: Guerra de Granada, 1482–1492) was a series of military campaigns between the Catholic Monarchs — Isabella I of Castile and Ferdinand II of Aragon — and the Nasrid Emirate of Granada, the last Muslim state in the Iberian Peninsula. It ended with the surrender of Granada on 2 January 1492, completing the Christian Reconquista after nearly eight centuries.

Background

By the 1480s Granada survived as a tributary vassal of Castile, but tribute disputes and the Granadan seizure of Zahara in 1481 — answered by the Castilian capture of Alhama in 1482 — ignited open war. The emirate was simultaneously torn by civil war between Emir Abu l-Hasan Ali, his son Muhammad XII (Boabdil) and his brother Muhammad XIII (el Zagal), a division Castile skilfully exploited, for example by capturing Boabdil at Lucena in 1483 and releasing him as a client.

Course of the war

The Catholic Monarchs waged a methodical war of sieges, using large feudal and municipal levies, the Santa Hermandad, and an artillery train of bombards that made medieval walls untenable. Key fortresses fell in sequence: Ronda (1485), Loja (1486), Málaga after a bitter siege (1487), Baza (1489) and Almería, until only the city of Granada remained. In 1491 the besiegers built the permanent camp-city of Santa Fe and blockaded the capital; starvation and negotiation followed.

Capitulation and aftermath

Under the Treaty of Granada (25 November 1491), Boabdil surrendered the city on generous terms guaranteeing Muslims their property, law and religion. On 2 January 1492 Ferdinand and Isabella received the keys of the Alhambra. The guarantees soon eroded: forced conversions under Cardinal Cisneros provoked revolts (1499–1501), and by 1502 Muslims in Castile faced conversion or exile; their Morisco descendants were expelled in 1609–1614. The victory, in the same year as Columbus's first voyage and the Alhambra Decree expelling Spain's Jews, became central to Spanish national mythology.
