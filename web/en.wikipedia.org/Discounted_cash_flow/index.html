Discounted cash flow

Each future cash flow is discounted back to its present value.

Discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation is a method used to estimate the value of an investment based on its expected future cash flows. Based on the time value of money, the method discounts projected future cash flows to their present value using a discount rate — often the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) — and sums them: an asset is worth the present value of all the cash it is expected to generate.

Formula

The value of an asset generating cash flows CF_t in periods t = 1…n, with discount rate r, is

DCF = Σ [ CF_t / (1 + r)^t ]

In valuing a company, analysts typically forecast free cash flow (to the firm or to equity) over an explicit projection period and add a terminal value capturing cash flows beyond it, often via a perpetual growth formula (the Gordon growth model) or an exit multiple. The result for free cash flow to the firm is the enterprise value; subtracting net debt gives the equity value.

Uses and limitations

DCF is widely used in investment banking, equity research, corporate finance, and capital budgeting (in the form of net present value and internal rate of return analysis). Its inputs — projected cash flows and the discount rate — are estimates, so outputs are highly sensitive to assumptions; small changes in the terminal growth rate or WACC can swing valuations substantially. Practitioners therefore commonly use sensitivity tables and cross-check DCF results against market multiples. Formal foundations derive from the work of Irving Fisher and from John Burr Williams's The Theory of Investment Value (1938).
