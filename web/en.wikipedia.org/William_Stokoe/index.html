William Stokoe

[Image: William Stokoe]

William Clarence Stokoe Jr. (July 21, 1919 – April 4, 2000) was an American linguist and a founder of the linguistic study of sign languages. A professor at Gallaudet University, he demonstrated that American Sign Language (ASL) is a full natural language with its own phonology, morphology and syntax, at a time when sign languages were commonly dismissed as mere pantomime.

Research

In his 1960 monograph Sign Language Structure and the 1965 Dictionary of American Sign Language on Linguistic Principles (with Dorothy Casterline and Carl Croneberg), Stokoe proposed that signs could be analysed into a finite set of simultaneously produced parameters — handshape, location, and movement, later called cheremes — analogous to the phonemes of spoken language. He also devised the Stokoe notation system for writing signs.

Legacy

Stokoe founded the journal Sign Language Studies in 1972. His work, initially resisted, transformed deaf education, linguistics and public recognition of sign languages, and is credited with helping spark the modern field of sign language linguistics.
