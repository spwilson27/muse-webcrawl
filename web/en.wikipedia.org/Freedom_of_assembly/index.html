Freedom of assembly

Freedom of assembly, sometimes termed the right of assembly, is the individual right to come together and collectively express, promote, pursue, and defend common ideas. It is recognised as a human right in international law, including Article 20 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and Article 21 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which protects peaceful assembly.

In the United States

The First Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits Congress from abridging "the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances". Through the Fourteenth Amendment, this protection applies to state and local governments. The Supreme Court has held that governments may impose reasonable, content-neutral time, place, and manner restrictions — such as permit requirements — but may not regulate assemblies based on their viewpoint, and has struck down laws granting officials unfettered discretion over permits. The right is closely linked to freedom of association, recognised in cases such as NAACP v. Alabama (1958).

Limits and controversies

Debates recur over assemblies that block traffic, encampments by protest movements, counter-demonstrations, and the policing of marches. International law allows restrictions necessary in a democratic society for public safety, public order, public health, or the protection of others' rights, but blanket bans on peaceful protest are generally treated as disproportionate. Freedom of assembly has underpinned movements from labour organising and suffrage marches to the civil rights movement's lunch counter sit-ins and mass rallies.
