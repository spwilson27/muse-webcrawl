Propeller

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A propeller (often called a screw) is a device with a rotating hub and radiating blades that are set at a pitch to form a helical spiral which, when rotated, exerts linear thrust upon a working fluid such as water or air. Propellers are used to pump fluid through a pipe or duct, or to create thrust to propel a boat through water or an aircraft through air.

The blades are specially shaped so that their rotational motion through the fluid causes a pressure difference between the two surfaces of the blade by Bernoulli's principle, exerting a force on the fluid. Most marine propellers are screw propellers with helical blades rotating on a propeller shaft with an essentially horizontal axis.

History

The principle of the screw propeller derives from the Archimedes' screw, used since antiquity to pump water. In the 18th and 19th centuries, inventors including Josef Ressel, Francis Pettit Smith, and John Ericsson developed practical screw propulsion for ships. Smith's and Ericsson's patents of 1836 led to trials demonstrating the superiority of screw propulsion over paddle wheels; the tug-of-war trial between HMS Rattler and HMS Alecto in 1845 is a famous demonstration. In aviation, the Wright brothers pioneered efficient twisted-blade propeller design for their 1903 Flyer, treating each blade as a rotating wing.

Theory

Propeller thrust is commonly analysed using momentum theory, which treats the propeller as an actuator disc imparting momentum to the fluid, and blade element theory, which resolves aerodynamic or hydrodynamic forces on individual blade sections. Key parameters include diameter, pitch (the theoretical distance advanced per revolution), blade area, and the number of blades — commonly from two to five for aircraft and up to seven for ships. At high speeds, effects such as cavitation in water and compressibility at aircraft blade tips limit performance.

Types

  • Fixed-pitch propellers, whose blade angle cannot change in operation.
  • Variable-pitch / constant-speed propellers, whose blade angle adjusts to maintain efficiency or constant rotational speed.
  • Controllable-pitch propellers on ships, allowing thrust reversal without reversing the shaft.
  • Ducted propellers (Kort nozzles) and contra-rotating propellers, used to improve efficiency in specific applications.

Applications

Propellers propel most small and medium aircraft, nearly all ships and boats, torpedoes, and many drones. Variants also appear in pumps, fans, wind turbines (which extract rather than impart energy), and cooling systems.
