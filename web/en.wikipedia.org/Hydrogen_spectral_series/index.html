Hydrogen spectral series

The visible (Balmer) emission lines of hydrogen

The hydrogen spectral series are the sets of spectral lines in the emission spectrum of the hydrogen atom. They arise when the electron in a hydrogen atom transitions between energy levels: a photon is emitted when the electron falls from a higher level to a lower one, and absorbed when it rises. The wavelengths of all hydrogen lines are given by the Rydberg formula, published by Johannes Rydberg in 1888, with each series corresponding to a fixed final level n′ and all initial levels n > n′.

The Balmer series

The Balmer series is historically the most important because four of its lines lie in the visible spectrum: H-alpha (656.3 nm, red), H-beta (486.1 nm, blue-green), H-gamma (434.0 nm, violet), and H-delta (410.2 nm, violet). Balmer's empirical formula of 1885, later generalised by Rydberg, was explained by Niels Bohr's model of the atom in 1913 — one of the first triumphs of quantum theory.

Significance

The H-alpha line gives emission nebulae and the Sun's chromosphere their characteristic red colour and is a principal diagnostic in astronomy. The Lyman-alpha line at 121.6 nm is the strongest feature in the ultraviolet spectra of distant galaxies and quasars, and the "Lyman-alpha forest" of absorption lines is used to study the intergalactic medium and the early universe.
