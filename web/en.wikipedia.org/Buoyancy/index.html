Buoyancy

Buoyancy is the upward force exerted by a fluid that opposes the weight of a partially or fully immersed object. In a column of fluid, pressure increases with depth because of the weight of the overlying fluid, so the pressure at the bottom of an object exceeds that at the top, producing a net upthrust.

Archimedes' principle

Archimedes' principle states that the upward buoyant force on a body immersed in a fluid is equal to the weight of the fluid that the body displaces. According to legend, Archimedes discovered the principle in his bath and ran through the streets of Syracuse crying "Eureka!" The principle explains why ships float: a vessel floats when it displaces a weight of water equal to its own weight. If an object's average density is less than that of the surrounding fluid it rises or floats (positive buoyancy); if greater, it sinks (negative buoyancy); if equal, it remains suspended (neutral buoyancy) — the condition divers and submarines adjust with ballast.

Applications

Buoyancy governs the design of ships and submarines, hot-air balloons and airships (where the "fluid" is air), life jackets and buoys, and techniques such as density measurement with a hydrometer. Stability — whether a floating body rights itself when tilted — depends on the relative positions of the centre of gravity and the centre of buoyancy, summarised by the metacentric height.
