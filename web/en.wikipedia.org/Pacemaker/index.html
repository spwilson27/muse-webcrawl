A pacemaker (or artificial pacemaker) is an implantable medical device that generates electrical impulses delivered by electrodes to the chambers of the heart, causing them to contract and thereby controlling the heart's rhythm. It is used when the heart's natural conduction system produces an abnormally slow or irregular heartbeat (bradycardia or arrhythmia) that causes symptoms such as fatigue, dizziness or fainting.

History

External electrical stimulation of the heart was explored in the early twentieth century. The first fully implantable pacemaker was designed by Swedish engineer Rune Elmqvist and implanted by surgeon Åke Senning into patient Arne Larsson on 8 October 1958; Larsson went on to outlive both the inventor and the surgeon, receiving 26 devices during his lifetime. In the United States, Wilson Greatbatch and William Chardack developed an improved implantable device in 1960. Early devices paced at a fixed rate; later "demand" pacemakers sensed the heart's intrinsic activity and paced only when needed.

How it works

A modern pacemaker consists of a hermetically sealed pulse generator — containing a lithium-iodide battery and electronic circuitry inside a titanium case — connected to one or more insulated leads threaded through a vein into the right atrium and/or right ventricle. The generator monitors the heart's electrical activity; if the intrinsic rate falls below a programmed threshold, it delivers a small electrical pulse that depolarises the myocardium. Contemporary devices are programmable, rate-responsive (adjusting to physical activity via accelerometers), and capable of storing diagnostic data.

Types

  • Single-chamber — one lead, usually in the right ventricle.
  • Dual-chamber — leads in both the right atrium and right ventricle, preserving atrioventricular synchrony.
  • Biventricular (cardiac resynchronisation therapy) — an additional lead paces the left ventricle, used in heart failure.
  • Leadless pacemakers — self-contained capsule devices implanted directly in the right ventricle, introduced in the 2010s.

Risks and precautions

Risks include infection, lead displacement, bleeding and device malfunction. Patients are advised about electromagnetic interference; modern devices are well shielded, but strong magnetic fields (such as those in some MRI settings and industrial equipment) require precautions, though many newer pacemakers are MRI-conditional.
