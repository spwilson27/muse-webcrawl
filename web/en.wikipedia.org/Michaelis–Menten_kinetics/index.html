Michaelis–Menten kinetics

FieldBiochemistry, enzyme kinetics
Proposed1913
ProponentsLeonor Michaelis, Maud Menten

Michaelis–Menten kinetics is one of the best-known models of enzyme kinetics. It is named after German biochemist Leonor Michaelis and Canadian physician Maud Menten, who proposed the model in 1913. The model takes the form of an equation describing the rate of enzymatic reactions, by relating reaction rate v to the concentration of a substrate S.

The formula is given by:

v = Vmax [S] / (Km + [S])

Here Vmax represents the maximum rate achieved by the system, occurring at saturating substrate concentration. The Michaelis constant Km is the substrate concentration at which the reaction rate is half of Vmax. G. E. Briggs and J. B. S. Haldane later derived the equation under the steady-state assumption, in which the concentration of the enzyme–substrate complex remains approximately constant over time.

Model

The simplest model involves an enzyme E binding a substrate S to form a complex ES, which then releases a product P while regenerating the free enzyme. Each step is characterised by rate constants k1, k−1 and k2. The Michaelis constant is defined as Km = (k−1 + k2) / k1. When k2 is much smaller than k−1, Km approximates the dissociation constant of the ES complex and thus measures the affinity of the enzyme for its substrate.

Applications

Michaelis–Menten kinetics is used to characterise enzyme activity in vitro and in drug development, where it helps determine how inhibitors alter reaction rates. Competitive, non-competitive and uncompetitive inhibition each modify the apparent values of Vmax and Km in characteristic ways. The model assumes a single substrate, no product inhibition, and a steady state; many real enzymes require more elaborate treatments such as allosteric models described by the Hill equation.
