Biscotti

Biscotti are Italian almond biscuits that originated in the Tuscan city of Prato. They are twice-baked, oblong-shaped, dry and crunchy, and are traditionally dipped in a drink — in Italy often Vin Santo, a dessert wine. The name derives from the Latin bis coctus, "twice cooked". In North America the word is used generically for the twice-baked biscuit, while in Italy the specific Prato variety is known as cantucci.

History

Twice-baked biscuits have ancient roots: Roman soldiers carried similar long-keeping rusk-like breads. The modern cantucci emerged in Prato, and their recipe was popularised in the 19th century by the baker Antonio Mattei, whose bakery still produces them. Catherine de' Medici is sometimes credited with bringing them to France, where similar biscuits are called croquants.

Preparation

A stiff dough of flour, sugar, eggs and whole unskinned almonds is formed into a slab, baked, then sliced into oblong pieces which are baked again until crisp and dry. Modern variations include hazelnuts, pistachios, chocolate chips, dried fruit and spices such as aniseed.

Use

Because they are very dry, biscotti are traditionally served dipped in a beverage: Vin Santo in Italy, coffee or tea elsewhere. Their low moisture gives them an exceptionally long shelf life.
