Glued laminated timber

Curved glulam arches spanning a large interior space.

Glued laminated timber, commonly called glulam, is a type of structural engineered wood product constituted by layers of dimensional lumber (laminations) bonded together with durable, moisture-resistant structural adhesives. The grain of all laminations runs parallel to the longitudinal axis of the member. By laminating smaller boards, large and strong structural members can be manufactured from relatively small trees, in straight or curved shapes of almost any length.

History

The principle of gluing timber laminations was patented in the 19th century, and the modern product was developed in Germany by Otto Hetzer, who received a patent in 1901 for weather-resistant glued laminated construction. Glulam was introduced to the United States in the 1930s; an early landmark structure was a research laboratory at the Forest Products Laboratory in Madison, Wisconsin (1934–35). It gained wide use for churches, schools, sports halls, and bridges after the Second World War.

Properties and uses

Glulam has a high strength-to-weight ratio, compares favorably with steel in fire performance because the charring of its large cross-sections is predictable, and offers architects great freedom of form: members can be cambered, arched, or tapered. Because defects in individual boards are distributed through the member, glulam makes efficient use of lower-grade timber. It is used for beams, columns, arches, and long-span roofs, and is a key material in modern mass timber construction alongside cross-laminated timber.

Manufacture

Boards are kiln-dried, graded, and finger-jointed into long laminations, which are face-bonded with adhesives such as melamine-formaldehyde or polyurethane under pressure, then planed, finished, and often fitted with connections before delivery.
