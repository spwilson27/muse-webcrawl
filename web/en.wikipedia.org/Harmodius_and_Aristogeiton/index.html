Harmodius and Aristogeiton

Harmodius (died 514 BC) and Aristogeiton (died 514 BC) were two Athenian lovers known together as the Tyrannicides for their assassination of Hipparchus, the brother of the tyrant Hippias, in 514 BC. Their act was later celebrated in democratic Athens as a founding symbol of resistance to tyranny, although the tyranny itself continued for several more years after their deaths.

Harmodius and Aristogeiton
Known as: The Tyrannicides
Died: 514 BC, Athens
Cause: Execution after the assassination of Hipparchus

The assassination

According to Thucydides and Aristotle, the killing arose from a personal grievance: Hipparchus had pursued Harmodius and, when rebuffed, insulted Harmodius's sister by barring her from a religious procession. During the Panathenaic festival, the pair attacked Hipparchus and killed him, but their plan to kill Hippias as well failed. Harmodius was killed on the spot by Hipparchus's guards, and Aristogeiton was captured, tortured, and executed.

Legacy

After the fall of the tyranny in 510 BC, a bronze statue group of the two men by Antenor was erected in the Agora; stolen by the Persians in 480 BC, it was replaced by a new group by Kritios and Nesiotes in 477/6 BC. Roman marble copies of this second group survive. The pair were honoured with heroic cult, drinking songs (skolia), and exemptions for their descendants, becoming enduring emblems of Athenian democracy.
