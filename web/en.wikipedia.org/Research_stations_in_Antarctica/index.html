Research stations in Antarctica

Multiple governments have set up permanent research stations in Antarctica. Many of the stations are staffed throughout the year, while others are occupied only during the austral summer. As of recent counts, there are roughly 70 permanent stations operated by about 30 countries, in addition to seasonal camps and field facilities.

History

The first permanent bases were established during and shortly after the Second World War. The International Geophysical Year of 1957–58 prompted a major expansion, with twelve nations establishing more than fifty stations. The Antarctic Treaty, signed in 1959 and entering into force in 1961, set aside the continent for peaceful scientific purposes and guarantees freedom of scientific investigation.

Major stations

The largest station is the United States' McMurdo Station on Ross Island, which can support more than 1,000 residents in summer. Other major facilities include the American Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station, Russia's Vostok Station (noted for recording the lowest natural temperature on Earth), Germany's Neumayer Station III, Australia's Mawson, Davis and Casey stations, the United Kingdom's Halley Research Station, France and Italy's Concordia Station, China's Great Wall and Zhongshan stations, Japan's Syowa Station, and India's Bharati and Maitri stations.

Research

Research conducted at the stations includes glaciology, meteorology, astronomy and astrophysics (taking advantage of the clear, dry air at high altitude), geomagnetism, oceanography, and biology. Ice cores drilled at stations such as Vostok and Concordia have yielded climate records extending hundreds of thousands of years into the past.

Logistics

Stations are supplied by ship, aircraft, and overland traverse. Because of the Antarctic winter darkness and extreme cold, many stations are isolated for months at a time, and winter-over crews must be self-sufficient.
