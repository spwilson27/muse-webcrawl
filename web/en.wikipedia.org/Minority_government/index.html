Minority government

A minority government, or minority cabinet, is a government formed in a parliamentary system when the governing party or coalition holds fewer than half of the seats in the legislature. Because it lacks an outright majority, such a government must secure support from opposition parties—issue by issue, or through negotiated agreements—in order to pass legislation and survive votes of confidence.

Operation

Minority governments survive by building ad hoc majorities. Common mechanisms include:

  • Confidence and supply agreements, in which an outside party guarantees support on confidence motions and budgets in exchange for policy concessions, without joining the cabinet;
  • Formal coalitions with junior partners (which may still fall short of a majority if other parties abstain);
  • Case-by-case bargaining, negotiating each bill with different partners.

Minority governments are often shorter-lived than majority governments, but in countries with traditions of consensus politics—such as Denmark, Sweden, Norway, and Canada—they are routine and can complete full terms.

Examples

Canada has frequently been governed by minority cabinets at the federal level, including those of Lester B. Pearson in the 1960s, Pierre Trudeau after 1972, Paul Martin (2004–2006), Stephen Harper (2006–2011), and Justin Trudeau (2019–2025). In the United Kingdom, minority governments have included those of Ramsay MacDonald in 1924 and 1929–31, Harold Wilson in 1974, James Callaghan in the late 1970s, John Major in 1996–97, and Theresa May after 2017. Spain, Sweden, and New Zealand have also seen extended periods of minority rule.

Contrast with other forms

A minority government differs from a majority government, where the ruling party controls more than half the seats, and from a coalition government, which describes how a cabinet is composed rather than its seat share—a coalition may command a majority or a minority. Analysts debate the trade-offs: minority governments can encourage broader consultation and legislative scrutiny, but may struggle to pass budgets and long-term programs.
