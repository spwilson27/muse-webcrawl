Pietra dura

Pietra dura or pietre dure, from Italian for "hard stone", is a term for the inlay technique of using cut and fitted, highly polished coloured stones to create images. The stones — marble, jasper, lapis lazuli, agate, malachite and other hardstones — are sliced thin, cut to shape with an abrasive bow saw, and assembled like a jigsaw puzzle, glued onto a backing slab and polished flat.

History

The craft has roots in ancient Roman opus sectile work and Byzantine inlay, but reached its height in Renaissance Florence, where the Medici grand dukes founded the Opificio delle Pietre Dure workshop in 1588. Florentine craftsmen produced tabletops, cabinets and panels with landscapes, flowers and birds of astonishing realism. The technique was carried to Mughal India, where it is known as parchin kari and adorns the Taj Mahal.

Technique

Because the image is formed from the natural colours and veining of the stones themselves, a master craftsman must match each piece of stone to the design, exploiting shading within the material. Finished work is exceptionally durable and retains its colours indefinitely.
