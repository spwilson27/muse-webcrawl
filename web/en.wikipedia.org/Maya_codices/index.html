Maya codices (singular codex) are folding books written in Maya hieroglyphic script by the pre-Columbian Maya civilization of Mesoamerica. Made of bark paper coated with a fine plaster and folded like a screen, they contained calendrical, astronomical and ritual information used by priests for divination and prophecy.

Destruction

Thousands of codices once existed, but Spanish conquistadors and missionary friars destroyed nearly all of them during the conquest of Yucatán and Guatemala as works of idolatry. Bishop Diego de Landa famously ordered the burning of dozens of Maya books at Maní in 1562, lamenting in his own account that the Maya "regretted [it] to an amazing degree."

The surviving codices

Only four codices are generally accepted as authentic. The Dresden Codex, the finest and most complete, contains elaborate astronomical tables, including a Venus table of great accuracy. The Madrid Codex (or Tro-Cortesianus) is the longest, concerned mainly with divinatory almanacs and hunting and beekeeping rites. The Paris Codex is fragmentary and deals with katun prophecies. The Maya Codex of Mexico (formerly the Grolier Codex), long disputed, was authenticated in 2018 and, dating to about 1115, is the oldest surviving book of the Americas.

Content and decipherment

The codices record the workings of the 260-day Tzolk'in calendar, Venus and eclipse cycles, and rituals tied to the agricultural year. Their study, accelerated by the phonetic decipherment of Maya writing in the later 20th century, complements the carved inscriptions on stone monuments.
