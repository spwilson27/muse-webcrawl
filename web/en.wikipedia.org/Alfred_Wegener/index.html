Alfred Wegener

Born: 1 November 1880, Berlin, German Empire
Died: November 1930, Greenland
Fields: Meteorology, geophysics, polar research
Known for: Continental drift theory

Alfred Lothar Wegener (1 November 1880 – November 1930) was a German polar researcher, geophysicist, and meteorologist. During his lifetime he was primarily known for his achievements in meteorology and as a pioneer of polar research, but today he is most remembered as the originator of continental drift theory, having hypothesised in 1912 that the continents are slowly drifting around the Earth.

Life and expeditions

Wegener earned a doctorate in astronomy in 1905 but turned to the new science of meteorology. He made four expeditions to Greenland (1906–08, 1912–13, 1929, and 1930), studying polar air circulation and making early use of kites and balloons for weather observation. With his brother Kurt he set an endurance record for balloon flight in 1906. He served and was wounded in the First World War, then became professor of meteorology at Graz in 1924. He died in November 1930 on the Greenland ice cap during a relief journey to the Eismitte station; his body was found the following spring.

Continental drift

In lectures in 1912 and in The Origin of Continents and Oceans (1915, repeatedly revised), Wegener assembled evidence — the jigsaw fit of South America and Africa, matching fossil species such as Mesosaurus and Glossopteris across oceans, and identical mountain chains — for a former supercontinent he called Pangaea. Lacking a plausible driving mechanism, most geologists rejected the theory.

Legacy

In the 1950s and 1960s, palaeomagnetism and seafloor spreading vindicated Wegener's core idea, and continental drift was incorporated into the theory of plate tectonics. The Alfred Wegener Institute for Polar and Marine Research in Bremerhaven bears his name.
