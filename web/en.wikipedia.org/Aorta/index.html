Aorta

SystemCirculatory system
SourceLeft ventricle of the heart
BranchesAscending aorta, aortic arch, descending (thoracic and abdominal) aorta
LengthAbout 30–40 cm in adults; ~2.5 cm diameter at origin

The aorta is the largest artery in the human body and the main trunk of the systemic circulation. It arises from the left ventricle of the heart, arches over the left main bronchus, and descends through the thorax and abdomen, distributing oxygenated blood through its branches to every organ before dividing into the two common iliac arteries. Its elastic wall smooths the pulsatile output of the heart into a steadier flow (the Windkessel effect).

Structure and course

The aorta is conventionally divided into sections. The ascending aorta (about 5 cm) rises from the aortic valve; the coronary arteries branch from the sinuses of Valsalva just above the valve. The aortic arch curves backward, giving off the brachiocephalic trunk, left common carotid, and left subclavian arteries to the head and arms. The descending aorta continues downward: its thoracic portion gives off bronchial, oesophageal, and intercostal branches, and after passing through the diaphragm at T12 it becomes the abdominal aorta, which supplies the gut (via the coeliac trunk and mesenteric arteries), kidneys, and gonads before bifurcating at about L4 into the common iliac arteries.

Histology

Like other arteries the aortic wall has three layers: the intima (endothelium), the media — exceptionally rich in concentric elastic lamellae, which allow the vessel to expand and recoil with each heartbeat — and the adventitia, which contains the vasa vasorum that nourish the wall itself. Baroreceptors in the aortic arch monitor blood pressure and feed into cardiovascular reflexes via the vagus nerve.

Clinical significance

Diseases of the aorta include atherosclerosis, aneurysms (abdominal aortic aneurysm being most common, typically in older smokers), and aortic dissection, in which blood tears into the media and can be rapidly fatal. Congenital conditions include coarctation of the aorta and a bicuspid aortic valve, which predisposes to stenosis and ascending aortic dilation. Connective-tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome weaken the aortic wall and increase the risk of dissection. Treatment ranges from surveillance and blood-pressure control to open graft replacement and endovascular stent-graft repair (EVAR/TEVAR).
