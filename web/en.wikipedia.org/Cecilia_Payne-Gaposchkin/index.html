Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin

Cecilia Helena Payne-Gaposchkin (née Payne; May 10, 1900 – December 7, 1979) was a British-American astronomer and astrophysicist. In her 1925 doctoral thesis she proposed that stars are composed primarily of hydrogen and helium — at the time a revolutionary conclusion contradicting the belief that stars had a composition similar to Earth's. Her work established the modern understanding of stellar composition and the temperature scale of stellar spectra.

Early life and education

Payne was born in Wendover, England, and studied at Newnham College, Cambridge, where she completed her studies but — because Cambridge did not award degrees to women until 1948 — received no degree. She moved to the United States in 1923 to work at the Harvard College Observatory under Harlow Shapley, and earned her PhD from Radcliffe College in 1925, the first person to receive a doctorate in astronomy there.

Career

Applying Meghnad Saha's ionization theory to stellar spectra, Payne showed that the variation in stellar spectral lines was due mainly to temperature, not composition, and that hydrogen was by far the most abundant element in stars. Astronomer Henry Norris Russell initially persuaded her to soften the conclusion, though he reached the same result himself four years later. Otto Struve later called her thesis "the most brilliant PhD thesis ever written in astronomy." Despite her contributions, Payne was long confined to low-paid technical positions because of her sex; in 1956 she finally became the first woman promoted to full professor within Harvard's Faculty of Arts and Sciences.

Legacy

Payne-Gaposchkin also studied variable stars, making over a million observations with her team. The asteroid 2039 Payne-Gaposchkin is named for her, and she received the Henry Norris Russell Lectureship in 1976.
