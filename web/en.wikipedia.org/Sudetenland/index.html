Sudetenland

The Sudetenland (German: Sudetenland; Czech: Sudety) is the historical German name for the northern, southern, and western areas of former Czechoslovakia which were inhabited primarily by Sudeten Germans. These German speakers had predominated in the border districts of Bohemia, Moravia, and Czech Silesia since the Middle Ages.

History

After the First World War, the region became part of the new state of Czechoslovakia despite the wishes of many of its German-speaking inhabitants to join Austria or Germany. During the 1930s, economic hardship and ethnic tensions strengthened the Sudeten German Party of Konrad Henlein, which demanded autonomy and received support from Nazi Germany.

The Munich Agreement of 29–30 September 1938, signed by Germany, Britain, France, and Italy without Czechoslovak participation, ceded the Sudetenland to Germany. German troops occupied the region in October 1938, and it was administered as the Reichsgau Sudetenland. After the Second World War, the Potsdam Conference sanctioned the transfer of the German population, and roughly three million Sudeten Germans were expelled to Germany and Austria under the Beneš decrees.

Legacy

The Munich Agreement became a byword for the appeasement of aggressive powers, and the Sudeten crisis is often cited as a key step on the road to the Second World War. Relations between the Czech Republic and the expellees' organizations remained sensitive long after 1989.
