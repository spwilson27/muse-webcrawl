Indoctrination

Indoctrination is the process of inculcating a person with ideas, attitudes, cognitive strategies or professional methodologies, typically in a way that discourages or prevents critical examination of the doctrine being taught. Originally, to "indoctrinate" simply meant to teach or instruct in a doctrine; the word acquired its strongly pejorative connotation in the twentieth century, particularly in discussions of totalitarian education and propaganda.

Distinguishing education from indoctrination

Philosophers of education have proposed several criteria for the distinction. The content criterion holds that teaching doctrines — propositions whose truth is disputed — as if certain is indoctrinatory. The aims criterion focuses on the teacher's intention that the pupil believe unshakably regardless of evidence. The methods criterion looks for teaching that bypasses rational scrutiny, such as appeals to authority, emotion, or repetition. Some philosophers, such as John Wilson and I. A. Snook, argue that intention is decisive; others note that all early education involves some uncritical acceptance.

Contexts

The term is applied to political education in authoritarian states, to military basic training (where it may be used neutrally by the military itself), to religious instruction, and to debates over school curricula, including disputes about patriotism, creationism, and climate change.
