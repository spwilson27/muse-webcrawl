Rocket propellant

Rocket propellant is the reaction mass of a rocket. In chemical rockets, the propellant is a chemical substance or mixture that undergoes an exothermic chemical reaction to produce hot gas, which is expelled through a nozzle to produce thrust in accordance with Newton's third law of motion. Rocket propellants may be solid, liquid, or hybrid (a combination, typically a solid fuel with a liquid or gaseous oxidizer).

Liquid propellants

Liquid-propellant rockets store fuel and oxidizer in separate tanks and feed them into a combustion chamber. Common combinations include:

  • Kerolox — refined kerosene (RP-1) and liquid oxygen, used by the Falcon 9 and the first stage of Saturn V.
  • Hydrolox — liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, offering the highest specific impulse of practical chemical propellants; used by the Space Shuttle main engines and Ariane 5.
  • Methalox — liquid methane and liquid oxygen, adopted for newer vehicles such as SpaceX's Starship because methane is easier to store and can potentially be synthesized on Mars.
  • Hypergolic propellants — such as monomethylhydrazine and nitrogen tetroxide, which ignite spontaneously on contact and can be stored at room temperature, at the cost of extreme toxicity.

Solid propellants

Solid-propellant rockets use a premixed grain of fuel and oxidizer cast inside the motor casing. Modern composite propellants typically combine ammonium perchlorate as oxidizer, powdered aluminium as fuel, and a rubbery binder such as HTPB. Solid motors are simple, storable, and deliver very high thrust, making them attractive for missiles and booster stages such as the Space Shuttle Solid Rocket Boosters, but they cannot be throttled, shut down, or restarted once ignited.

Performance

Propellant performance is measured principally by specific impulse (Isp), the thrust produced per unit weight of propellant consumed per second. Chemical propellants range from roughly 200 seconds for simple solids to about 450 seconds for hydrolox in vacuum. The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation relates a rocket's attainable change in velocity to its exhaust velocity and mass ratio, which is why high-performance propellants and lightweight structures are so important to spaceflight.

Other types

Cold gas thrusters expel an inert compressed gas without combustion. Hybrid rockets store oxidizer and fuel in different phases. Nuclear thermal rockets heat a propellant — usually hydrogen — in a nuclear reactor instead of burning it, roughly doubling achievable specific impulse.
