Quaternions and spatial rotation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Unit quaternions, known also as versors, provide a convenient mathematical notation for representing spatial orientations and rotations of elements in three-dimensional space. A unit quaternion q = cos(θ/2) + sin(θ/2)(u) encodes a rotation by angle θ about a unit axis u, and a vector v is rotated by the conjugation v′ = qvq⁻¹.

Advantages

Compared with Euler angles, quaternions do not suffer from gimbal lock and give a single, unambiguous representation up to sign. Compared with rotation matrices, they are more compact (four numbers instead of nine), cheaper to compose and renormalise, and interpolate smoothly — notably with spherical linear interpolation (slerp), widely used in computer animation.

Mathematics

The unit quaternions form the 3-sphere S³ and a double cover of the rotation group SO(3): q and −q represent the same rotation. They are isomorphic to the group SU(2), which connects them to the description of spin in quantum mechanics. William Rowan Hamilton devised quaternions in 1843, famously carving the multiplication rules into Brougham Bridge in Dublin.

Applications

Unit quaternions are standard in computer graphics and game engines, robotics, aerospace attitude control, and molecular dynamics, wherever orientations must be stored, composed, and interpolated robustly.
