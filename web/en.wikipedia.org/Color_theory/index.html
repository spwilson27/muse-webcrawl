Color theory

Color theory (or colour theory) is the body of practical guidance for color mixing and the visual effects of color combinations, used in art, design and marketing. It draws on geometry, optics and the psychology of perception, organising colors by their relationships on the color wheel and categorising them as primary, secondary and tertiary.

The color wheel

Isaac Newton's experiments with prisms (published in Opticks, 1704) showed that white light splits into a spectrum, and Newton arranged the spectral hues in a circle. The traditional painter's wheel uses red, yellow and blue as primary colors, from which secondary colors (orange, green, violet) and tertiary mixtures are derived. In additive mixing, used for light and screens, the primaries are red, green and blue; in modern printing, cyan, magenta and yellow are the subtractive primaries.

Color harmony

Harmonious schemes are described by geometric relationships on the wheel: complementary colors lie opposite each other and produce strong contrast; analogous colors are adjacent; triadic schemes use three evenly spaced hues; split-complementary and tetradic schemes combine these principles. Colors are also described as warm (reds, oranges, yellows) or cool (greens, blues, violets), and by value (lightness), saturation and hue — the three dimensions formalised in systems such as the Munsell color system.

Psychology and use

Color associations affect branding, interface design and interiors, though their effects are strongly cultural and contextual. Theorists from Goethe, whose Theory of Colours (1810) emphasised perception, to Josef Albers, whose Interaction of Color (1963) demonstrated the relativity of color appearance, have shaped how artists are taught to use color.
