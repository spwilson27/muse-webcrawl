Polydnavirus

Polydnaviruses (PDVs) are a family of insect viruses, Polydnaviridae, that exist in a mutualistic relationship with certain parasitoid wasps. The name derives from their genome, which consists of multiple segments of double-stranded, superhelical DNA (poly-dispersed DNA virus). Uniquely, the virus genome is integrated into the genome of the wasp, and the virus replicates only in the reproductive tract of female wasps; it does not replicate in the secondary host (usually a caterpillar) into which it is delivered.

Biology

Polydnaviruses are associated with two groups of parasitoid wasps: ichnoviruses occur in ichneumonid wasps of the subfamilies Campopleginae and Banchinae, while bracoviruses occur in braconid wasps of the microgastroid complex. The viral genome persists as a provirus integrated into the wasp's own chromosomes. In the ovaries of female wasps, viral particles are produced in the calyx cells and accumulate in the calyx fluid.

When the female wasp lays her eggs inside a lepidopteran caterpillar, she injects virus particles along with the eggs and venom. In the caterpillar, the viral genes are expressed but the virus does not replicate. The gene products suppress the caterpillar's immune system — in particular, they prevent the encapsulation of the wasp eggs by the host's haemocytes — and alter the host's development and physiology so that it becomes a suitable environment for the developing wasp larvae.

Evolution

The association is an ancient example of mutualism and endogenous viral domestication: the wasp obtains a highly effective immunosuppressive delivery system, while the virus is transmitted vertically through the wasp germline. Bracoviruses appear to descend from a nudivirus that integrated into an ancestral braconid genome roughly 100 million years ago. Because the "virus" cannot reproduce itself outside the wasp, polydnaviruses are sometimes cited as borderline cases in definitions of viruses and of organisms.
