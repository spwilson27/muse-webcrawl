Massacre of the Innocents

The Massacre of the Innocents is the biblical account, given in the Gospel of Matthew, of the killing of all male children aged two and under in Bethlehem and the surrounding region, ordered by Herod the Great after the Magi told him of the birth of the "king of the Jews". The Holy Family escaped by fleeing to Egypt. The victims, the Holy Innocents, are venerated in Christianity as the first martyrs, and their feast day falls in late December (December 28 in the Western church).

Biblical account

According to Matthew, the Magi visited Jerusalem asking for the newborn king of the Jews. Herod, alarmed, directed them to Bethlehem and asked them to report back; warned in a dream, they returned home by another route. Joseph, also warned by an angel, fled with Mary and the infant Jesus to Egypt. Herod then ordered the killing of all boys in Bethlehem aged two and under, "in accordance with the time he had learned from the Magi". Matthew presents the event as a fulfilment of Jeremiah's prophecy of Rachel weeping for her children.

Historicity

The episode appears only in Matthew, and no contemporary source — including Josephus, who catalogues Herod's crimes — mentions it. Most modern scholars regard the account as theological narrative rather than history, noting parallels Matthew draws with Pharaoh's killing of Hebrew infants and the Exodus. Others argue the small scale implied (Bethlehem was a village; the number of victims may have been a dozen or fewer) could explain the silence of other sources, and that the act fits Herod's documented ruthlessness in his last years.

In art and devotion

The Holy Innocents were commemorated from the early centuries of the church, and the feast is among the oldest in the Christmas season. The massacre became a major subject in Western art — treated by Giotto, Bruegel (whose version transposes the scene to a snow-covered Flemish village under Spanish occupation), and Rubens, among many others. The Coventry Carol, sung by the mothers of Bethlehem, originates in a medieval mystery play.
