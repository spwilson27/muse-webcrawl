Golden eagle

Scientific nameAquila chrysaetos
FamilyAccipitridae
Wingspan1.8 – 2.3 m (5 ft 11 in – 7 ft 7 in)
Conservation statusLeast Concern (IUCN)

The golden eagle (Aquila chrysaetos) is a bird of prey living in the Northern Hemisphere. It is the most widely distributed species of eagle, found across North America, Europe, and Asia, typically in mountainous and open country. Like all eagles, it belongs to the family Accipitridae.

Description

The golden eagle is a large raptor, dark brown in colour with lighter golden-brown plumage on the nape — the feature that gives it its name. It has broad wings held in a shallow V in flight and powerful talons. Females are larger than males, weighing up to about 6.7 kg. Golden eagles use their agility and speed combined with powerful feet to hunt rabbits, marmots, ground squirrels, and other medium-sized mammals and birds; they are capable of stooping at speeds exceeding 240 km/h.

Distribution and habitat

Six subspecies are recognised across a vast range. The species favours partially open country, mountains, hills, and cliffs, and usually avoids dense forest and heavily populated areas. In Scotland and other parts of its range it is an apex predator and an iconic species.

Relationship with humans

The golden eagle is the national bird of several countries, including Mexico, and appears on Mexico's flag and coat of arms. In Central Asia, Kazakh and Kyrgyz hunters traditionally hunt with trained golden eagles (berkutchi). Although the global population is stable, the species declined historically under persecution and remains protected in many countries.
