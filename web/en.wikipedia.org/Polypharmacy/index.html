Polypharmacy is the concurrent use of multiple medications by one individual, most commonly defined in research and clinical practice as the regular use of five or more medicines. It is especially prevalent among older adults, many of whom live with several chronic conditions (multimorbidity), each treated according to disease-specific guidelines. While polypharmacy is often appropriate — many patients genuinely benefit from several drugs — it is associated with a growing risk of harm as the number of medicines increases.

Risks

Taking many medicines raises the probability of drug–drug interactions, adverse drug reactions, prescribing cascades (in which a side effect is mistaken for a new condition and treated with another drug), medication errors and poor adherence. In older people, polypharmacy is associated with falls, fractures, cognitive impairment, hospitalization and mortality. Certain drug classes are disproportionately risky in the elderly; criteria such as the Beers Criteria and STOPP/START help identify potentially inappropriate medications.

Causes

Polypharmacy arises from multimorbidity, single-disease clinical guidelines that do not account for patients with several conditions, fragmented care among multiple prescribers, automatic repeat prescribing without review, and patient expectations. It has become more common as populations age and as preventive treatments (such as statins and antihypertensives) are prescribed more widely.

Management

Structured medication reviews and deprescribing — the supervised tapering or stopping of drugs that are no longer beneficial or are harmful — are the main responses. Clinicians weigh life expectancy, patient goals and time-to-benefit; a regimen appropriate at 65 may not be at 90. Pharmacist-led reviews and shared decision-making improve the appropriateness of prescribing.
