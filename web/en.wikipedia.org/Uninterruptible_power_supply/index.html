Uninterruptible power supply

An uninterruptible power supply (UPS) is an electrical apparatus that provides emergency power to a load when the input power source or mains power fails. A UPS differs from an auxiliary generator in that it provides near-instantaneous protection against input power interruptions by supplying energy stored in batteries, supercapacitors or flywheels. Typical on-battery runtimes range from a few minutes — enough for an orderly computer shutdown — to hours in mission-critical installations, and UPS systems are standard equipment in data centres, hospitals and industrial control systems.

Topologies

Three main designs are defined by IEC 62040. In an offline/standby UPS the load runs directly from the mains and the inverter switches on only when power fails, with a transfer time of a few milliseconds. A line-interactive UPS adds an autotransformer that corrects voltage sags and surges without using the battery. An online/double-conversion UPS continuously rectifies incoming AC to DC and inverts it back, so the load always runs from the inverter and experiences no transfer gap; this gives the best protection at the cost of efficiency and price.

Batteries and ratings

Most UPS units use sealed valve-regulated lead-acid (VRLA) batteries, though lithium-ion packs are increasingly common for their longer life and smaller footprint. Capacity is rated in volt-amperes (VA) and watts; large installations reach hundreds of kilowatts to several megawatts, often in parallel redundant (N+1) configurations. Output waveforms range from simulated (stepped) sine waves in inexpensive units to pure sine waves in premium models.

Applications

Beyond computers, UPS systems protect telecommunications equipment, emergency lighting controls, medical devices and point-of-sale systems, and serve as the bridge between a mains failure and the start-up of standby diesel generators.
