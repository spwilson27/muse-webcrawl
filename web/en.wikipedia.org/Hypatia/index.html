Hypatia (born c. 350–370; died March 415 AD) was a neoplatonist philosopher, astronomer, and mathematician who lived in Alexandria, Egypt, then part of the Eastern Roman Empire. She was a prominent thinker of the School of Alexandria, where she taught philosophy and astronomy. She is the first female mathematician whose life is reasonably well recorded.

Hypatia was the daughter of the mathematician Theon of Alexandria, with whom she may have collaborated on commentaries on Ptolemy's Almagest and Euclid's Elements. She edited or wrote commentaries on Diophantus's Arithmetica and Apollonius's Conics, though her writings survive only in fragments or through the work of others.

Life and teaching

Hypatia headed the Neoplatonic school in Alexandria from about 400 AD. Her students included pagans and Christians, among them Synesius of Cyrene, who later became a bishop and whose letters to her survive. Contemporary sources describe her as a popular and respected public lecturer, noted for her wisdom, virtue and eloquence. She advised Orestes, the Roman governor (prefect) of Alexandria.

Death

In March 415 AD, during a period of violent conflict between Orestes and the Christian bishop Cyril of Alexandria, a Christian mob seized Hypatia, accused of exacerbating the conflict, and murdered her, stripping her body and tearing it apart with ostraka (pot shards or roof tiles) before burning the remains. Her death shocked the empire and has traditionally been treated as a turning point marking the decline of classical intellectual life in Alexandria, though scholars debate how directly her murder affected the city's scholarly culture.

Legacy

Hypatia became a powerful symbol in later centuries — for Enlightenment critics of religious dogmatism, for nineteenth-century writers such as Charles Kingsley (whose 1853 novel Hypatia popularised her story), and for modern feminists as an emblem of women's learning. Her life is dramatised in the 2009 film Agora. She has also been taken up, sometimes anachronistically, as a martyr for science and reason.
