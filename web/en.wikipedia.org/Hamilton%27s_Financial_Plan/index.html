Hamilton's Financial Plan

AuthorAlexander Hamilton, Secretary of the Treasury
Years1790–1791
Key reportsPublic Credit (1790), National Bank (1790), Manufactures (1791)

Hamilton's financial plan was a set of economic policies proposed by Alexander Hamilton, the first United States Secretary of the Treasury, in a series of reports to Congress in 1790 and 1791. Its goals were to establish the credit of the new federal government, assume and fund the Revolutionary War debts, create a national bank, and encourage American manufacturing.

Components

The First Report on the Public Credit (1790) proposed funding the national debt at par and federal assumption of state debts — the latter passing only after the Compromise of 1790, which located the permanent capital on the Potomac. The Second Report proposed the Bank of the United States, chartered in 1791. The Report on Manufactures (1791) advocated tariffs and subsidies to promote industry, though most of its recommendations were not adopted at the time.

Opposition and legacy

The plan was fiercely opposed by Thomas Jefferson and James Madison, who viewed it as favouring speculators and financiers and exceeding federal constitutional powers; the dispute helped give rise to the first American political parties. Hamilton's system nevertheless laid foundations for American public finance, a national currency framework, and federal support of economic development.
