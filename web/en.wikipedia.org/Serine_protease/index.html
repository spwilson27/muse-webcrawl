Serine protease

The catalytic triad of a typical serine protease.

Serine proteases are enzymes that cleave peptide bonds in proteins, using a serine residue in their active site as the nucleophile that attacks the carbonyl carbon of the scissile bond. They are among the most abundant and best studied proteases, making up over a third of all known proteolytic enzymes, and are found in eukaryotes, prokaryotes, archaea and viruses.

Mechanism

Most serine proteases use a catalytic triad of serine, histidine and aspartate residues. The histidine acts as a base, accepting a proton from the serine hydroxyl group and greatly increasing its reactivity; the aspartate orientates and polarises the histidine. The activated serine attacks the substrate carbonyl, forming a covalent acyl-enzyme intermediate and releasing the first product; a water molecule then hydrolyses the intermediate to release the second product and regenerate the enzyme. Specificity is largely determined by pockets adjacent to the active site that accommodate particular amino-acid side chains of the substrate.

Families and examples

Serine proteases are grouped into clans and families by structure and mechanism. The chymotrypsin-like family includes the digestive enzymes trypsin, chymotrypsin and elastase, the blood-clotting factors such as thrombin, and immune-system proteases. The subtilisin family evolved the same catalytic triad independently, an example of convergent evolution. Other members include prolyl oligopeptidase and the viral proteases of hepatitis C and coronaviruses.

Biological roles and inhibition

These enzymes participate in digestion, blood coagulation, fibrinolysis, complement activation, hormone processing, apoptosis and viral maturation. Because uncontrolled proteolysis is dangerous, they are typically synthesised as inactive zymogens and are regulated by inhibitors such as the serpins. Dysregulation is implicated in emphysema, thrombosis, cancer and viral disease, and several serine proteases are major drug targets.
