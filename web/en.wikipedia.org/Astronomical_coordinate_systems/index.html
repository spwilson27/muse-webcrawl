Astronomical coordinate systems

Astronomical coordinate systems are frameworks for specifying the positions of celestial objects on the celestial sphere. Like geographic coordinates on Earth, they use two angular coordinates measured from a fundamental plane and a reference direction. Different systems serve different purposes: some are fixed to the observer's horizon, others to Earth's equator, the ecliptic, or the plane of the Milky Way.

Horizontal system

The horizontal (altitude–azimuth) system uses the observer's horizon as its fundamental plane. Altitude measures angular height above the horizon; azimuth measures bearing around it. It is convenient for pointing telescopes but varies with time and location as Earth rotates.

Equatorial system

The equatorial system, the most widely used for cataloguing stars, projects Earth's equator onto the celestial sphere. Its coordinates are declination (analogous to latitude) and right ascension (analogous to longitude, measured in hours eastward from the March equinox). Because of precession, equatorial coordinates are quoted for a standard epoch such as J2000.0.

Ecliptic system

The ecliptic system takes the plane of Earth's orbit as fundamental, with ecliptic latitude and longitude; it is natural for describing the Sun, Moon and planets.

Galactic system

The galactic system, defined by the IAU in 1958, is aligned with the Milky Way: galactic latitude is measured from the galactic plane and longitude from the direction of the galactic centre in Sagittarius. It is used in studies of galactic structure.

Conversion and epochs

Converting between systems uses spherical trigonometry; converting equatorial coordinates between epochs must account for precession, nutation and proper motion. The International Celestial Reference System (ICRS), realised by the positions of distant quasars, provides the modern inertial standard.
