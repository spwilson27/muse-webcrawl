Higher education in Germany

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Higher education in Germany is delivered by several hundred institutions, most of them public and funded by the federal states (Länder), which hold constitutional responsibility for education. Germany has around 400 higher education institutions, including more than 100 universities and an even larger number of universities of applied sciences (Fachhochschulen), attended by roughly 2.9 million students. German public universities traditionally charged no or only nominal tuition fees; after a period in the 2000s when some states introduced fees of around €500 per semester, all states had abolished general tuition fees again by 2014, and public higher education remains essentially tuition-free for domestic and international students alike, apart from semester contributions and exceptions in some states for non-EU students and second degrees.

Types of institutions

  • Universities (Universitäten) offer a broad range of subjects, combine teaching with research, and hold the right to award doctorates. Technical universities (Technische Universitäten) focus on engineering and the natural sciences.
  • Universities of applied sciences (Fachhochschulen, now often styled Hochschulen für angewandte Wissenschaften) emphasise professionally oriented teaching, applied research, and smaller class sizes; they generally cannot award doctorates, though some states have begun granting limited doctoral rights.
  • Colleges of art and music (Kunst- und Musikhochschulen) train professional artists, designers, and musicians.
  • Private institutions enrol a minority of students and may charge substantial fees.

Degrees and the Bologna Process

Historically, German universities awarded integrated long degrees such as the Diplom and Magister, and the Staatsexamen (state examination) in regulated professions such as law, medicine, pharmacy, and teaching. Under the Bologna Process, Germany converted most programmes to the two-tier bachelor's and master's structure from the 2000s onward; bachelor's degrees typically take three years and master's degrees a further two. Exceptions remain: medicine, law, and some other subjects still conclude with the Staatsexamen, and some Diplom programmes persist. The doctorate (Promotion) is the highest academic degree and traditionally involves an individual research project supervised by a professor, though structured doctoral programmes have become more common.

Access and admission

The usual entrance qualification is the Abitur, earned at a Gymnasium, or the Fachhochschulreife for universities of applied sciences; vocationally qualified applicants may also gain access under certain conditions. Admission to popular subjects—notably medicine, psychology, and veterinary medicine—is restricted by numerus clausus, with places allocated largely on the basis of school grades. International students form a large and growing share of enrolment, attracted by free tuition and an increasing number of English-taught programmes; Germany is one of the world's most popular study destinations for foreign students.

Governance and funding

Higher education is primarily a responsibility of the sixteen states, which fund the institutions' basic budgets; the federal government contributes through programmes such as the Excellence Strategy, which funds clusters of excellence and a small group of "universities of excellence", and through joint research funding. Non-university research organisations such as the Max Planck Society, Fraunhofer Society, Helmholtz Association, and Leibniz Association work closely with universities but are institutionally separate.

History

Germany's oldest universities date to the late Middle Ages—Heidelberg (1386), Cologne (1388), and Leipzig (1409) among them. The modern research university model is associated with Wilhelm von Humboldt and the founding of the University of Berlin in 1810, which combined teaching and research and influenced higher education worldwide. German universities were brought under Nazi control (Gleichschaltung) after 1933, expelling Jewish and dissenting scholars; after 1945 the systems of East and West Germany diverged until reunification in 1990. The post-war decades saw mass expansion of enrolment and the creation of many new universities and Fachhochschulen.
