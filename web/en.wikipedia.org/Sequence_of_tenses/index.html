Sequence of tenses

The sequence of tenses (Latin: consecutio temporum) is a rule in the grammars of some languages governing the agreement in tense between a subordinate clause and the main clause that governs it. The concept is central to the use of reported (indirect) speech and to subjunctive clauses, where the tense of the subordinate verb is determined, wholly or partly, by the tense of the main verb.

In English

In English, sequence of tenses is most visible in reported speech, a phenomenon often called backshifting. A present-tense statement reported after a past-tense verb of saying is usually shifted into the past: "I am tired" becomes "She said that she was tired." Similarly, will becomes would, and the past simple may become the past perfect: "I saw him" → "He said he had seen him." Backshifting can be suspended when the reported content is still true at the time of reporting: "She said the Earth is round."

In Latin

In classical Latin grammar, the sequence of tenses is a strict rule for subjunctive subordinate clauses. Tenses are divided into primary (present, future, future perfect, and perfect with present force) and historic (imperfect, perfect, pluperfect). A subordinate subjunctive follows the group of the main verb, and its tense expresses action as simultaneous with, prior to, or subsequent to the main verb: present or perfect subjunctive after primary tenses, imperfect or pluperfect after historic tenses.

In other languages

Languages differ widely: Russian, for example, generally preserves the tense of the original utterance in indirect speech, while many Romance languages apply their own sequence rules in subjunctive clauses after verbs of wishing, doubting, and commanding.
