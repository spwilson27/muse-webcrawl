Wave theory of light

The wave theory of light holds that light is a wave phenomenon — in its modern form, a transverse electromagnetic wave. The theory was first articulated in a mathematical form by Christiaan Huygens in his Treatise on Light (1690), which explained reflection and refraction by treating each point on a wavefront as a source of secondary wavelets (Huygens' principle), and correctly predicted that light travels more slowly in denser media.

Competition with the corpuscular theory

Isaac Newton's rival corpuscular theory, set out in his Opticks (1704), modelled light as a stream of tiny particles. Newton's authority kept the corpuscular view dominant through the 18th century, although it struggled with phenomena such as diffraction, described in detail by Francesco Grimaldi in the 1660s.

Triumph of the wave theory

In 1801 Thomas Young performed his double-slit experiment, demonstrating interference fringes that only waves could produce. Augustin-Jean Fresnel then developed the mathematics of diffraction and interference comprehensively; the bright spot Poisson predicted — intending to refute Fresnel — was observed by Arago in 1818 and became famous evidence for the wave theory (the Poisson/Arago spot). Foucault's 1850 measurement that light is slower in water than in air, a direct prediction of the wave theory and contradiction of the corpuscular one, settled the matter for most physicists.

Electromagnetic synthesis and after

James Clerk Maxwell's equations (1865) predicted electromagnetic waves travelling at the measured speed of light, identifying light as an electromagnetic disturbance, and Hertz's experiments of 1887–88 confirmed the theory. The supposed medium of the waves, the luminiferous aether, was never detected; Einstein's special relativity (1905) removed the need for it. The same year, Einstein's explanation of the photoelectric effect reintroduced particle-like quanta (photons), and the two pictures were reconciled in quantum electrodynamics through wave-particle duality.
