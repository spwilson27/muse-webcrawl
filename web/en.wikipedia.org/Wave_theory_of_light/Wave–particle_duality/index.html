Wave–particle duality

Wave–particle duality is the concept in quantum mechanics that every particle or quantum entity may be described as either a particle or a wave. It expresses the inability of the classical concepts "particle" or "wave" to fully describe the behaviour of quantum-scale objects. As Albert Einstein wrote: "It seems as though we must use sometimes the one theory and sometimes the other, while at times we may use either. We are faced with a new kind of difficulty."

History

The debate over whether light consists of waves or particles began in the 17th century, with Christiaan Huygens championing waves and Isaac Newton particles. The wave theory prevailed in the 19th century through the experiments of Young and Fresnel and Maxwell's electromagnetic theory. Then Einstein's 1905 explanation of the photoelectric effect introduced light quanta (photons), and Arthur Compton's 1923 scattering experiments showed photons carry momentum. De Broglie's 1924 hypothesis extended wave behaviour to matter, confirmed by electron diffraction in 1927.

Key experiments

The double-slit experiment is the classic demonstration: entities such as electrons produce an interference pattern characteristic of waves, yet are registered individually as particles, and attempts to observe which slit they traverse destroy the interference. Delayed-choice and quantum-eraser variants sharpen the puzzle of how the experimental arrangement determines which aspect appears.

Interpretation

Bohr's principle of complementarity treats wave and particle pictures as complementary descriptions of one reality. In modern quantum field theory, both aspects arise from quantized fields: the wave function propagates and interferes according to the Schrödinger equation, while interactions occur at discrete points with quantized energy and momentum.
