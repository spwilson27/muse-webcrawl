Wave-particle duality

Wave-particle duality is the concept in quantum mechanics that fundamental entities — such as photons and electrons — exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties, depending on how they are measured. It is a central, and famously counterintuitive, feature of quantum theory: a quantum object is neither a classical wave nor a classical particle, but shows interference like a wave while being detected in discrete, localized events like a particle.

Historical development

Light was described first as particles (Newton) and then triumphantly as a wave (Young, Fresnel, Maxwell). In 1905 Albert Einstein explained the photoelectric effect by proposing that light itself is quantized into packets of energy, later called photons. Conversely, in 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that matter has wave properties, with wavelength λ = h/p; electron diffraction experiments by Davisson and Germer in 1927 confirmed this.

Evidence

In the double-slit experiment, particles sent one at a time through two slits gradually build up an interference pattern, as if each particle passes through both slits as a wave yet lands as a single point. Diffraction and interference have since been demonstrated with atoms and large molecules, while Compton scattering and the photoelectric effect display particle-like energy and momentum exchange.

Modern view

Quantum field theory unifies the picture: particles are excitations of underlying quantum fields, and their behaviour is computed from a wave function. Niels Bohr framed duality as complementarity — wave and particle descriptions are mutually exclusive yet jointly necessary views of the same reality.
