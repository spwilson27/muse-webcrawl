Imitation

A child imitating an adult's actions.

Imitation (from Latin imitatio, "a copying, imitation") is a behavior whereby an individual observes and replicates another's behavior. Imitation is also a form of social learning that leads to the development of traditions and culture. It allows for the transfer of information, behaviors, customs, and so on, between individuals and down generations without the need for genetic inheritance. The word imitation can be applied to animals and to humans, ranging from simple mimicry to the sophisticated reproduction of skills, and the concept has been central to philosophy since Plato and Aristotle treated art itself as imitation (mimesis) of nature.

Psychology and neuroscience

Imitation is particularly important in early human development: infants begin to copy facial expressions within weeks of birth, and children learn language, manners, and practical skills largely through imitating caregivers and peers. Albert Bandura's social learning experiments, including the Bobo doll studies, showed that children reproduce behaviors they observe in models, especially when those behaviors are rewarded. The discovery of mirror neurons in the 1990s — brain cells that fire both when an action is performed and when the same action is observed — was proposed as a neural basis for imitation and possibly empathy, though the strength of these claims remains debated.

Imitation in animals and machines

True imitation must be distinguished from simpler forms of social influence such as contagion and emulation; robust evidence exists in primates, dolphins, and some birds, including vocal learning in songbirds and parrots. In technology, imitation learning trains robots and AI systems from human demonstrations, and large language models acquire much of their behavior by imitating patterns in text.
