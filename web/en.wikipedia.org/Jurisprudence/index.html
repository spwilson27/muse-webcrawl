Jurisprudence

Jurisprudence
Also called: Philosophy of law; legal theory
Key questions: What is law? What makes law valid? How do law and morality relate?

Jurisprudence is the theory and philosophy of law. Scholars of jurisprudence seek to understand the nature of legal systems, the relationship between law and morality, the basis of legal authority and obligation, and the methods by which laws should be interpreted and applied.

Major schools

Natural law theory, running from Aristotle and Aquinas to modern thinkers such as John Finnis, holds that law's validity depends in part on its moral content — an unjust law is in some sense defective as law. Legal positivism, developed by Jeremy Bentham, John Austin, and H. L. A. Hart, insists on the separability of law and morality: law is valid because it is posited by a recognized authority according to accepted procedures. Hart's The Concept of Law (1961), with its account of primary and secondary rules and the "rule of recognition," set the agenda for later debate, notably with Ronald Dworkin, who argued that judges in hard cases rely on moral principles that are themselves part of the law.

Legal realism, associated with Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. and Scandinavian and American jurists of the early 20th century, emphasizes what officials actually do rather than formal doctrine. Other major approaches include interpretivism, critical legal studies, feminist jurisprudence, and law and economics, the last applying economic analysis of efficiency to legal rules.

Scope

Beyond general jurisprudence — the study of law's nature in the abstract — the term also covers comparative and particular jurisprudence: the theoretical study of specific branches such as contract, tort, or constitutional law, and of specific legal traditions, including Roman, Islamic, and Jewish jurisprudence.
