Vaccines and autism

Extensive scientific investigation has found no link between vaccines and autism. The claim that vaccines cause autism spectrum disorder originated with a fraudulent 1998 paper by British ex-physician Andrew Wakefield concerning the MMR vaccine, and has been thoroughly discredited. Scientific consensus, supported by large epidemiological studies in several countries, is that there is no relationship between vaccination — including with the MMR vaccine or with vaccines containing thiomersal — and the incidence of autism.

Origins of the claim

In 1998, Wakefield and colleagues published a paper in The Lancet suggesting a link between the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine and a syndrome of bowel disease and developmental regression. The study, based on twelve children, was later shown to be fraudulent: data had been misrepresented, and Wakefield had undisclosed financial conflicts of interest. The Lancet fully retracted the paper in 2010, and Wakefield was struck off the UK medical register.

Evidence

Numerous large-scale studies have compared autism rates in vaccinated and unvaccinated children and found no difference. A 2019 Danish study of more than 650,000 children again found that MMR vaccination did not increase the risk of autism, including in subgroups considered at higher risk. Rates of autism also continued to rise in Japan after the MMR vaccine was withdrawn, and fell or remained unchanged in settings where thiomersal was removed, contradicting the hypothesised causes. Reviews by bodies including the World Health Organization, the US Institute of Medicine (now the National Academy of Medicine) and the American Academy of Pediatrics have concluded that the evidence favours rejection of a causal relationship.

Consequences

The publicity surrounding the claim led to drops in vaccination coverage in the United Kingdom and elsewhere, followed by outbreaks of measles and other preventable diseases. Public health authorities regard vaccine hesitancy fuelled by the episode as a significant health threat, and the episode is frequently cited as a case study in scientific fraud and in the communication of risk.
