Tillage

Tillage is the agricultural preparation of soil by mechanical agitation of various types, such as digging, stirring and overturning. It is performed to create a suitable seedbed, bury crop residues and weeds, aerate the soil, mix in fertilisers and manure, and control pests that live in the topsoil. Human-powered tillage uses tools such as hoes and spades; animal- and machine-powered tillage uses ploughs, harrows, cultivators and rotary tillers drawn by draught animals, two-wheel tractors or four-wheel tractors.

Types

Primary tillage is the deeper, rougher operation performed after harvest or on fallow land, typically with a mouldboard or chisel plough, which loosens and inverts the topsoil. Secondary tillage follows, producing a finer, level seedbed with harrows, discs and rollers, and destroying emerging weeds. Depending on intensity and how much residue is left on the surface, systems are grouped as conventional tillage (full inversion, little residue cover), reduced tillage, conservation tillage (at least 30% residue cover), and no-till farming, in which seed is drilled directly into undisturbed soil.

Effects

Tillage can improve drainage and warm the soil in spring, incorporate organic matter, and give crops an early advantage over weeds. Against this, repeated tillage breaks down soil structure and soil aggregates, accelerates the oxidation of soil organic matter, leaves the surface exposed to wind and water erosion, and can form a compacted "plough pan" beneath the worked layer. Loss of topsoil by erosion and the release of carbon dioxide from disturbed soil are major concerns, and they motivate reduced-tillage and no-till systems, cover cropping and contour cultivation, especially on sloping or fragile land.

History

Farmers have tilled soil since the Neolithic, first with digging sticks and hoes, then with the ard and later the mouldboard plough, which spread through medieval Europe and supported the three-field system. The steel plough of the 19th century and the tractor of the 20th made deep, fast tillage universal in industrial agriculture. The Dust Bowl of the 1930s in North America, partly blamed on intensive ploughing of prairie soils, prompted soil-conservation research and, eventually, the modern conservation-tillage movement.
