Religious Freedom Restoration Act

RFRA was signed into law on 16 November 1993.

The Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 (RFRA), 42 U.S.C. §§ 2000bb–2000bb-4, is a United States federal law that prohibits the government from substantially burdening a person's exercise of religion unless the burden is the least restrictive means of furthering a compelling governmental interest. It was passed almost unanimously by Congress and signed by President Bill Clinton on 16 November 1993.

Background and scope

RFRA was a direct response to the Supreme Court's decision in Employment Division v. Smith (1990), which held that neutral laws of general applicability do not violate the Free Exercise Clause even when they burden religion (there, denying unemployment benefits to Native American Church members fired for sacramental peyote use). Congress sought to restore the stricter Sherbert test.

In City of Boerne v. Flores (1997) the Court held RFRA unconstitutional as applied to state and local governments, exceeding Congress's power under the Fourteenth Amendment; RFRA therefore continues to bind only the federal government. More than twenty states have enacted their own "state RFRAs".

Major cases

In Gonzales v. O Centro Espírita (2006) the Court unanimously applied RFRA to protect a small church's sacramental use of a hallucinogenic tea. In Burwell v. Hobby Lobby Stores (2014), a 5–4 Court held that RFRA protects closely held for-profit corporations, exempting them from the Affordable Care Act's contraceptive mandate where less restrictive means existed — a decision that made RFRA a major front in American culture-war litigation, including subsequent controversies over state RFRAs in Indiana and Arkansas in 2015.
