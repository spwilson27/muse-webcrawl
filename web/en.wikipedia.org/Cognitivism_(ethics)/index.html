Cognitivism (ethics)

In metaethics, cognitivism is the view that ethical sentences express propositions, and therefore can be true or false (truth-apt). Cognitivists hold that moral judgements state facts or beliefs about the world — for example, that the sentence "murder is wrong" expresses a proposition that is either true or false — whereas non-cognitivists deny this, treating moral utterances as expressions of emotion, commands, or prescriptions instead.

Varieties

Cognitivism encompasses a range of theories. Moral realists hold that some ethical propositions are true in virtue of objective features of the world. Error theorists, such as J. L. Mackie, are also cognitivists: they agree that moral sentences state truth-apt claims, but hold that those claims are all false because there are no objective moral properties. Subjectivist and relativist theories take moral truths to depend on the attitudes of individuals or cultures.

Contrast with non-cognitivism

Non-cognitivist theories — including emotivism (A. J. Ayer, C. L. Stevenson), prescriptivism (R. M. Hare), and expressivism — deny that moral language primarily describes the world. The debate between cognitivists and non-cognitivists turns on the semantics of moral language, moral psychology (including the relation between moral judgement and motivation), and the Frege–Geach problem, which challenges non-cognitivists to explain moral terms in embedded contexts such as conditionals and negations.
