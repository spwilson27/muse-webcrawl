History of Wales

The history of Wales begins with the arrival of human beings in the region thousands of years ago. Wales has been inhabited by modern humans for at least 29,000 years, but it began to take a recognisably Welsh identity with the emergence of Brythonic kingdoms after the Roman withdrawal from Britain in the 5th century. Wales was formed as a country under Gruffydd ap Llywelyn in 1057, yet it never achieved a lasting single state before its conquest by Edward I of England in the late 13th century.

Prehistory

The Red Lady of Paviland, a burial in the Gower Peninsula dating to about 29,000 years ago, is among the earliest known ceremonial burials in Western Europe. Neolithic and Bronze Age communities left monuments such as Pentre Ifan and Bryn Celli Ddu. In the Iron Age, Wales was divided among Celtic tribes, including the Silures in the south-east, the Ordovices in the north-west and the Deceangli in the north-east.

Roman Wales

The Roman conquest of Wales took place between AD 48 and 78. The Silures under Caratacus and later the Ordovices resisted fiercely until Agricola's campaigns subdued them. The Romans built forts such as Isca Augusta (Caerleon), with its amphitheatre and baths, and Deva (Chester) on the border. Gold was mined at Dolaucothi. By the late 4th century Roman control was fading.

The age of the saints and the kingdoms

After the Roman withdrawal, Wales fragmented into kingdoms including Gwynedd, Powys, Dyfed and Gwent. This was the "age of the saints", when figures such as Saint David (Dewi) founded monasteries and the Welsh Church developed its own character. The Welsh language emerged from Common Brittonic. In the late 8th century Offa of Mercia built Offa's Dyke along the eastern frontier. In the 10th century Hywel Dda (Hywel the Good) codified Welsh law.

Viking and Norman incursions

Viking raids struck the coasts from the late 8th century. After 1066 the Normans advanced into Wales, establishing the Welsh Marches — a frontier zone of semi-independent Marcher lordships. Gruffydd ap Llywelyn (r. 1055–1063) briefly united nearly all of Wales before his defeat and death.

The princes and the Edwardian conquest

In the 13th century Llywelyn the Great of Gwynedd built a principality, and his grandson Llywelyn ap Gruffudd was recognised by Henry III in the Treaty of Montgomery (1267) as Prince of Wales. Conflict with Edward I led to wars in 1277 and 1282–83; Llywelyn was killed in 1282 and his brother Dafydd executed in 1283. The Statute of Rhuddlan (1284) annexed the principality to England, and Edward ringed Gwynedd with great castles — Conwy, Caernarfon, Harlech and Beaumaris. In 1301 the king's eldest son was created Prince of Wales, a title borne by heirs to the throne ever since.

Late medieval Wales and Glyndŵr's revolt

In 1400 Owain Glyndŵr was proclaimed Prince of Wales and led a major revolt that for a time controlled much of the country; he summoned Welsh parliaments at Machynlleth and Harlech and planned a university and independent church. The revolt faded by about 1415, and harsh penal laws followed. In 1485 Henry Tudor, a Welshman of the line of Penmynydd, won the crown at Bosworth and founded the Tudor dynasty.

Union with England

Under Henry VIII, the Laws in Wales Acts 1535 and 1542 integrated Wales into the Kingdom of England, applying English law and administration and barring Welsh speakers from office. In 1588 Bishop William Morgan's Welsh translation of the Bible was published, a landmark that helped preserve the Welsh language. Wales remained overwhelmingly rural and became a stronghold of Nonconformist religion after the Methodist revival of the 18th century.

Industrial Revolution

From the late 18th century, the iron and coal industries transformed south Wales. Merthyr Tydfil became the iron capital of the world, and the South Wales Valleys exported steam coal across the globe; Cardiff became the greatest coal port in the world. Explosive population growth and harsh conditions produced unrest, including the Merthyr Rising (1831), the Chartist Newport Rising (1839) and the Rebecca Riots (1839–44) in the west.

Modern Wales and devolution

Cardiff was declared the capital in 1955. In 1997 a referendum narrowly approved devolution, and the National Assembly for Wales (now the Senedd) opened in 1999. The Welsh language, which had declined through the 20th century, has been supported by education and broadcasting, including the S4C television channel launched in 1982.
