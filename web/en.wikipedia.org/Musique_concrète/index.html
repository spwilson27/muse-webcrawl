Musique concrète

Musique concrète (French for "concrete music") is a type of electroacoustic music made from recorded sounds — fragments of natural, environmental, mechanical, or musical sounds — which are edited, transformed and assembled into compositions, typically on magnetic tape. It contrasts with "abstract" music composed from notation: the composer works directly with the concrete material of sound itself.

Origins

The technique was developed in Paris by Pierre Schaeffer, a radio engineer working at French broadcasting (RTF). His first studies, the Cinq études de bruits (1948), including the Étude aux chemins de fer made from train sounds, were broadcast that year and coined the term. In 1950 Schaeffer and Pierre Henry produced the Symphonie pour un homme seul, a landmark of the genre. Schaeffer founded the Groupe de Recherche de Musique Concrète (later GRM) and developed a theory of the "sound object" (objet sonore) and "reduced listening" — hearing sounds apart from their sources and meanings.

Techniques and influence

Composers manipulated tape by splicing, reversing, changing speed, looping, filtering and mixing. Analogous work was done by the BBC Radiophonic Workshop, by Halim El-Dabh in Cairo in the 1940s, and in American tape music. Musique concrète's methods — sampling everyday sound, tape loops, and studio-as-instrument production — prefigure sampling, hip-hop turntablism, plunderphonics, sound design and much electronic dance music, and its ideas remain foundational in acousmatic music, in which sounds are presented through loudspeakers without visible sources.
