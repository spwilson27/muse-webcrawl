Fredholm integral equation

In mathematics, a Fredholm integral equation is an integral equation whose solution gives rise to Fredholm theory, the study of Fredholm kernels and Fredholm operators. The equations were studied by the Swedish mathematician Erik Ivar Fredholm in a landmark series of papers around 1900–1903.

Kinds

A Fredholm equation of the first kind has the form f(x) = ∫ K(x, t) φ(t) dt over a fixed interval, where the kernel K and the function f are known and φ is the unknown. A Fredholm equation of the second kind includes the unknown function outside the integral as well: φ(x) = f(x) + λ ∫ K(x, t) φ(t) dt. Equations of the second kind are generally far better behaved and are central to applications, while equations of the first kind are typically ill-posed.

Theory

Fredholm showed that equations of the second kind with a continuous kernel admit at most one solution except for a discrete set of values of the parameter λ (the eigenvalues of the kernel), establishing an analogue of linear-algebra results in infinite dimensions. This is summarized by the Fredholm alternative: either the inhomogeneous equation has a unique solution for every f, or the homogeneous equation has nontrivial solutions, in which case the inhomogeneous equation is solvable only under orthogonality conditions. Fredholm's work was a major stimulus for David Hilbert's development of Hilbert space theory.
