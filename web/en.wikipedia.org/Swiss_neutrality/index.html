Swiss neutrality

The Swiss flag; Switzerland has maintained permanent armed neutrality for over two centuries.

Swiss neutrality is one of the main principles of Switzerland's foreign policy, stipulating that Switzerland shall not become involved in armed conflicts between other states. This policy is self-imposed and designed to ensure the country's external security and promote peace. Switzerland has the oldest policy of military neutrality in the world: it has not fought an international war since 1815, when its permanent neutrality was recognized by the great powers at the Congress of Vienna.

Origins

The roots of neutrality lie in the early modern Confederation's abstention from the wars of religion and the defeat at the Battle of Marignano (1515), after which the Swiss largely gave up expansionist warfare while continuing to export mercenary soldiers. The Peace of Westphalia (1648) recognized Swiss independence from the Holy Roman Empire. In 1815 the Treaty of Paris confirmed Switzerland's "perpetual neutrality", which Austria, Britain, France, Prussia, and Russia guaranteed.

Armed neutrality

Swiss neutrality is armed: Switzerland maintains a militia army and mobilized heavily in both world wars to deter invasion. During the Second World War the country was surrounded by Axis powers, traded with both sides, and its conduct (including refugee policy and financial dealings) later drew historical scrutiny and official inquiries such as the Bergier commission.

Neutrality and international organizations

Neutrality kept Switzerland out of the United Nations until a 2002 referendum approved membership; Switzerland had earlier joined the League of Nations with a special "differential neutrality" status. Switzerland is not a member of NATO or the European Union, though it cooperates through Partnership for Peace and bilateral agreements. It hosts the International Committee of the Red Cross and many international organizations in Geneva, and serves as a protecting power and host for peace talks. After Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, Switzerland adopted EU sanctions against Russia, prompting debate about how neutrality applies in a changing security environment, while maintaining its ban on re-export of Swiss war materiel to conflict zones as a core legal constraint.
