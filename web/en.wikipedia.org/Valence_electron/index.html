Valence electron

The electrons in the outermost shell are the valence electrons.

In chemistry and physics, valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost shell of an atom that can participate in the formation of chemical bonds with other atoms. For main-group elements, the valence electrons are those in the highest principal energy level (n shell); for transition metals, electrons in an incomplete (n−1)d subshell also behave as valence electrons.

Role in bonding and properties

The number of valence electrons largely determines an element's chemical properties, which is why elements in the same group of the periodic table — having equal valence counts — behave similarly. Atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons so as to achieve a full outer shell, usually eight electrons (the octet rule), forming ionic or covalent bonds. A filled valence shell accounts for the inertness of the noble gases.

Electrical properties

In solids, whether valence electrons are localised or free to move determines conductivity: metals have loosely held valence electrons forming an electron "sea," semiconductors have a filled valence band separated by a small band gap from the conduction band, and insulators have a large gap. Valence electron counts also underlie concepts such as oxidation state and the spectrochemical behaviour of complexes.
