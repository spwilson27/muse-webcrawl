History of Libya

The history of Libya spans the prehistoric cultures of the Sahara, Phoenician and Greek colonisation, Roman and Byzantine rule, the Arab conquest, Ottoman administration, Italian colonisation, the independent kingdom, the Gaddafi era and the civil wars that followed his fall in 2011.

Ancient Libya

Rock art in the Tadrart Acacus testifies to a once-green Sahara inhabited by pastoral peoples. In antiquity the coast was settled by Phoenicians in Tripolitania and by Greeks who founded Cyrene and the cities of Cyrenaica from the seventh century BCE. Rome absorbed both regions; under Septimius Severus, a native of Leptis Magna who became emperor, the city was lavishly rebuilt. The Berber (Amazigh) Garamantes controlled desert trade routes from the Fezzan.

Islamic and Ottoman periods

Arab armies conquered Libya in the seventh century, and over time the population became predominantly Arab and Muslim. After periods of Fatimid, Almohad and Hafsid rule, the Ottomans took Tripoli in 1551. From 1711 the semi-autonomous Karamanli dynasty ruled; the young United States fought the Barbary Wars against Tripoli in 1801–05 and 1815. Ottoman direct rule was restored in 1835, and the Sanusi order became a powerful force in Cyrenaica.

Italian colonisation

Italy invaded in 1911, taking Libya from the Ottomans, but resistance — especially by Sanusi forces and Omar Mukhtar in Cyrenaica — continued for two decades and was suppressed with great brutality, including concentration camps and the deaths of a large share of Cyrenaica's population. Italian Libya was formally unified in 1934, and tens of thousands of Italian settlers arrived. North Africa campaigns of the Second World War, including the battles of El Alamein and the Siege of Tobruk, were fought across Libya.

Kingdom and Gaddafi era

Libya became independent in 1951 as a federal kingdom under King Idris I of the Sanusi. Oil was discovered in 1959 and transformed the economy. In 1969 Colonel Muammar Gaddafi seized power in a coup and ruled for 42 years, proclaiming a "Jamahiriya" (state of the masses) governed nominally by popular committees and ideologically by his Green Book, while in practice concentrating power personally. Libya's support for armed groups abroad and the 1988 Lockerbie bombing brought sanctions, which were lifted in the 2000s as Gaddafi renounced weapons programmes.

2011 and aftermath

In 2011, amid the Arab Spring, protests in Benghazi escalated into civil war; NATO intervened under a UN mandate, and Gaddafi was captured and killed in October 2011. Elections followed, but rival governments and militias plunged Libya into renewed civil war from 2014, with the country divided between competing authorities in Tripoli and the east, and Islamic State briefly holding Sirte. A UN-backed Government of National Unity formed in 2021, but elections have been repeatedly postponed and the political division persists.
