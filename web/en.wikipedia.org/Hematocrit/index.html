Hematocrit

Other namesPacked cell volume (PCV), volume of packed red cells (VPRC)

The hematocrit (also spelled haematocrit), also known by several other names, is the volume percentage (vol%) of red blood cells (RBCs) in blood, measured as part of a blood test. It is normally about 45% for men and 40% for women; the rest of the blood volume is mostly plasma, with a thin "buffy coat" of white blood cells and platelets. Because red cells carry oxygen, hematocrit is a core indicator of the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity.

Measurement

Historically the hematocrit was measured directly: blood is drawn into a capillary tube, sealed and centrifuged, and the height of the packed red-cell column is read as a fraction of the total. Modern automated analysers instead calculate it from the red-cell count and mean corpuscular volume (MCV). The name itself comes from the instrument ("haematokrit", Greek for "blood separator"), introduced by the Swedish physiologist Magnus Blix in the 1890s and popularised by Wintrobe's tube in the 20th century.

Clinical significance

A low hematocrit indicates anaemia — from blood loss, reduced red-cell production (iron, B12 or folate deficiency; chronic disease; bone-marrow failure) or increased destruction (haemolysis). A high hematocrit occurs in polycythaemia (primary, as in polycythaemia vera, or secondary to hypoxia, altitude, smoking or erythropoietin), and in dehydration, where the plasma volume falls. Extremely high values increase blood viscosity and the risk of thrombosis. Hematocrit is also monitored in transfusion medicine, dialysis, pregnancy and sports.

Doping and altitude

Because raising red-cell mass improves endurance performance, blood doping and recombinant erythropoietin (EPO) abuse aim to elevate hematocrit; sports bodies have used hematocrit thresholds and biological passports as screening tools. Conversely, athletes train at altitude to stimulate natural erythropoiesis, since residents at high altitude have physiologically higher hematocrits.
