Conflict (narrative)

Conflict between opposing forces drives a story's plot.

Conflict is a literary device characterised by a struggle between two opposing forces, usually a protagonist and an antagonist, or forces within or around a character. Conflict provides the tension that drives a narrative's plot: the protagonist's desire meets obstacles, and the story's events follow from attempts to overcome them, building through rising action toward a climax and resolution.

Types

Writing guides commonly classify conflict by the nature of the opposing force. Character versus character pits the protagonist against an antagonist, as in a hero–villain rivalry. Character versus self is internal conflict, a struggle with one's own desires, fears, or beliefs. Character versus society opposes the individual to laws, customs, or institutions, while character versus nature involves survival against animals, weather, or disease. Later additions include character versus technology and character versus fate or the supernatural. A single work typically combines several types, with one forming the central conflict.

Role in dramatic structure

From Aristotle's Poetics onward, theorists have treated conflict as essential to drama. In Freytag's pyramid the conflict is introduced in the exposition, intensified through complications, and settled in the denouement. Not all narrative traditions privilege conflict equally: the East Asian four-part structure known as kishōtenketsu, used in Chinese, Korean, and Japanese storytelling, can develop and resolve a narrative through juxtaposition and a surprising turn rather than opposition, a contrast often cited in discussions of conflict-centred Western plotting.

Resolution

Conflict is concluded by the protagonist's victory, defeat, compromise, or transformation. In tragedy, resolution often comes through the protagonist's destruction; in comedy, through reconciliation or exposure. Open-ended works may leave conflict deliberately unresolved.
