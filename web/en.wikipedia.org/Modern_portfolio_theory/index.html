Modern portfolio theory

Modern portfolio theory (MPT), or mean–variance analysis, is a mathematical framework for assembling a portfolio of assets such that the expected return is maximized for a given level of risk. It is a formalization and extension of diversification in investing — the idea that owning different kinds of financial assets reduces overall risk compared with owning only one type.

History

MPT was introduced by Harry Markowitz in his 1952 paper "Portfolio Selection", published in The Journal of Finance, and developed in his 1959 book. Markowitz shared the 1990 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Merton Miller and William F. Sharpe for work on financial economics.

Mathematical framework

In the model, an asset's return is treated as a random variable, and a portfolio is a weighted combination of assets. The portfolio's expected return is the weighted average of expected returns, while its variance depends not only on the individual assets' variances but also on their pairwise correlations. By combining assets whose returns are not perfectly correlated, an investor can reduce portfolio variance without sacrificing expected return. The set of portfolios that offer the highest expected return for each level of risk forms the efficient frontier.

Assumptions and criticism

The theory assumes, among other things, that investors are rational and risk-averse, that asset returns are adequately described by mean and variance (often modelled as normally distributed or with quadratic utility), and that markets are frictionless. Critics note that real returns exhibit fat tails and skewness, correlations change during crises, and investors behave irrationally; these observations motivated post-modern portfolio theory and behavioural approaches. MPT nonetheless underlies much of institutional portfolio management, the capital asset pricing model (CAPM), and robo-advisory allocation algorithms.
