Katyn massacre

The Katyn massacre was a series of mass executions of nearly 22,000 Polish military officers and intelligentsia carried out by the Soviet NKVD in April and May 1940. The victims, prisoners of war captured after the Soviet invasion of Poland in 1939, were murdered at several sites, most famously the Katyn forest near Smolensk, on the orders of the Soviet leadership following a proposal by NKVD chief Lavrentiy Beria approved by Joseph Stalin.

Background

After the Soviet invasion of eastern Poland on 17 September 1939, hundreds of thousands of Polish soldiers and police were taken prisoner. Officers were concentrated in camps at Kozelsk, Starobelsk and Ostashkov. On 5 March 1940 the Soviet Politburo approved Beria's proposal to execute the officers and thousands of other Polish prisoners held in western Ukraine and Belarus.

The killings

During April and May 1940 the prisoners were transported in small groups to execution sites, shot in the back of the head and buried in mass graves. About 4,400 victims from Kozelsk were buried in the Katyn forest; others were killed at the NKVD headquarters in Kharkiv and at Kalinin (Tver) and buried at Pyatykhatky and Mednoye. In total, 21,857 people were murdered according to later Soviet documents.

Discovery and denial

Nazi Germany announced the discovery of the Katyn graves in 1943; the Soviet Union blamed the Germans and maintained the lie for nearly five decades, a falsehood that poisoned Polish–Soviet relations and was echoed in an official Soviet counter-narrative. In 1990 the Soviet government finally admitted NKVD responsibility, and in 1992 Russia handed Poland copies of the key documents, including Beria's proposal and the Politburo decision. Cemeteries and memorials now mark the burial sites, and 13 April is observed in Poland as a day of remembrance for the victims.
