Seawater

Seawater, or salt water, is water from a sea or ocean. On average, seawater in the world's oceans has a salinity of about 3.5% (35 grams per litre), meaning that every kilogram of seawater contains roughly 35 grams of dissolved salts, predominantly sodium and chloride ions. Seawater is denser than fresh water and freezes at about −2 °C.

Composition

The proportions of the major dissolved ions in seawater are nearly constant worldwide, a regularity known as Marcet's principle. Sodium and chloride together make up about 85% of dissolved salts. The salts originate from weathering of rocks on land, carried to the sea by rivers, and from hydrothermal vents and volcanic activity.

Properties

Salinity and temperature together determine seawater density, driving thermohaline circulation. Seawater's salt content depresses its freezing point and raises its boiling point relative to pure water. Surface ocean pH has fallen from about 8.2 toward 8.1 since the industrial era as the oceans absorb anthropogenic carbon dioxide, a process called ocean acidification.

Human use

Humans cannot safely drink seawater, but it is the source of sea salt, is used for cooling and shipping, and is increasingly desalinated in arid coastal regions to produce drinking water.
