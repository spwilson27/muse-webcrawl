Aromatic hydrocarbon

An aromatic hydrocarbon or arene is a hydrocarbon with sigma bonds and delocalised pi electrons between carbon atoms forming rings. The term "aromatic" was originally applied because many of the first compounds discovered had sweet or pleasant odours, but in modern chemistry it denotes a specific electronic structure conferring unusual stability, exemplified by benzene.

Properties and structure

Aromatic rings have planar structures in which pi electrons are delocalised around the ring, satisfying Hückel's rule of 4n + 2 pi electrons. This delocalisation makes aromatic compounds much more stable than their formulas suggest and causes them to undergo electrophilic aromatic substitution rather than the addition reactions typical of alkenes. Benzene, toluene and the xylenes — the BTX aromatics — are high-volume industrial feedstocks produced from petroleum reforming and steam cracking.

Types and significance

Monocyclic arenes include benzene, toluene and styrene. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), such as naphthalene, anthracene and benzo[a]pyrene, contain fused rings and are formed by incomplete combustion of organic matter; several PAHs are potent carcinogens, and they are common environmental pollutants. Aromatic structures occur widely in nature and in pharmaceuticals, dyes, polymers and agrochemicals. Benzene itself is toxic and carcinogenic, and its use is tightly regulated.
