Hypertriglyceridemia

Hypertriglyceridemia is the presence of high amounts of triglyceride in the blood. Triglycerides are the most abundant fatty molecule in most organisms. Hypertriglyceridemia occurs as an isolated finding or as part of broader dyslipidemia, often alongside low HDL cholesterol and small dense LDL particles. Elevated triglycerides are associated with atherosclerosis and increased cardiovascular risk, and at very high levels (above about 500–1,000 mg/dL) they are a well-established cause of acute pancreatitis.

Classification

Common cut-offs classify fasting triglycerides as normal below 150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L), borderline-high at 150–199 mg/dL, high at 200–499 mg/dL, and very high at 500 mg/dL or above. Levels exceeding 1,000 mg/dL may produce a creamy appearance of blood plasma (lipemia).

Causes

Most hypertriglyceridemia is multifactorial — a combination of genetic susceptibility with lifestyle and secondary factors:

  • Obesity, insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes
  • Excess alcohol intake
  • Diets high in refined carbohydrates and sugars
  • Hypothyroidism, chronic kidney disease, nephrotic syndrome
  • Medications including some beta-blockers, thiazides, estrogens, retinoids, corticosteroids and certain antipsychotics and antiretrovirals
  • Pregnancy

Rare monogenic causes include familial chylomicronemia syndrome, caused by deficiency of lipoprotein lipase (LPL) or its cofactors (APOC2, APOA5, GPIHBP1, LMF1), in which triglycerides may be extremely elevated from childhood.

Signs and symptoms

Moderate elevations usually cause no symptoms and are found on blood testing. Severe hypertriglyceridemia can cause eruptive xanthomas (small yellowish skin papules), lipemia retinalis (a milky appearance of retinal vessels), hepatosplenomegaly, and recurrent abdominal pain from acute pancreatitis, the most dangerous complication.

Treatment

Management begins with lifestyle measures: weight loss, restriction of alcohol and sugars, aerobic exercise, and treatment of secondary causes such as diabetes. Drug therapy includes fibrates (first-line for severe elevations to prevent pancreatitis), prescription omega-3 fatty acids, statins (when cardiovascular risk dominates), niacin, and newer agents targeting APOC3 or ANGPTL3 for severe genetic forms. In extreme cases with pancreatitis, insulin infusions or plasmapheresis may be used to lower triglycerides rapidly.
