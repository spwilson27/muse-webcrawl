Camel

Kingdom:Animalia
Phylum:Chordata
Class:Mammalia

Family:Camelidae

A camel (from Latin: camelus) is an even-toed ungulate in the genus Camelus that bears distinctive fatty deposits known as "humps" on its back. The three surviving species are the one-humped dromedary, the two-humped Bactrian camel, and the wild Bactrian camel.

Species and distribution

The dromedary (Camelus dromedarius) accounts for about 94% of the world's roughly 35 million camels and lives throughout the Middle East, North and East Africa, and South Asia; a large feral population exists in Australia. The domestic Bactrian camel (C. bactrianus) inhabits Central Asia, while the wild Bactrian camel (C. ferus) survives only in remote parts of Mongolia and China and is critically endangered. Camels originated in North America some 45 million years ago and spread to Asia across the Bering land bridge.

Adaptations

Camels are famously adapted to desert life. They tolerate losses of more than 30% of their body water, allow their body temperature to swing widely through the day, and produce highly concentrated urine and dry faeces. The hump stores fat, not water. Oval red blood cells keep flowing when dehydrated and let camels drink over 100 litres in minutes. Double rows of eyelashes, sealable nostrils, and broad padded feet protect against sand and heat.

Domestication and uses

Camels were domesticated roughly 3,000–4,000 years ago and long served as the "ships of the desert", carrying trade goods across the Sahara and the Silk Road. They provide milk, meat, wool, leather, and transport, and are used in racing in the Arabian Peninsula. Camel milk is a staple for many pastoralist peoples.
