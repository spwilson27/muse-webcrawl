William Billings (7 October 1746 – 26 September 1800) was an American composer and singing-school teacher, widely regarded as the first American composer of significant choral music and the father of American choral singing. A largely self-taught tanner from Boston, he created a distinctly American idiom of hymnody — robust, rhythmically direct and harmonically unconventional — that spread through New England singing schools.

Life and works

Billings published his first tunebook, The New-England Psalm-Singer, in 1770; its frontispiece was engraved by Paul Revere, and it was the first published collection of music entirely by an American composer. Five further collections followed, notably The Singing Master's Assistant (1778) — containing the famous fuguing tune "Chester", which became a patriotic anthem of the Revolution with its words "Let tyrants shake their iron rod" — and The Continental Harmony (1794), which included his celebrated canon "When Jesus Wept" and the Christmas carol "Boston" ("Methinks I see a heav'nly host"). His anthem "David's Lamentation" and the hymn tune "Africa" remain in circulation.

Billings taught singing schools across Massachusetts, counted Samuel Adams among his friends, and helped organise Boston's Stoughton Musical Society. Despite his fame he died in poverty, leaving his widow and six children nearly destitute. He was inducted into the Songwriters Hall of Fame in 1970, and his music was revived by twentieth-century scholars and performers of early American music.

Style

Billings's part-writing freely breaks European rules of counterpoint — parallel fifths, modal melodies and open sonorities abound — producing the vigorous sound later carried into shape-note and Sacred Harp traditions of the American South and West.
