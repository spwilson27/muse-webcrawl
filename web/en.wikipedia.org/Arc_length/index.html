Arc length

Arc length is the distance between two points along a section of a curve. Determining the length of an irregular arc segment by approximating the arc by many small connected sections is called rectifying a curve. Since a rectifiable curve's arc length can be approximated by summing the lengths of the small segments, the arc length is defined as the limit of the sums of polygonal approximations as the number of segments approaches infinity.

Formula

For a curve defined by a function y = f(x) whose derivative is continuous on the interval [a, b], the arc length from a to b is

L = ∫ab √(1 + (dy/dx)²) dx

For a curve defined parametrically by x = x(t) and y = y(t), the arc length from t = α to t = β is

L = ∫αβ √((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²) dt

In polar coordinates, with r = r(θ), the element of arc length is ds = √(r² + (dr/dθ)²) dθ.

History

The problem of rectification occupied mathematicians for centuries. Ancient methods, such as the method of exhaustion used by Archimedes to approximate the circumference of a circle, supplied lengths only for special curves. In 1659 Hendrik van Heuraet published a general method reducing rectification to finding an area, and in the same year William Neile found the arc length of the semicubical parabola, the first nontrivial algebraic curve to be rectified. The development of calculus by Newton and Leibniz gave the integral formulas used today.

Curves without finite length

Not every curve has a finite arc length. The graph of a function such as x·sin(1/x) near the origin, and fractal curves such as the Koch snowflake, are non-rectifiable: their lengths within any interval containing the problematic point are unbounded, a fact related to the coastline paradox.
