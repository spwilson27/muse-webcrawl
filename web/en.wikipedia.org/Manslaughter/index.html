Manslaughter

Manslaughter is the unlawful killing of a person without the intent required for murder.

Manslaughter is a common law legal term for homicide considered by law as less culpable than murder. The distinction between murder and manslaughter is sometimes said to have first been made by the ancient Athenian lawmaker Draco in the seventh century BC. Manslaughter is generally the unlawful killing of a human being that lacks malice aforethought — the intention to kill or to cause grievous bodily harm — or that occurs under mitigating circumstances.

Voluntary manslaughter

Voluntary manslaughter involves an intentional killing that is mitigated by circumstances the law recognises as reducing culpability. The classic example is a killing in the "heat of passion" following adequate provocation — words or acts that would cause a reasonable person to lose self-control. Some jurisdictions have replaced or supplemented provocation with doctrines of "loss of control", diminished responsibility, or extreme emotional disturbance. Infanticide statutes in some countries similarly treat a mother's killing of her infant, when her mental balance is disturbed by childbirth, as a distinct mitigated offence.

Involuntary manslaughter

Involuntary manslaughter is an unintentional killing resulting from culpable negligence or recklessness, or from an unlawful act that is not itself a serious felony. Common-law systems recognise several varieties: criminally negligent manslaughter, where the defendant's grossly negligent conduct creates a high risk of death; unlawful-act (or misdemeanour) manslaughter, where death results from a criminal act; and corporate manslaughter, under which companies can be prosecuted when organisational failings cause a death, as under the UK Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007. Killing by grossly negligent driving is treated as a separate offence in some jurisdictions (such as causing death by dangerous driving in the United Kingdom).

Sentencing

Penalties vary widely by jurisdiction and category. In England and Wales manslaughter carries a maximum sentence of life imprisonment, with guideline starting points ranging from very short terms for low-culpability cases to decades for gross negligence or loss-of-control killings. In the United States, voluntary and involuntary manslaughter are defined by state law (and federally, at 18 U.S.C. § 1112), with maximum terms commonly of 10–15 years for voluntary manslaughter.
