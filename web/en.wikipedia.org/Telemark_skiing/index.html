Telemark skiing

Telemark skiing is a skiing technique that combines elements of alpine and Nordic skiing, using the Telemark turn. The technique is named after the Telemark region of Norway, where it originated in the 19th century. Telemark skiing uses a specialized binding in which only the toe of the ski boot is fixed to the ski, leaving the heel free to lift — hence its frequent description as "free-heel skiing".

History

The Telemark turn was popularized by Sondre Norheim, a Norwegian skier from Morgedal in Telemark county, who demonstrated the technique in races in the 1860s and 1870s. Norheim also pioneered the side-cut ski and the birch binding that held the heel loosely while securing the toe. As alpine skiing with fixed-heel bindings developed in the early 20th century, Telemark technique fell out of mainstream use, but it was revived in the United States in the 1970s by backcountry skiers seeking versatile equipment for touring and descending.

Technique

In a Telemark turn, the skier drops into a lunge position: the inside (uphill) ski is slid back so its tip is roughly beside the boot of the outside ski, the inside knee is bent deeply, and the heel of the inside boot is raised while the ball of the foot remains on the ski. The skis are edged and pressured to carve the turn, and the skier rises and transitions into a lunge on the opposite side for the next turn. The free heel allows the same equipment to be used for cross-country touring, climbing with skins, and descending.

Equipment

Telemark equipment includes skis similar in shape to alpine skis, flexible boots with a bellows at the toe, and bindings that attach only at the toe. Traditional three-pin bindings gave way to cable bindings, and later to the New Telemark Norm (NTN) system, introduced in 2007, which added features such as step-in convenience and releasability while retaining the free heel.

Competition

Telemark racing is governed by the International Ski and Snowboard Federation (FIS), which runs a Telemark World Cup circuit. Races combine giant slalom gates with features such as a jump, a banked 360-degree berm (the "loom"), and a cross-country skating section, demanding both alpine speed and Nordic endurance. The discipline has repeatedly campaigned for inclusion in the Winter Olympic Games.
