Hague Agreement

The Hague Agreement concerning the International Deposit of Industrial Designs, also known as the Hague System, provides a mechanism for registering an industrial design in several countries by means of a single application, filed in one language and with one set of fees. The system is administered by the International Bureau of the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) in Geneva, Switzerland.

History

The Hague Agreement was first adopted in 1925 and entered into force in 1928. It has been revised several times, notably by the London Act (1934), the Hague Act (1960), and the Geneva Act (1999). The Geneva Act, which entered into force in 2003, modernized the system, allowing intergovernmental organizations such as the European Union to join and making the system compatible with examining offices. The United States and Japan acceded in 2015, substantially expanding the system's reach.

Operation

An applicant files a single international application with WIPO, directly or through a national office, designating the contracting parties in which protection is sought. A single application may cover up to 100 designs in the same class of the Locarno Classification. WIPO examines the application for formalities and publishes the registration; each designated office may then refuse protection on substantive grounds within a prescribed period, typically six or twelve months. Registered designs are generally protected for an initial five-year term, renewable in five-year periods up to a minimum total of fifteen years under the Geneva Act.
