West German student movement

The West German student movement was a series of protests by left-wing students in the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) during the 1960s, reaching its peak in 1967–68. Inspired by the American civil rights movement, opposition to the Vietnam War, and New Left theory, the movement demanded university reform, denounced the alleged authoritarian structures and Nazi-era continuities of the West German state, and called for radical democratization of society.

Background and course

The movement's principal organization was the Sozialistischer Deutscher Studentenbund (SDS), the former student wing of the SPD, whose best-known spokesman was Rudi Dutschke. A turning point came on 2 June 1967, when the student Benno Ohnesorg was shot dead by a police officer during a demonstration in West Berlin against the visiting Shah of Iran. His death radicalized the movement and triggered protests across the country.

On 11 April 1968 Dutschke was shot and seriously wounded by Josef Bachmann, an attack many students blamed on incitement by the Springer press, especially Bild. The ensuing "Easter riots" were among the most violent disturbances in the Federal Republic's history up to that point. The passage of the Emergency Acts (Notstandsgesetze) in May 1968, despite mass protests by the extra-parliamentary opposition (APO), marked the movement's climax and the beginning of its fragmentation.

Legacy

After 1969 the movement splintered into orthodox communist "K-Gruppen", a spontaneous countercultural scene, and — at its violent fringe — armed groups such as the Red Army Faction. Many participants later took the "long march through the institutions"; figures from the movement, including Joschka Fischer, went on to senior roles in German politics, notably in the Green Party. The movement is credited with liberalizing West German society, universities, and attitudes toward the Nazi past.
