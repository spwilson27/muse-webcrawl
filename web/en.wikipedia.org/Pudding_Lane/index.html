Pudding Lane is a minor street in the City of London, England, running from Eastcheap to Monument Street. It is famous as the location of Thomas Farriner's bakery, where the Great Fire of London is generally understood to have started in the early hours of Sunday, 2 September 1666. The fire destroyed more than 80 per cent of the walled city over the following four days.

Name and early history

The lane's name does not derive from desserts: "puddings" was an old term for offal, and the lane was a route by which butchers from Eastcheap carried animal entrails down to Thames-side barges for disposal. In the middle ages the lane was also known for the trades concentrated there. At the time of the fire it was a narrow street of timber-framed, jettied houses near the northern end of London Bridge—conditions that let the flames spread rapidly between overhanging upper storeys.

The Great Fire

Shortly after midnight, a fire broke out at Farriner's bakery (the premises of the King's baker). Farriner maintained that he had raked out the ovens' embers before bed; the blaze, perhaps from a stray spark, ignited the house and, aided by a strong east wind and a summer drought, spread through the warehouses of Thames Street. Diarist Samuel Pepys recorded climbing the Tower of London to watch the fire and burying his wine and Parmesan cheese in his garden. A plaque on the wall of a building in Pudding Lane today marks the traditionally accepted site of the fire's origin.

Commemoration

Nearby stands the Monument, a Doric column designed by Sir Christopher Wren and Robert Hooke, completed in 1677. At 202 feet (61.5 m) tall—equal to its distance from the bakery site—it commemorates the fire and the rebuilding of the city that followed. In the aftermath, anti-Catholic suspicion fixed blame on foreigners; an inscription blaming "Popish frenzy", added to the Monument, was only removed in the 19th century.
