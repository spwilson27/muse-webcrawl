Sun-synchronous orbit

A Sun-synchronous orbit (SSO), also called a heliosynchronous orbit, is a nearly polar orbit around a planet in which the satellite passes over any given point of the planet's surface at the same local mean solar time. The orbit's plane precesses about one degree per day eastward, matching Earth's mean orbital motion around the Sun, so the satellite's orbital plane keeps a constant angle to sunlight.

Principle

The precession arises from Earth's equatorial bulge (the J2 perturbation), which exerts a torque on the orbit. By choosing an altitude and a slightly retrograde inclination greater than 90°, the nodal precession can be made exactly equal to 360° per year — the rate at which the Earth–Sun direction rotates. The satellite then crosses the equator at the same local time on every pass, giving consistent lighting conditions for imaging.

Applications

Consistent illumination makes SSO ideal for Earth-observing satellites, whose images can be compared over time: weather satellites, environmental monitors such as Landsat and the Copernicus Sentinels, and reconnaissance satellites use such orbits. Particular crossing times define variants, such as dawn–dusk orbits, where the satellite rides the terminator and enjoys near-constant sunlight on its solar panels.

Technical details

For circular orbits around Earth, the required inclination increases with altitude; practical Sun-synchronous orbits lie between about 400 and 1,000 km altitude, with orbital periods around 100 minutes and roughly 14–15 orbits per day. Ground tracks repeat after a designed number of days for many missions.
