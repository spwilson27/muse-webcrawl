Miller–Urey experiment

The Miller–Urey experiment (or Miller experiment) was a landmark chemical experiment conducted in 1952 by Stanley Miller under the supervision of Harold Urey at the University of Chicago. It tested the hypothesis that conditions on the early Earth favoured chemical reactions that synthesised organic compounds — including amino acids — from inorganic precursors.

The experiment

Miller sealed methane, ammonia, hydrogen, and water vapour in a sterile glass apparatus and subjected the circulating gases to continuous electrical sparks, simulating lightning. After about a week, roughly 10–15% of the carbon had formed organic compounds; analysis revealed several amino acids, the building blocks of proteins.

Significance

Published in 1953, the experiment provided the first experimental support for the Oparin–Haldane "primordial soup" hypothesis of the origin of life. Later re-analyses of Miller's archived samples, including experiments using volcanic gases, found an even wider diversity of amino acids. Although the composition of the early atmosphere is now thought to have differed from the strongly reducing mixture Miller used, follow-up experiments with more realistic gas mixtures have still produced organic building blocks.
