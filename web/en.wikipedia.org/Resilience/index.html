Resilience

This article is about the general concept. For psychological resilience, see Psychological resilience. For ecological resilience, see Ecological resilience.

Resilience is the capacity of a system, community, or individual to withstand, adapt to, and recover from disturbance or adversity. The term is used across many fields, including psychology, ecology, materials science, engineering, and disaster management, with related but distinct meanings in each.

Psychological resilience

In psychology, resilience refers to the process and outcome of successfully adapting to difficult or challenging life experiences, especially through mental, emotional, and behavioral flexibility. Research associated with developmental psychologists such as Emmy Werner and Ann Masten — who called resilience "ordinary magic" — shows that it arises from ordinary adaptive systems such as supportive relationships, problem-solving skills, and self-regulation rather than rare personal qualities.

Ecological resilience

In ecology, resilience is the capacity of an ecosystem to respond to a perturbation or disturbance by resisting damage and recovering quickly. The concept was formalised by C. S. Holling in 1973, distinguishing it from stability: a resilient system may fluctuate widely yet persist, whereas crossing a threshold can shift the system into a different regime, as when a lake becomes eutrophic.

Engineering and materials

In materials science, resilience is the ability of a material to absorb energy when deformed elastically and to return it when unloaded; the modulus of resilience is measured up to the yield point. In engineering and infrastructure planning, resilience denotes the ability of systems — power grids, transport networks, supply chains — to anticipate, absorb, and rapidly recover from disruptions, and is closely related to climate change adaptation and disaster risk reduction.
