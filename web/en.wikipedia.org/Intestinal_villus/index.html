Intestinal villus

Intestinal villus

LocationMucosa of the small intestine
SizeAbout 0.5–1.6 mm long; roughly 10–40 per mm²
StructureCore of lamina propria with a capillary network and a central lacteal, covered by enterocytes with microvilli
FunctionGreatly increase surface area for absorption of nutrients
RenewalEpithelium replaced every few days from stem cells in the crypts of Lieberkühn

Intestinal villi (singular: villus) are small, finger-like projections that extend into the lumen of the small intestine. Together with the circular folds and the microvilli of the absorptive cells, they multiply the internal surface area of the gut enormously, providing the large area required for efficient absorption of digested nutrients into the blood and lymph.

Each villus is covered by absorptive enterocytes topped with a brush border of microvilli.

Structure

A villus consists of a core of connective tissue (the lamina propria) containing a network of blood capillaries and a single blind-ending lymphatic vessel called a lacteal. The surface is covered by a single layer of columnar epithelial cells, mostly absorptive enterocytes whose apical membranes bear dense microvilli — the "brush border" carrying digestive enzymes such as lactase and sucrase — interspersed with mucus-secreting goblet cells and hormone-secreting enteroendocrine cells. Between the villi lie the crypts of Lieberkühn, whose stem cells continually renew the epithelium; cells migrate from crypt to villus tip over a few days and are shed.

Function

Absorbed monosaccharides, amino acids, and small peptides pass into the capillary blood and travel via the hepatic portal vein to the liver. Long-chain fatty acids and monoglycerides are reassembled into triglycerides inside enterocytes, packaged into chylomicrons, and discharged into the lacteal, reaching the blood via the lymphatic system and thoracic duct. Villi are tallest and most numerous in the jejunum, the principal site of absorption, and shorter and sparser toward the ileum; they are absent from the large intestine.

Clinical significance

Flattening or destruction of villi — villous atrophy — drastically reduces absorptive area and causes malabsorption. It is the characteristic lesion of coeliac disease, in which an immune reaction to gluten damages the mucosa, and also occurs in tropical sprue and some infections. Diagnosis classically rests on small-bowel biopsy graded by the Marsh classification. Recovery on a gluten-free diet restores villus architecture in coeliac disease.
