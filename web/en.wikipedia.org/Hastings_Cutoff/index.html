Hastings Cutoff

The Hastings Cutoff was an alternative route for westward emigrants to the United States, proposed by Lansford Hastings in his 1845 Emigrants' Guide to Oregon and California. The route branched off the Oregon Trail at Fort Bridgely, crossed the Wasatch Mountains and the Great Salt Lake Desert in present-day Utah, and rejoined the California Trail near the Humboldt River. It is best known for its role in the Donner Party disaster of 1846–47.

The route

Hastings promoted the cutoff as a shortcut saving several hundred miles, although he had travelled only parts of it and never with wagons. The route required crossing the rugged Wasatch Range via Weber Canyon and then an roughly 80-mile waterless drive across the salt flats west of the Great Salt Lake — far harder than Hastings represented.

The Donner Party

In 1846 the Donner–Reed Party chose the cutoff, losing weeks hacking a road through the Wasatch and losing cattle and wagons in the desert crossing. The delays left them late crossing the Sierra Nevada, where early snows trapped them for the winter; many died of starvation. Other 1846 parties that took the cutoff also suffered, and the route was largely abandoned by wagon traffic thereafter.

Legacy

Parts of the cutoff were later followed by the first transcontinental telegraph line, the Lincoln Highway and Interstate 80. The Hastings Cutoff remains a cautionary tale in the history of the American West about unreliable guidebooks and the costs of advertised shortcuts.
