The Torridonian is the informal name for a sequence of mostly fluvial red sandstones of Neoproterozoic age that outcrops in the Northwest Highlands of Scotland, named for Loch Torridon. The Torridonian sandstone forms some of Scotland's most distinctive mountains — including Liathach, Beinn Eighe, Beinn Alligin and Slioch — whose terraced, fortress-like profiles reflect the thick, horizontally bedded strata resting unconformably on ancient Lewisian gneiss.

Stratigraphy and deposition

The succession, up to about 7 km thick though nowhere complete, is divided into the Stoer Group (roughly 1,200 million years old) and the younger, more extensive Torridon Group (roughly 1,000–950 million years old), separated by an angular unconformity. The sediments were laid down by rivers and in lakes on the margin of the ancient continent Laurentia, in a rift setting, largely as braided-river sandstones and conglomerates with mudstone lake deposits. The Stoer Group includes the Stac Fada Member, an ejecta deposit from a meteorite impact about 1.2 billion years ago — among the oldest known impact deposits in Britain.

Fossils and significance

The lake shales of the Torridon Group preserve some of the oldest known non-marine eukaryotic fossils: microscopic organic-walled microfossils of algae and other protists around a billion years old, described in detail in the 2010s, which provide rare evidence of early freshwater ecosystems. The Torridonian is also famous in the history of geology: the unconformity at its base on the much older Lewisian gneiss, and the overlying Cambrian quartzites, featured in nineteenth-century debates over the structure of the Northwest Highlands.
