Monologue

A monologue is a speech presented by a single character, most often to express their thoughts aloud, or directly address the audience or another character. The term derives from the Greek monologos, 'speaking alone'.

Monologues are a staple of theatre, film, and television, and are also used in other forms such as stand-up comedy, slam poetry, and one-person shows.

In theatre

In drama, a monologue is an extended uninterrupted speech by one character in a play. It differs from a soliloquy in that a monologue is addressed to other characters or the audience, whereas a soliloquy is the character speaking to themselves, revealing inner thoughts. Hamlet's 'To be, or not to be' is usually classed as a soliloquy.

Entrance and exit monologues, audition monologues, and comic set pieces are all standard features of theatrical practice.

Dramatic and interior monologue

The dramatic monologue is a poetic form, perfected by Robert Browning in works such as 'My Last Duchess', in which a single speaker addresses a silent listener and reveals more about themselves than they intend.

The interior monologue is a narrative device in fiction that presents a character's inner thoughts, closely related to stream of consciousness writing, as used by James Joyce and Virginia Woolf.

In comedy

Stand-up comedy is traditionally built around a comic monologue, and late-night television hosts open their shows with one. Comic monologues also feature in variety traditions and in the work of performers such as Bob Newhart, whose one-sided telephone conversations are classic examples.
