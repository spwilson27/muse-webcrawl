Hashemites

The Hashemites (Arabic: الهاشميون, romanized: al-Hāshimiyyūn), also known as the House of Hashim, are the royal family of Jordan, which they have ruled since 1921. The family had ruled the Sharifate of Mecca since the 10th century, as Emir of Mecca, and supplied kings of the Hejaz, Syria, and Iraq during and after the First World War.

Origins

The Hashemites trace their ancestry to Hashim ibn Abd Manaf, the great-grandfather of the Islamic prophet Muhammad, and thus to the Quraysh tribe of Mecca. Members of the family, as Sharifs of Mecca, served as custodians and emirs of the holy city under various overlords, including the Abbasids, Fatimids, Mamluks, and Ottomans, from the 10th century until 1924.

The Arab Revolt and the post-war kingdoms

During the First World War, Sharif Hussein bin Ali, Emir of Mecca, launched the Arab Revolt (1916–1918) against the Ottoman Empire in alliance with Britain, following the McMahon–Hussein Correspondence, which the Arabs understood as promising Arab independence. Hussein's sons were installed as monarchs across the region: Faisal became King of Syria (briefly, in 1920) and then King of Iraq (1921–1933); Abdullah became Emir, and later King, of Transjordan (now Jordan); and Ali briefly succeeded his father as King of the Hejaz before it was conquered by Ibn Saud in 1925.

Later history

The Hashemite monarchy in Iraq ended when King Faisal II was killed in the 14 July 1958 revolution. The Jordanian branch endures: Abdullah I was assassinated in 1951, his son Talal reigned briefly, and Talal's son Hussein ruled from 1952 until his death in 1999. He was succeeded by his son Abdullah II, the current King of Jordan. The family also briefly formed the Arab Federation with Iraq in 1958, a union dissolved the same year. The Hashemites' descent from Muhammad and their historic custodianship of Mecca underpin the Jordanian monarchy's religious legitimacy, including its custodianship of Muslim and Christian holy sites in Jerusalem.
