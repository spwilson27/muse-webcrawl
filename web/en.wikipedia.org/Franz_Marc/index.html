Franz Moritz Wilhelm Marc (8 February 1880 – 4 March 1916) was a German printmaker and painter, one of the key figures of German Expressionism. With Wassily Kandinsky he founded Der Blaue Reiter (The Blue Rider) in 1911. His mature works are characterised by bright primary colours and stark, simplified images of animals, invested with deep spiritual meaning.

Life and work

Marc was born in Munich, the son of a landscape painter. He studied at the Academy of Fine Arts in Munich and spent formative periods in Paris, where he encountered Post-Impressionism and the work of Van Gogh and Gauguin. From 1910 he developed his distinctive language of fractured, radiant colour and developed a private colour symbolism: blue for spirituality and masculinity, yellow for feminine joy, red for the material world. Animals — horses, deer, foxes, cattle — became his vehicle for a vision of purity and renewal, culminating in works such as The Large Blue Horses (1911), The Tower of Blue Horses (1913) and the apocalyptic Fate of the Animals (1913).

Death and legacy

Marc volunteered for the German army in 1914 and was killed by shrapnel at the Battle of Verdun on 4 March 1916, aged 36. His work was later condemned by the Nazis as "degenerate"; The Tower of Blue Horses disappeared after 1945 and remains missing. Today Marc is regarded as one of the most important German artists of the twentieth century, and the Franz Marc Museum in Kochel am See houses a major collection of his work.
