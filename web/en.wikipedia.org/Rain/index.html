Rain is a form of precipitation in which liquid water droplets, formed by condensation of atmospheric water vapour, grow heavy enough to fall under gravity. Rain is a major component of the water cycle and is responsible for depositing most of the fresh water on Earth. It provides water for hydroelectric plants, crop irrigation, and drinking water supplies, and shapes landscapes through erosion. Most rain-producing clouds form as moist air rises and cools — along weather fronts, over mountains (orographic lift), or by convection on warm days.

Formation

Within a cloud, microscopic droplets form on condensation nuclei such as dust and salt particles. Droplets grow to raindrops mainly by two processes: collision and coalescence in warm clouds, and the Bergeron process in cold clouds, where ice crystals grow at the expense of supercooled droplets (because saturation vapour pressure is lower over ice) and then fall, melting into rain. Droplets of about 0.5 mm diameter or more count as rain; smaller ones are drizzle, while droplets that evaporate before reaching the ground produce virga. Falling drops are not teardrop-shaped: small drops are nearly spherical, and large ones flatten into a hamburger-bun shape before breaking up around 4–5 mm.

Types and measurement

Meteorologists classify rain by its generating mechanism — stratiform (steady, widespread rain from frontal systems), convective (intense, localised showers and thunderstorms), and orographic (forced ascent over terrain). Rainfall is measured as depth in millimetres by rain gauges, estimated over areas by weather radar, and classified by intensity from light (<2.5 mm/h) to violent (>50 mm/h). Exceptional events include the world record 1.83 m of rain in 24 hours at Cilaos, Réunion, during a tropical cyclone in 1952, and the extraordinary annual averages of Meghalaya, India — Cherrapunji and Mawsynram receive roughly 11–12 metres per year, the highest on Earth.

Effects

Rain sustains agriculture and ecosystems but in excess causes flooding, landslides, and disease; its absence brings drought. Pollutants washed from the air or dissolved in drops produce acid rain, damaging forests, lakes, and buildings. Humans have long attempted to modify rain, from rain dances to modern cloud seeding, with contested effectiveness. The smell after rain on dry earth — petrichor — comes from plant oils and geosmin, a compound produced by soil bacteria. Climate change is intensifying the water cycle: warmer air holds about 7% more moisture per degree Celsius, increasing heavy-rainfall extremes in most regions.

Cultural significance

Rain features in myth and religion worldwide — from the rain gods Tlaloc, Zeus, and Indra to the biblical Flood — and in art and literature as a symbol of renewal, melancholy, and fertility. Rain dances, rainmaking rituals, and prayers for rain attest to its existential importance for farming societies.
