AuthorAlfred, Lord Tennyson
Published9 December 1854, in The Examiner

SubjectCharge of the Light Brigade, Battle of Balaclava (25 October 1854)

"The Charge of the Light Brigade" is an 1854 narrative poem by Alfred, Lord Tennyson, the Poet Laureate, commemorating the British light cavalry charge against Russian forces during the Battle of Balaclava in the Crimean War. It is one of the most famous poems of the Victorian era.

Background

On 25 October 1854, owing to a misunderstood order from Lord Raglan, some 670 troopers of the Light Brigade under Lord Cardigan charged directly into a valley defended by Russian artillery on three sides — Tennyson's "valley of Death" — suffering heavy casualties (about 110 killed and 160 wounded) before withdrawing. William Howard Russell's dispatches in The Times reported that "someone had blundered"; Tennyson read the report and wrote the poem within minutes, it is said, publishing it on 9 December 1854.

The poem

The poem's galloping dactylic metre imitates hoofbeats. Its refrains — "Half a league, half a league, / Half a league onward", "Cannon to right of them, / Cannon to left of them, / Cannon in front of them" — and the lines "Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die" celebrate dutiful heroism while acknowledging the blunder: "Was there a man dismay'd? / Not tho' the soldier knew / Some one had blunder'd". The final stanza asks, "Honour the charge they made! / Honour the Light Brigade, / Noble six hundred!"

Reception and recordings

The poem became hugely popular; a thousand copies were printed as a broadside for soldiers in the Crimea. In 1890 Tennyson, aged 81, recorded himself reading it onto a wax cylinder for Thomas Edison's agent — one of the earliest surviving recordings of a poet reading his own work.
