Body worn video

Body worn video (BWV), commonly implemented as a body camera or body-worn camera (BWC), is a video recording system that is typically utilized by law enforcement to record their interactions with the public. The cameras are worn on the torso of the officer's uniform, generally on the chest or shoulder, and similar recorders are also used by military personnel, firefighters, healthcare workers, and journalists.

History

Early trials of body-worn cameras by police took place in the United Kingdom in the mid-2000s, including a Plymouth pilot in 2006. Adoption accelerated sharply in the United States after 2014, when public controversy over police use of force prompted federal funding for camera programs and departments across the country began deploying them at scale.

Equipment and policies

Modern units record video and audio, buffer a short period of video before activation, tag recordings with time and location metadata, and upload footage to evidence management systems when docked. Departments set policies on when cameras must be activated, how long footage is retained, and when recordings may be released to the public or used in court.

Research and debate

Studies of the effects of body cameras have produced mixed results: some early randomized trials found large reductions in complaints and use of force, while later multi-site research found smaller or negligible average effects. Supporters argue the cameras improve accountability, evidence quality, and public trust; critics raise concerns about privacy, the discretion officers retain over activation, selective release of footage, and cost.
