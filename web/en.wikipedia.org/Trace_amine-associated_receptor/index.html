Trace amine-associated receptors (TAARs), sometimes called trace amine receptors, are a family of G protein-coupled receptors discovered in 2001 that are activated by trace amines — endogenous compounds such as β-phenethylamine, tyramine, octopamine and tryptamine that occur in the nervous system at much lower concentrations than the classical monoamine neurotransmitters.

Members and distribution

Humans have nine TAAR genes (TAAR1–TAAR9), several of which are pseudogenes. TAAR1 is expressed in the brain, gut, pancreas and immune cells, and responds to a broad range of trace amines and amphetamine-like compounds. Most other TAARs are expressed in the olfactory epithelium, where they function as odorant receptors detecting volatile amines, including those in urine and decay odours.

Functions

TAAR1 acts as a modulator of monoaminergic neurotransmission: it interacts with dopamine transporter and dopamine D2 receptor signalling and is activated by amphetamine and methamphetamine. TAAR1 agonists have therefore been investigated as treatments for schizophrenia, addiction and metabolic disorders; the TAAR1 agonist ulotaront was developed for schizophrenia. In the olfactory system, TAAR-mediated detection of amines guides innate behaviours such as predator avoidance in rodents.
