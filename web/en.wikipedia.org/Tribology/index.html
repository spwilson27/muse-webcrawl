Tribology

Tribology is the science and engineering of understanding friction, lubrication and wear between interacting surfaces in relative motion. The word comes from the Greek tribos (rubbing). The field spans physics, chemistry, materials science and mechanical engineering, and it was formally named in a 1966 British report by H. Peter Jost, which estimated large economic savings from applying tribological knowledge.

Friction

Friction between dry surfaces is often summarised by the empirical laws associated with Leonardo da Vinci and later Guillaume Amontons and Charles-Augustin de Coulomb: friction force is proportional to normal load and roughly independent of apparent contact area and sliding velocity. Modern tribology explains these laws through contact between microscopic asperities, adhesion and deformation.

Lubrication

Lubrication regimes range from boundary lubrication, where surface films only a few molecules thick carry the load, through mixed lubrication to full hydrodynamic and elastohydrodynamic lubrication, in which a pressurised fluid film completely separates the surfaces. The Stribeck curve summarises how friction varies with lubricant viscosity, speed and load. Lubricants include oils, greases and solid films such as molybdenum disulfide and graphite.

Wear

Wear mechanisms include adhesive, abrasive, fatigue and corrosive wear. Bearings, gears, engines, artificial joints and hard-disk drives are major application areas; biotribology studies natural joints and contact lenses, while nanotribology examines friction at atomic scales.
