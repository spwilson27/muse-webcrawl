Eternal flame

Eternal flame
PurposeMemorial, religious, or symbolic
FuelNatural gas, propane, or oil
Notable examplesJFK eternal flame, Tomb of the Unknown Soldier (Paris), Hiroshima Peace Park

An eternal flame is a flame, lamp, or torch that burns continuously for an indefinite period. Most eternal flames are ignited and tended intentionally, but some are natural phenomena caused by gas leaks, peat fires, and coal seam fires, which can burn for years or even centuries.

Religious and cultural significance

Long-burning flames have held religious significance since antiquity. The eternal flame in the Temple in Jerusalem, the fire temples of Zoroastrianism — some of which, such as the Atash Behram in Yazd, Iran, are said to have burned for over a thousand years — and the sacred hearth fires of Greek and Roman religion all treated an unextinguished flame as a symbol of divine presence, purity, and continuity.

Memorials

In the modern era the eternal flame is most commonly a memorial feature. The flame beneath the Arc de Triomphe in Paris, lit in 1923 over the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, is rekindled every evening. The eternal flame at the John F. Kennedy grave site in Arlington National Cemetery, lit by Jacqueline Kennedy in 1963, became one of the most widely copied memorial designs. Eternal flames also burn at the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park, at Yad Vashem, and at countless war memorials worldwide, as well as at the Martin Luther King Jr. Center in Atlanta and the 9/11 memorial sites.

Natural eternal flames

Natural gas seeps can sustain flames for millennia. The Yanar Dag "burning mountain" in Azerbaijan, the Chimaera flames in Turkey (associated with the myth of the Chimera), and the Eternal Flame Falls in New York State, where a small gas seep burns behind a waterfall, are well-known examples. Underground coal fires, such as the Burning Mountain in Australia, may burn for thousands of years without any human ignition.
