Direct action

Direct action
TypePolitical and economic activism
Term popularisedEarly 20th century; Voltairine de Cleyre's essay "Direct Action" (1912)
ExamplesStrikes, boycotts, sit-ins, occupations, sabotage, blockades
Contrasted withElectoral politics, lobbying, litigation

Direct action is a form of activism in which participants use their own power to achieve goals directly, rather than appealing to authorities or intermediaries to act on their behalf. It originated as a theory of practice within the labour movement and anarchism — where the strike, and ultimately the general strike, was its paradigmatic form — and has since been used by movements of many kinds, including the civil rights, environmental, and anti-globalization movements.

Forms

Direct action ranges from nonviolent tactics — strikes, boycotts, sit-ins, occupations, road and port blockades, work-to-rule, rent strikes — to property damage and sabotage; some traditions also include armed resistance. Nonviolent direct action overlaps with civil disobedience, though civil disobedience classically involves openly breaking a law to protest it, whereas direct action may be lawful (a strike) or aimed at physically achieving the goal itself (occupying a building to house the homeless, blockading a logging road).

History

The phrase was used by the Industrial Workers of the World and French syndicalists in the first decade of the twentieth century; Voltairine de Cleyre's 1912 essay defended it broadly as the action of individuals and groups acting for themselves. Martin Luther King Jr. adopted the term in the "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (1963), describing nonviolent direct action as a means to create crisis and tension that forces negotiation. From the 1990s, movements such as Earth First!, Reclaim the Streets, the global justice movement, and later Extinction Rebellion and climate camps have used mass direct action prominently.

Theory and debate

Anarchist and syndicalist theory values direct action as prefigurative — embodying the self-organisation it seeks — and as building workers' and communities' own power. Debates concern its effectiveness relative to institutional politics, the ethics of property damage and violence, and the risks of repression and public backlash.
