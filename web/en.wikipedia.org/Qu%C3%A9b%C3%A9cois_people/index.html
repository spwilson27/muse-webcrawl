Québécois people

The Québécois (French: Québécois, feminine Québécoise) are the people of Quebec, Canada. In its narrower ethnic sense, the term refers to the French-speaking descendants of the settlers of New France, who form the large majority of Quebec's population; in a broader civic sense it denotes all residents of Quebec regardless of origin. Quebec nationalism, the Quiet Revolution, and two sovereignty referendums (1980 and 1995) have shaped a distinct Québécois identity within Canada.

Origins

The ethnic Québécois descend mainly from the roughly 10,000 French colonists who settled the St. Lawrence valley between 1608 and the British conquest of 1763, supplemented by the filles du roi and soldiers of the Carignan-Salières regiment. Isolated after the conquest and sustained by high birth rates — the so-called "revenge of the cradle" — this founding population grew into several million descendants, though extensive emigration carried many to New England and Ontario in the 19th and early 20th centuries.

Modern identity

Until the 1960s, French-Canadian identity centred on the Catholic Church, the French language, and rural parish life under the ideology of survivance. The Quiet Revolution secularised Quebec society, built a modern provincial state, and shifted identification from "French Canadian" to the territorial "Québécois." Language laws such as the Charter of the French Language (1977) made French the common public language, and immigrants to Quebec are increasingly counted within the civic definition of the Québécois nation, which the Canadian House of Commons recognised in 2006 as "a nation within a united Canada."
