Vulcan (mythology)

Vulcan (Latin: Volcānus or Vulcānus) is the Roman god of fire, including the destructive fire of volcanoes, and of metalworking and the forge. He is the Roman counterpart of the Greek god Hephaestus. Vulcan was among the most ancient gods of the Roman pantheon; his cult, overseen by the flamen Volcanalis, was one of the oldest in Rome, and his festival, the Volcanalia, was celebrated on 23 August, at the height of the summer heat when the risk of fire was greatest.

Mythology

In Roman accounts adapted from Greek myth, Vulcan was the son of Jupiter and Juno. Thrown from Olympus by his mother (or, in other versions, by Jupiter), he fell for a day and landed on the island of Lemnos, lamed by the fall—explaining his characteristic limp. As the divine smith, he forged Jupiter's thunderbolts, the arms of Aeneas (at the request of Venus, described in Book 8 of Virgil's Aeneid), and the chariot of the Sun. His workshops were said to lie beneath Mount Etna in Sicily and on the Lipari Islands, where the Cyclopes served as his assistants; volcanic eruptions were the visible signs of his labour.

Vulcan was married to Venus, goddess of love, whose infidelity with Mars was a popular subject of myth and art. In one famous episode, Vulcan trapped the lovers in a nearly invisible net of fine chains and displayed them to the laughter of the other gods.

Cult and worship

Vulcan's shrine in Rome, the Volcanal, stood in the Roman Forum area and was among the city's most ancient sacred places. Because of his association with destructive fire, his temples were often built outside city walls. During the Volcanalia, fish and small animals were thrown into fires as offerings in place of human lives, and people began work by candlelight to use his gift of fire beneficially. After the Great Fire of Rome in 64 AD, the emperor Domitian built Vulcan a new altar and required annual offerings of a red calf and a red boar.

Legacy

The English word "volcano" derives from Vulcan's name, via the island of Vulcano in the Aeolian Islands, which the Romans believed to be his forge. Vulcan remains a common figure in Western art and literature, from Velázquez's Apollo in the Forge of Vulcan to the fictional planet Vulcan in Star Trek.
