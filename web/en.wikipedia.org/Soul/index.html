Soul

The Egyptian ba, a manifestation of the soul

The soul is the incorporeal essence of a living being, in many religious and philosophical traditions believed to constitute the true self, to be separable from the body, and often to survive bodily death. Concepts of the soul vary widely: some traditions regard it as unique to humans, others attribute souls to all living things, or even to rivers, mountains, and objects.

Ancient thought

Ancient Egyptian religion distinguished several aspects of the person, including the ka (vital essence) and ba (personality). In Greek philosophy, the psyche was the principle of life; Plato argued for an immortal, tripartite soul of reason, spirit, and appetite, while Aristotle, in De Anima, defined the soul as the form or first actuality of a living body, distinguishing nutritive, sensitive, and rational souls. Hindu thought centres on the ātman, the eternal self identified in Vedānta with Brahman, subject to saṃsāra (rebirth) until liberation (moksha); Buddhism, by contrast, teaches anattā, the doctrine of no permanent self.

Abrahamic traditions

In Judaism, Christianity, and Islam the soul (Hebrew nephesh, Greek psyche, Arabic nafs/rūḥ) is created by God and is the seat of the person. Christian theology debated the soul's origin and nature through theologians such as Augustine and Aquinas; mainstream teaching holds the soul immortal and subject to judgement after death. René Descartes' substance dualism — the soul as res cogitans, a thinking thing distinct from extended matter — framed the modern "mind–body problem".

Modern perspectives

Contemporary philosophy of mind and neuroscience generally explain mental life through the brain, and many thinkers reject a separable soul; others maintain dualist or non-reductive views. Belief in a soul remains widespread worldwide and continues to shape ethics, law, and practices surrounding death and personhood.
