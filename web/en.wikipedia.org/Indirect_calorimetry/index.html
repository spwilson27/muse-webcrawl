Indirect calorimetry

Indirect calorimetry
MeasuresOxygen consumption (VO₂), carbon dioxide production (VCO₂)
OutputsResting energy expenditure, respiratory quotient
UsesNutrition support, metabolic research, exercise physiology

Indirect calorimetry is a technique that measures energy expenditure by analysing respiratory gases. Because the oxidation of metabolic fuels consumes oxygen and produces carbon dioxide in predictable proportions, measuring oxygen uptake (VO₂) and carbon dioxide output (VCO₂) allows calculation of the energy a person or animal is expending, without directly measuring heat production as in direct calorimetry.

Method

The subject breathes through a mouthpiece, mask, or ventilated canopy hood connected to gas analysers and a flow meter. From VO₂ and VCO₂, energy expenditure is computed with equations such as the Weir equation (1949). The ratio VCO₂/VO₂, the respiratory quotient (RQ), indicates the fuel mix being oxidized: values near 0.7 indicate fat oxidation, while 1.0 indicates carbohydrate oxidation.

Applications

Clinicians use indirect calorimetry to determine resting energy expenditure in critically ill patients so that nutritional support can be tailored, avoiding both underfeeding and overfeeding. It is also used in sports science, obesity research, and the calibration of wearable activity monitors. Simplified devices such as handheld metabolic analysers and whole-room calorimeters are variants of the same principle.

Limitations

Accurate measurement requires steady-state conditions, leak-free circuits, and precise gas calibration. Results can be distorted by high inspired oxygen concentrations, air leaks around masks or ventilator circuits, and certain clinical states such as severe acidosis.
