Exciton

An exciton is a bound state of an electron and an electron hole which are attracted to each other by the electrostatic Coulomb force. It is an electrically neutral quasiparticle that can exist in insulators, semiconductors, and some liquids, and is regarded as an elementary excitation of condensed matter that can transport energy without transporting net electric charge.

The concept of an exciton was first proposed in 1931 by Yakov Frenkel, and later developed by Gregory Wannier and Nevill Mott. Excitons occur when a photon is absorbed by a semiconductor and promotes an electron from the valence band into the conduction band, leaving behind a positively charged hole; the two remain bound by Coulomb attraction.

Types

Two limiting forms are distinguished. Frenkel excitons are small, with the electron and hole on the same or neighbouring unit cells; they occur in materials with small dielectric constants, such as alkali halide crystals and organic molecular crystals. Wannier–Mott excitons extend over many lattice spacings, occurring in semiconductors with large dielectric constants, where screening reduces the binding to a few millielectronvolts and the radius can reach several nanometres.

Properties and significance

Excitons have finite lifetimes, eventually recombining with emission of light or phonons; their optical signatures appear as absorption peaks just below the band gap. Because they are bosons with integer spin, excitons can in principle undergo Bose–Einstein condensation at sufficiently low temperatures and high densities. Excitons play a crucial role in the operation of organic solar cells, light-emitting diodes, and photosynthesis, where energy transfer between pigment molecules proceeds via exciton migration. At interfaces between materials, spatially separated charge-transfer excitons and bound exciplexes can form, and two excitons may bind into a biexciton.
