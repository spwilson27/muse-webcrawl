White dwarf

A white dwarf is a stellar core remnant composed mostly of electron-degenerate matter. A white dwarf is very dense: its mass is comparable to the Sun's, while its volume is comparable to Earth's. A white dwarf's low luminosity comes from the emission of residual thermal energy; no fusion takes place in a white dwarf. The nearest known white dwarf is Sirius B, the companion of the bright star Sirius.

Formation and evolution

When a main-sequence star of low or medium mass (less than roughly eight solar masses) exhausts its nuclear fuel, it sheds its outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving behind the hot, inert core — a white dwarf composed mainly of carbon and oxygen. Lacking any ongoing fusion, the star is supported against gravitational collapse solely by electron degeneracy pressure. Over billions of years a white dwarf radiates away its stored heat and cools, eventually becoming a cold, dark "black dwarf" — though the universe is not yet old enough for any black dwarfs to exist.

Physics

Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar showed in 1930 that electron degeneracy pressure can support a white dwarf only up to about 1.44 solar masses — the Chandrasekhar limit. Above this limit a white dwarf in a binary system that accretes enough matter (or merges with another white dwarf) can explode as a Type Ia supernova, an event of remarkably uniform brightness used as a standard candle for measuring cosmic distances.

Discovery

The first white dwarf discovered was 40 Eridani B, in a triple system observed by William Herschel in 1783. Sirius B, inferred from the wobble of Sirius by Friedrich Bessel and photographed spectroscopically in the early 20th century, provided early proof of such objects' extreme density. White dwarfs are classified by their spectra, most commonly DA (hydrogen-rich atmospheres).
