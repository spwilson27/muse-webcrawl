Shakuhachi

A shakuhachi, the Japanese end-blown bamboo flute.

The shakuhachi (尺八) is a Japanese longitudinal end-blown bamboo flute. It was originally introduced from China into Japan in the 7th or 8th century and was later used by monks of the Fuke school of Zen Buddhism in the practice of suizen ("blowing meditation"). The instrument traditionally is made of the root end of madake bamboo and has four finger holes on the front and one thumb hole on the back.

The name shakuhachi derives from its standard length of one shaku (about 30.3 cm) and eight sun—1.8 shaku in total, roughly 54.5 cm—tuned to D. Instruments are made in many lengths, each length still generically called a shakuhachi followed by its size in sun. Because it is an end-blown flute without a fipple, the player shapes the embouchure directly against the sharpened rim (the utaguchi), allowing great control over pitch and timbre, including the bent notes (meri and kari) essential to its repertoire.

History

During the Edo period, the Fuke sect's komusō monks—recognisable by the woven baskets (tengai) worn over their heads—played the shakuhachi as a spiritual discipline and were granted special travel privileges by the Tokugawa shogunate, which some rōnin used for espionage. After the sect was abolished in 1871 during the Meiji Restoration, shakuhachi playing was briefly banned before being re-established as a musical art.

Repertoire and modern use

The solo honkyoku pieces of the Fuke tradition form the spiritual core of the repertoire; the instrument also features in the sankyoku ensemble with koto and shamisen, and in min'yō folk music. Since the 20th century the shakuhachi has been adopted internationally in jazz, film scores, new-age, and contemporary classical music.
