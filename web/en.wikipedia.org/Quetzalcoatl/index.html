Quetzalcoatl

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Quetzalcoatl (Nahuatl: Quetzalcōātl, "feathered serpent") is a deity in Aztec and wider Mesoamerican culture and mythology. The name combines quetzalli, the brilliant tail feather of the resplendent quetzal, with cōātl, "serpent". Worship of a feathered serpent is documented across Mesoamerica from the Olmec era onward, appearing prominently at Teotihuacan, among the Toltecs at Tula, and at the Maya city of Chichen Itza as Kukulkan.

Mythology

In Aztec tradition Quetzalcoatl is a creator god associated with wind (in his aspect as Ehecatl), the planet Venus, learning, and the priesthood. Myths tell how he descended to the underworld Mictlan to retrieve the bones of past humanity and restore them with his own blood, creating the people of the current era, and how he gave humans maize. The legendary Toltec priest-king Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl, exiled from Tula according to later accounts, became entangled with his cult; the popular story that the Aztec emperor Moctezuma II believed Cortés to be the returning Quetzalcoatl is now regarded by most historians as a post-conquest invention.

Legacy

The feathered serpent remains one of the most recognisable symbols of pre-Columbian Mexico: the pterosaur Quetzalcoatlus is named after him, the image appears in Mexican art and national iconography, and his pyramid at Teotihuacan — the Temple of the Feathered Serpent — is famous for its carved serpent heads.
