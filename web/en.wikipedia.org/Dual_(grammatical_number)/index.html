Dual (grammatical number)

This article is about the grammatical category. For other uses, see Dual (disambiguation).

Grammatical number systems across languages

The dual is a grammatical number that some languages use in addition to singular and plural. When a noun or pronoun appears in dual form, it is interpreted as referring to precisely two of the things named. The dual is distinct from the plural, which refers to two or more, and from trial and paucal numbers found in some languages.

Distribution

The dual was present in Proto-Indo-European and is preserved in several modern languages, including Arabic (Standard Arabic and many dialects), Slovenian, Sorbian, Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Sanskrit historically, and Ancient Greek. It is also found in many Semitic, Eskimo–Aleut, Austronesian and Australian Aboriginal languages. In Slovenian, the dual is used consistently for all nouns, adjectives and verbs referring to two entities, and native speakers regard its loss in most other Slavic languages as notable.

Historical development

In Old English, personal pronouns had distinct dual forms — wit "we two" and git "you two" — which were lost by the Middle English period. Old Norse likewise retained dual pronouns. In the history of Greek, the dual was already fading in Classical Attic and disappeared entirely in Koine. Lithuanian dialects preserve dual verb forms, though standard Lithuanian has largely lost them.

Expression

Languages mark the dual in different ways: through special noun inflections, dedicated pronouns, verb agreement, or combinations of these. In Arabic, nouns take the suffixes -ān (nominative) and -ayn (accusative/genitive). In Inuktitut and Yupik, dual endings are obligatory. Some languages, such as Hebrew, retain only fossilized dual forms for naturally paired objects like eyes, hands and certain units of time.
