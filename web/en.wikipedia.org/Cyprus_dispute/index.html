Cyprus dispute

The Cyprus dispute, also known as the Cyprus conflict or Cyprus problem, is an ongoing dispute between Greek Cypriots and Turkish Cypriots over the island of Cyprus in the eastern Mediterranean. It has divided the island since 1974 and remains one of the longest-running unresolved conflicts in international diplomacy.

Background

Cyprus gained independence from Britain in 1960 under a constitution sharing power between the Greek Cypriot majority and the Turkish Cypriot minority, with Greece, Turkey and the United Kingdom as guarantor powers. Intercommunal violence in 1963–64 led Turkish Cypriots to withdraw from government, and United Nations peacekeepers were deployed in 1964.

The 1974 crisis

In July 1974 the Greek military junta backed a coup in Cyprus aimed at enosis, union with Greece. Turkey responded with a military intervention, citing its rights as a guarantor power, and Turkish forces occupied the northern third of the island. Large numbers of Greek Cypriots fled south and Turkish Cypriots moved north, leaving the island effectively partitioned along the Green Line.

Northern Cyprus

In 1983 the Turkish Cypriot administration unilaterally declared independence as the Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus. The declaration was condemned by the UN Security Council and is recognised only by Turkey. The internationally recognised Republic of Cyprus joined the European Union in 2004, with EU law suspended in the areas outside its effective control.

Peace efforts

Numerous rounds of UN-sponsored negotiations have sought a bizonal, bicommunal federation. The 2004 Annan Plan was approved by Turkish Cypriot voters but rejected by Greek Cypriot voters in simultaneous referendums. Talks collapsed at Crans-Montana in 2017 and no comprehensive settlement has been reached since.
