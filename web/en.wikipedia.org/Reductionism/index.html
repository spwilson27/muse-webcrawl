Reductionism

Reductionism is any of several related philosophical positions regarding the connections between phenomena, holding that a complex system is nothing but the sum of its parts, and that an account of it can therefore be reduced to accounts of its individual constituents. Reductionist thinking underlies much of modern science: chemistry is explained in terms of physics, biology in terms of chemistry, and psychology, in some views, in terms of neuroscience.

Levels of organisation that reductionism seeks to connect.

Types

Ontological reductionism holds that higher-level entities are nothing over and above lower-level ones. Methodological reductionism is the strategy of studying systems by breaking them into parts. Theory reductionism concerns the relation between scientific theories, as when thermodynamics was reduced to statistical mechanics. Ernest Nagel's model of inter-theoretic reduction required bridge laws connecting the vocabularies of the two theories.

In science

Reductionism has driven major successes: the reduction of genetics to molecular biology, and of much of chemistry to quantum mechanics. Critics note that it may fail for phenomena exhibiting emergence, where system-level behaviour — such as temperature, life, or consciousness — is not straightforwardly predictable from parts. Biologists such as Ernst Mayr distinguished reductionism as a research strategy from the claim that organisms are "nothing but" molecules.

Criticism

Anti-reductionists argue that reduction can lose explanatory power, that multiple realizability (the same mental state realised by different physical states) blocks type-reduction in psychology, and that greedy reductionism — a term coined by Daniel Dennett for overreaching reduction — skips explanatory levels. Holism and systems biology are often presented as counterweights, though many philosophers treat the positions as complementary.
