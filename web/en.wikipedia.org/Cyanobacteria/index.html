Cyanobacteria

Cyanobacteria are a phylum of gram-negative bacteria that obtain energy via photosynthesis. Formerly called "blue-green algae," they are among the oldest known life forms: their oxygenic photosynthesis is credited with the Great Oxidation Event about 2.4 billion years ago, which transformed Earth's atmosphere. Chloroplasts in plants and algae evolved from an ancient cyanobacterium captured by endosymbiosis.

Morphology and ecology

Cyanobacteria occur as single cells, colonies, and filaments. Some filamentous forms differentiate specialized cells: heterocysts that fix atmospheric nitrogen, and akinetes (resting spores). They inhabit oceans, freshwater, soils, and extreme environments; marine genera such as Prochlorococcus and Synechococcus contribute a major share of oceanic primary production.

Blooms and toxins

Nutrient enrichment of lakes and coastal waters can trigger harmful algal blooms of cyanobacteria such as Microcystis, some of which produce cyanotoxins (microcystins, anatoxins) poisonous to animals and humans. Other species, notably Spirulina (Arthrospira), are cultivated as food, and nitrogen-fixing species are used as biofertilizer in rice paddies.
