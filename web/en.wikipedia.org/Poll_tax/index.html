Poll tax

A poll tax, also known as a head tax or capitation, is a tax levied as a fixed sum on every liable individual, regardless of income or resources. Because the amount is the same for rich and poor, poll taxes are highly regressive, and they have repeatedly provoked political conflict, from medieval revolts to modern controversies over their use as a condition of voting.

Historical examples

England imposed poll taxes in 1377, 1379 and 1380 to finance the Hundred Years' War; the third, levied at a shilling a head, helped trigger the Peasants' Revolt of 1381. Further English poll taxes were levied in the seventeenth century. The Ottoman jizya, a per-capita tax on non-Muslim subjects, functioned for centuries. In colonial and apartheid South Africa, hut and poll taxes were used to compel Africans into wage labour, and a poll tax on Indians in Natal helped provoke Gandhi's early campaigns.

Voting and disenfranchisement

In the United States, several Southern states after Reconstruction made payment of a poll tax a prerequisite for voting, a measure (often cumulative and combined with literacy tests) that disenfranchised Black voters and many poor whites. The Twenty-fourth Amendment (1964) banned poll taxes in federal elections, and the Supreme Court in Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections (1966) struck them down in state elections as well.

Community Charge

In the United Kingdom, the Community Charge introduced by Margaret Thatcher's government in Scotland in 1989 and in England and Wales in 1990—a flat per-adult local tax dubbed the "poll tax"—provoked mass non-payment and riots in London in March 1990, contributed to Thatcher's fall, and was replaced by the Council Tax in 1993.
