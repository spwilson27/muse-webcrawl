Gluten-free diet

A gluten-free diet (GFD) is a nutritional plan that strictly excludes gluten — a mixture of prolamin proteins found in wheat and in related grains such as barley and rye, and in hybrids such as triticale. A gluten-free diet includes naturally gluten-free foods such as most meats, fish, fruits, vegetables, rice and potatoes, as well as specially manufactured substitutes for bread, pasta and baked goods.

Medical indications

The gluten-free diet is the only accepted treatment for coeliac disease, an autoimmune disorder in which gluten triggers damage to the small intestine. It is also used in the management of dermatitis herpetiformis, wheat allergy, and gluten ataxia, and is often tried by people reporting non-coeliac gluten sensitivity, a condition whose existence and mechanism remain debated. For people with coeliac disease, even small traces of gluten — through cross-contamination — can provoke intestinal injury, so the diet must be followed strictly and for life.

Regulation and labelling

In many jurisdictions, foods labelled "gluten-free" must contain less than 20 parts per million of gluten, a threshold set by the Codex Alimentarius and adopted by regulators including the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Union. Oats are naturally gluten-free but are frequently contaminated with wheat during production, so only certified gluten-free oats are considered safe for people with coeliac disease.

Popularity and criticism

From the early 2010s the diet became a popular health trend among people without a medical need to avoid gluten, with surveys suggesting that a substantial share of consumers in some countries bought gluten-free products. Dietitians caution that commercial gluten-free products are often lower in fibre and micronutrients and higher in sugar and fat than the foods they replace, and that self-diagnosing gluten intolerance may delay the diagnosis of coeliac disease.
