The Joint Chiefs of Staff (JCS) is the body of the most senior uniformed leaders within the United States Department of Defense, which advises the president of the United States, the secretary of defense, the Homeland Security Council and the National Security Council on military matters. The composition of the Joint Chiefs of Staff is defined by statute and consists of a chairman (CJCS), a vice chairman (VJCS), the chiefs of the Army, Marine Corps, Navy and Air Force, the chief of Space Operations, and the chief of the National Guard Bureau.

History

The JCS originated in the Second World War, when President Franklin D. Roosevelt established an American counterpart to the British Chiefs of Staff Committee to coordinate strategy with the Combined Chiefs of Staff; its first members included Admiral William D. Leahy, who served as chief of staff to the commander in chief. The National Security Act of 1947 gave the body a statutory basis, and amendments in 1949 created the office of chairman and formally established the Joint Chiefs of Staff. General Omar Bradley became the first chairman in August 1949.

Roles and responsibilities

Following the Goldwater–Nichols Department of Defense Reorganization Act of 1986, the chairman is the principal military adviser to the president and secretary of defense, but operational command of combat forces runs from the president through the secretary of defense to the commanders of the combatant commands — the Joint Chiefs as a body have no executive authority over troops. The individual service chiefs retain responsibility for organising, training and equipping their services. The Joint Staff, headquartered at the Pentagon, supports the chairman.

Membership

Members are appointed by the president, by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, typically for staggered terms. The chairman is the highest-ranking and most senior military officer in the United States Armed Forces. The vice chairman is the second-highest-ranking officer. The commandant of the Coast Guard, an armed service under the Department of Homeland Security except when operating as part of the Navy, is not a statutory member but is frequently invited to attend meetings.
