Heme (American English), or haem (British English), is a precursor to hemoglobin and a cofactor consisting of an iron (Fe) atom contained in the centre of a large heterocyclic organic ring called a porphyrin — in the most common form, protoporphyrin IX. Hemes are most commonly recognised as components of hemoglobin, the red pigment in blood, but are also found in a number of other biologically important hemoproteins such as myoglobin, cytochromes, catalases and endothelial nitric oxide synthase. The word is from Greek haima, "blood".

Structure and types

The porphyrin ring of four pyrrole subunits binds the central iron ion through four nitrogen atoms; the iron can also bind ligands above and below the ring plane — in hemoglobin, a histidine of the globin chain on one side and, reversibly, an oxygen molecule on the other. Types differ in the ring's side chains: heme a (in cytochrome c oxidase), heme b (in hemoglobin, myoglobin, cytochrome b5 and many enzymes), heme c (covalently attached in cytochrome c), and heme o, among others.

Biological roles

Hemoproteins exploit the iron centre's reversible binding and redox chemistry for: (i) oxygen transport and storage (hemoglobin, myoglobin); (ii) electron transfer in respiration and photosynthesis (cytochromes); (iii) catalysis of oxidation reactions (cytochrome P450 enzymes that metabolise drugs and toxins, peroxidases, catalase); and (iv) sensing and signalling (gas sensors for O2, CO and NO). Free heme is cytotoxic and pro-inflammatory, so cells tightly regulate its synthesis and degradation.

Synthesis and degradation

Heme is synthesised in an eight-step pathway beginning with glycine and succinyl-CoA (via δ-aminolevulinic acid and porphobilinogen) in mitochondria and cytosol, mainly in bone marrow and liver; inherited defects of the pathway cause the porphyrias. Degradation by heme oxygenase opens the ring to biliverdin (releasing iron and carbon monoxide), which biliverdin reductase converts to bilirubin — the pigment whose accumulation causes jaundice and which is excreted via bile.
