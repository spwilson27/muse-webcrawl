An actuary is a professional with advanced mathematical skills who deals with the measurement and management of risk and uncertainty. The discipline, actuarial science, applies methods of probability, statistics and financial theory to assess risk in insurance, pension funds, finance and other industries, and to design financially sound insurance policies, pension plans and investment strategies.

History

The profession grew from 17th- and 18th-century developments in probability and life tables. Edmond Halley's 1693 mortality table and James Dodson's work led to the founding of the Equitable Life Assurance Society in London in 1762, the first to price premiums scientifically. The Institute of Actuaries was founded in London in 1848, and professional bodies followed in the United States (1889), and worldwide. The title derives from the Latin actuarius, a clerk in the Roman senate.

Work

Actuaries price insurance products, calculate reserves insurers must hold, value pension scheme liabilities, and advise on capital and solvency under regimes such as Solvency II. They build and validate models of mortality, morbidity, claims frequency and severity, and financial markets. Practice areas include life insurance, general (property and casualty) insurance, health, pensions, investments, and the newer field of data science and climate risk.

Qualification

Qualification typically requires passing a series of professional examinations in mathematics, statistics, economics and specialist subjects while working in the field, taking several years; fully qualified actuaries are fellows of their national association and use post-nominals such as FIA (UK) or FSA (US life) and FCAS (US casualty). Actuaries are consistently ranked among the best-compensated and least stressful professional occupations in survey listings.
