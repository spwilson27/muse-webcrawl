Texas Panhandle

The Texas Panhandle is a region of the US state of Texas consisting of the northernmost 26 counties of the state. The Panhandle is a rectangular area bordered by New Mexico to the west and Oklahoma to the north and east. It is distinguished from the rest of Texas by its high-plains geography, cooler and drier climate, and flat, largely treeless landscape.

Geography

The Panhandle covers about 67,000 km² (25,800 sq mi), roughly ten percent of Texas's total area, and lies mostly on the Llano Estacado, a vast tableland bounded by the Caprock Escarpment. Its principal river is the Canadian River, which cuts the Palo Duro Canyon — the second-largest canyon in the United States — southeast of Amarillo. Elevations range from about 550 m in the east to more than 1,400 m in the northwest, making it the highest and coldest part of Texas, with genuine winters and occasional blizzards.

Demographics and economy

The largest city is Amarillo, with a population of about 200,000; the region's total population is around 430,000. Ranching and irrigated agriculture dominate the economy, drawing heavily on the Ogallala Aquifer for cotton, wheat, sorghum, and feedlots that supply a large share of US beef. The area also produces oil and natural gas, and hosts the Pantex plant near Amarillo, the United States' principal facility for assembling and disassembling nuclear weapons.

History

The region was the heartland of the Comanche (the Comanchería) until the Red River War of 1874–75 drove the Southern Plains tribes onto reservations. Large cattle ranches such as the XIT and JA ranches followed; the XIT, paid out in land for building the Texas State Capitol, was among the largest ranches in the world. The 1930s Dust Bowl struck the Panhandle especially hard.
