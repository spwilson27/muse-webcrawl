Submarine-launched ballistic missile

A submarine-launched ballistic missile (SLBM) is a ballistic missile capable of being launched from submarines. Modern variants usually are multiple independently targetable reentry vehicles (MIRVs), each of which carries a nuclear warhead and allows a single launched missile to strike several targets. Submarine-launched ballistic missiles operate in a different way from submarine-launched cruise missiles.

SLBMs carried by nuclear-powered ballistic missile submarines (SSBNs) constitute the sea-based leg of the nuclear triad and are generally considered the most survivable component of a nuclear arsenal, providing an assured second-strike capability.

History

The Soviet Union achieved the first live launch of a ballistic missile from a submarine in 1955, when an R-11FM was fired from the surfaced submarine B-67. The United States followed with the solid-fuelled Polaris, launched submerged for the first time from USS George Washington on 20 July 1960. Polaris was succeeded by Poseidon and then the Trident I and Trident II missiles, the latter also arming Britain's Vanguard-class submarines. France developed its own series from the M1 to the M51, while China fielded the JL-1 and JL-2, and India the K-15 and K-4.

Launch method

Modern SLBMs are "cold-launched": a gas generator ejects the missile from its tube while the submarine is submerged, and the rocket motor ignites once the missile has broached the surface. This protects the submarine from the rocket exhaust. Missiles are stored in vertical tubes in the submarine's hull — 12 to 24 tubes on current SSBN classes.

Strategic role

Because SSBNs on patrol are extremely difficult to locate, SLBM forces underpin deterrence by guaranteeing retaliation even after a disarming first strike. This survivability shaped Cold War arms control: SLBM launchers were counted in SALT and START treaties, and MIRVed SLBMs were a central topic of debates about crisis stability.
