Vladimir K. Zworykin

Vladimir Kosma Zworykin (July 29, 1888 – July 29, 1982) was a Russian-American engineer and inventor, a pioneer of television technology. Working at Westinghouse and then RCA, he invented the iconoscope (an electronic camera tube) and the kinescope (the cathode-ray display tube), the two halves of the first practical all-electronic television system, for which he is often called the "father of television" — a title he shares with other pioneers such as Philo Farnsworth and John Logie Baird.

Early life and emigration

Zworykin studied engineering at the St. Petersburg Institute of Technology, where he worked with Boris Rosing, a pioneer of cathode-ray television experiments, and later studied X-rays in Paris. After service in the First World War and the turmoil of the Russian Revolution, he emigrated to the United States in 1919 and joined Westinghouse in Pittsburgh. In 1923 he filed the patent application for his iconoscope camera tube, and in 1924 for the kinescope receiver.

Electronic television at RCA

Westinghouse initially showed little interest, but after a 1929 demonstration Zworykin was recruited by David Sarnoff to lead television development at RCA in Camden, New Jersey. His team greatly improved the iconoscope's sensitivity, and the electronic system was publicly showcased at the 1939 New York World's Fair, launching RCA's broadcasts. Patent interference battles with Philo Farnsworth ended with RCA licensing Farnsworth's patents — the first time RCA paid royalties to an outside inventor.

Later work

During the Second World War Zworykin's laboratory developed infrared night-vision devices and early guided-missile television guidance. As head of RCA's electronic research he also contributed to the electron microscope, photoelectric cells and early computing devices. He later promoted medical electronics, co-founding the International Federation for Medical Electronics. Zworykin received many honours, including the IEEE Medal of Honor in 1966, and reportedly remarked that he disliked what television had become, preferring its technical promise to its programming.
