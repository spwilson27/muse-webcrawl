Deinterlacing

Deinterlacing is the process of converting interlaced video into a non-interlaced or progressive form. Interlaced video signals are those in which alternate fields — sets of odd and even lines — are captured or displayed at slightly different times. They are common in traditional television systems such as NTSC, PAL, and SECAM, and in some high-definition formats such as 1080i.

Video signals from modern sources, such as progressive-scan DVD players, video game consoles, and computer video cards, are already progressive, but older broadcast material and many recordings exist in interlaced form. Because most modern displays (LCD, plasma, and OLED screens) are inherently progressive, interlaced signals must be deinterlaced before they can be shown, either by the display itself or by an upstream device.

The interlacing problem

Simply displaying the two fields of an interlaced frame together can produce visible artifacts, most notably a "combing" effect on moving objects: because the fields were captured at different instants, horizontal edges of moving objects appear offset between alternate lines, like the teeth of a comb. Deinterlacing algorithms attempt to reconstruct full frames with minimal artifacts.

Methods

The simplest method, sometimes called "bob" deinterlacing, displays each field as a full frame by interpolating the missing lines, preserving temporal resolution at the cost of vertical detail. "Weave" deinterlacing combines two fields into one frame, preserving full detail for still images but producing combing on motion. Motion-adaptive methods choose between these strategies per region or pixel, while motion-compensated methods estimate object movement between fields to reconstruct frames more accurately. Modern approaches may also use edge-directed interpolation or machine learning.
