The Farnese Hercules (Italian: Ercole Farnese) is an ancient statue of Hercules, probably an enlarged copy made in the early third century AD by Glykon of Athens of a much older Greek original by Lysippos, from the fourth century BC. It is now in the National Archaeological Museum in Naples, Italy.

Description

The colossal marble statue, 3.17 metres tall, shows a weary Hercules leaning on his club, which has the skin of the Nemean lion draped over it. Behind his back, in his right hand, he holds the apples of the Hesperides, indicating that this moment follows the completion of his labours. The hero's exaggerated musculature and exhausted pose made the work one of the most famous depictions of Hercules in Western art.

History

The statue was excavated in 1546 from the ruins of the Baths of Caracalla in Rome and entered the Farnese collection, from which it takes its name. The legs were initially missing and were restored; the original legs, found later, were eventually reattached in the 18th century. The sculpture was enormously influential, admired by artists from Michelangelo onward and copied across Europe.
