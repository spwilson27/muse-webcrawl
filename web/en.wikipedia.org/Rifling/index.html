Rifling

Rifling is the term for machining helical grooves into the internal surface of a gun barrel, for the purpose of exerting torque on and thus imparting a spinning motion to a projectile around its longitudinal axis. This spin serves to gyroscopically stabilize the projectile, improving its aerodynamic stability and accuracy. A barrel so treated is said to be rifled, in contrast to a smoothbore barrel.

Rifling is typically described by its twist rate, which indicates the distance the projectile must travel to complete one full revolution — for example, "1 turn in 10 inches" (1:10). A shorter twist rate (such as 1:7) spins the bullet faster and is needed to stabilize longer, heavier projectiles, while an insufficient twist leads to tumbling in flight. The raised portions of the rifled bore are called lands and the recessed portions grooves; the diameter measured across the lands is the bore diameter, and across the grooves, the groove diameter.

History

Gun barrels had been rifled since the late 15th century in the region of Augsburg and Nuremberg in modern Germany, well before the principle of spin stabilization was understood. Early rifled arms were slow to load because a tight-fitting ball had to be forced down the grooves, so rifles remained specialist weapons for hunters and sharpshooters while armies used smoothbore muskets. The invention of the expanding Minié ball in the 1840s allowed rifled muskets to load as quickly as smoothbores, transforming infantry warfare during the American Civil War and other mid-19th-century conflicts.

Manufacture

Most modern rifling is produced by one of several methods. In cut rifling, a single hook-shaped cutter removes a small amount of metal from one groove at a time over many passes. In button rifling, a hardened carbide button with the rifling pattern in reverse is forced through the bore, displacing metal. Hammer forging beats a barrel blank around a mandrel carrying the rifling profile, while broaching pulls a series of cutters of increasing size through the bore. Polygonal rifling, in which the grooves have rounded rather than sharp-edged profiles, is used in some pistol barrels.

Spin and stability

A spinning projectile resists the aerodynamic tendency of an elongated bullet to tumble end over end. Formulas such as the Miller twist rule estimate the stability factor for a given bullet and twist rate. Excessive spin, however, can keep a bullet's nose pointed along its original line of departure rather than following its trajectory arc, and can even cause thin-jacketed bullets to disintegrate in flight.
