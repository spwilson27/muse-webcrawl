Demethylation

Demethylation is the chemical process resulting in the removal of a methyl group (CH3) from a molecule. It is the reverse of methylation and occurs in many biological contexts, including the metabolism of drugs and other xenobiotics, the degradation of natural products such as lignin, and — most prominently in recent research — the regulation of DNA and histone modifications in epigenetics.

Biological roles

In the liver, enzymes of the cytochrome P450 family catalyse the demethylation of many drugs; for example, codeine is O-demethylated to morphine, a step on which its analgesic effect largely depends. In the biosphere, microbial demethylation of lignin-derived compounds such as vanillate is an important step in carbon cycling. N-demethylation is also involved in the breakdown of alkaloids and caffeine.

Epigenetic demethylation

DNA methylation at cytosine residues (5-methylcytosine) is a key epigenetic mark associated with gene silencing. Its removal occurs passively, when maintenance methylation fails during DNA replication, or actively, through enzymatic pathways. In the best-characterised active pathway, TET enzymes oxidise 5-methylcytosine successively to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, 5-formylcytosine, and 5-carboxylcytosine, after which thymine DNA glycosylase and the base excision repair machinery restore an unmodified cytosine. Genome-wide waves of demethylation in the early embryo and in primordial germ cells are essential for resetting epigenetic information between generations. On histones, lysine-specific demethylases (such as LSD1/KDM1A) and the JmjC-domain family of demethylases remove methyl groups from lysine and arginine residues, dynamically regulating chromatin structure and gene expression.
