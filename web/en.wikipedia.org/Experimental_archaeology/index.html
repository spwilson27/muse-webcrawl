Experimental archaeology

Experimental archaeology is a field of study that attempts to answer archaeological questions by replicating or testing past processes, technologies, and behaviors under controlled conditions. Instead of relying solely on excavated remains, practitioners rebuild structures, reproduce artifacts using period techniques, and observe the results — including how sites and objects decay over time.

Examples

Famous experiments include Thor Heyerdahl's Kon-Tiki expedition (1947), which sailed a balsa raft across the Pacific to test the feasibility of pre-Columbian contact with Polynesia, and his later Ra reed-boat voyages. At Butser Ancient Farm in England, begun by Peter Reynolds in 1972, researchers have built Iron Age roundhouses and run long-term storage and farming experiments, while the Overton Down earthwork (begun 1960) buries materials to monitor decades of decay. Flintknapping replication, ancient bronze casting, Roman siege-engine reconstructions, and full-scale replica ships such as the Drakkar longships are other standard methods. Modern flintknappers' debris patterns are compared with archaeological assemblages to test hypotheses about tool production.

Method and criticism

Rigorous experimental archaeology formulates a hypothesis, controls variables, and records outcomes so results can be compared with the archaeological record. Critics note that a successful replication demonstrates only that something was possible, not that it happened that way in the past, and that experiments can be distorted by modern assumptions. The field is related to, but distinguished from, historical reenactment and "primitive technology" hobbies by its explicit research design and publication of results.
