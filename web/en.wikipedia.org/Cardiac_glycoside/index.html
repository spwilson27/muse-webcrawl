Cardiac glycoside

Cardiac glycosides are a class of organic compounds that increase the force of contraction of the heart and decrease its rate. They occur naturally in plants such as foxglove (Digitalis), oleander, and lily of the valley, and in some animal toxins. The best-known member, digoxin, remains in clinical use for heart failure and rate control in atrial fibrillation.

Mechanism

Cardiac glycosides inhibit the sodium–potassium ATPase pump in cardiac muscle cell membranes. The resulting rise in intracellular sodium reduces the activity of the sodium–calcium exchanger, so calcium accumulates inside the cell and more is available for contraction (positive inotropy). Increased vagal tone slows conduction through the atrioventricular node (negative dromotropy), which is useful in atrial fibrillation.

Clinical use and toxicity

William Withering described the use of foxglove for "dropsy" in 1785. Digoxin has a narrow therapeutic index: toxicity causes nausea, visual disturbances (including yellow-tinted vision), and potentially fatal arrhythmias, and is worsened by low potassium. Severe poisoning is treated with digoxin-specific antibody fragments (Fab). The large DIG trial (1997) showed digoxin reduced hospitalisations for heart failure but did not reduce overall mortality.
