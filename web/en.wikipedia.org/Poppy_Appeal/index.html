Poppy Appeal

The Poppy Appeal is an annual fundraising campaign run by the Royal British Legion in the United Kingdom in the weeks leading up to Remembrance Sunday, the Sunday closest to Armistice Day (11 November). Volunteers distribute artificial red poppies in return for donations supporting veterans, serving personnel, and their families. First held in 1921, it raises tens of millions of pounds each year.

Remembrance poppies are worn in the United Kingdom each November.

Origins

The remembrance poppy was inspired by John McCrae's 1915 poem "In Flanders Fields". American professor Moina Michael popularized wearing silk poppies, and Anna Guérin promoted them in Britain and France. The British Legion adopted the poppy in 1921, ordering nine million made by disabled ex-servicemen; the first appeal raised over £106,000. From 1922 poppies were produced at the Poppy Factory in Richmond, London, and are now also made in Scotland by Lady Haig's Poppy Factory.

The appeal today

Around 40 million poppies are distributed annually by some 300,000 collectors. The appeal traditionally launches in late October, and poppies are worn by politicians, broadcasters, and sports teams; its symbolism occasionally generates controversy over perceived pressure to wear one and over variants such as the white poppy promoted by the Peace Pledge Union since the 1930s. Parallel poppy appeals run in other Commonwealth countries, including Canada, Australia, and New Zealand (where ANZAC Day poppies predominate).
