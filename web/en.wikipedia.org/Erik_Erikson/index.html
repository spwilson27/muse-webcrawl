Erik Homburger Erikson (born Erik Salomonsen; 15 June 1902 – 12 May 1994) was a German-American developmental psychologist and psychoanalyst best known for his theory of the psychosocial stages of human development and for coining the term identity crisis. Although he held no bachelor's degree, he taught at institutions including Harvard, Yale, and the University of California, Berkeley.

Life

Erikson was born in Frankfurt to a Danish mother; the identity of his biological father was never disclosed to him, a fact that shaped his lifelong interest in identity. As a young man he travelled in Europe as an artist and teacher before being invited to Vienna, where he trained in psychoanalysis and was analysed by Anna Freud. Fleeing the rise of Nazism, he emigrated to the United States in 1933. His studies of child-rearing among the Sioux and Yurok peoples informed his view that development must be understood within its social and cultural setting.

Psychosocial stages

In Childhood and Society (1950), Erikson proposed that personality develops through eight stages across the whole lifespan, each defined by a psychosocial conflict whose resolution yields a corresponding virtue:

  • Trust vs. mistrust (infancy) — hope
  • Autonomy vs. shame and doubt (toddlerhood) — will
  • Initiative vs. guilt (preschool) — purpose
  • Industry vs. inferiority (school age) — competence
  • Identity vs. role confusion (adolescence) — fidelity
  • Intimacy vs. isolation (young adulthood) — love
  • Generativity vs. stagnation (middle age) — care
  • Integrity vs. despair (late life) — wisdom

Unlike Freud's psychosexual stages, Erikson's model extends development to the end of life and stresses the ego and society rather than the drives.

Psychohistory and legacy

Erikson pioneered "psychohistory" with Young Man Luther (1958) and Gandhi's Truth (1969), the latter winning the Pulitzer Prize and the National Book Award. His concepts of identity and the identity crisis became part of everyday vocabulary, and his stage theory remains a standard framework in developmental psychology and education.
