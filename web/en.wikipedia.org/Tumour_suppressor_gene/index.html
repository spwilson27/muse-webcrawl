Tumour suppressor gene

Loss of tumour suppressor function removes a brake on cell proliferation

A tumour suppressor gene (or tumor suppressor gene) is a gene that reduces the probability that a cell in a multicellular organism will turn into a tumor cell. Such genes encode proteins that regulate the cell cycle, promote apoptosis (programmed cell death), maintain DNA integrity, or inhibit cell proliferation. A mutation or deletion of a tumor suppressor gene increases the probability of the formation of a tumor.

Two-hit hypothesis

Alfred Knudson's two-hit hypothesis, proposed in 1971 from studies of the childhood eye cancer retinoblastoma, holds that both copies (alleles) of a tumor suppressor gene must generally be inactivated for its protective effect to be lost. In hereditary cancer syndromes, one defective copy is inherited, so only a single further "hit" in a cell is needed — explaining why inherited forms of retinoblastoma appear earlier and in both eyes.

Examples

  • RB1 — the retinoblastoma gene, the first tumor suppressor identified; it regulates passage through the cell-cycle restriction point.
  • TP53 — encodes the p53 protein, called the "guardian of the genome"; mutated in more than half of human cancers.
  • BRCA1 and BRCA2 — involved in DNA double-strand break repair; inherited mutations greatly raise the risk of breast and ovarian cancer.
  • APC — mutated in familial adenomatous polyposis and most colorectal cancers.
  • PTEN — a phosphatase that opposes growth signalling through the PI3K/AKT pathway.

Functions

Tumor suppressor proteins may repress genes essential for cell-cycle progression, couple the cell cycle to DNA damage checkpoints, trigger apoptosis if damage is irreparable, participate in DNA repair, or regulate cell adhesion and contact inhibition.
