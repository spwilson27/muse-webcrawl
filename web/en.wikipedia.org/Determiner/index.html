Determiner

A determiner, also called a determinative, is a word, phrase or affix that occurs together with a noun or noun phrase and generally serves to express the reference of that noun or noun phrase in context. Determiners include articles (the, a), demonstratives (this, that), possessive determiners (my, their), quantifiers (some, many, every), numerals and interrogatives (what, which). They typically precede adjectives and the noun: "the big house", "those three books".

Function

Determiners mark definiteness, deixis, possession and quantity, converting a common noun's description into a referring expression. In many languages they agree with the noun in gender, number and case, and in some languages definiteness is expressed by a suffix rather than a separate word, as in Scandinavian languages and Romanian. Languages such as Russian, Chinese and Japanese lack articles altogether, performing parts of the same work with word order, classifiers and context.

Determiner phrase hypothesis

Traditional grammar treated the determiner as a modifier inside the noun phrase. In 1987 Steven Abney proposed the DP hypothesis, arguing that the determiner is itself the head of a determiner phrase (DP) containing the noun phrase — a move that aligned noun phrases structurally with clauses and influenced much subsequent generative syntax. The term "determiner" is sometimes reserved for the word class, "determinative" for the function, avoiding confusion where phrases such as "the man's" act as determiners.

Classes in English

English determiners occupy a fixed slot and are mutually exclusive in central position: one cannot say "*the my book". Grammars commonly distinguish predeterminers (all, both, half), central determiners (articles, demonstratives, possessives), and postdeterminers (numerals and quantifiers such as many).
