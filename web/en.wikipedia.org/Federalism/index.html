Federalism

Federalism is a mode of government that combines a general ("federal") government with regional governments (provinces, states, cantons, territories or other subunits) in a single political system, dividing the powers between the two levels so that each has authority that the other cannot unilaterally remove. About 40 percent of the world's population lives in federal countries, which include the United States, Germany, India, Brazil, Mexico, Canada, Australia and Switzerland. Federalism contrasts with a unitary state, in which a single central government holds supreme authority, and with a confederation, in which the constituent units retain ultimate sovereignty.

Origins and theory

The term derives from the Latin foedus, meaning covenant or treaty. Modern federal theory was shaped by the United States Constitution of 1787 and defended in The Federalist Papers by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay. Madison described the American system as "partly federal and partly national": the federal government acts directly on citizens in enumerated areas, while the states retain broad residual powers. Later thinkers, including Johannes Althusius and Pierre-Joseph Proudhon, developed alternative covenantal and decentralist conceptions of federation.

Features

Federal systems characteristically include a written constitution that allocates competences, an umpire (usually a constitutional court) to resolve disputes between levels, a bicameral legislature in which one chamber represents the constituent units, and fiscal arrangements dividing taxation and transfers. Powers may be exclusive to one level or concurrent. Scholars distinguish "dual federalism," in which the layers operate separately, from "cooperative federalism," in which they jointly administer programmes. Some federations are "coming together" unions of formerly independent states; others are "holding together" devolutions designed to accommodate linguistic or ethnic diversity, as in India and Belgium.

Criticisms

Critics note that federalism can produce duplication, obscure accountability, entrench regional inequality and complicate national responses to emergencies. Defenders argue that it disperses power, allows policy experimentation — Justice Louis Brandeis called the states "laboratories of democracy" — and brings government closer to citizens.
