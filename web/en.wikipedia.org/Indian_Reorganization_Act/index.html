Indian Reorganization Act

EnactedJune 18, 1934
Other namesWheeler–Howard Act; Indian New Deal
SponsorJohn Collier (Commissioner of Indian Affairs)

The Indian Reorganization Act (IRA) of 1934, also known as the Wheeler–Howard Act, was a United States federal law that reversed the policy of allotment and assimilation of Native Americans established by the Dawes Act of 1887. Sometimes called the "Indian New Deal", it restored some tribal self-government, halted the sale of tribal lands, and authorised funds to repurchase land and support tribal enterprises.

The Act was championed by Commissioner of Indian Affairs John Collier.

Provisions

The Act ended further allotment of tribal land, extended restrictions on the sale of allotted lands, and allowed tribes to organise governments under constitutions and bylaws approved in referendums. It created a revolving credit fund for tribal corporations, authorised appropriations to buy back land, and gave preference to Native Americans in Bureau of Indian Affairs employment. Tribes voted on whether to accept the Act; some, including the Navajo Nation, rejected it, partly over its livestock-reduction provisions.

Legacy

The IRA halted the catastrophic loss of the tribal land base — some 90 million acres had been lost since 1887 — and laid foundations for modern tribal governments, although critics note its imposed constitutional models and uneven implementation. It was partially reversed during the termination era of the 1950s and later superseded by the self-determination policy of the 1970s.
