Nea Moni (Greek: Νέα Μονή, "New Monastery") is an 11th-century Orthodox monastery on the island of Chios, Greece. Situated on the Provateio Oros mountain in the island's interior, it is celebrated for its mosaics, which rank among the finest surviving examples of middle Byzantine art. Together with the monasteries of Hosios Loukas and Daphni, it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1990.

History

According to tradition, the monastery was founded where three monks discovered a miraculous icon of the Virgin Mary hanging from a myrtle branch. The Byzantine emperor Constantine IX Monomachos, who had been exiled to nearby Lesbos before his accession, vowed to build a church on the site if he became emperor. After his accession in 1042 he fulfilled the vow, and the katholikon (main church) was constructed between about 1042 and 1049 with imperial funding and craftsmen sent from Constantinople.

The monastery flourished during the Byzantine and Genoese periods, accumulating estates and privileges. Its fortunes declined under Ottoman rule, and it suffered severely during the Chios massacre of 1822, when much of the community was killed and the buildings were plundered and burned. An earthquake in 1881 caused further damage, collapsing the dome of the katholikon, which was subsequently rebuilt in a slightly altered form.

Architecture and mosaics

The katholikon follows the "insular octagonal" plan, a variant of the cross-in-square type in which the dome is carried on eight piers close to the walls. The church is renowned for its gold-ground mosaics, executed around the middle of the 11th century by artists from the imperial workshops of Constantinople. Surviving mosaic panels depict scenes from the life of Christ, including the Baptism, Crucifixion, Anastasis (Resurrection), and the Washing of the Feet, together with figures of saints. The mosaics are noted for their expressiveness and their transitional style between the austere classicism of the Macedonian era and the more emotional art of the Komnenian period.

The fortified complex also includes a refectory, monks' cells, a cistern, and the Church of St. Luke, whose chapel houses an ossuary commemorating victims of the 1822 massacre.
