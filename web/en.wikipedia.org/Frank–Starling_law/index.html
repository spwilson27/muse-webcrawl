Frank–Starling law

The Frank–Starling curve: stroke volume rises with ventricular filling up to a plateau

The Frank–Starling law of the heart (also known as Starling's law or the Frank–Starling mechanism) states that the stroke volume of the heart increases in response to an increase in the volume of blood in the ventricles before contraction (the end-diastolic volume), when all other factors remain constant. In Starling's words, "the energy of contraction is a function of the length of the muscle fibres" composing the myocardium.

Mechanism

As the ventricle fills more, cardiac muscle fibres are stretched, increasing the affinity of troponin C for calcium and improving the overlap of actin and myosin filaments so that each fibre contracts with greater force. The law allows cardiac output to synchronise with venous return: whatever volume returns to the heart is pumped onward, keeping the outputs of the right and left ventricles matched beat by beat.

History and clinical relevance

The principle was described by the German physiologist Otto Frank in frog hearts (1895) and by the English physiologist Ernest Starling in mammalian heart–lung preparations (1914–1918). Plotted as ventricular function curves, the relationship shifts upward with increased contractility (for example from sympathetic stimulation) and downward in heart failure, where the curve flattens and filling pressures rise. The Frank–Starling relationship underlies day-to-day matching of cardiac output to the body's demands and the response to changes in posture, exercise, and blood volume.
