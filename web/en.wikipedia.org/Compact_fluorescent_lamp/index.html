Compact fluorescent lamp

A compact fluorescent lamp (CFL), also called a compact fluorescent light or energy-saving light, is a fluorescent lamp designed to replace an incandescent light bulb. The tube is folded or coiled into a compact shape, usually a spiral or a set of parallel tubes, and the lamp incorporates an electronic ballast in its base so that it fits standard screw or bayonet sockets.

How it works

Like other fluorescent lamps, a CFL produces light by passing an electric current through a tube containing argon and a small amount of mercury vapour. The discharge emits ultraviolet light, which excites a phosphor coating on the inside of the tube, causing it to glow in visible light. The built-in ballast regulates the current and provides the starting voltage.

Advantages and history

CFLs use roughly one quarter to one third of the electricity of an equivalent incandescent lamp and last many times longer — typically 8,000 to 15,000 hours versus about 1,000. Although fluorescent lamps had existed since the 1930s, compact versions suitable for household sockets became commercially successful in the 1980s and 1990s, notably following designs by Philips (the SL lamp, 1980) and the spiral lamp popularised in the 1990s. Energy-efficiency standards and incandescent phase-outs in many countries drove mass adoption in the 2000s.

Drawbacks and decline

Disadvantages include a warm-up period to full brightness, reduced lifetime with frequent switching, mediocre colour rendering in early models, and the presence of mercury, which requires careful disposal if a lamp breaks. From the 2010s onward, CFLs have been largely displaced by LED lamps, which are more efficient still, contain no mercury, and switch on instantly.
