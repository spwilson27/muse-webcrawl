Votum


Language: Latin
Literal meaning: "vow", "wish", "promise"
Related terms: vote, votive, ex-voto

A votum (plural vota) is a Latin word meaning a vow, wish or solemn promise, in Roman religion typically a promise made to a deity in exchange for a desired favour. The English words "vote" and "votive" both derive from it.

In Roman religion

In ancient Rome a votum was a formal bargain with a god: the worshipper promised a gift, sacrifice, temple, games or other offering if the god granted the request — victory in battle, recovery from illness, a safe voyage. Such vows could be private or public (vota publica), undertaken by magistrates on behalf of the state; consuls traditionally made vows for the welfare of the republic, and emperors later took annual vows (vota suscepta) that were ceremonially renewed and fulfilled (vota soluta), occasions recorded on coins. A fulfilled vow was often commemorated by a votive offering or an altar inscribed V.S.L.M. — votum solvit libens merito, "willingly and deservedly fulfilled the vow".

Later uses

In Roman Catholic usage, votum refers to religious vows and to the formal opinions delivered by consultors of Vatican congregations. In German academic tradition, a Votum is a written expert opinion or a professor's formal assessment, a usage reflected in the word's broader sense of a considered judgement or expression of opinion — the root from which the modern political "vote" developed.
