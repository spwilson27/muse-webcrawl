Expletive (linguistics)

In linguistics, an expletive is a word or phrase that contributes nothing to the propositional meaning of a sentence but fills a syntactic or metrical position. The term derives from the Latin explere, "to fill out". Notable examples in English are the dummy pronoun it in "it is raining" and the existential there in "there are many problems".

Expletive subjects

English requires an overt subject in finite clauses, so when a verb has no semantic subject — as with weather verbs — a dummy it is inserted. Similarly, in extraposed constructions such as "it is clear that he left", the it occupies the subject position while the logical subject appears later in the sentence. In existential sentences, unstressed there introduces the presence of something: "there is a book on the table".

Languages differ in whether they permit such elements. So-called pro-drop languages such as Spanish and Italian can omit subjects entirely, while other languages use different dummy elements; French uses il in il pleut ("it is raining").

In generative grammar

Within generative linguistics, expletives are analysed as elements merged in a sentence purely to satisfy the Extended Projection Principle, the requirement that clauses have subjects. Because they carry no thematic role, they are a classic argument that syntactic structure is not determined by meaning alone.

Other uses of the term

"Expletive" is also used more loosely for words inserted to add emphasis or fill metre in verse, and in everyday usage the word most often refers to profanity — expletives in that sense are sometimes called "expletive interjections". Linguists distinguish the syntactic sense carefully from the profane one.
