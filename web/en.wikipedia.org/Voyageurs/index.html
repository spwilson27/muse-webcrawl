Voyageurs

Voyageurs were French Canadian fur traders who travelled by canoe through the interior of North America during the 17th to early 19th centuries. Hired by fur companies such as the North West Company and the Hudson's Bay Company, they transported trade goods and furs between Montreal, the Great Lakes and the western interior.

Work and life

Working in brigades of large canots du maître or smaller northern canoes, voyageurs paddled up to 14–16 hours a day, portaged canoes and packs of about 90 pounds (41 kg) each over carrying places, and subsisted on rations such as pemmican, corn and grease. They were renowned for their songs, distinctive dress including the ceinture fléchée sash, and festivals such as the rendezvous at Grand Portage and later Fort William.

Legacy

Voyageurs played a major role in exploration and in relations — including marriages à la façon du pays — with Indigenous peoples, contributing to the emergence of the Métis. Voyageurs National Park in Minnesota commemorates their routes and history.
