Motivated reasoning

Motivated reasoning is a phenomenon in cognitive science and social psychology in which people's reasoning is biased by their emotional state, desires, and identity. Motivated reasoning is the tendency of people to process information in a way that suits their needs or goals, rather than to reach dispassionate conclusions. In other words, motivated reasoning is an emotion-driven form of decision-making, exemplified by the process of reasoning to conclusions that fit pre-existing preferences, even when the reasoning process is flawed or the evidence contradicts those conclusions.

Psychologist Ziva Kunda, who popularized the term in a landmark 1990 paper, distinguished between accuracy goals, which motivate people to arrive at correct conclusions, and directional goals, which motivate people to arrive at particular, desired conclusions. Directional goals can bias memory search, hypothesis construction, and the standards of evidence a person applies: people tend to ask "Can I believe this?" of congenial evidence and "Must I believe this?" of uncongenial evidence.

Mechanisms

Motivated reasoning is closely related to confirmation bias, in which people search for, interpret, and recall information in ways that confirm their prior beliefs. Proposed mechanisms include biased evaluation of evidence, selective exposure to information sources, and the use of identity-protective cognition, in which forming beliefs that signal loyalty to a social group takes precedence over accuracy.

Applications

The concept is widely used to explain political polarization, resistance to scientific consensus on topics such as climate change and vaccination, and partisan differences in perceptions of the economy. Research by Dan Kahan and colleagues suggests that higher science comprehension can, on identity-laden topics, increase rather than decrease polarization, because skilled reasoners are better at defending identity-congruent conclusions.

Criticism

Some researchers argue that apparent motivated reasoning can reflect rational Bayesian updating from different priors or different trusted sources, and that laboratory demonstrations do not always generalize. Others emphasize accountability and incentives for accuracy as ways of reducing directional bias.
