Expulsion of the Acadians

Date1755–1764
LocationAcadia (present-day Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island, and Maine)
DeportedMore than 11,500 of roughly 14,100 Acadians
Also known asLe Grand Dérangement

The Expulsion of the Acadians (French: Le Grand Dérangement, "the Great Upheaval") was the forced removal by the British of the Acadian people from the present-day Canadian Maritime provinces of Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, and Prince Edward Island, and from northern Maine. Between 1755 and 1764, more than 11,500 Acadians were deported during the French and Indian War.

Background

The Acadians were French settlers who had lived in Acadia since the early 17th century. After Britain gained control of peninsular Nova Scotia in 1713, the Acadians refused unconditional oaths of allegiance, insisting on neutrality. During the French and Indian War, British officials under Governor Charles Lawrence concluded that the Acadians posed a security risk.

The deportations

The deportations began in 1755 at Grand-Pré, where Acadian men and boys were summoned to the church and informed of the forfeiture of their lands and their removal. Villages were burned to prevent return, and families were loaded onto ships bound for the Thirteen Colonies, Louisiana, France, and elsewhere. Thousands died of disease, starvation, and shipwreck; the sinking of the ships Duke William and Violet in 1758 alone cost over 700 lives.

Legacy

Many exiles eventually settled in Louisiana, where their descendants became known as Cajuns. Others returned to the Maritimes after 1764. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's poem Evangeline (1847) popularized the story. In 2003, Queen Elizabeth II issued a royal proclamation acknowledging the deportation and designating July 28 as a day of commemoration.
