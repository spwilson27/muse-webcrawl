Peerage of France

The Peerage of France (French: Pairie de France) was the highest order of the French nobility under the Ancien Régime. At its core stood the Twelve Peers (Douze Pairs), the great vassals of the Crown who played a ceremonial and constitutional role at coronations.

The Twelve Peers

Tradition traced the peers to Charlemagne's paladins. By the later Middle Ages the twelve comprised six ecclesiastical peers — the archbishop of Reims, the bishops of Laon, Langres, Beauvais, Châlons, and Noyon — and six lay peers: the dukes of Burgundy, Normandy, and Aquitaine (Guyenne) and the counts of Flanders, Champagne, and Toulouse. At a coronation at Reims, the peers bore the royal regalia and symbolically supported the crown.

As the great fiefs reverted to the crown, kings replaced them by creating new duchy-peerages (duchés-pairies) for favoured families, and membership of the Parlement of Paris — the cour des pairs — for judging cases involving peers became the institution's chief practical function. Peers also sat in the royal council and ranked immediately after princes of the blood.

Abolition

The peerage was abolished during the French Revolution in 1790. Napoleon created his own imperial nobility, and the Restoration Charter of 1814 established a new hereditary Chamber of Peers, made non-hereditary in 1831 and abolished with the Second Republic in 1848.
