Crofting

Crofts are small agricultural holdings, usually held in tenancy, in the Scottish Highlands and Islands.

Crofting (Scottish Gaelic: croitearachd) is a form of land tenure and small-scale food production particular to the Scottish Highlands and the islands of Scotland. A croft is a small agricultural unit, most of which are situated in the crofting counties in the north of Scotland: Shetland, Orkney, Caithness, Sutherland, Ross-shire, Inverness-shire and Argyll. A crofter is normally the tenant of the croft, paying rent to a landlord, and shares grazing rights on common land with neighbouring crofters in a township.

History

The system took shape during the Highland Clearances of the 18th and 19th centuries, when tenants were removed from inland farms to small coastal plots intended to supply labour for kelp and fishing industries while landowners converted the interior to sheep farms and deer forests. Insecurity of tenure and rack-renting provoked the Crofters' War of the 1880s — rent strikes and land raids — leading to the Napier Commission and the Crofters' Holdings (Scotland) Act 1886, which granted security of tenure, fair rents and rights of succession.

Modern crofting

Successive Crofting Acts, most recently in 1993, 2007 and 2010, consolidated crofters' rights, including the right to buy their crofts. Crofters must reside on or near the croft and cultivate it or put it to another purposeful use. The Crofting Commission regulates the system from Inverness. Crofting supports distinctive landscapes, Gaelic culture and biodiversity, though most crofters combine crofting with other employment.
