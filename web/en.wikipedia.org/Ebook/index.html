An electronic book, commonly called an e-book or eBook, is a book publication made available in digital form, consisting of text, images, or both, readable on the flat-panel display of computers or other electronic devices—including dedicated e-readers, tablets, and smartphones. Although sometimes defined as "an electronic version of a printed book," some e-books exist without a printed equivalent. In some cases, e-books are also print-on-demand products.

History

Project Gutenberg, launched by Michael S. Hart in 1971 with a digitised Declaration of Independence, was the first large digital library. The concept grew through the 1980s–1990s with handheld e-book readers (Sony's Data Discman, the Franklin eBookMan, the Rocket eBook, and the SoftBook). The Kindle (2007) and subsequent e-ink devices, together with tablets like the iPad (2010), brought e-books into the mainstream; Amazon's Kindle Store surpassed print sales in the early 2010s.

Formats

Major formats include EPUB (open standard, reflowable), PDF (fixed layout), MOBI and Amazon's proprietary AZW3/KFX, plain text, and HTML-based formats. Reflowable formats adapt text to screen size and user preferences (font, size, spacing), while PDFs preserve the publisher's fixed page layout. Digital rights management (DRM) is often applied to commercial titles, restricting copying and lending.

Reading devices and software

Dedicated e-readers (Amazon Kindle, Kobo, PocketBook) use electrophoretic (E Ink) displays that mimic paper and run for weeks on a charge. Reading apps on tablets, phones, and computers include Apple Books, Google Play Books, Kindle, Kobo, and open-source tools such as Calibre, which also manages libraries and converts formats.

Publication and distribution

E-books are sold by online bookstores (Amazon, Apple Books, Kobo, Google Play Books, Barnes & Noble Nook) and lent by public libraries via platforms such as OverDrive/Libby. Self-publishing services (Kindle Direct Publishing, Smashwords) let authors publish without traditional publishers, transforming the market's economics: the marginal cost of an additional copy is near zero, though discoverability remains a challenge.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include portability (thousands of titles on one device), searchability, adjustable typography for accessibility, instant purchasing, and lower production/distribution costs. Disadvantages include eye strain on backlit screens, DRM and platform lock-in, licensing restrictions (purchases are often licences, not ownership), and the loss of the tactile experience and resale rights of print. Market share has stabilised at roughly a fifth to a quarter of trade book sales in major English-language markets, with print remaining dominant.
