Ventilator

A ventilator is a medical device that provides mechanical ventilation by moving breathable air into and out of the lungs, to deliver breaths to a patient who is physically unable to breathe or who breathes insufficiently. Modern ventilators are computerized, microprocessor-controlled machines used principally in intensive care medicine, anaesthesia, and emergency medicine.

Function

Ventilators deliver a mixture of air and oxygen at controlled volumes, pressures, flow rates, and frequencies. Key parameters include tidal volume, respiratory rate, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), and the fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2). Modes of ventilation range from fully controlled breaths to pressure-support modes in which the machine assists the patient's own spontaneous breathing, aiding weaning.

History

Early devices included the bellows used by Paracelsus and Vesalius. The most famous early ventilator was the iron lung, a negative-pressure device developed by Philip Drinker and Louis Agassiz Shaw in 1928 and widely used during the polio epidemics of the mid-20th century. In 1952, during the Copenhagen polio epidemic, anaesthetist Bjørn Ibsen pioneered positive-pressure ventilation via tracheostomy, dramatically reducing mortality and laying the foundation for modern intensive care. Subsequent decades brought electronic microprocessor ventilators with sophisticated monitoring and alarms.

Risks

Mechanical ventilation carries risks including ventilator-associated pneumonia, barotrauma, and ventilator-induced lung injury, so clinicians aim to use the lowest effective pressures and to liberate patients from the machine as early as safely possible.

During the COVID-19 pandemic

The COVID-19 pandemic caused worldwide concern over ventilator shortages, prompting emergency production programs and renewed interest in simpler emergency ventilators.
