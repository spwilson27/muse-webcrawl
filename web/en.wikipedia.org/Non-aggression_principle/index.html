Non-aggression principle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The non-aggression principle (NAP), also called the non-aggression axiom, is a concept in libertarian philosophy holding that aggression — the initiation of physical force against persons or their property, or threats of such force — is inherently illegitimate. Force used in self-defense or in response to aggression is regarded as legitimate. The principle is foundational to libertarian ethics, underpinning arguments for self-ownership, private property and a minimal or stateless society.

Formulation and history

The principle was popularized by Ayn Rand, who held that "no man may initiate the use of physical force against others", and developed by Murray Rothbard, who made the non-aggression axiom the basis of his anarcho-capitalist theory in works such as For a New Liberty (1973) and The Ethics of Liberty. Related ideas appear earlier in the natural-law tradition and in the harm principle articulated by John Stuart Mill.

Applications and debates

Libertarians apply the NAP to argue against taxation (as compelled transfer), military conscription, drug prohibition and other state action. Philosophical debates concern what counts as aggression — including disputes over fraud, pollution and other externalities, abortion, intellectual property, the status of children, and retaliation and restitution — and whether the principle can be justified as an axiom, derived from self-ownership, or defended on consequentialist grounds. Critics argue that the principle presupposes a contested theory of property rights and cannot by itself resolve conflicts over what constitutes one's property or the proportional response to aggression.
