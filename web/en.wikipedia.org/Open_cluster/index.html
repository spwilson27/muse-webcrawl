Open cluster

An open cluster is a group of up to a few thousand stars that formed from the same giant molecular cloud and are loosely bound to each other by mutual gravitational attraction. Open clusters are found only in spiral and irregular galaxies, where active star formation occurs, and are important objects for testing theories of stellar evolution because their members share nearly the same age and chemical composition.

Properties

Open clusters are typically a few tens of light-years across and contain young stars — most are less than a few hundred million years old. Because their mutual gravity is weak, they are gradually disrupted over hundreds of millions of years by the galaxy's tidal forces and encounters with other clouds, so few survive to ages of several billion years. More than 1,100 open clusters are known in the Milky Way, and surveys such as the Gaia mission have greatly expanded the catalogue.

Examples

The best-known open clusters include the Pleiades (M45) and the Hyades in Taurus — the nearest open cluster to Earth — the Double Cluster in Perseus, the Wild Duck Cluster (M11), and the young, massive clusters such as the Jewel Box in the southern sky.

Scientific importance

Because cluster stars lie at essentially the same distance and age, plotting their colour against brightness (a Hertzsprung–Russell diagram) reveals the main-sequence turnoff from which the cluster's age can be read, and cluster distances calibrate the cosmic distance ladder. Open clusters contrast with globular clusters, which are denser, much more populous, and far older, orbiting in galactic halos.
