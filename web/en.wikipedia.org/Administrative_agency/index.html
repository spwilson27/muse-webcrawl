Administrative agency

TypeGovernment body
Created byLegislature (enabling statute) or executive
FunctionsRulemaking, adjudication, enforcement
ExamplesEPA, SEC, FCC (United States)

An administrative agency (also called a regulatory agency, board, or commission) is a permanent or semi-permanent organisation in the machinery of government that is responsible for the oversight and administration of specific functions, such as regulation, standard-setting and enforcement. Agencies are created by legislatures (or sometimes by executive action) through enabling statutes that define their jurisdiction and powers, because modern legislatures cannot practically write rules for every technical detail of complex fields such as food safety, securities markets, environmental protection or telecommunications.

Agencies typically combine three kinds of power: they make rules (rulemaking) that have the force of law, they investigate and prosecute violations (enforcement), and they decide individual cases (adjudication) through administrative law judges and internal appeal boards. In the United States, agency procedure is governed by the Administrative Procedure Act of 1946, and agency actions are subject to judicial review. Some American agencies, such as the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Federal Communications Commission, are "independent" — their leaders serve fixed terms and can be removed by the president only for cause — while others sit inside executive departments.

Criticism and debate

Because agencies wield legislative, executive and judicial functions together, they are sometimes called a "fourth branch of government" and are criticised for a democratic deficit, bureaucratic inefficiency, and the risk of regulatory capture by the industries they oversee. Supporters respond that agencies supply expertise, continuity and flexibility that legislatures and courts cannot.
