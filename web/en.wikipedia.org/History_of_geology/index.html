History of geology


Hutton's unconformity at Siccar Point, Scotland

The history of geology traces the study of the Earth from ancient speculation about fossils and minerals to the modern science of plate tectonics. Geology emerged as a distinct discipline in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, when the principles of stratigraphy, uniformitarianism and deep time transformed understanding of Earth's age and processes.

Ancient and medieval thought

Greek thinkers such as Xenophanes noted fossil shells far from the sea and inferred that land had once been submerged. Theophrastus wrote on stones and minerals; Strabo and others discussed earthquakes and volcanoes. In China, Shen Kuo (11th century) correctly inferred that fossil bamboo indicated past climate change and that landforms erode over time. Ibn Sina (Avicenna) described the formation of mountains and the petrifaction of fossils. Leonardo da Vinci argued that fossils were remains of once-living organisms. In the 17th century, Nicolaus Steno established foundational principles of stratigraphy: superposition, original horizontality and lateral continuity.

Foundations: Hutton, Werner and Smith

The German mineralogist Abraham Gottlob Werner promoted "Neptunism", holding that all rocks precipitated from a universal ocean. James Hutton countered with "Plutonism" and uniformitarianism, arguing in his Theory of the Earth (1788, 1795) that geological features arise from processes observable today operating over immense time — "no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end". John Playfair popularised Hutton's ideas (1802). William Smith produced the first geological map of England (1815) using fossil succession to correlate strata. Georges Cuvier and Alexandre Brongniart correlated Paris Basin strata with fossils, while Cuvier championed catastrophism to explain extinctions and faunal turnovers.

Lyell, Darwin and deep time

Charles Lyell's Principles of Geology (1830–33) systematised uniformitarianism and deeply influenced Charles Darwin, whose observations of uplifted shorelines and coral atolls applied geological reasoning to biology; Darwin also proposed subsidence theory for atoll formation. The "Devonian controversy" and mapping surveys refined the geological timescale. Attempts to date the Earth physically — Lord Kelvin's cooling calculations gave ages of tens of millions of years — conflicted with geologists' and evolutionists' need for far greater antiquity. Glacial theory, proposed by Louis Agassiz (1837–40), explained erratics and scratched bedrock by past ice ages.

Radiometric dating and continental drift

The discovery of radioactivity (1896) undermined Kelvin's assumptions by revealing an internal heat source, and radiometric dating by Arthur Holmes and others established an Earth billions of years old; the currently accepted age, about 4.54 billion years, derives from Clair Patterson's lead isotope work (1956). Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912, citing matching coastlines, fossils and geology, but lacked a mechanism and was widely rejected.

Plate tectonics revolution

Post-war oceanography revealed mid-ocean ridges, magnetic striping of the seafloor and deep ocean trenches. In the 1960s, seafloor spreading (Hess, Dietz), magnetic reversal evidence (Vine–Matthews–Morley), transform faults (J. Tuzo Wilson) and Wadati–Benioff earthquake zones converged into plate tectonics, unifying drift, mountain building, volcanism and earthquakes. Since then, geology has expanded into planetary science, geochemistry, geophysics and the study of Earth systems, mass extinctions (the Alvarez impact hypothesis, 1980) and anthropogenic climate change.
