Glomerular filtration rate

Filtration occurs at the glomerulus within each nephron of the kidney.

Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is the volume of fluid filtered from the renal glomerular capillaries into Bowman's capsule per unit time. It is the standard overall index of kidney function: a falling GFR is the hallmark of kidney disease, and GFR thresholds define the stages of chronic kidney disease. A normal GFR in young adults is roughly 90–120 mL/min/1.73 m2, declining with age.

Measurement

GFR cannot be measured directly in routine practice; it is determined from the clearance of a filtration marker. The gold standard uses exogenous markers such as inulin or iohexol. Clinically, GFR is usually estimated (eGFR) from serum creatinine using equations such as the CKD-EPI formula, which incorporate age and sex; cystatin C provides an alternative marker less dependent on muscle mass. Contemporary CKD-EPI equations (2021) no longer include a race coefficient.

Physiology

Filtration is driven by the balance of hydrostatic and oncotic pressures across the glomerular capillary wall and is regulated by the renin–angiotensin system, tubuloglomerular feedback, and afferent and efferent arteriolar tone. The kidneys filter about 180 litres of plasma per day, almost all of which is reabsorbed along the nephron.

Clinical significance

Chronic kidney disease is classified into GFR categories G1 (≥90) through G5 (<15, kidney failure). Persistent GFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 for more than three months indicates chronic kidney disease. GFR also guides drug dosing, contrast studies, and decisions about dialysis and transplantation.
