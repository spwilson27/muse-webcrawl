Golgotha

Golgotha
Other nameCalvary (Latin: Calvariae locus)
Meaning"Place of the skull" (Aramaic: Gûlgaltâ)

Golgotha, also called Calvary, was, according to the canonical Gospels, a site immediately outside Jerusalem's walls where Jesus was crucified. The name derives from the Aramaic word Gûlgaltâ, "place of the skull". In Latin the word was translated as Calvariae locus, from which the English "Calvary" comes.

Biblical accounts

All four Gospels mention the place. Matthew 27:33 says Jesus was brought "to a place called Golgotha (which means 'the place of the skull')"; Mark 15:22 and John 19:17 say the same. Luke 23:33 calls it simply "the place that is called The Skull" (Kranion). John adds that the place was near the city and near a garden with a new tomb, in which Jesus was buried.

Location

Since at least the 4th century, tradition has identified Golgotha with a site inside the present Church of the Holy Sepulchre in the Christian Quarter of Jerusalem's Old City. Constantine's mother Helena is said to have identified the spot around 325 AD, and the church was consecrated in 335. Although the site now lies within the city walls, in the 1st century it stood outside the "second wall" of Jerusalem, consistent with the Gospel accounts.

Alternative locations have been proposed, most notably the "Garden Tomb" and nearby skull-shaped hill identified in the 19th century (sometimes called Gordon's Calvary), north of the Damascus Gate. Scholars remain divided, though the Holy Sepulchre site is the more widely accepted.

Interpretations of the name

Explanations for "place of the skull" include that the hill resembled a skull, that it was an execution ground littered with skulls, or, in a tradition reported by Origen, that Adam's skull was buried there.
