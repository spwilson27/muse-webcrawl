Colonial Williamsburg is a living-history museum and private foundation in Williamsburg, Virginia. Its 301-acre Historic Area includes many buildings from the 18th century — during which Williamsburg was the capital of the Colony and Commonwealth of Virginia from 1699 to 1780 — as well as colonial revivals and reconstructed structures. Costumed interpreters portray colonial life, and tradespeople demonstrate crafts such as blacksmithing, printing, and shoemaking.

History of the restoration

The restoration was the brainchild of the Reverend Dr. W. A. R. Goodwin, rector of Bruton Parish Church, who persuaded philanthropist John D. Rockefeller Jr. to fund the project beginning in the late 1920s under strict secrecy while properties were acquired. Buildings that postdated the colonial era were demolished or moved, surviving 18th-century structures were restored, and missing ones — including the Capitol and the Governor's Palace — were reconstructed on their original foundations. The project helped launch the American historic preservation movement.

Interpretation

Colonial Williamsburg is noted for its first-person and third-person costumed interpretation and for its "Historic Triangle" location with Jamestown and Yorktown. In recent decades its programming has expanded to address slavery and the lives of the enslaved majority of 18th-century Williamsburg's population — over half the town was enslaved — following criticism of earlier sanitized portrayals.
