ALGOL

ALGOL (short for "Algorithmic Language") is a family of imperative computer programming languages originally developed in the mid-1950s by an international committee of computer scientists. It profoundly influenced later language design and was the standard medium for describing algorithms in academic publications for decades.

History

ALGOL 58, first called the International Algebraic Language, was refined into ALGOL 60, whose report (edited by Peter Naur) introduced block structure, lexical scope, and the Backus–Naur form for describing syntax. ALGOL 68, designed under Adriaan van Wijngaarden, was more powerful but controversial for its complexity. Committee members included John Backus, John McCarthy, Alan Perlis, and Niklaus Wirth, several of whom went on to shape computing.

Influence

ALGOL introduced concepts now universal: structured programming constructs, nested functions, and call-by-name parameter passing. It directly influenced Pascal, C, and the Simula languages that pioneered object-oriented programming. Tony Hoare remarked that ALGOL was "so far ahead of its time that it was not only an improvement on its predecessors but also on nearly all its successors."
