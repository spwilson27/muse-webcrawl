A bronchus (plural: bronchi) is a passage or airway in the respiratory system that conducts air into the lungs. The first bronchi to branch from the trachea are the right main bronchus and the left main bronchus, also known as the primary bronchi. These are the widest bronchi and enter the lungs at each hilum, where they branch into narrower secondary bronchi, then into tertiary bronchi, which branch further into small bronchioles.

Structure

The trachea divides at the carina into two main bronchi: the right main bronchus, which is wider, shorter and more vertical than the left, and the left main bronchus, which is longer and more horizontal. Because the right bronchus is more vertical, aspirated foreign objects are more likely to enter it.

Each main bronchus gives off secondary (lobar) bronchi — three on the right and two on the left, corresponding to the lobes of each lung — which in turn divide into tertiary (segmental) bronchi supplying the bronchopulmonary segments. The walls of the bronchi contain cartilage plates, smooth muscle and mucous glands; as the bronchi branch into smaller bronchioles, the cartilage disappears.

Function

The bronchi conduct air from the trachea to the bronchioles and ultimately to the alveoli, where gas exchange occurs. They also help warm, humidify and filter inhaled air. Mucus secreted by goblet cells and submucosal glands, together with the beating of cilia on the bronchial epithelium, traps and removes particles in the mucociliary escalator.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the bronchi is called bronchitis. In asthma, the bronchial smooth muscle constricts (bronchospasm), narrowing the airways. Chronic bronchitis, together with emphysema, forms part of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). A bronchoscope can be passed through the bronchi to visualise the airways and take samples.
