Linocut

Linocut is a printmaking technique, a variant of woodcut, in which a sheet of linoleum (sometimes mounted on a wooden block) is used as the relief surface. The design is cut into the linoleum with gouges and knives, leaving the raised, uncut areas to receive ink; the sheet is then printed by hand or with a press. Because linoleum has no directional grain, it can be cut freely in any direction, though it is softer and less durable than wood for very large editions.

History

Linoleum was patented in 1860 by Frederick Walton as a floor covering, and artists began adapting it for relief printing in the early twentieth century. Members of Die Brücke used linocut around 1905, and the technique was taken up by expressionist and modernist artists including Henri Matisse and Pablo Picasso, whose reduction linocuts of the 1950s and 1960s — printed from a single block progressively cut away between colour layers — are celebrated examples. In Mexico, the Taller de Gráfica Popular used linocut for political posters, and the technique became a staple of school art education worldwide.

Technique

The artist draws the design on the block (remembering it will print in mirror image), then carves away areas meant to stay blank using V- and U-shaped gouges. The surface is inked with a roller (brayer), paper is laid over it, and pressure applied by press, baren, or the back of a spoon. Multi-colour prints are made with separate blocks or by the reduction method, in which one block is re-cut between printings, so the edition cannot be reprinted — hence its nickname, the "suicide print".

Characteristics

Linocut favours bold shapes, strong contrasts, and fluid carved marks; fine detail is possible with sharp tools and firmer modern materials, but the soft surface resists the crisp fine lines of wood engraving. Its cheapness and simplicity have kept it popular for book illustration, posters, and fine-art editions alike.
