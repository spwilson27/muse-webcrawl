The Kingdom of Aragon (Aragonese: Reino d'Aragón; Spanish: Reino de Aragón) was a medieval and early modern kingdom on the Iberian Peninsula, corresponding to the modern-day autonomous community of Aragon in Spain. It originated in 1035 from the partition of the Kingdom of Navarre, and later became the senior partner in the Crown of Aragon.

History

Ramiro I, illegitimate son of Sancho III of Navarre, inherited the county of Aragón in 1035 and styled himself king, absorbing Sobrarbe and Ribagorza. His successors expanded southward against the Muslim taifa kingdoms: Alfonso I "the Battler" took Zaragoza in 1118, making it the capital. In 1137 the marriage of Queen Petronilla of Aragon to Count Ramon Berenguer IV of Barcelona created the dynastic union known as the Crown of Aragon, while the Kingdom of Aragon retained its own laws (fueros), Cortes, and institutions.

Within the Crown, Aragon was the titular head, but Mediterranean expansion was driven largely through Catalonia and later Valencia and the Balearics; James I and his successors conquered Mallorca, Valencia, Sicily, Sardinia, and Naples. The kingdom guarded its privileges jealously — the Union of Aragon extracted the Privilegio de la Unión (1287), and the office of the Justicia Mayor defended subjects against royal overreach, famously invoked in the Antonio Pérez revolt of 1591.

End

The dynastic union with Castile (1479) created Spain, but Aragon kept its institutions until the War of the Spanish Succession, when Philip V abolished the fueros of Aragon by the Nueva Planta decrees of 1707, integrating the kingdom into a centralised Spanish state. Aragon today is an autonomous community within Spain; the historical kingdom's arms — the four red pallets on gold — remain its emblem.
