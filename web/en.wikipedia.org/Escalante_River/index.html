The Escalante River is a tributary of the Colorado River in south-central Utah, United States. It flows about 90 miles (145 km) from its headwaters on the slopes of the Aquarius Plateau, near the town of Escalante, southeastward to join the Colorado River at what is now Lake Powell, in Garfield and Kane counties.

Course

The river begins at the confluence of Birch Creek and North Creek, west of the town of Escalante, and winds east and then south through the Escalante Canyons section of the Grand Staircase–Escalante National Monument. Along its course it is joined by numerous tributaries, including Pine Creek, the Boulder Creek system, Harris Wash, and the gulches of the Straight Cliffs. The river is famous for its deeply incised, winding sandstone canyons, including Death Hollow, which joins via Mammoth Creek.

History

The Escalante River is believed to be the last major river in the contiguous United States to be discovered and mapped; it was not charted until 1872, during the second Powell expedition under Almon Thompson. The river and town were named for Silvestre Vélez de Escalante, a Franciscan friar who crossed the region in the Domínguez–Escalante expedition of 1776, though he likely never saw the river itself.

Recreation and conservation

The river's canyons are a popular backpacking and packrafting destination. The riparian corridor supports Fremont cottonwood groves and is critical habitat in an otherwise arid landscape. Much of the drainage lies within the Grand Staircase–Escalante National Monument, designated in 1996, and portions near the river are within designated wilderness study areas. Invasive Russian olive and tamarisk removal has been a focus of restoration groups such as the Escalante River Watershed Partnership.
