Pishtaq

A pishtaq (Persian: پیشطاق, romanized: pišṭāq) is the formal rectangular frame around an arched iwan — a monumental entrance portal — in Islamic architecture. The term is used mainly in Persian and Central Asian architecture. The pishtaq usually rises above the roofline of the surrounding building and is richly decorated with calligraphy, glazed tilework, and geometric patterns.

Description

The pishtaq consists of a tall rectangular frame containing a pointed arch, within which the actual doorway sits, often opening onto a vaulted iwan hall. Vertical bands of Arabic or Persian calligraphy, commonly Quranic verses or foundation inscriptions, frame the arch. The spandrels are frequently decorated with arabesque or floral tile mosaics, and the whole portal is often flanked by slender turrets or engaged columns.

History and examples

The form developed in Persia and became a hallmark of Seljuk, Timurid, Safavid, and Mughal architecture. Famous examples include the pishtaqs of the madrasas of the Registan in Samarkand, the Shah Mosque in Isfahan, the Gur-e Amir mausoleum, and the gateway of the Taj Mahal complex in Agra, whose Great Gate features a monumental pishtaq faced in red sandstone with white marble inlay.

In Mughal India, the pishtaq became central to the charbagh tomb and mosque design, often paired with a central dome and four corner minarets. The form continues to be used in modern mosques and civic buildings across the Muslim world.
