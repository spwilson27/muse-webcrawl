Naïve realism

Naïve realism
FieldPhilosophy of perception
Also calledDirect realism, common-sense realism
Rival viewsRepresentative realism, idealism

Naïve realism, also known as direct realism or common-sense realism, is the view in the philosophy of perception that the senses provide us with direct awareness of objects as they really are. On this view, when we perceive a red apple, the redness and shape we experience are genuine properties of the apple itself, not properties of an internal mental image or representation.

Varieties

Contemporary defenders often hold a relational or disjunctivist version: veridical perception is a relation between a perceiver and mind-independent objects and their properties, while hallucinations and illusions are fundamentally different states, not instances of the same kind of experience with different content. Philosophers associated with versions of the view include John McDowell, Bill Brewer, and M. G. F. Martin.

Objections

The classic challenge is the argument from illusion: a straight stick looks bent in water, so what is directly experienced seems to be something bent, which the stick is not. Perspectival variation (a coin looks elliptical from an angle) and perceptual constancy phenomena pose related problems. Idealists and representative realists, from Berkeley and Locke onward, have argued that such cases show we are directly aware only of our own ideas or sense-data.
