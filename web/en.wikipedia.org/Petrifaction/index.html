Petrifaction

A polished section of petrified wood showing preserved growth rings.

In geology, petrifaction or petrification (from Ancient Greek pétra, "rock, stone") is the process by which organic material becomes a fossil through the replacement of the original material and the filling of the original pore spaces with minerals. Petrified wood is the most well-known result of this process, but all organisms, from bacteria to vertebrates, can become petrified.

Processes

Petrifaction takes place through a combination of two similar processes:

  • Permineralization — Mineral-laden groundwater fills the empty spaces within an organism (such as the cell lumina in wood or the pores of bone) and precipitates minerals there, reinforcing the original structure without destroying it.
  • Replacement — The original material is dissolved and replaced molecule by molecule by minerals such as silica (chalcedony or opal), calcite, pyrite, or hematite, preserving fine anatomical detail in a new substance.

Silicification is the most common pathway for wood; it often begins when trees are buried rapidly by volcanic ash, whose silica-rich waters permeate the buried logs. Because petrifaction can replicate cellular structure in extraordinary detail, petrified specimens are scientifically valuable for reconstructing ancient anatomy and environments.

Petrified forests

Famous deposits include Petrified Forest National Park in Arizona, the Yellowstone petrified forests, the Lesbos petrified forest in Greece, and petrified forests in Argentina, Namibia, and Indonesia. Under the right chemical conditions, petrifaction can occur remarkably quickly in geological terms, and artificial petrifaction has been produced in laboratories using silicate solutions.

In folklore

Petrifaction is a common theme in mythology and folklore: creatures such as Medusa, the basilisk, and the cockatrice were believed to turn living beings to stone, and many natural stone formations have been explained in legend as petrified people or animals.
