Shell game


The shell game as performed with three walnut shells and a small pea.

The shell game (also known as thimblerig, three shells and a pea, and the old army game) is a classic confidence trick disguised as a game of chance. It involves three identical containers (traditionally walnut shells, thimbles, or cups) and a small soft ball or pea. The operator shuffles the shells rapidly, and the mark is invited to bet on which shell hides the ball.

How the trick works

Although the game appears winnable, the operator uses sleight of hand to control the pea: it can be palmed, slipped under a different shell, or removed entirely, so the mark can only win when the operator wishes to encourage further betting. Confederates known as shills pretend to play and win, drawing in genuine victims. The closely related three-card Monte applies the same principles with playing cards.

History

Versions of the game date to ancient times; a form using cups and balls appears in ancient Egyptian and Roman entertainment and is one of the oldest tricks in conjuring. The shell game became notorious in the 19th century in the United States, where operators such as Canada Bill Jones worked riverboats and frontier towns. "Shell game" has since entered the language as a metaphor for any deceptive reshuffling that conceals where money or assets really are.

Legality

Because the outcome is controlled by fraud rather than chance, the shell game is illegal as gambling fraud in most jurisdictions, and street performances on tourist thoroughfares are a perennial target of police enforcement. As pure entertainment, however, the cups and balls routine remains a staple of close-up magic.
