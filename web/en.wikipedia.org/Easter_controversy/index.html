Easter controversy

The Easter controversy refers to a series of disputes in early Christianity over the correct date for celebrating Easter, the feast of the Resurrection of Jesus. The principal controversy, known as the Quartodeciman controversy, pitted churches in the Roman province of Asia, which observed the feast on the 14th day of the Jewish month of Nisan regardless of the day of the week, against the church of Rome and others, which held that Easter should always fall on a Sunday.

The Quartodeciman controversy

The Quartodecimans (from the Latin for "fourteenth") ended their Lenten fast and celebrated the Pascha on 14 Nisan, the date of Passover, following the practice they attributed to the apostle John and Philip. Other churches celebrated on the Sunday following 14 Nisan, emphasizing the day of the Resurrection. Around 155 AD, Bishop Anicetus of Rome and Polycarp of Smyrna discussed the difference but parted in peace. Around 190, Pope Victor I attempted to excommunicate the Asian churches for the Quartodeciman practice; Irenaeus of Lyons intervened, urging tolerance, and the threatened excommunication was not carried through.

Council of Nicaea

The First Council of Nicaea in 325 decreed that Easter should be celebrated by all churches on the same Sunday and independently of the Jewish calendar. The council's decision, communicated in a letter of Constantine, established the principle of a common date, though it did not specify a complete computational method. Over subsequent centuries the computus developed at Alexandria — Easter as the first Sunday after the first full moon on or after the spring equinox (fixed as March 21) — became standard in the West.

Later disputes

Competing tables, notably the 84-year cycle used in the British Isles and the cycles of Victorius of Aquitaine and Dionysius Exiguus, caused further regional differences. In Britain and Ireland the Celtic practice conflicted with the Roman dating until the Synod of Whitby in 664 adopted the Roman computus in Northumbria; holdouts such as Iona conformed by 716. The Gregorian calendar reform of 1582, which Eastern Orthodox churches did not adopt for liturgical purposes, perpetuated different Easter dates between Western and Eastern Christianity, a divergence that persists to the present day and occasionally coincides. Modern ecumenical discussions, including a 1997 consultation in Aleppo, have proposed a common date, but no change has been adopted.
