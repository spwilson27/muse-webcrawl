Moon type

Moon type, also known as the Moon system of embossed reading or Moon code, is a system of raised-line writing for blind and partially sighted people, developed in the 1840s by Dr William Moon of Brighton, England. Unlike braille, which uses dot patterns, Moon type uses simplified, partially sight-like raised characters derived from the ordinary printed alphabet, embossed on thick paper.

History

William Moon (1818–1894), who lost his sight at the age of 21, found the embossed reading systems of his day difficult and set out to design characters that resembled ordinary letters and could be read by touch. He published his system in the 1840s and founded the Moon's Society for Embossing and Supplying Literature to the Blind in Brighton, which printed religious texts, classics, and periodicals, and distributed them worldwide.

Characteristics

Moon characters are raised line forms, each up to about a centimetre tall, read by tracing with the fingertip. Because the letters retain a resemblance to print shapes, the system is often found easier to learn by people who lose their sight later in life and already know the printed alphabet, and by readers with reduced fingertip sensitivity, including some elderly readers and people with diabetic neuropathy. Its disadvantages are bulk and slowness: Moon books are large, production is expensive, and reading speeds are lower than fluent braille.

Use today

Moon type never displaced braille as the primary tactile literacy medium, but it continues to serve a niche readership in the United Kingdom and elsewhere, with material produced by specialist presses and modern embossers. It is recognised alongside braille in UK provision for visually impaired readers.
