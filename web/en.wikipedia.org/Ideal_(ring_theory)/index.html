Ideal (ring theory)

In mathematics, and more specifically in ring theory, an ideal of a ring is a special subset of its elements. Ideals generalise certain subsets of the integers, such as the even numbers or the multiples of 3, and they allow the construction of quotient rings in analogy to factor groups in group theory and to modular arithmetic.

Definition. Let R be a ring. A subset I of R is a left ideal if it is an additive subgroup of R and, for every r ∈ R and x ∈ I, the product rx belongs to I. A right ideal is defined symmetrically (with xr ∈ I); a two-sided ideal is both. In a commutative ring all three notions coincide.

History

Ernst Kummer introduced "ideal numbers" in the 1840s to restore unique factorisation in cyclotomic fields. Richard Dedekind redefined the concept as sets of numbers (1871) and extended it to rings of algebraic integers in general algebraic number fields, showing that ideals of such rings factor uniquely into prime ideals even when elements do not. Emmy Noether later placed ideals at the centre of modern commutative algebra, and ideals play a corresponding role in algebraic geometry through the ideal–variety correspondence.

Types of ideals

  • Principal ideal — generated by a single element a, written (a); in ℤ the ideals are exactly (n) = nℤ.
  • Prime ideal — a proper ideal P such that if ab ∈ P then a ∈ P or b ∈ P; equivalently the quotient R/P is an integral domain.
  • Maximal ideal — a proper ideal contained in no larger proper ideal; equivalently R/M is a field.
  • Primary, irreducible, radical ideals — refinements used in decomposition theorems such as the Lasker–Noether theorem.

Operations and quotients

Ideals can be added, multiplied and intersected; every ideal is the kernel of a ring homomorphism, and conversely every kernel is an ideal, which is why ideals are exactly the subsets by which one can quotient a ring. The Chinese remainder theorem relates quotients by products of comaximal ideals. Sets of ideals ordered by inclusion form a lattice, a central object in Hilbert's basis theorem arguments — every ideal of a Noetherian ring is finitely generated.
