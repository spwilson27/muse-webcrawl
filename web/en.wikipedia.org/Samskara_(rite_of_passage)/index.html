Samskara (Sanskrit: संस्कार, saṃskāra) is a series of rites of passage in Hinduism that mark and sanctify the major stages of an individual's life, from conception to death. The word derives from sam ("well, completely") and kara ("making, doing"), conveying the sense of refining or perfecting a person. The rites are described in the Grihya Sutras and later Dharmashastra literature, and their performance is traditionally linked to the twice-born (dvija) varnas.

The sixteen samskaras

Although lists vary between texts and regions, the most commonly cited enumeration is of sixteen rites, the Shodasha Samskaras. They include Garbhadhana (conception), Pumsavana (praying for the unborn child), Simantonnayana (parting of the mother's hair), Jatakarman (birth rites), Namakarana (naming), Nishkramana (first outing), Annaprashana (first solid food), Chudakarana (first haircut), Karnavedha (ear piercing), Vidyarambha (beginning of learning), Upanayana (the sacred-thread ceremony, marking formal education), Vedarambha (study of the Vedas), Keshanta (first shaving), Samavartana (graduation and return home), Vivaha (marriage) and Antyeshti (funeral rites).

Purpose and significance

The samskaras are understood both as sacraments that purify and as social markers that integrate the individual into family and community at each life stage. Traditionally they were accompanied by offerings into the sacred fire (homa), Vedic mantras, and feasting of relatives and priests. The Upanayana in particular has historically been a defining rite, initiating boys of the Brahmin, Kshatriya and Vaishya varnas into studentship.

Modern practice

In contemporary Hindu practice, the number of samskaras actually observed has contracted; birth, naming, first-feeding, sacred-thread, marriage and funeral rites remain the most widely performed, with considerable regional and family variation. Reform movements have variously simplified the rituals or extended some of them, such as Upanayana, to girls and to all varnas.
