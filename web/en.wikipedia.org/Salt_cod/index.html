Salt cod is cod (usually Atlantic cod, Gadus morhua) that has been preserved by salting and drying. Produced for over 500 years in the North Atlantic fisheries, it was a staple trade good of the Atlantic world: cheap, durable protein that could be stored for months without refrigeration and shipped across oceans.

Production

Fish are gutted, headed and split, then salted in layers for weeks, washed, and dried on wooden racks or flakes, traditionally outdoors in cold coastal climates such as the Lofoten Islands of Norway. The related but distinct stokkfisk (stockfish) is dried without salt. Before cooking, salt cod must be soaked in water for one to three days, with changes of water, to rehydrate and desalinate it.

History

Basque, Portuguese, English and French fleets fished the Grand Banks of Newfoundland from the 16th century, and salt cod became a cornerstone of trade between Europe, Africa and the Caribbean — including the grim "triangular trade", in which lower grades fed enslaved people on plantations. Cod wealth shaped Newfoundland, Iceland and coastal Norway, and cod disputes ("Cod Wars") later flared between Iceland and the United Kingdom.

Culinary use

Salt cod is central to Portuguese cooking — tradition claims 365 recipes for bacalhau, from bacalhau à Brás to Christmas Eve dishes — and to Spanish, Basque, Provençal (brandade), Italian and Caribbean cuisines, including Jamaica's national dish, ackee and saltfish.
