Smoking (cooking)

Food being smoked in a smokehouse

Smoking is the process of flavouring, browning, cooking, or preserving food by exposing it to smoke from burning or smouldering material, most often wood. Meat, fish and cheeses are the foods most commonly smoked. In Europe, alder is the traditional smoking wood, but oak is now more common; in North America, hickory, mesquite, oak, pecan, apple and cherry woods are widely used.

Methods

Cold smoking exposes food to smoke at temperatures of roughly 20–30 °C (68–86 °F), flavouring and preserving without cooking; it is used for salmon (lox), cheese and some sausages, often after curing. Hot smoking takes place at 52–80 °C (126–176 °F) and fully cooks the food while flavouring it. Smoke roasting or pit barbecuing combines smoking with higher-temperature roasting, as in American barbecue, where meats such as brisket and pork shoulder are cooked low and slow for many hours.

Preservation and chemistry

Smoke deposits phenols and other antimicrobial and antioxidant compounds on food surfaces, inhibits bacterial growth, and the accompanying drying further extends shelf life — historically the main purpose of the technique. Smoke also imparts colour through the Maillard reaction and carbonyl compounds. Because wood smoke contains polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, some of which are carcinogenic, commercial production is regulated in many countries; liquid smoke is an alternative flavouring made by condensing smoke into water.
