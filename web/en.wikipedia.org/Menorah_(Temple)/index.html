Menorah (Temple)

The menorah (Hebrew: מְנוֹרָה) is described in the Hebrew Bible as the seven-lamp ancient Hebrew lampstand made of pure gold and used in the tabernacle set up by Moses in the wilderness and later in the Temple in Jerusalem. Fresh olive oil was burned daily to light its lamps. The menorah has been a symbol of Judaism since ancient times and is the emblem on the coat of arms of the modern State of Israel.

Biblical description

The Book of Exodus (chapter 25) gives detailed instructions for the menorah: hammered from a single talent of pure gold, with a central shaft and three branches on each side, decorated with cups, knobs and flowers shaped like almond blossoms, and seven lamps in total. It stood in the Holy Place of the tabernacle opposite the table of showbread.

History

A menorah stood in the First Temple and again in the Second Temple, where its daily lighting was a central priestly duty; the Hanukkah story concerns the rededication of the Temple and the relighting of the menorah after its desecration under Antiochus IV. After the Roman destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE, the menorah was carried to Rome in triumph, as depicted on the Arch of Titus, where it is shown with curved branches. Its subsequent fate is unknown and the subject of many legends.

Symbolism

Commentators have read the seven lamps as representing the seven days of creation, the planets, or divine light and knowledge. The nine-branched Hanukkah menorah (hanukkiah) is a distinct ritual object commemorating the eight days of Hanukkah plus a helper light.
