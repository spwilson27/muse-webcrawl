Operant conditioning

Operant conditioning, also called instrumental conditioning, is a learning process in which the likelihood of a voluntary behaviour is modified by its consequences — reinforcement or punishment. It differs from classical conditioning, which pairs stimuli to shape involuntary responses. The concept was developed principally by the American psychologist B. F. Skinner, building on Edward Thorndike's "law of effect".

A Skinner box used to study operant behaviour in rats and pigeons.

Key concepts

Reinforcement

Reinforcement increases the frequency of a behaviour. Positive reinforcement adds a desirable stimulus (such as food or praise); negative reinforcement removes an aversive stimulus (such as stopping an electric shock), which also strengthens behaviour.

Punishment

Punishment decreases a behaviour's frequency: positive punishment adds an aversive stimulus, while negative punishment removes a desirable one. Extinction occurs when a previously reinforced behaviour is no longer reinforced and gradually declines.

Shaping and schedules

Shaping is the reinforcement of successive approximations toward a target behaviour. Reinforcement may follow fixed or variable schedules of ratio (responses) or interval (time), which produce characteristic patterns of responding; variable-ratio schedules, as in gambling, produce especially persistent behaviour.

The Skinner box

Skinner studied animals in an "operant chamber" or Skinner box, in which a rat's lever press or a pigeon's key peck could deliver food. His work underpinned behaviourism, programmed learning and behaviour modification, and remains influential in animal training, education and behavioural therapy.
