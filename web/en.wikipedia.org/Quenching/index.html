Quenching

In materials science, quenching is the rapid cooling of a workpiece in water, oil, gas or air to obtain certain material properties. A type of heat treating, quenching prevents undesired low-temperature processes, such as phase transformations, from occurring by reducing the time window during which the material is in the thermodynamically unstable state where such transformations are favourable.


Quenching a heated steel part

Quench hardening of steel

For steel, quenching from the austenitic temperature range traps carbon in a distorted body-centred tetragonal structure called martensite, which is extremely hard and strong but brittle. The part is usually subsequently tempered by reheating to a lower temperature, trading some hardness for much greater toughness. The cooling medium controls the severity of the quench: brine and water cool fastest, oils more slowly, and still or forced air slowest, with the choice balancing hardness against the risk of distortion and cracking.

Stages and technique

When a hot part is immersed in a liquid quenchant, three stages occur: a vapour blanket first insulates the part, then collapses into violent nucleate boiling that removes heat rapidly, and finally convection takes over as the surface cools below the liquid's boiling point. Polymer quenchants and interrupted quenching techniques such as martempering and austempering are used to control these stages. Quenching is also applied to aluminium alloys (followed by age hardening) and to glasses, where rapid cooling from the outside produces the compressive surface stresses exploited in tempered glass.
