The Fianna (singular fian) were bands of young, landless warriors in early medieval Ireland who lived by hunting and raiding, outside the normal structures of kinship and the tuath (kingdom). In Irish mythology and later folklore, the term refers above all to the celebrated warband led by the hero Fionn mac Cumhaill, whose exploits form the core of the Fenian Cycle of Irish literature.

Historical fianna

Early Irish law texts describe the fian as a recognised institution: a company of youths, often the sons of nobles who had not yet inherited land, who spent a period living in the wilderness, hunting, guarding borders, and fighting for hire. Membership was a stage of life rather than a permanent profession; in time a member might marry, inherit, and settle, or die in the wild. Fianna were associated with the liminal world beyond settled society, and their leaders, called rígféinnid, could command considerable followings.

The mythological Fianna

In legend, the Fianna of Fionn mac Cumhaill served the High King of Ireland at Tara, defending the country against invaders while feuding with rival clans such as the sons of Morna. Famous members included Fionn's son Oisín the poet, his grandson Oscar, the swift-footed Caílte mac Rónáin, the trickster Conan Maol, and the tragic lovers Diarmuid Ua Duibhne and Gráinne. Their stories were preserved in medieval manuscripts such as the twelfth-century Acallam na Senórach ("The Colloquy of the Ancients") and in oral tradition in Ireland and Gaelic Scotland for centuries.

Admission tests

Later tradition gave the Fianna famous trials of skill: a candidate had to defend himself in a pit against nine spearmen, run through a forest without breaking a twig or having a braid of hair disturbed, leap a branch at head height, stoop under one at knee height, and extract a thorn from his foot while running — as well as master poetry and renounce vengeance against the band.

Legacy

The word survived in Irish political vocabulary: the nineteenth- and twentieth-century Fenian movement took its name from the Fianna, as did the Fianna Fáil party, the republican youth organisation Fianna Éireann, and the Irish military cap badge inscribed "FF" for Fianna Fáil — "Soldiers of Destiny".
