Adaptive optics

Adaptive optics (AO) is a technology used to improve the performance of optical systems by reducing the effect of incoming wavefront distortions. It works by measuring the distortions in a wavefront and compensating for them with a device, usually a deformable mirror, that has a shape able to counteract them. Adaptive optics is used in astronomy to correct for the blurring effect of the Earth's atmosphere, as well as in microscopy, ophthalmology (retinal imaging), and free-space optical communication.

Principle

An adaptive optics system has three main components: a wavefront sensor (commonly a Shack–Hartmann sensor) that measures the aberration of incoming light, a deformable mirror whose surface is shaped by many actuators, and a control system that computes the mirror shape needed to cancel the measured distortion, typically updating hundreds or thousands of times per second. Because a bright reference source is needed to measure the wavefront, astronomers often create an artificial laser guide star by exciting sodium atoms in the upper atmosphere.

History

The concept was proposed by Horace Babcock in 1953, but the technology matured only in the 1990s, after declassification of military work done during the Cold War and advances in computing and deformable mirrors. AO is now standard on large ground-based telescopes such as the Keck, VLT and Gemini observatories, allowing them to approach the diffraction limit and, in the near-infrared, to exceed the sharpness of images from the Hubble Space Telescope.

Other applications

In ophthalmology, AO combined with scanning laser ophthalmoscopy permits imaging of individual photoreceptor cells in the living human retina. It is also used to correct aberrations in microscopy, high-power lasers, and optical data storage.
