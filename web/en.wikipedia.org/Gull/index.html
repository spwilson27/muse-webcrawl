Gull

Gulls, often called seagulls, are seabirds of the family Laridae, closely related to terns and skimmers. There are more than 50 species, distributed worldwide from the Arctic to the Antarctic, ranging from the small little gull to the great black-backed gull with a wingspan of about 1.6 m.

Description

Most gulls are white or grey with black markings on the wings and head, with stout, slightly hooked bills and webbed feet. They are highly adaptable generalists: they catch fish and marine invertebrates, scavenge carrion and refuse, steal food from other birds (kleptoparasitism), and increasingly exploit human food waste inland. Many species are long-lived — wild gulls may survive over 30 years — and mate for life.

Behaviour

Gulls breed in colonies on coasts, islands and increasingly on urban rooftops, defending small territories around the nest. They are intelligent and social, with complex calls and displays; herring gull chicks peck at the red spot on the parent's bill to stimulate feeding, a classic example in ethology. Several species have expanded dramatically in cities, where conflicts over noise, droppings and snatched food are common.
