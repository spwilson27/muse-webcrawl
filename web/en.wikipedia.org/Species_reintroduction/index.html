Species reintroduction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Species reintroduction is the deliberate release of a species into the wild, from captivity or other areas where the organism is capable of survival. It is part of conservation translocation, a set of techniques used in conservation biology to restore populations of plants and animals that have been extirpated from part or all of their historical range. The goal of species reintroduction is to establish a healthy, genetically diverse, self-sustaining population in an area where it has been extirpated, or to augment an existing population.

Species that may be eligible for reintroduction are typically threatened or endangered in the wild. Reintroduction of a species can also be used for pest control; for example, wolves being reintroduced to a wild area to curb an overpopulation of deer. Because reintroduction may involve returning native species to localities where they had been extirpated, some prefer the term "reestablishment".

History

One of the earliest successful reintroduction programs involved the American bison, which was restored to parts of its former range in the early twentieth century. The Arabian oryx, extinct in the wild by 1972, was reintroduced to Oman beginning in 1982 under Operation Oryx. The California condor was brought back from a total population of 27 birds through captive breeding and release. The reintroduction of grey wolves to Yellowstone National Park in 1995 is often cited as a landmark case, credited with triggering a trophic cascade that altered the park's ecology.

Sources of specimens

Individuals for release may come from captive breeding programs in zoos, from wild populations elsewhere (wild-to-wild translocation), or from propagation of plants in botanical gardens. Genetic considerations are important: source populations should be genetically similar to the original native stock and diverse enough to avoid inbreeding depression.

Challenges

Reintroductions frequently fail. Released animals may lack survival skills, fail to find mates, or encounter the same threats — habitat loss, hunting, disease, or human-wildlife conflict — that caused the original decline. Successful programs therefore require habitat assessment, threat removal, long-term monitoring, and often "soft release" methods in which animals are acclimatized gradually.

Notable examples

Well-known reintroductions include the peregrine falcon in North America, the golden lion tamarin in Brazil, the Przewalski's horse in Mongolia, the black-footed ferret in the Great Plains, the beaver in parts of Europe, and the lynx in several European mountain ranges. The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) publishes guidelines for reintroductions and other conservation translocations.
