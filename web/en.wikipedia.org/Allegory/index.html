Allegory is a form of representation in literature, art and thought in which characters, events and settings stand for abstract ideas, moral qualities or political realities, so that a narrative carries a second, deeper meaning beyond its surface story. The word derives from the Greek allēgoria, "speaking otherwise than openly".

History

Allegorical interpretation is ancient: Homer's epics were read allegorically in classical antiquity, and Plato used allegory deliberately, most famously in the Allegory of the Cave in the Republic. Allegory flourished in the Middle Ages, when the Bible was read on four levels (literal, allegorical, moral and anagogical) and personification allegories such as Prudentius' Psychomachia and The Romance of the Rose shaped European letters. Dante's Divine Comedy, Spenser's The Faerie Queene and Bunyan's The Pilgrim's Progress (1678) are monuments of the form.

Modern allegory

Though Enlightenment and Romantic critics often demoted allegory below the symbol, it persisted in political satire and fable. George Orwell's Animal Farm (1945) is the classic modern political allegory, mapping the Russian Revolution and Stalinism onto a farmyard tale. Twentieth-century criticism (notably Walter Benjamin and Angus Fletcher) revived theoretical interest in allegory, and it remains a central device in fantasy, film and graphic narrative.

Types and related devices

Allegory differs from metaphor and symbolism by its sustained, systematic correspondence across a whole narrative. Related forms include the fable (with animal actors and an explicit moral), the parable, and personification allegory, in which abstractions such as Justice, Death or Everyman act as characters.
