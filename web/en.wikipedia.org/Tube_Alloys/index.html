Tube Alloys was the research and development programme authorised by the United Kingdom, with participation from Canada, to develop nuclear weapons during the Second World War. Starting before the American Manhattan Project, it was the first national programme aimed at building an atomic bomb. Because of its sensitivity, the project was given the deliberately misleading cover name "Tube Alloys".

Origins

In 1940, émigré physicists Otto Frisch and Rudolf Peierls at the University of Birmingham calculated that only a small quantity of uranium-235 would be needed for a bomb, showing that an atomic weapon was practical. Their memorandum prompted the creation of the MAUD Committee, whose 1941 reports concluded that a bomb could be built in time to affect the war. The British government accordingly set up the Tube Alloys directorate.

Merger with the Manhattan Project

Britain shared its research with the United States, but the American programme quickly outgrew it. After the Quebec Agreement of 1943, Tube Alloys was subsumed into the Manhattan Project, and British scientists—including James Chadwick, Peierls and Klaus Fuchs—worked at Los Alamos and other sites. A joint Anglo-Canadian laboratory in Montreal worked on reactor design.

Post-war programme

After the war the United States ended collaboration under the McMahon Act of 1946. Britain therefore revived an independent programme, drawing on Tube Alloys' work, which led to the first British atomic test, Operation Hurricane, in 1952.
