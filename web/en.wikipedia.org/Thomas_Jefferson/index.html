Thomas Jefferson (13 April 1743 – 4 July 1826) was an American statesman, diplomat, lawyer, architect, philosopher and Founding Father who served as the third president of the United States from 1801 to 1809. He was the primary author of the Declaration of Independence (1776) and is regarded as one of the most influential promoters of republicanism and the ideals of the Enlightenment in America.

Early life and education

Jefferson was born at Shadwell, Virginia, the third of ten children of Peter Jefferson, a planter and surveyor, and Jane Randolph, of one of Virginia's most prominent families. He studied at the College of William & Mary, read law under George Wythe, and was admitted to the bar in 1767. He inherited a large estate, including enslaved people, from his father and later from his father-in-law John Wayles, and built his neoclassical mansion Monticello on a mountaintop near Charlottesville.

Revolutionary career

Jefferson entered politics in the Virginia House of Burgesses in 1769. His pamphlet A Summary View of the Rights of British America (1774) argued that the colonies were bound to Britain only through the king. As a delegate to the Second Continental Congress he was chosen to draft the Declaration of Independence; its preamble, proclaiming that "all men are created equal" with rights to "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness", became one of the most famous statements of human rights. During the Revolutionary War he served as governor of Virginia (1779–1781).

Minister to France and Secretary of State

Jefferson succeeded Benjamin Franklin as minister to France in 1785, witnessing the opening stages of the French Revolution. Returning in 1790, he became George Washington's first Secretary of State, clashing with Alexander Hamilton over foreign policy, the national bank and the scope of federal power. He resigned in 1793 and organised the opposition Democratic-Republican Party with James Madison.

Presidency (1801–1809)

After the deadlocked election of 1800, Jefferson was chosen president by the House of Representatives — the "Revolution of 1800". He reduced military spending, cut taxes, and repealed the Alien and Sedition Acts' remaining provisions. In 1803 his administration completed the Louisiana Purchase from France, nearly doubling the territory of the United States for about $15 million, and dispatched the Lewis and Clark Expedition to explore the new lands. Re-elected in 1804, his second term was dominated by the tensions leading toward the War of 1812; his Embargo Act of 1807, intended to keep America neutral between Britain and France, badly damaged the U.S. economy and was repealed as he left office.

Later life and legacy

In retirement at Monticello, Jefferson founded the University of Virginia (1819) and designed its buildings and curriculum. He died on 4 July 1826 — the fiftieth anniversary of the Declaration — hours before his old rival and friend John Adams. Jefferson's legacy is double-edged: he articulated universal ideals of liberty while enslaving more than 600 people over his lifetime, and scholars widely accept that he fathered children with Sally Hemings, an enslaved woman at Monticello. His face appears on the nickel and Mount Rushmore.
