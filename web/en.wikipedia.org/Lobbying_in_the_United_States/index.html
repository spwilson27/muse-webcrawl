Lobbying in the United States

Lobbying in the United States is the activity of attempting to influence the actions, policies, or decisions of government officials — most commonly legislators and regulators — on behalf of corporations, trade associations, labor unions, advocacy groups, and other interests. Lobbying is protected as petitioning the government under the First Amendment, but it is regulated by disclosure laws at federal and state levels. Reported federal lobbying spending regularly exceeds $4 billion per year, and the word "lobbyist" derives from the lobbies of the Capitol and, in earlier lore, of Willard's Hotel in Washington, D.C.

History

Interest-group pressure is as old as the republic: James Madison warned of "factions" in The Federalist No. 10. Nineteenth-century railroads and trusts became notorious for buying influence, prompting state regulation and, eventually, federal laws. The Foreign Agents Registration Act (1938) targeted foreign propaganda; the Federal Regulation of Lobbying Act (1946) first required registration; the Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995 tightened definitions and reporting; and the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007, passed after the Jack Abramoff scandal, added gift bans and revolving-door restrictions.

Practice

Lobbyists provide research, draft legislation, testify at hearings, organize grassroots campaigns, and arrange meetings between clients and officials. Much of the profession is staffed by former legislators and staffers, whose expertise and connections are valuable — the "revolving door." The largest spenders are typically the U.S. Chamber of Commerce, health-care and pharmaceutical groups, technology firms, and defense contractors. Besides "direct" lobbying, firms run "grassroots" (astroturf and genuine) campaigns mobilizing voters to contact their representatives.

Debate

Defenders argue lobbyists supply expertise and represent interests that might otherwise be unheard; critics argue that moneyed interests distort policy, for instance in tax expenditures and regulation, and that reforms — closing loopholes, cooling-off periods, real-time disclosure — remain weak. Empirical scholarship finds lobbying most effective at blocking change and shaping low-visibility technical provisions.
