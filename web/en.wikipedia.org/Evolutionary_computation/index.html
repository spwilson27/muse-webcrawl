Evolutionary computation

FieldComputer science, artificial intelligence
Main familiesGenetic algorithms, evolution strategies, genetic programming, evolutionary programming
Key operatorsSelection, recombination (crossover), mutation
InspirationBiological evolution and natural selection

Evolutionary computation is a family of algorithms for global optimization inspired by biological evolution, and the subfield of artificial intelligence (more precisely, computational intelligence) that studies them. In a typical evolutionary algorithm, a population of candidate solutions to a problem is maintained; each candidate is scored by a fitness function, and new generations are produced by variation operators — chiefly mutation and recombination (crossover) — followed by selection favouring the fitter individuals. Over many generations the population tends to improve, discovering solutions that may be difficult to design by hand.

Main families

Historically, several schools developed in parallel: genetic algorithms (John Holland, 1970s) using encoded strings and crossover; evolution strategies (Ingo Rechenberg and Hans-Paul Schwefel, Germany) evolving real-valued parameters with self-adaptive mutation; evolutionary programming (Lawrence Fogel) evolving finite-state machines and later real-valued predictors; and genetic programming (John Koza) evolving computer programs, usually represented as trees. Modern practice blurs these boundaries, and related methods such as differential evolution and estimation-of-distribution algorithms belong to the same broad family.

Applications

Evolutionary algorithms are used wherever problems are rugged, discontinuous, poorly understood, or lack usable gradient information: antenna and aerodynamic design, timetabling and scheduling, circuit layout, financial modelling, robotics control, and hyperparameter tuning in machine learning. Evolved designs have occasionally surprised engineers — for example, the eccentric but high-performing antenna flown on NASA's ST5 mission. The no-free-lunch theorems caution that no optimizer beats all others across every problem; evolutionary methods earn their keep on problems matching their assumptions.
