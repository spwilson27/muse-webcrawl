Guillain–Barré syndrome

Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) is a rare autoimmune disorder in which the body's immune system attacks the peripheral nerves. It typically begins with weakness and tingling in the legs that spreads to the arms and upper body, and can progress over days to weeks to near-total paralysis and respiratory failure. It is the most common cause of acute flaccid paralysis in the developed world, with an incidence of roughly 1–2 cases per 100,000 people per year.

Causes

GBS usually follows an infection; the best-established trigger is the bacterium Campylobacter jejuni, a common cause of food poisoning, and influenza, cytomegalovirus, Epstein–Barr virus and Zika virus are also implicated. The immune response is thought to cross-react with gangliosides on peripheral nerve myelin or axons (molecular mimicry), producing the demyelinating form (AIDP) common in Europe and North America, or axonal forms (AMAN, AMSAN) more frequent in Asia and Latin America.

Diagnosis and course

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by cerebrospinal fluid showing raised protein with a normal white cell count ("albuminocytological dissociation") and by nerve conduction studies. Weakness reaches its worst within about four weeks; most patients then recover over months, though roughly a fifth are left with significant disability and mortality is around 3–7%, usually from respiratory or autonomic complications.

Treatment

Intravenous immunoglobulin and plasma exchange are equally effective and hasten recovery; corticosteroids are not beneficial. Care is supportive, with monitoring of breathing (a substantial minority need mechanical ventilation), blood pressure and heart rhythm, plus physiotherapy and rehabilitation.

History

The syndrome is named for French neurologists Georges Guillain and Jean Alexandre Barré, who described it with André Strohl in 1916 in two soldiers of the First World War.
