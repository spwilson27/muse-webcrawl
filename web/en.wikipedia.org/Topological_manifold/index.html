In topology, a branch of mathematics, a topological manifold is a topological space that locally resembles real n-dimensional Euclidean space. Topological manifolds form one of the most important classes of topological spaces, of central importance in geometry, topology and physics. A topological manifold is a locally Euclidean topological space satisfying additional regularity conditions — in most definitions, that it be Hausdorff and second countable.

Definition

A topological space X is called locally Euclidean of dimension n if every point has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open subset of Euclidean space Rn (or equivalently to the open unit ball). Such a homeomorphism is called a chart, and a collection of charts covering X is an atlas. A topological manifold of dimension n, or n-manifold, is a locally Euclidean space of dimension n that is also Hausdorff and second countable. Requiring the space to be Hausdorff prevents pathologies such as the "line with two origins", while second countability ensures, among other things, that the space admits partitions of unity and embeds in some finite-dimensional Euclidean space.

Examples

Euclidean space Rn itself is an n-manifold. The circle is a 1-manifold and the sphere, torus, cylinder and Möbius strip are 2-manifolds. Real and complex projective spaces, Grassmannians and Lie groups provide further examples in all dimensions. Manifolds with boundary — in which points may have neighbourhoods homeomorphic to a half-space — include the closed ball and the annulus.

Classification and theory

Manifolds of dimension 1 and 2 are completely classified: every connected compact 1-manifold is a circle, and compact surfaces are determined by orientability and genus (together with the number of boundary components). Three-manifolds are the subject of the geometrisation theorem proved by Grigori Perelman, which implies the Poincaré conjecture. In dimensions four and above classification is impossible in a strong algorithmic sense, and the study of manifolds divides into low-dimensional topology and the surgery-theoretic methods of high-dimensional topology. A foundational question is whether every topological manifold admits additional structure — smooth or piecewise-linear — which fails in general in high dimensions (the Hauptvermutung and triangulation problems), although every manifold of dimension at most three can be smoothed.
