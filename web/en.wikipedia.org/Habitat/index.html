Habitat

In ecology, a habitat is the type of natural environment in which a particular species of organism lives. It is characterised by both physical factors, such as soil, moisture, temperature range, and light, and biotic factors, including the availability of food and the presence or absence of predators. A species' habitat is the place where it can find food, shelter, protection, and mates for reproduction.

Types

Habitats are often grouped into broad biome categories — terrestrial habitats such as forests, grasslands, deserts, and tundra; freshwater habitats such as rivers, lakes, and wetlands; and marine habitats such as coral reefs, estuaries, and the deep sea. Smaller-scale microhabitats, such as the underside of a decaying log, can support specialised communities within a larger habitat.

Habitat and niche

A habitat is distinct from an ecological niche: the habitat is the organism's environment or "address", while the niche is its functional role and set of requirements within that environment. Several species may share a habitat while occupying different niches, reducing direct competition.

Conservation

Habitat loss — through destruction, degradation, and fragmentation — is the primary cause of species decline and extinction worldwide. Conservation strategies therefore emphasise protecting and restoring habitats, maintaining habitat corridors between isolated patches, and designating protected areas such as national parks and nature reserves.
