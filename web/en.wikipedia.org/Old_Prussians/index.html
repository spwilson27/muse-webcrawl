Old Prussians

The Old Prussians (Prussian: Prūsai) were a Baltic people inhabiting Prussia — the land between the lower Vistula and the lower Neman rivers, in what is now northeastern Poland and Russia's Kaliningrad Oblast. Organized into tribal groups such as the Sambians, Warmians, Bartians, and Galindians, they spoke Old Prussian, a West Baltic language related to Lithuanian and Latvian, and practised a pagan religion centred on sacred groves, fire, and the god Perkūns.

Conquest and assimilation

After failed Polish attempts at conquest and mission, the Teutonic Order launched the Prussian Crusade in the 1230s; over some fifty years of warfare, punctuated by great uprisings, the Order subdued the Prussian tribes, built castles, and settled German colonists. The Old Prussian language declined over the following centuries and became extinct in the early eighteenth century, leaving only three catechisms and a short word list (the Elbing Vocabulary) as its main records.

Legacy

The conquerors' state took the Prussians' name, and "Prussia" eventually became the name of the German kingdom that unified Germany — though its people were by then German-speaking. Since the late twentieth century, small revival movements have attempted to reconstruct the Old Prussian language from the surviving texts.
