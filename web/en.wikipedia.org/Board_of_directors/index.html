Board of directors

A board of directors is an executive committee that supervises the activities of an organisation, typically a corporation or a nonprofit organisation. It is elected by shareholders in a company (in the case of most for-profit corporations) or appointed according to the organisation's governing documents. The board's powers, duties, and responsibilities are determined by government regulations and the organisation's own constitution and bylaws. These authorities may specify the number of members of the board, how they are to be chosen, and how often they are to meet.

Boards typically meet periodically to review performance and set strategy.

Roles and responsibilities

In a stock corporation, the board is elected by the shareholders and is the highest authority in the management of the corporation. The board generally acts on behalf of the shareholders, owes them fiduciary duties of loyalty and care, and is responsible for appointing and overseeing the chief executive officer (CEO), setting broad strategy and risk appetite, approving major transactions and budgets, ensuring accurate financial reporting, and safeguarding the organisation's assets. Day-to-day management is delegated to executives; the board supervises rather than manages.

Structure

Boards commonly divide their work among committees, most importantly the audit committee (oversight of financial reporting and external audit), the remuneration or compensation committee (executive pay), and the nominating/governance committee (board composition and succession). Members may be executive directors (employees, such as the CEO) or non-executive directors; independent directors, who have no material relationship with the company, are considered essential for objective oversight in modern corporate governance codes.

In one-tier systems (common in the US and UK), executives and non-executives sit on a single board. In two-tier systems (common in Germany and the Netherlands), a management board runs the company under the supervision of a separate supervisory board.

Legal duties and liability

Directors' duties are codified in many jurisdictions—for example, sections 171–177 of the UK Companies Act 2006, and the fiduciary principles developed by the Delaware Court of Chancery in the United States. The business judgment rule protects directors who make informed, good-faith decisions from liability for outcomes that turn out badly. Reforms following corporate scandals such as Enron and WorldCom, including the US Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002, strengthened requirements for audit committee independence and internal controls.

Criticism and reform

Boards have been criticised for rubber-stamping management decisions, excessive executive pay, lack of diversity, and insufficient independence from dominant CEOs. Activist investors and stewardship codes have pushed for greater accountability, board refreshment, and disclosure. Diversity of gender, background, and expertise on boards is now mandated or encouraged in many countries.
