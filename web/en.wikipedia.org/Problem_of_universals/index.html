Problem of universals

FieldMetaphysics
Key positionsRealism; nominalism; conceptualism; trope theory
Key figuresPlato; Aristotle; Boethius; William of Ockham

The problem of universals is a central question in metaphysics concerning the status of general properties and kinds — universals such as redness, humanity, or triangularity — and their relation to the particular things that possess them. When many different objects are all red, is there a single entity, the universal redness, that each one instantiates, or is their similarity merely a matter of shared names or resemblances?

Main positions

Realism, descending from Plato's theory of Forms, holds that universals exist independently of particulars and of minds; Aristotle's moderate realism placed universals in the things themselves. Nominalism, associated with William of Ockham, denies that universals exist as entities at all: only particulars exist, and general terms are names (nomina) applied to many things. Conceptualism occupies a middle ground, treating universals as concepts formed by the mind. Modern trope theory replaces universals with particularised properties — this apple's individual redness — which resemble one another.

History

The problem was transmitted to medieval philosophy by Boethius's commentary on Porphyry's Isagoge, and the dispute between realists and nominalists became a defining controversy of scholasticism, with consequences for theology and logic. It remains live in contemporary philosophy, connected to questions about natural kinds, laws of nature, and abstract objects in science and mathematics.
