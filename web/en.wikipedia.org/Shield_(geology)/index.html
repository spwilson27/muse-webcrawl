Shield (geology)


Exposed ancient bedrock in the Canadian Shield, one of the world's largest shields.

A shield is a large area of exposed Precambrian crystalline igneous and high-grade metamorphic rocks that form the ancient geological core of a continent. Shields are the oldest parts of cratons, having survived for billions of years with little deformation since the Precambrian. The term was coined by Eduard Suess after the shield-like, gently convex shape of the Canadian Shield.

Characteristics

Shields are tectonically stable, with no significant seismic or volcanic activity, and have been little affected by plate movements since their formation. Their surfaces were deeply eroded over hundreds of millions of years, producing low-relief plains interrupted by rounded hills. Together with the sediment-covered platforms, shields make up the cratons that form the nuclei around which continents grew by accretion.

Examples

  • The Canadian Shield, covering about half of Canada and extending into the northern United States.
  • The Baltic (Fennoscandian) Shield of Finland, Sweden, Norway, and northwestern Russia.
  • The Aldan and Anabar shields of Siberia.
  • The Amazonian and São Francisco shields of South America.
  • The African shields, including the West African and Congo shields.
  • The Yilgarn and Pilbara blocks of the Australian Shield.

Economic significance

Because shields expose some of the oldest rocks on Earth at the surface, they are major sources of mineral wealth: nickel, gold, copper, iron ore, uranium, and diamonds are mined from shield regions in Canada, Australia, Africa, Brazil, and Russia. The ancient greenstone belts preserved within shields are especially prospective for gold and base metals.
