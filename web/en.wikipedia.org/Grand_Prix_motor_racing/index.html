Grand Prix motor racing

Grand Prix motor racing, a form of motorsport competition, has its roots in organised automobile racing that began in France in 1894. "Grand Prix" (French for "grand prize") came to denote the premier events of the sport, traditionally held over a weekend of practice, qualifying, and a race of at least 305 kilometres. Grand Prix racing evolved into Formula One, whose World Championship began in 1950, and the name is also used by other series, most notably Grand Prix motorcycle racing (MotoGP).

Origins

The first organised automobile competitions were reliability trials and city-to-city road races, such as Paris–Rouen in 1894. In 1906 the Automobile Club de France staged the first race formally called a Grand Prix, at Le Mans, won by Ferenc Szisz in a Renault. Other nations followed with their own Grands Prix — Italy, Belgium, Spain, Britain, Germany, and Monaco, whose 1929 street race became perhaps the most famous of all.

The championship era

A European Championship for drivers was contested in the 1930s, dominated by German "Silver Arrows" teams Mercedes-Benz and Auto Union and drivers such as Tazio Nuvolari and Rudolf Caracciola. After the Second World War, racing resumed under the new Formula One rules agreed in 1946–47, and in 1950 the FIA launched the World Championship of Drivers, beginning at Silverstone with Giuseppe Farina and Alfa Romeo as its first champion.

The format

A Grand Prix weekend traditionally includes practice sessions, a qualifying session determining the starting grid, and the race itself. The name "Grand Prix" is used beyond Formula One for rounds of IndyCar (e.g. the Grand Prix of Long Beach), MotoGP, and endurance racing.
