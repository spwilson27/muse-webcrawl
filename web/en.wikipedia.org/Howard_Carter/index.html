Howard Carter (9 May 1874 – 2 March 1939) was a British archaeologist and Egyptologist who became world-famous after discovering the intact tomb of the 18th Dynasty Pharaoh Tutankhamun in November 1922, the best-preserved pharaonic tomb ever found in the Valley of the Kings.

Early life

Carter was born in Kensington, London, the youngest child of Samuel John Carter, an artist, and Martha Joyce Carter. He grew up in Swaffham in Norfolk. Largely self-educated in art, his talent for drawing brought him to the attention of local patrons; the Amhersts of Didlington Hall sponsored his first trip to Egypt.

Career in Egypt

Carter first went to Egypt in 1891, at the age of 17, as a tracer and draughtsman for the Egypt Exploration Fund, copying tomb paintings at Beni Hasan. He worked under William Matthew Flinders Petrie at Amarna and later at Deir el-Bahari, recording the reliefs of Queen Hatshepsut's mortuary temple. In 1899 he was appointed Inspector-General of Monuments for Upper Egypt by the Egyptian Antiquities Service, overseeing work at Thebes and excavating in the Valley of the Kings.

After resigning in 1905 following a dispute with French tourists at Saqqara (the "Saqqara Affair"), Carter spent several years painting, dealing in antiquities, and guiding tourists, before being introduced in 1907 to George Herbert, 5th Earl of Carnarvon.

Discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb

Lord Carnarvon financed Carter's excavations in the Valley of the Kings from 1914, though work was interrupted by the First World War. By 1922, after years of fruitless searching, Carnarvon was prepared to end the funding. Carter persuaded him to allow one final season. On 4 November 1922, Carter's workmen uncovered a step cut into the bedrock beneath the tomb of Ramesses VI. Further excavation revealed a stairway leading to a sealed doorway bearing Tutankhamun's cartouches — tomb KV62.

On 26 November 1922, in Carnarvon's presence, Carter made a small breach in the doorway and peered inside by candlelight. When Carnarvon asked, "Can you see anything?", Carter replied, "Yes, wonderful things." The tomb proved to contain over 5,000 artefacts, including the pharaoh's solid-gold funerary mask, and it took Carter and his team nearly a decade to catalogue and clear it.

Later life and death

After completing the clearance of the tomb in 1932, Carter retired from excavation and became a part-time agent for collectors and museums, including the Cleveland Museum of Art and the Detroit Institute of Arts. He published a popular three-volume account, The Tomb of Tut.ankh.Amen (1923–1933), written with A. C. Mace. Carter died of Hodgkin's disease in London on 2 March 1939, aged 64, and was buried in Putney Vale Cemetery. His epitaph quotes the Wishing Cup from the tomb: "May your spirit live, may you spend millions of years, you who love Thebes, sitting with your face to the north wind, your eyes beholding happiness."
