Vaginal birth after caesarean

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A hospital delivery room — placeholder]

Vaginal birth after caesarean (VBAC) is the practice of birthing a baby vaginally after a previous baby has been delivered by caesarean section (C-section). A trial of labour after caesarean (TOLAC) succeeds in roughly 60–80% of cases, depending on the reason for the earlier caesarean, whether labour is induced, and whether the woman has had a previous vaginal birth.

Background

For much of the 20th century, the dictum "once a caesarean, always a caesarean" (associated with Edwin Cragin, 1916) governed obstetric practice, partly because classical vertical uterine incisions carried high rupture risk. With the widespread adoption of lower-segment transverse incisions, evidence from the 1980s onward showed that many women could safely attempt vaginal delivery. Guidelines from bodies such as ACOG and the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists support offering TOLAC to appropriately selected candidates.

Risks and benefits

The principal risk of TOLAC is uterine rupture at the scar site, occurring in roughly 0.5–1% of trials of labour after one low-transverse caesarean; rupture can endanger mother and baby and requires immediate surgical capability. Successful VBAC avoids major surgery, with shorter recovery and lower risks of infection and placental complications in future pregnancies than repeat caesarean. Elective repeat caesarean, by contrast, is scheduled and predictable but carries surgical risks that accumulate with each operation.

Eligibility

Candidates are typically women with one prior low-transverse caesarean, a clinically adequate pelvis, and no other uterine scars or contraindications to vaginal birth. Previous classical or T-shaped incisions, prior rupture, and certain fetal presentations generally rule out TOLAC.
