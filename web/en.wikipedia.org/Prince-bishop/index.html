Prince-bishop

A prince-bishop is a bishop who is also a civil prince and sovereign ruler of a territory, typically within the Holy Roman Empire. Prince-bishops combined spiritual authority over a diocese with temporal rule over a prince-bishopric (Hochstift), holding a seat and vote in the Imperial Diet. The most powerful were the three ecclesiastical electors — the archbishops of Mainz, Cologne, and Trier — who helped choose the Holy Roman Emperor.

Prince-bishops ruled territories such as Würzburg, Salzburg, and Liège.

Development

The institution grew from Ottonian and Salian imperial policy: emperors granted counties and regalian rights to loyal bishops, whose offices could not become hereditary and thus remained dependable pillars of imperial power — a system whose control over appointments provoked the Investiture Controversy of the 11th–12th centuries. Major prince-bishoprics included Salzburg (ruled by prince-archbishops), Würzburg, Bamberg, Münster, Passau, Liège, and the prince-bishoprics of the Baltic, such as Riga.

Abolition

The prince-bishoprics were swept away during the Napoleonic era: the German mediatisation of 1803 secularised nearly all ecclesiastical principalities, and Liège had already been annexed by France. A notable British parallel was the County Palatine of Durham, whose bishops held princely temporal powers until 1836. The sovereign Prince-Bishopric of Montenegro, ruled by vladikas of the Petrović-Njegoš dynasty, survived until 1852, and the Archbishop of Urgell remains ex officio co-prince of Andorra.
