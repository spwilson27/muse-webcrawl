Deep frying

Deep frying is a cooking method in which food is submerged in hot fat, usually oil, at temperatures typically between 160 °C and 190 °C (320–375 °F). The rapid heat transfer cooks food quickly and produces a crisp, golden surface.

Technique

When food enters hot oil, surface water boils off violently, and the escaping steam helps keep oil from soaking into the interior while a crust forms. Properly deep-fried food is therefore surprisingly low in absorbed fat; if the oil is too cool the crust forms slowly and the food becomes greasy, while overheated oil degrades and smokes. Foods are often coated in batter or breadcrumbs before frying. Common deep-fried foods include French fries, doughnuts, fried chicken, tempura and spring rolls.

History and health

Frying in olive oil was known in classical antiquity and in ancient Mesopotamia and China, and the technique spread worldwide with industrial seed oils and the electric deep fryer. Deep-fried foods are energy-dense, and reused oils accumulate polar compounds and, in starchy foods cooked at high temperature, acrylamide — reasons health authorities recommend moderation.
