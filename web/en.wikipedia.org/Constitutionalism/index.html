Constitutionalism

Constitutionalism
Related ideasRule of law, separation of powers, judicial review
Key textsThe Federalist Papers, Constitutional Government (McIlwain)

Constitutionalism is "a compound of ideas, attitudes, and patterns of behavior elaborating the principle that the authority of government derives from and is limited by a body of fundamental law". Political organizations are constitutional to the extent that they "contain institutionalized mechanisms of power control for the protection of the interests and liberties of the citizenry, including those that may be in the minority".

Meaning and usage

The term is used both descriptively — referring to how a polity is actually constrained by its constitution — and prescriptively, as a commitment that government power should be limited. Constitutionalism goes beyond the mere existence of a written constitution: a state may possess a constitution on paper while government is unconstrained in practice ("sham constitutionalism"). Core institutional devices associated with constitutionalism include an entrenched, supreme constitution, separation of powers, an independent judiciary with the power of judicial review, protected fundamental rights, and regular free elections.

History

Ideas of limited government trace to medieval notions of fundamental law and documents such as Magna Carta (1215). Modern constitutionalism developed with the English Civil War and Glorious Revolution, Locke's theory of government by consent, Montesquieu's doctrine of separated powers, and the American (1787) and French (1789–91) revolutionary constitutions. In the 20th century it spread globally, though scholars debate "new constitutionalism", abusive constitutionalism (using constitutional amendment to entrench power), and the rise of populist challenges to liberal constitutional orders.
