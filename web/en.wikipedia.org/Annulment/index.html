Annulment

Annulment is a legal procedure within secular and religious legal systems for declaring a marriage null and void. Unlike divorce, it is usually retroactive, meaning that an annulled marriage is considered to be invalid from the beginning almost as if it had never taken place (though some jurisdictions provide that the marriage is only void from the date of the annulment). In legal terminology, an annulment makes a void marriage or a voidable marriage null.

Grounds

Grounds for annulment vary by jurisdiction but commonly include lack of consent, fraud or misrepresentation, bigamy, underage marriage, incest, mental incapacity, duress, and failure to consummate the marriage. Because annulment treats the marriage as though it never existed, it can affect property division, legitimacy presumptions, and eligibility for spousal benefits differently from divorce.

Religious annulment

In the Roman Catholic Church, an ecclesiastical tribunal may issue a declaration of nullity, finding that a sacramental marriage never validly existed because an essential element was missing at the time of the wedding. Other faiths have their own procedures; in Judaism, for example, annulment (hafka'at kiddushin) is rare and exceptional, distinct from the get (divorce document).

History

Annulment played a prominent role in European history: the refusal of Pope Clement VII to annul the marriage of Henry VIII of England to Catherine of Aragon precipitated the English Reformation and the creation of the Church of England.
