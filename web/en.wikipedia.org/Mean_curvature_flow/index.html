Mean curvature flow

In the field of differential geometry, mean curvature flow is an example of a geometric flow of hypersurfaces in a Riemannian manifold — for instance, smooth surfaces in 3-dimensional Euclidean space. Intuitively, a family of surfaces evolves under mean curvature flow if the normal component of the velocity of a point on the surface is proportional to the mean curvature of the surface. For example, a round sphere evolves under mean curvature flow by shrinking inward uniformly in all directions; the sphere shrinks until it collapses to a point.

Evolution of a surface under mean curvature flow: convex regions shrink and the surface tends toward roundness before developing singularities.

Formal definition

Let x : Σ × [0, T) → Rn+1 be a smooth family of immersions of hypersurfaces. This family is a mean curvature flow if it satisfies the evolution equation

∂x/∂t = −Hν,

where H is the mean curvature and ν the unit normal of the hypersurface at the point x. Thus hypersurfaces move in the direction of their outward-pointing unit normal with speed proportional to the mean curvature. Mean curvature flow is the gradient flow for the area functional and is closely related to the heat equation — indeed, for graphs it takes the form of a nonlinear parabolic partial differential equation.

Properties

Like the heat flow, mean curvature flow smooths out irregularities and tends to make surfaces rounder. However, unlike the heat equation, it can develop singularities in finite time: a dumbbell-shaped surface, for instance, can pinch off at its neck, forming a singularity before the rest of the surface has shrunk away. Gerhard Huisken proved in 1984 that all convex, closed surfaces in Rn+1 evolve toward a round point under the flow. Richard Hamilton and others developed methods of surgery — continuing the flow past singularities — analogous to the techniques used with Ricci flow.

Applications

Mean curvature flow models physical phenomena such as the motion of grain boundaries in annealing metals and the evolution of interfaces in phase transitions (the Allen–Cahn equation approximates it). It is also used in image processing and computer graphics for surface smoothing and fairing.
