Monomer

A monomer (from Greek mono- "one" and -mer "part") is a molecule that can react together with other monomer molecules to form a larger polymer chain or three-dimensional network in a process called polymerization. Monomers are typically small molecules with low molecular weight. The word monomer came from Greek monos and meros, meaning "one part"; many monomers incorporate the concept of one repeating structural unit.

Classification

Monomers can be classified in many ways. They can be subdivided into two broad classes, depending on the type of polymer that they form. Monomers that participate in condensation polymerization have a different stoichiometry than monomers that participate in addition polymerization.

Monomers may also be natural or synthetic. Natural monomers include amino acids (which form proteins), nucleotides (which form nucleic acids), glucose and other sugars (which form polysaccharides), and isoprene (which forms natural rubber). Synthetic monomers include ethylene, vinyl chloride, styrene, tetrafluoroethylene, and methyl methacrylate, which yield many common plastics.

Examples

Common monomers
Ethylene→ Polyethylene
Vinyl chloride→ Polyvinyl chloride (PVC)
Styrene→ Polystyrene
Propylene→ Polypropylene
Glucose→ Starch, cellulose, glycogen
Amino acids→ Proteins

The largest-volume monomer is ethylene, the building block of polyethylene, the world's most common plastic. Vinyl chloride is polymerized to PVC, used in pipes and construction. Styrene gives polystyrene, used in packaging and insulation.

Polymerization

In addition polymerization, monomers with double or triple bonds join without losing any atoms. In condensation polymerization, monomers react with the loss of a small molecule, usually water. Polymers formed from a single kind of monomer are homopolymers; those formed from two or more different monomers are copolymers. The number of monomer units in a polymer is its degree of polymerization.
