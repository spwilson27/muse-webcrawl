Gamma Knife

ManufacturerElekta AB (Leksell Gamma Knife)
InventorLars Leksell
First clinical use1968, Stockholm
Radiation sourceCobalt-60 (192 sources in current models)
FieldStereotactic radiosurgery

The Gamma Knife is a device used to treat brain tumors, vascular malformations, and other abnormalities in the brain through stereotactic radiosurgery. Despite its name, it involves no blade or incision: instead, many precisely focused beams of gamma radiation converge on a target, delivering a high dose at the intersection point while sparing surrounding tissue. The technique was developed by Swedish neurosurgeon Lars Leksell, who treated the first patient in 1968.

How it works

The patient's head is fixed in a stereotactic frame, and imaging (MRI, CT, or angiography) maps the target in three-dimensional coordinates. In the Leksell Gamma Knife Icon, 192 cobalt-60 sources are arranged in a hemispherical array; their narrow beams are collimated to meet at a single isocenter with sub-millimeter accuracy. Multiple "shots" at different positions and sizes sculpt the dose to irregular lesions.

Indications

Gamma Knife radiosurgery is used for benign and malignant brain tumors (including meningiomas, acoustic neuromas/vestibular schwannomas, pituitary adenomas, and brain metastases), arteriovenous malformations, trigeminal neuralgia, and certain functional disorders. Treatment is usually outpatient and delivered in a single session.
