Juno is a NASA space probe orbiting the planet Jupiter. Built by Lockheed Martin and operated by the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, it was launched from Cape Canaveral Air Force Station on 5 August 2011 as part of the New Frontiers programme, and entered a polar orbit of Jupiter on 5 July 2016. The mission investigates Jupiter's composition, gravitational field, magnetic field, and polar magnetosphere, and searches for clues about the planet's formation.

Mission profile

Juno travelled to Jupiter on a trajectory that included a gravity assist from Earth in October 2013. A planned 14-day science orbit was lengthened to about 53 days after a problem with helium valves in the main engine made engineers reluctant to perform the burn that would have shortened the orbit; the longer orbit still allowed the science objectives to be met and reduced radiation exposure. The mission was extended to continue science orbits and close flybys of the moons Ganymede, Europa, and Io.

Spacecraft

Juno is the first Jupiter orbiter powered by solar panels rather than radioisotope generators. Its three solar arrays, the largest ever flown on a deep-space probe at launch, span about 20 metres. The spin-stabilised spacecraft carries its sensitive electronics in a titanium radiation vault. Three LEGO minifigures representing Galileo Galilei and the Roman gods Jupiter and Juno were carried aboard as an outreach payload, along with a plaque dedicated to Galileo.

Instruments

The payload includes the Microwave Radiometer (MWR), which probes Jupiter's deep atmosphere; the Jovian Infrared Auroral Mapper (JIRAM); magnetometers (MAG); the Gravity Science instrument; energetic particle detectors (JEDI); the Waves radio and plasma wave instrument; an ultraviolet spectrograph (UVS); and JunoCam, a visible-light camera flown largely for public engagement.

Findings

Juno revealed Jupiter's poles covered in clusters of cyclones, measured a magnetic field considerably stronger and more irregular than expected, and provided evidence that the planet's core is large and "dilute" rather than sharply bounded. Its gravity measurements showed the zonal jets extend thousands of kilometres deep, and microwave sounding revealed ammonia distribution deep into the atmosphere.
