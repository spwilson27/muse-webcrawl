Numerical integration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Rectangular approximation of a definite integral

In analysis, numerical integration comprises a broad family of algorithms for calculating the numerical value of a definite integral. The term numerical quadrature is more or less a synonym for numerical integration, especially applied to one-dimensional integrals; the term cubature is used for higher dimensions.

Reasons for numerical integration

Numerical integration is needed when the antiderivative of the integrand cannot be expressed in elementary functions (as with e−x²), when the integrand is known only at sampled points (as with experimental data), or when the integral arises inside a larger computation.

Methods

The classical Newton–Cotes formulas approximate the integrand by a polynomial over subintervals. The trapezoidal rule uses straight-line segments; Simpson's rule uses parabolas. Gaussian quadrature chooses sample points optimally to integrate polynomials of higher degree exactly. For difficult integrands, adaptive quadrature subdivides the interval where the function varies most, and Monte Carlo integration uses random sampling, especially valuable in high dimensions.

Error analysis

Errors depend on the step size and the smoothness of the integrand; Richardson extrapolation and Romberg integration exploit this dependence to obtain higher accuracy from coarse calculations.
