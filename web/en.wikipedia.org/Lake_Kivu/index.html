Lake Kivu is one of the African Great Lakes. It lies on the border between the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Rwanda, in the Albertine Rift, the western branch of the East African Rift. The lake empties into the Ruzizi River, which flows southwards into Lake Tanganyika.

Geography

The lake covers about 2,700 km² at an elevation of 1,460 metres and reaches a maximum depth of about 480 metres, making it the eighteenth deepest lake in the world. Its bed lies in a rift valley being pulled apart by tectonic forces. Idjwi Island, one of the largest inland lake islands in the world, lies within it. Shore towns include Goma and Bukavu in Congo and Kibuye and Cyangugu in Rwanda.

Dissolved gases

Lake Kivu is one of only three known "exploding lakes", along with Lake Nyos and Lake Monoun in Cameroon. Its deep waters contain enormous quantities of dissolved carbon dioxide (about 300 km³) and methane (about 60 km³), generated by bacterial activity and volcanic sources. A limnic eruption, if triggered by volcanic activity or a landslide, could release these gases and threaten the two million people living in the basin. After the 2002 eruption of nearby Mount Nyiragongo sent lava through Goma into the lake, no gas release occurred.

Methane extraction

Rwanda has developed projects to extract the dissolved methane for electricity generation, notably the KivuWatt plant near Kibuye, which also reduces the gas hazard by drawing methane-rich water from depth. Sardine fisheries (the introduced Tanganyika sardine, Limnothrissa miodon) are economically important.
