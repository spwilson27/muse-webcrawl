Cone cell

Cone cells are photoreceptor cells in the retinas of vertebrate eyes. They respond differently to light of different wavelengths and are thus responsible for color vision; they also function best in relatively bright light, as opposed to rod cells, which work better in dim light. Cones are densely packed in the fovea centralis, a sharply focused point at the centre of the human retina.

Types

Humans normally have three types of cones, sensitive mainly to short (S, blue), medium (M, green) and long (L, red) wavelengths of light, with peak sensitivities roughly around 420, 534 and 564 nanometres respectively. The differing responses of the three cone types underlie trichromatic color vision; many other mammals are dichromats with two cone types, while many birds and some fish are tetrachromats with four.

Structure

A cone consists of a synaptic terminal, an inner segment containing the nucleus and mitochondria, and an outer segment — a modified cilium — in which stacks of membranous discs carry the light-sensitive opsin pigments. Cones are shorter and more conical than rods, and their outer-segment discs remain continuous with the cell membrane.

Function

When light strikes the photopigment (photopsin), the molecule changes shape and triggers a cascade that hyperpolarises the cell and modulates signals passed through bipolar and ganglion cells to the brain. Because cones require more light than rods to respond, vision shifts from cone-dominated (photopic) to rod-dominated (scotopic) as illumination falls, producing changes such as the Purkinje shift. Defects in cone opsin genes cause forms of color blindness, and degeneration of cones leads to cone dystrophies and loss of central vision.
