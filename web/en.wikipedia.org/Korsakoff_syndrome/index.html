Korsakoff syndrome

Damage to the mammillary bodies and thalamus underlies the amnesia of Korsakoff syndrome.
SpecialtyNeurology, psychiatry
SymptomsSevere memory loss, confabulation
CausesThiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency
Named afterSergei Korsakoff

Korsakoff syndrome (also Korsakoff's syndrome, or amnesic-confabulatory syndrome) is a chronic memory disorder caused by severe deficiency of thiamine (vitamin B1). Korsakoff's is often, though not always, preceded by an episode of Wernicke encephalopathy, so the combined syndrome is named Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome.

Signs and symptoms

The syndrome is characterised by disproportionate memory loss relative to other cognitive functions: profound anterograde amnesia (inability to form new memories), retrograde amnesia, and confabulation, in which patients invent plausible stories to fill memory gaps. Other functions such as attention and social behaviour may be relatively preserved.

Causes

The most common cause is chronic alcohol misuse, which impairs thiamine intake and absorption, but the condition also occurs with malnutrition, eating disorders, bariatric surgery and prolonged vomiting. Damage concentrates in the mammillary bodies, medial thalamus and other diencephalic structures.

Treatment

Early treatment with high-dose thiamine may prevent progression from Wernicke encephalopathy; established Korsakoff syndrome often leaves lasting memory deficits, managed with rehabilitation, structured environments and memory aids. Abstinence from alcohol is essential.
