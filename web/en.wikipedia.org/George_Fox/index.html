George Fox (July 1624 – 13 January 1691) was an English Dissenter, who was a founder of the Religious Society of Friends, commonly known as the Quakers or Friends. The son of a Leicestershire weaver, he lived in a time of great social upheaval and war, and rebelled against the religious and political authorities by proposing an unusual, uncompromising approach to the Christian faith.

Ministry

After a period of spiritual searching in the 1640s, Fox began preaching in 1647, teaching that every person could experience the "Inner Light" of Christ directly, without need of clergy, sacraments, or consecrated buildings. His followers were nicknamed "Quakers" after Fox bade a magistrate tremble at the word of the Lord. Fox travelled throughout Britain, and later to Ireland, the Caribbean, and North America, enduring repeated imprisonment for blasphemy and for refusing to swear oaths or remove his hat before social superiors.

Legacy

Fox organised the Friends into monthly and yearly meetings, giving the movement a durable structure, and his Journal, published posthumously in 1694, remains a classic of spiritual autobiography. His marriage in 1669 to Margaret Fell, sometimes called the "mother of Quakerism", joined two of the movement's leading families. Quaker commitments to pacifism, plain dress, and social equality trace directly to Fox's teaching.
