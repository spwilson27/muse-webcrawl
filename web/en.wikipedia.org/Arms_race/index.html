Arms race

An arms race occurs when two or more states compete to increase their military capability, each seeking superiority over the other. Arms races can take place in peacetime and may precede or accompany wars; they often involve rapid growth in spending, troop numbers, or the development of new weapons technologies.

Classic examples

The Anglo-German naval arms race before the First World War, centred on Britain's Dreadnought battleships and Germany's High Seas Fleet, is a classic case. The Cold War nuclear arms race between the United States and the Soviet Union produced tens of thousands of nuclear warheads and drove the development of intercontinental ballistic missiles, ballistic missile submarines, and missile defence systems.

Dynamics

Analysts describe arms races with action–reaction models, in which each side's buildup responds to the other's, and with security dilemma arguments: measures one state takes for defence appear threatening to rivals, provoking countermeasures that leave both less secure. Lewis Fry Richardson developed mathematical models of arms race dynamics in the early 20th century.

Arms control

Arms control agreements — from the Washington Naval Treaty of 1922 to the SALT, INF, and New START treaties — are intended to limit or terminate arms races.

Other uses

The term is also used metaphorically, for example for the evolutionary "arms race" between predators and prey or hosts and parasites.
