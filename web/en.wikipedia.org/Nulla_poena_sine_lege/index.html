Nulla poena sine lege

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Scales of justice, symbol of legality in punishment

Nulla poena sine lege (Latin for "no penalty without a law") is a legal principle that a person should not be punished or criminally penalised for an act that was not prohibited by law at the time it was committed. It is one of the oldest and most fundamental principles of criminal law in the civil law tradition and is closely related to the maxim nullum crimen sine lege ("no crime without a law").

Meaning

The principle expresses the idea of legality in criminal law: punishment may be imposed only on the basis of a pre-existing legal norm. It protects individuals from arbitrary state power and from retroactive criminal legislation. In its fuller classical formulation it is often expanded to nulla poena sine lege, nulla poena sine crimine, nullum crimen sine poena legali — there is no punishment without a law, no punishment without a crime, and no crime without a legal punishment.

Requirements

Modern formulations of the principle, notably those associated with the German jurist Paul Johann Anselm von Feuerbach, break it down into several sub-requirements: the law must be written (lex scripta), it must precede the act (lex praevia), it must be certain and clear (lex certa), and it must be strictly construed, prohibiting analogy to the defendant's detriment (lex stricta).

International protection

The principle is enshrined in Article 7 of the European Convention on Human Rights, Article 15 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, and Article 11(2) of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, which provides that no one shall be held guilty of any penal offence on account of any act which did not constitute a penal offence under national or international law at the time when it was committed.
