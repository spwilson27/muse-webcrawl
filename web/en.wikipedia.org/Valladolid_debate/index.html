Valladolid debate

The Valladolid debate (1550–1551) was the first moral debate in European history about the rights and treatment of the Indigenous peoples of the Americas by colonisers. Held at the Colegio de San Gregorio in Valladolid, Spain, it pitted the Dominican friar Bartolomé de las Casas against the humanist scholar Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda before a junta of theologians convened by Charles V.

Background

Reports of atrocities in the Indies, the New Laws of 1542 limiting the encomienda system, and Las Casas's agitation led the crown in 1550 to suspend new conquests pending the debate on whether war against the Indians could be just.

Arguments

Sepúlveda, who never attended in person and whose treatise Democrates Alter was read out, argued from Aristotle's doctrine of natural slavery that the Indians were barbarians incapable of self-rule, and that practices such as human sacrifice justified conquest and forced conversion. Las Casas, over five days of reading his Apología, countered that the Indians were rational beings with their own legitimate polities and cultures, that they could only be converted peacefully, and that Spanish violence was unjust and tyrannical.

Outcome and legacy

The junta issued no clear verdict; conquests resumed under new terminology of "pacification", and the encomienda persisted, though Las Casas's critique shaped Spanish colonial law and the broader European debate about the humanity and rights of colonised peoples. The debate is now a landmark in the history of international law and human rights.
