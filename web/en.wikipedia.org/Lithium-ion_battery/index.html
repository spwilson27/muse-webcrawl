A lithium-ion battery (Li-ion) is a type of rechargeable battery in which lithium ions move from the negative electrode through an electrolyte to the positive electrode during discharge, and back when charging. Li-ion batteries are characterised by high energy density, low self-discharge and no memory effect, and they power most portable electronics, electric vehicles and grid storage systems.

History

The technology grew out of work during the 1970s oil crisis. M. Stanley Whittingham developed the first functional lithium battery with a titanium disulfide cathode; John Goodenough's group showed that lithium cobalt oxide greatly increased the voltage; and Akira Yoshino created the first commercially viable design using a carbonaceous anode. Sony commercialised the battery in 1991. The three pioneers shared the 2019 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Chemistry and construction

A cell consists of a positive electrode (commonly a lithium metal oxide such as lithium cobalt oxide, lithium iron phosphate, or nickel-manganese-cobalt oxide), a graphite negative electrode, a separator, and a lithium-salt electrolyte in an organic solvent. Common formats include cylindrical cells (18650, 21700), prismatic cells and pouch cells.

Performance and safety

Lithium-ion cells degrade with time and charge cycles, faster at high temperatures and states of charge. Damaged or overheated cells can undergo thermal runaway and catch fire, so battery packs incorporate management electronics, fusing and thermal controls. Recycling of lithium, cobalt and nickel is an expanding industry.
