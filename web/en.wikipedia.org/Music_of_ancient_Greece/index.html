Music of ancient Greece

The music of ancient Greece was an integral part of Greek life, accompanying religious ritual, theatre, athletics, warfare, and social gatherings. Music (mousikē) was held to shape character and the state itself, and it formed, with gymnastics, the core of education.

Instruments

The principal stringed instruments were the lyre, associated with Apollo and amateur education, and the larger, heavier kithara, played by professionals. The chief wind instrument was the aulos, a double-reed pipe played in pairs, linked with Dionysian worship and the theatre. Others included the panpipe (syrinx), trumpets such as the salpinx, and percussion instruments including hand drums, cymbals, and rattles.

Theory

Greek musical theory, founded by Pythagoras and developed by writers such as Aristoxenus and Ptolemy, analyzed intervals through ratios and organized pitch into tetrachords and the system of harmoniai — octave species or modes such as Dorian, Phrygian, and Lydian, each believed to possess a distinct ethical character (ethos). This theory profoundly influenced medieval and Renaissance music.

Surviving music

Although most ancient Greek music is lost, around sixty fragments survive in notation, including the Seikilos epitaph (the oldest surviving complete musical composition), the Delphic Hymns, and hymns by Mesomedes. Ancient Greek notation used separate alphabetic systems for vocal and instrumental music.
