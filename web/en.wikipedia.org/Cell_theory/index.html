Cell theory

Cell theory
Field: Cell biology
Proposed: 1838–1839
Key figures: Matthias Schleiden, Theodor Schwann, Rudolf Virchow

Cell theory is a fundamental principle of biology stating that all living organisms are composed of cells, that the cell is the basic unit of life, and that all cells arise from pre-existing cells. Formulated in the 19th century, it replaced earlier ideas of spontaneous generation for cellular life and unified botany and zoology.

History

Robert Hooke coined the term cell in 1665 after observing cork. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek observed living microorganisms. In 1838 Matthias Schleiden concluded all plant tissues are made of cells, and in 1839 Theodor Schwann extended this to animals. In 1855 Rudolf Virchow popularised omnis cellula e cellula — every cell from a cell — building on work by Robert Remak.

Modern principles

Modern cell theory adds that cells contain hereditary information in DNA passed during division, that all cells are basically similar in chemical composition, and that energy flow occurs within cells. Viruses are generally excluded because they are not cellular.

Significance

The theory underpins cell biology, medicine, and genetics. See also cell and microscope.
