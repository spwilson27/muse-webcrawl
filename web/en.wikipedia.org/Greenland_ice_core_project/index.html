Greenland ice core project

The Greenland Ice Core Project (GRIP) was a multinational European research project, organised through the European Science Foundation, to drill through the Greenland ice sheet and retrieve an ice core recording past climate. The drilling site, at the summit of the ice sheet (72°35′N 37°38′W, about 3,238 m above sea level), reached bedrock in 1992 at a depth of 3,028.8 metres, recovering ice dating back more than 100,000 years.

Aims and results

GRIP aimed to reconstruct past climate from the layers of compacted snow in the core — measuring stable isotopes (a proxy for temperature), trapped air bubbles (past atmospheric gases), acidity, dust and volcanic ash. Analysis revealed the abrupt climate shifts known as Dansgaard–Oeschger events during the last glacial period, when temperatures over Greenland could swing by many degrees within decades. The lowest sections of the core, covering the Eemian interglacial, proved disturbed by ice folding near the bed, complicating interpretation — a problem shared with the parallel American GISP2 core drilled some 30 km away, whose records agree closely for most of their length.

Successors

GRIP was followed by the North Greenland Ice Core Project (NGRIP, 1999–2003), which retrieved undisturbed Eemian ice, and by NEEM (2008–2012). Together the Greenland cores complement the Antarctic records (Vostok, EPICA) in documenting Quaternary climate.
