Iran–Contra affair

The Iran–Contra affair (Persian: ماجرای ایران-کنترا), also referred to as the Iran–Contra scandal, was a political scandal in the United States that occurred during the second term of the Reagan administration. Between 1981 and 1986, senior administration officials secretly facilitated the sale of arms to Iran — which was subject to an arms embargo and at war with Iraq — and used proceeds from the sales to fund the Contras, anti-communist rebels fighting the Sandinista government of Nicaragua.

Background

The arms sales were intended in part to secure the release of American hostages held in Lebanon by Hezbollah. The funding of the Contras violated the Boland Amendment, a congressional prohibition on military aid to the rebels. The scheme was run through the National Security Council, with Lieutenant Colonel Oliver North playing a central operational role.

Exposure and investigation

The affair became public in November 1986, after a Lebanese magazine reported the arms sales and a supply plane carrying weapons for the Contras was shot down over Nicaragua. The Tower Commission, congressional hearings in 1987, and an independent counsel investigation followed. Fourteen administration officials were indicted, including Secretary of Defense Caspar Weinberger; several convictions were later vacated or pardoned, most prominently by President George H. W. Bush in December 1992.
