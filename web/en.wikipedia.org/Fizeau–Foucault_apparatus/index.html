Fizeau–Foucault apparatus

The Fizeau–Foucault apparatus refers to the instruments built by French physicists Hippolyte Fizeau and Léon Foucault in 1849–1850 to make the first terrestrial measurements of the speed of light, previously known only from astronomical observations.

Fizeau's toothed wheel (1849)

Fizeau directed a beam of light through the gaps of a rapidly rotating toothed wheel to a mirror 8.6 km away at Montmartre and back. At a certain rotation speed, the returning light was blocked by a tooth instead of passing through the next gap — an eclipse of the beam — from which the light's travel time, and thus its speed, could be computed. He obtained about 313,000 km/s.

Foucault's rotating mirror (1850)

Foucault improved the method using a rotating mirror: light reflected to a distant fixed mirror and back struck the rotating mirror at a slightly different angle, and the deflection measured the travel time. In 1850 he also placed a tube of water in the path and showed that light travels slower in water than in air — a result that supported the wave theory of light over the corpuscular theory. His refined measurement of 1862 gave 298,000 km/s, within about 0.6% of the modern value.

Significance

These experiments brought the measurement of the speed of light into the laboratory and became the basis of later precision methods by Albert A. Michelson and others.
