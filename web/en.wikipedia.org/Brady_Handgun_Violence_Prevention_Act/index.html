The Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act, commonly known as the Brady Act, is a United States federal law enacted in 1993 that requires background checks on firearm purchasers and, in its original interim form, imposed a five-day waiting period on handgun sales.

Background

The law is named for James Brady, the White House press secretary who was seriously wounded in the 1981 assassination attempt on President Ronald Reagan. Brady and his wife Sarah campaigned for years for handgun controls. The bill was first introduced in 1987 and passed after a long legislative struggle.

Provisions and aftermath

The permanent provisions established the National Instant Criminal Background Check System (NICS), operated by the FBI, which launched in 1998; licensed dealers must run purchasers through the system before completing a sale. In Printz v. United States (1997), the Supreme Court struck down the interim requirement that state and local officers conduct checks, holding it violated the Tenth Amendment. The Brady Act remains a foundational element of federal firearms regulation in the United States.
