Indonesian literature

Indonesian literature is the literature produced in the Indonesian archipelago, from classical works in Malay, Javanese, Sundanese, and other regional languages to modern writing in the national language, Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia). Its history spans court epics and Islamic poetry, the colonial-era novel, the revolutionary generation of 1945, and a flourishing contemporary scene.

Classical and colonial literature

Traditional literatures include Malay hikayat (romances and chronicles) such as the Sejarah Melayu and the epic Hikayat Hang Tuah, Javanese kakawin poetry derived from Sanskrit models, and oral traditions across the islands. Under Dutch colonial rule, the state publisher Balai Pustaka (founded 1917) issued the first modern Indonesian novels, including Marah Rusli's Sitti Nurbaya (1922). The Pujangga Baru ("New Poets") movement of the 1930s, associated with Sutan Takdir Alisjahbana and the poet Amir Hamzah, debated how far Indonesian culture should turn toward the West.

The Generation of '45 and after

The poet Chairil Anwar revolutionized Indonesian verse during the revolution, and the "Generation of '45" set the tone for the young republic. Pramoedya Ananta Toer, Indonesia's most celebrated novelist, wrote his Buru Quartet — including This Earth of Mankind (1980) — while a political prisoner on Buru island; his works were banned under Suharto's New Order. The magazine Horison (founded 1966) became a central institution for writers navigating authoritarian rule.

Contemporary literature

Since the fall of Suharto in 1998, Indonesian writing has diversified. Ayu Utami's novel Saman (1998) opened a wave of women's writing sometimes labelled sastra wangi. Eka Kurniawan's Beauty Is a Wound (2002) won international acclaim in translation, as did Andrea Hirata's bestselling The Rainbow Troops (2005). Indonesia was Guest of Honour at the 2015 Frankfurt Book Fair, a milestone for its translation and publishing industries.
