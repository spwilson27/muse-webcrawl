Western canon

The Western canon is the body of books, art, music and philosophy traditionally regarded as the most important and influential in shaping Western culture. It typically begins with the works of ancient Greece and Rome — Homer, Plato, Aristotle, Virgil — and the Bible, and extends through Dante, Shakespeare, Cervantes, Milton, Goethe and the major writers, composers and thinkers of Europe and the Americas.

Formation

The canon was shaped over centuries by educational curricula, anthologies and criticism. In the twentieth century, programmes such as the "Great Books" movement at the University of Chicago, promoted by Robert Maynard Hutchins and Mortimer Adler, sought to define a list of essential works for liberal education; the Great Books of the Western World set (1952) collected 517 works by 130 authors.

Debate

From the 1960s onwards the canon became the subject of the "canon wars", as scholars argued that the traditional list overrepresented dead white European men and excluded women, minorities and non-Western voices. Universities revised reading lists to include figures such as Toni Morrison and works from other cultures, while defenders of the traditional canon, most prominently Harold Bloom in The Western Canon (1994), argued that aesthetic value and influence, not representation, should govern selection. Bloom placed Shakespeare at the centre of the canon.

The debate broadened questions about literary value, cultural literacy and who decides what is read in schools and universities.
