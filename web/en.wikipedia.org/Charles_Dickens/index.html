Charles John Huffam Dickens (7 February 1812 – 9 June 1870) was an English novelist and social critic, regarded by many as the greatest novelist of the Victorian era. His works, typically published in weekly and monthly serial instalments, enjoyed unprecedented popularity during his lifetime and continue to be widely read. He created some of the world's best-known fictional characters — Oliver Twist, Ebenezer Scrooge, Miss Havisham, Mr Micawber — and his fiction combined vivid caricature, social observation, and campaigning against poverty and injustice.

Early life

Dickens was born in Portsmouth and moved to London as a boy. When he was twelve, his father John Dickens was imprisoned for debt in the Marshalsea, and Charles was sent to work pasting labels on pots of boot blacking at Warren's Blacking Factory — an experience of shame and misery that haunted his writing and informed his portrayal of child labour and the debtors' prisons. Returning to school briefly, he became a junior clerk in a law office, learned shorthand, and worked as a parliamentary reporter before publishing sketches under the pen name "Boz."

Career

The Pickwick Papers (1836–37) made him famous at twenty-four. There followed a torrent of major novels: Oliver Twist, Nicholas Nickleby, The Old Curiosity Shop, and the novella A Christmas Carol (1843), which reshaped popular conceptions of Christmas. In the 1850s he founded and edited the weekly journals Household Words and All the Year Round, and produced his great mature novels: Bleak House, an assault on the Court of Chancery; Hard Times; Little Dorrit; A Tale of Two Cities (1859), set during the French Revolution; and Great Expectations (1861). David Copperfield (1849–50), his "favourite child," drew most directly on his own life.

Dickens was also a tireless performer of his own works: from 1858 he gave paid public readings, including tours of America in 1842 and 1867–68, which drew enormous audiences but further strained his health. He survived the Staplehurst rail crash of 1865, helping rescue passengers; the experience shook him badly.

Personal life and death

He married Catherine Hogarth in 1836; they had ten children before separating in 1858 amid his relationship with the actress Ellen Ternan, which remained largely concealed in his lifetime. Dickens died of a stroke at Gad's Hill Place, Kent, on 9 June 1870, leaving The Mystery of Edwin Drood unfinished. Against his wish for a private funeral, he was buried in Poets' Corner, Westminster Abbey.

Legacy

Dickens's influence on the English novel — his episodic plotting, memorable eccentrics, and moralised social vision — is immense, and the adjective "Dickensian" now evokes both his grotesque urban world and the squalor he campaigned against.
