Kiln

A kiln is a thermally insulated chamber, a type of oven, that produces temperatures sufficient to complete some process, such as hardening, drying, or chemical changes. Kilns have been used for millennia to turn objects made from clay into pottery, tiles, and bricks.

Uses

Pottery and brick kilns fire clay at temperatures from about 600 °C to over 1,400 °C. Industrial kilns include rotary kilns used in cement production, where raw meal is heated to form clinker; lime kilns that calcine limestone; and kilns for drying lumber and other materials. Metal smelting and cremation also employ kiln-like furnaces.

Types

Traditional designs include the updraft and downdraft kilns, bottle kilns of the Staffordshire Potteries, climbing (noborigama) kilns of Japan, and beehive kilns. Modern kilns may be fuelled by wood, coal, gas, or electricity, and include continuous tunnel kilns through which ware moves on cars, and periodic kilns loaded and unloaded between firings. Electric kilns with programmable controllers are standard in studios and schools.

Process

Firing involves controlled heating and cooling; atmosphere matters — oxidation and reduction firings produce different glaze colours and effects. Pyrometers and pyrometric cones measure heat-work during firing.
