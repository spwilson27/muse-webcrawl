Bombing of Guernica

The bombing of Guernica (26 April 1937) was an aerial attack on the Basque town of Guernica (Gernika) in northern Spain during the Spanish Civil War, carried out by Nazi Germany's Condor Legion and Fascist Italy's Aviazione Legionaria on behalf of Francisco Franco's Nationalist forces. It was one of the first bombings of a defenceless civilian target by a modern air force and is widely condemned as a war crime and an act of terror bombing. Guernica, the traditional seat of the Basque parliament under the Oak of Gernika, had deep symbolic importance for Basque identity.

Guernica in ruins after the attack; much of the town was destroyed by bombs and fire.

The attack

On Monday 26 April 1937—market day, when the town was crowded with townspeople and refugees—waves of German and Italian aircraft attacked in the late afternoon. Heinkel He 111 and Junkers Ju 52 bombers dropped high-explosive and incendiary bombs while fighters strafed roads and fields. The Rentería bridge, later claimed as the military target, survived essentially undamaged, while much of the town centre burned. Fire services from Bilbao could not reach the town in time; most of Guernica's buildings were destroyed or damaged.

Casualties and controversy

The Basque government reported 1,654 dead and 889 wounded; modern historians' estimates are generally lower, often in the range of 150–300, though the exact toll remains debated. Franco's regime initially denied the bombing and blamed Basque forces for burning the town, a falsehood maintained for decades. Foreign journalists, notably George Steer of The Times, reported the attack to the world, and Herbert Southworth's research later documented the cover-up.

Picasso and cultural legacy

The bombing inspired Pablo Picasso's monumental anti-war painting Guernica, completed in June 1937 for the Spanish Republic's pavilion at the Paris World's Fair. The painting became one of the most famous artworks of the 20th century and a universal symbol of the suffering of civilians in war; a tapestry copy hangs at the United Nations headquarters in New York. In 1997, German president Roman Herzog formally apologised for Germany's role, and the German Bundestag acknowledged the Condor Legion's responsibility.
