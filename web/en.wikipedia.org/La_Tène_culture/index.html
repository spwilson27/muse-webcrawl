La Tène culture

La Tène metalwork is famed for its curvilinear, "Celtic" ornament.

The La Tène culture was a European Iron Age culture, named after the type site at La Tène on Lake Neuchâtel in Switzerland, where thousands of objects were deposited in the lake in the later first millennium BCE. It developed from the earlier Hallstatt culture from about 450 BCE until the Roman conquest in the 1st century BCE, and is widely associated with the Celts. La Tène material spread across much of Europe, from Ireland and Iberia to Anatolia (Galatia).

Material culture

La Tène art is distinguished by flowing curvilinear designs — spirals, tendrils, and stylized animals — worked on torcs, brooches (fibulae), swords and scabbards, shields, helmets, and drinking vessels. Masterpieces include the Gundestrup cauldron, the Battersea Shield, and the gold torcs of hoards such as Snettisham. Society was organized in tribes and oppida — large fortified towns that emerged in the late period — with wealthy burials, including chariot graves, attesting to a warrior aristocracy. The culture's expansion is linked to historically recorded Celtic migrations, including the invasion of Italy (the sack of Rome c. 390 BCE), the attack on Delphi in 279 BCE, and settlement in central Anatolia.

Decline

From the late 2nd century BCE, pressure from Germanic peoples to the north and Rome to the south eroded La Tène territory; Gaul fell to Julius Caesar in the 50s BCE, and the culture survived longest in Britain and Ireland, where its art style influenced early medieval Insular art.
