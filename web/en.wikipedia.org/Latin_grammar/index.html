Latin grammar

Latin grammar, as described in modern textbooks and reference grammars, is the grammar of the Latin language as written in the classical period, especially the prose of Cicero and Caesar. Latin is a heavily inflected language: nouns, pronouns, adjectives, and verbs change their endings to express grammatical function, so word order is comparatively free and is used largely for emphasis and style, the unmarked order being subject–object–verb.

Nouns and cases

Latin nouns belong to one of five declensions and have three genders — masculine, feminine, and neuter. They inflect for number (singular and plural) and for six cases: nominative (subject), vocative (address), accusative (direct object and motion toward), genitive (possession), dative (indirect object), and ablative (separation, means, and many prepositional uses). Adjectives agree with their nouns in case, number, and gender. Remnants of an older locative case survive in a few words.

Verbs

Verbs belong to four conjugations and inflect for person, number, tense, mood, and voice. The tense system comprises the present, imperfect, future, perfect, pluperfect, and future perfect, built on two stems (present and perfect). Moods include the indicative, subjunctive (used extensively in subordinate clauses), and imperative. Non-finite forms include infinitives, participles, the gerund, and the gerundive. Deponent verbs have passive forms with active meanings.

Syntax

Characteristic constructions include the ablative absolute, indirect statement with the accusative and infinitive, purpose and result clauses with ut and the subjunctive, and the sequence of tenses governing subjunctive use in subordinate clauses. The traditional study of Latin grammar, codified by ancient grammarians such as Donatus and Priscian, shaped grammatical description in Europe for more than a millennium.
