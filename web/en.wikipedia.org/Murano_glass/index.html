Murano glass

Murano glass is glassware made on the island of Murano, in the Venetian Lagoon near Venice, Italy. For centuries Murano was the foremost centre of European luxury glassmaking, famed for innovations in clear glass, enamelled and multicoloured decoration, mirrors, and chandeliers, and its techniques were guarded as state secrets.

History

Venetian glassmaking is documented from the early Middle Ages. In 1291 the Venetian Republic ordered all glass furnaces moved to Murano, ostensibly to protect the city from fire but also to concentrate and control the industry. Murano's glassmasters (maestri vetrai) enjoyed high status but were forbidden to leave the republic or reveal trade secrets, on pain of severe punishment. In the fifteenth century Angelo Barovier developed cristallo, an exceptionally clear, colourless glass imitating rock crystal, alongside milk-white lattimo glass and elaborate enamel painting. Murano glass dominated European luxury markets in the sixteenth century.

Decline and revival

Competition from Bohemian and English glass, and the fall of the Venetian Republic in 1797, sent the industry into decline. A revival began in the mid-nineteenth century with workshops such as Salviati, and in the twentieth century Murano firms collaborated with artists and designers, producing the sculptural studio glass for which the island remains famous.

Techniques

Traditional techniques include lampworking, filigree (filigrana) with embedded white or coloured threads, millefiori "thousand flowers" canes, gold-leaf inclusion, and sommerso layering. Today "Murano glass" is a protected regional brand, and the island's showrooms and museum, the Museo del Vetro, draw glass collectors from around the world — though artisans continue to contend with cheap imported imitations.
