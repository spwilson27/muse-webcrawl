Reformed epistemology

Reformed epistemology is a school of thought in the philosophy of religion concerning the epistemic justification of belief in God. Associated especially with Alvin Plantinga, Nicholas Wolterstorff, and William Alston, it holds that belief in God can be properly basic — rationally acceptable without being inferred from evidence or argument — in much the way that beliefs in other minds, the past, or the external world are.

The movement takes inspiration from the Reformed theology of John Calvin.

Background

The view takes its name and theological inspiration from the Reformed tradition, particularly John Calvin's notion of a sensus divinitatis, an innate sense of God. Plantinga developed the position in response to the evidentialist objection to religious belief — the claim, pressed by philosophers such as W. K. Clifford, that it is wrong to believe anything on insufficient evidence.

Proper basicality and warrant

In Warranted Christian Belief (2000), Plantinga argued that beliefs produced by cognitive faculties functioning properly, in a congenial environment, according to a design plan successfully aimed at truth, have warrant — the quality that turns true belief into knowledge. On his "Aquinas/Calvin model", belief in God arises from the sensus divinitatis and can therefore be warranted without argument, though sin may damage the faculty. Critics raise the "Great Pumpkin objection" — that the approach would license any community's beliefs as properly basic — to which Plantinga replied that properly basic beliefs are occasioned by specific circumstances, not arbitrary.

Influence

Reformed epistemology helped drive the revival of analytic philosophy of religion from the 1970s onward, and is one of the most discussed positions in contemporary religious epistemology.
