Kon-Tiki was a balsa-wood raft sailed across the Pacific Ocean in 1947 by the Norwegian explorer and writer Thor Heyerdahl and a crew of five. Named after the Inca sun god Viracocha, called "Kon-Tiki" in some traditions, the voyage was an experimental expedition intended to demonstrate that pre-Columbian South Americans could, in principle, have crossed the Pacific on primitive rafts and settled the islands of Polynesia.

The expedition

Heyerdahl's theory — that Polynesia was settled in part from the east, from South America, rather than solely from Southeast Asia — was rejected by most scholars. To show its physical plausibility he built a raft using only materials and methods available to pre-Columbian peoples: nine balsa logs lashed with hemp rope, a bamboo cabin, and a square sail bearing the image of Kon-Tiki. The balsa was felled in the jungles of Ecuador and floated down to Callao, Peru.

The raft left Callao on 28 April 1947, towed clear of the coast before picking up the Humboldt Current and the trade winds. For 101 days the crew drifted westward, surviving on fish — including flying fish that landed on deck — and stored provisions, and proving that balsa remained buoyant far longer than critics predicted. On 7 August 1947 the raft struck the reef at Raroia in the Tuamotu Islands; the crew waded ashore unharmed.

Impact and legacy

Heyerdahl's book about the voyage, The Kon-Tiki Expedition (1948), became an international bestseller, and the 1950 documentary film won an Academy Award. The original raft is preserved at the Kon-Tiki Museum in Oslo. Modern genetic and linguistic evidence indicates that Polynesians descend overwhelmingly from Austronesian voyagers from the west, though later research has found some evidence of contact between Polynesians and Indigenous South Americans, lending Heyerdahl's voyage a more nuanced legacy than a simple refutation.
