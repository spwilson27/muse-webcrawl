Origins of the American Civil War

The origins of the American Civil War lay in the complex issues of slavery, expansion, and sectional conflict that divided the United States in the decades before 1861. Historians generally agree that slavery, and in particular the question of its extension into the western territories, was the central cause of the conflict, though economic differences, states' rights, and political failures also played roles.

Slavery and expansion

From the founding of the republic, slavery was embedded in the Southern economy, while the Northern states gradually abolished it. The Missouri Compromise of 1820 and the Compromise of 1850 attempted to balance free and slave states. The Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854 repealed the Missouri Compromise and led to violent conflict in "Bleeding Kansas." The Supreme Court's Dred Scott decision of 1857 held that Congress could not prohibit slavery in the territories.

Political crisis

The collapse of the Whig Party and the rise of the Republican Party, committed to halting slavery's expansion, alarmed the South. The election of Abraham Lincoln in 1860 prompted South Carolina to secede in December 1860, followed by ten other states forming the Confederate States of America. The war began on April 12, 1861, with the Confederate attack on Fort Sumter.

Historiography

Debate over the war's causes has continued among historians. The "Lost Cause" tradition minimized slavery's role, but modern scholarship overwhelmingly identifies the defense and expansion of slavery as the Confederacy's central motivation, as stated in the secession declarations themselves.
