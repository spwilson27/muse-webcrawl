Sapir–Whorf hypothesis

The Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, also known as the Whorf hypothesis or Whorfianism, is a concept in linguistics proposing that the structure of a language influences its speakers' worldview and cognition, and thus different languages lead their speakers to different perceptions of the world. In its strong form, known as linguistic determinism, the hypothesis holds that language determines thought and that linguistic categories limit and restrict cognitive categories. In its weaker and more widely accepted form, linguistic relativity, language merely influences certain kinds of cognitive processes in non-trivial ways.

Edward Sapir (left) and Benjamin Lee Whorf, the linguists after whom the hypothesis is named.

Origins

The hypothesis is named after the anthropological linguist Edward Sapir and his student Benjamin Lee Whorf. Neither man ever formally stated the idea as a single hypothesis; rather, it was developed from their writings on the languages of Native America. Whorf argued, for example, that the Hopi language's treatment of time differed fundamentally from that of European languages, leading Hopi speakers toward a different conception of temporality. Franz Boas, Sapir's teacher, had earlier noted that language classifications could shape how speakers categorised experience.

Evidence and research

Empirical studies have found support for weak effects of language on thought in domains such as colour perception — speakers of languages that distinguish more colour categories may discriminate shades faster — spatial reference, where speakers of languages using absolute directions (such as Guugu Yimithirr) display remarkable orientation abilities, and grammatical gender, which can subtly affect descriptions of inanimate objects. Critics such as Steven Pinker have argued that thought is conducted in a pre-linguistic "language of thought" and that the strong version of the hypothesis is false, a view supported by the ability of pre-verbal infants and non-human animals to form concepts.

Influence

Despite controversy within linguistics, the hypothesis has had a lasting influence on anthropology, psychology, philosophy, and popular culture, inspiring works of fiction such as the film Arrival (2016), and reviving interest in linguistic diversity as languages continue to disappear worldwide.
