Pontiac's War

Pontiac's War, also known as Pontiac's Conspiracy or Pontiac's Rebellion, was a conflict launched in 1763 by a loose confederation of Native American tribes, primarily from the Great Lakes, Illinois Country, and Ohio Country regions, who were dissatisfied with British policies in the Great Lakes region following the British victory in the French and Indian War (1754–1763). It is named for the Odawa leader Pontiac, the most prominent of many native leaders in the conflict.

Causes

After France ceded its North American claims east of the Mississippi to Britain in 1763, Native peoples who had traded and allied with the French faced a new regime under General Jeffery Amherst, which cut off the distribution of gifts, restricted the sale of ammunition, and tolerated encroaching settlement. The religious teaching of the Delaware prophet Neolin, who preached rejection of European goods and ways, helped inspire a pan-Indian resistance movement.

Course of the war

The war began in May 1763 when Pontiac led an attempt to seize Fort Detroit by surprise; it failed, and he besieged the fort instead. Native forces captured eight of the twelve British forts they attacked, including Fort Sandusky, Fort Michilimackinac, and Fort Presque Isle, and inflicted heavy losses. Fort Detroit, Fort Pitt, and Fort Niagara held out; at Fort Pitt, British officers gave besieging Delawares blankets from the smallpox hospital in a notorious attempt at biological warfare. Expeditions under Henry Bouquet defeated a Native force at Bushy Run (1763) and compelled peace in the Ohio Country in 1764. Pontiac formally made peace at Oswego in 1766.

Aftermath

Although the British retained their forts, the war forced a policy reversal: the Royal Proclamation of 1763 reserved lands west of the Appalachians for Native peoples, and Britain resumed gift diplomacy and trade regulation. The proclamation line angered colonists and became one of the grievances preceding the American Revolution. Pontiac was murdered by a Peoria warrior in 1769.
