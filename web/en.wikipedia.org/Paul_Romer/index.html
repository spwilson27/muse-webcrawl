Paul Michael Romer (born November 6, 1955) is an American economist who pioneered endogenous growth theory, explaining how technological change and ideas drive long-run economic growth from within the economic system rather than arriving from outside it. In 2018 he shared the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with William Nordhaus, receiving his half "for integrating technological innovations into long-run macroeconomic analysis." He served as Chief Economist of the World Bank from 2016 to 2018.

Education and career

Romer, the son of former Colorado governor Roy Romer, studied mathematics and physics before earning a PhD in economics from the University of Chicago in 1983, where Robert Lucas Jr. was a major influence. He taught at the University of Rochester, the University of Chicago, the University of California, Berkeley, and Stanford University, where he also founded Aplia, an education-technology company. He later joined New York University, founding the Marron Institute of Urban Management, and in 2023 moved to Boston College.

Endogenous growth theory

Romer's seminal papers of 1986 and 1990 transformed growth economics. His 1990 model treated knowledge as a non-rival good—usable by everyone simultaneously, unlike physical goods—produced by profit-seeking firms under monopolistic competition. Because ideas generate increasing returns and spillovers, markets left alone underinvest in research, giving policy a role in subsidising innovation and education. The framework revived growth theory after decades in which the Solow model treated technology as unexplained ("exogenous"), and it underlies modern models of research and development, trade, and urban agglomeration.

Other work

Romer proposed "charter cities"—new cities in developing countries operating under imported legal and administrative rules—as a route to reform, an idea that influenced Honduras's special development regions (ZEDEs) before being discontinued there and becoming controversial. At the World Bank he resigned as Chief Economist in 2018 after a dispute over his criticism of the Doing Business rankings' treatment of Chile. He has also been an outspoken critic of what he calls "mathiness" in macroeconomics and of the profession's tolerance for models detached from facts.
