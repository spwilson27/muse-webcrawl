Sir Henry Head

Sir Henry Head (4 August 1861 – 8 October 1940) was an English neurologist who conducted pioneering work on the somatosensory system and the sensory nerves, and on aphasia. His studies of referred pain gave rise to the concept of Head's zones, areas of skin hypersensitivity associated with visceral disease.

Self-experimentation

In a celebrated experiment, Head had his own radial and external cutaneous nerves surgically divided in 1903 and, with W. H. R. Rivers, charted the return of sensation over five years. From this he distinguished protopathic sensibility (crude, poorly localised) from epicritic sensibility (fine discrimination), publishing the results in Studies in Neurology (1920). Though the dichotomy was later criticised, the work was foundational for sensory neurology and for the mapping of dermatomes.

Career

Head trained at Cambridge and University College Hospital, London, and worked at the National Hospital, Queen Square, and the London Hospital. During the First World War he studied nerve injuries and cerebral lesions in wounded soldiers, work extended in his two-volume Aphasia and Kindred Disorders of Speech (1926), which challenged purely diagrammatic accounts of language in the brain. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1899, edited the journal Brain, and was knighted in 1927. He also published poetry. Late in life he developed Parkinson's disease and documented his own symptoms.
