Paul Vidal de la Blache (22 January 1845 – 5 April 1918) was a French geographer regarded as the founder of modern French geography and one of the founders of human geography. He developed the concept of possibilism—the view that the natural environment offers societies a range of possibilities rather than determining their development—and emphasised the study of small, homogeneous regions (pays) and their distinctive ways of life, or genres de vie.

Life and career

Vidal was born in Pézenas in southern France and educated at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, taking his doctorate in ancient history in 1872 after study in Athens. He taught at the University of Nancy, where his interest shifted from history to geography, then returned to the École Normale Supérieure in 1877 and moved to the Sorbonne in 1898. Over four decades he trained nearly the entire first generation of French academic geographers, including Emmanuel de Martonne, Albert Demangeon, and Jean Brunhes.

In 1891 he co-founded the journal Annales de Géographie. His Tableau de la géographie de la France (1903), the introductory volume to Ernest Lavisse's history of France, presented the country as a mosaic of regional personalities united by circulation and history. His masterwork, Principes de géographie humaine, was published posthumously in 1921 by de Martonne from his manuscripts.

Ideas

Against the environmental determinism associated with Friedrich Ratzel, Vidal argued that nature sets limits and offers opportunities, but culture, history, and human choice shape how societies respond—the essence of possibilism, a term popularised by historian Lucien Febvre. His method combined physical and human geography at the regional scale, describing how a population's genre de vie (way of life) inscribes itself in the landscape. French regional monographs in this tradition dominated the discipline for decades, and his influence extended to geopolitics—he advised the French delegation preparing for the post–First World War settlement—and to the Annales school of history.
