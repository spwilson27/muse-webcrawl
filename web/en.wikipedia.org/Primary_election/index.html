Primary election

Primary elections, or primaries, are elections held to determine which candidates will run for an affiliated political party in a forthcoming general election. Originating in the United States during the Progressive Era, primaries are now also used in many other countries. In the U.S., primaries are administered by state governments under state law, and their rules vary considerably: open primaries allow any registered voter to participate, while closed primaries restrict voting to registered party members.

History

Before primaries, party nominees were chosen by conventions and caucuses dominated by party leaders — the "smoke-filled room" of political folklore. Progressive reformers in the early 20th century introduced direct primaries to give ordinary voters control over nominations. Wisconsin adopted the first statewide direct primary law in 1903, and the institution spread rapidly. Presidential primaries became decisive after the reforms that followed the 1968 Democratic National Convention, when the McGovern–Fraser Commission shifted power from party officials to primary voters.

Types

Closed primaries require voters to be registered with the party. Open primaries let voters choose any party's ballot on election day. Semi-closed and semi-open systems fall in between. Some states, including California and Washington, use a top-two primary, in which all candidates appear on one ballot and the two highest vote-getters advance regardless of party. Caucuses — party-run meetings rather than state-run elections — remain in use in a few states.

Criticism and debate

Primaries are criticized for low turnout, domination by the most motivated (often most ideological) voters, and the front-loading of presidential contests. Defenders argue they democratize candidate selection and that alternatives concentrate power in party elites.
