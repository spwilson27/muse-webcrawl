Margin (finance)

In finance, margin is the collateral that a holder of a financial instrument must deposit with a counterparty (most commonly a broker or exchange) to cover some or all of the credit risk the holder poses to that counterparty. Margin is closely associated with leveraged trading: buying securities with borrowed money through a margin account.

Margin buying

An investor who buys on margin borrows part of the purchase price from the broker. The initial margin is the fraction the investor must supply — in the United States, Regulation T has long set it at 50% for equities — while the maintenance margin is the minimum equity that must remain in the account, commonly 25% or higher under exchange and house rules.

Margin calls

If the value of the securities falls so that the investor's equity drops below the maintenance requirement, the broker issues a margin call: the investor must deposit more funds or securities, or the broker will liquidate positions, typically without prior notice. Forced selling during market declines can amplify crashes, as in 1929, when margin lending had fuelled speculative buying.

Margin in derivatives markets

Futures and cleared derivatives use margin differently: both sides post initial margin as a performance bond, and accounts are marked to market daily, with variation margin transferred to reflect gains and losses. Central counterparties collect margin to mutualise default risk.
