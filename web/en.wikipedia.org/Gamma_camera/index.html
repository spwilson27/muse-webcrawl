Gamma camera

Image placeholder: a modern dual-head gamma camera

A gamma camera (also called a scintillation camera or Anger camera) is a device used to image the distribution of gamma radiation-emitting radionuclides that have been administered to a patient, a technique known as scintigraphy. Scintigraphy is an important part of nuclear medicine imaging, used to assess the function of organs such as the thyroid, heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, gallbladder, and skeleton.

Operation

The patient is given a radiopharmaceutical, commonly a compound labelled with technetium-99m, which accumulates in the tissue of interest and emits gamma rays. The camera head is positioned close to the patient. A collimator — a thick lead plate perforated by thousands of parallel holes — admits only gamma rays travelling in roughly the right direction. Behind the collimator, a large sodium iodide crystal doped with thallium converts each gamma photon into a flash of light (a scintillation), and an array of photomultiplier tubes detects the flashes and computes the position of each event, building up a two-dimensional image of radioactivity.

History

The gamma camera was developed by American physicist Hal Anger at the Donner Laboratory in Berkeley, California, in the late 1950s; his 1957 design, the Anger scintillation camera, became the standard instrument of nuclear medicine for decades. Rotating the camera head around the patient and reconstructing the data tomographically produces single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), and modern systems often combine SPECT with computed tomography (SPECT/CT) for anatomical localisation.

Applications

Gamma cameras are used for bone scans, myocardial perfusion imaging, thyroid imaging, renal scans, lung ventilation/perfusion studies, and sentinel lymph node localisation, among many other investigations.
