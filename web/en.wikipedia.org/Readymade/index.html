Readymade

A readymade is an ordinary manufactured object that an artist selects and designates as a work of art, typically with little or no modification. The term was coined by the French artist Marcel Duchamp in 1915–1916 to describe works such as his Bottle Rack and Fountain, which challenged conventional definitions of art, craftsmanship and taste.

History

Duchamp produced his first readymades in Paris around 1913–1914, mounting a bicycle wheel on a stool (Bicycle Wheel) and purchasing a galvanised bottle-drying rack (Bottle Rack). After moving to New York, he refined the concept with works such as In Advance of the Broken Arm (a snow shovel, 1915). Duchamp described his choices as guided by "visual indifference" and an absence of good or bad taste.

Fountain

The most famous readymade is Fountain (1917), a standard porcelain urinal signed "R. Mutt" and submitted by Duchamp to the first exhibition of the Society of Independent Artists in New York. Although the society had promised to show all works submitted for a fee, the urinal was hidden from view. An anonymous editorial in the journal The Blind Man defended the work, arguing that whether Mr Mutt made the fountain with his own hands was unimportant: "He CHOSE it." In a 2004 poll of art professionals, Fountain was voted the most influential artwork of the modern era.

Types

Duchamp distinguished unaltered readymades from "assisted readymades", which combine or modify objects — as in Bicycle Wheel — and from "rectified readymades", such as L.H.O.O.Q., a reproduction of the Mona Lisa with a moustache and beard drawn on it.

Influence

The readymade profoundly influenced 20th-century art, preparing the ground for Dada, Surrealism, Pop art and Conceptual art, and raising questions about authorship, originality and the role of institutions that remain central to art theory.
