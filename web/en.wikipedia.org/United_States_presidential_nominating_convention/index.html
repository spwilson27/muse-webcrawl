A United States presidential nominating convention is a political convention held every four years in the United States by most political parties that field candidates in the presidential election. The formal purpose is to select the party's nominees for president and vice president, adopt a statement of party principles and goals (the platform), and unify the party for the general election campaign.

History

Before conventions, presidential candidates were chosen by congressional caucuses — the "King Caucus" system that collapsed in the disputed election of 1824. The Anti-Masonic Party held the first national nominating convention in Baltimore in September 1831, followed by the National Republicans and, in 1832, the Democrats. Conventions soon became the dominant selection method, controlled by party organizations and state delegations led by political bosses in the famous "smoke-filled rooms".

The system began shifting toward primaries in the Progressive Era, but party leaders still often controlled the outcome. The chaotic 1968 Democratic National Convention in Chicago — where Hubert Humphrey, who had not entered any primaries, won the nomination amid anti-Vietnam War protests — led to the McGovern–Fraser Commission reforms, which bound delegates to primary and caucus results and made the modern primary process decisive. Since 1976, every major-party nominee has been effectively determined before the convention.

Delegates

Delegates are allocated to states roughly in proportion to population and past party voting strength, and are chosen through state primaries and caucuses held from January or February through June of the election year. Most Democratic delegates are pledged to candidates in proportion to primary results, supplemented by unpledged "superdelegates" (party leaders), whose power was curtailed after 2016. Republican rules vary by state and include proportional and winner-take-all allocation. A candidate who secures a majority of delegates before the convention is the presumptive nominee; if no candidate does, the convention becomes "contested" or "brokered", with multiple ballots — last occurring in 1952 for the Democrats (three ballots) and in multiple-ballot form for Republicans in 1948.

The modern convention

Modern conventions are four-day, televised events held in the summer, culminating in the nominees' acceptance speeches. Keynote addresses have launched national careers — most famously Barack Obama's 2004 Democratic keynote. Conventions traditionally produce a polling "bounce" for the nominee. The 2020 conventions were largely virtual due to the COVID-19 pandemic, with the Democrats holding a remote roll call of states.
