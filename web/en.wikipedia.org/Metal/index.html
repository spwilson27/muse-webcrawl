A metal is a material that, when freshly prepared, polished, or fractured, shows a lustrous appearance and conducts electricity and heat relatively well. Metals are typically ductile (can be drawn into wires) and malleable (can be hammered into thin sheets). In chemistry, metals form the great majority of the elements of the periodic table and are characterised by atoms that readily lose electrons to form positive ions (cations). Their cohesion is explained by metallic bonding, in which valence electrons are delocalised over a lattice of positive ions — the "electron sea" model that accounts for their conductivity and workability.

Classification

Metals are conventionally divided into groups including the alkali metals (lithium, sodium, potassium, ...), alkaline earth metals, transition metals (iron, copper, gold, ...), post-transition metals (aluminium, tin, lead, ...), and the lanthanides and actinides. Metalloids such as silicon and germanium show intermediate properties, while nonmetals (oxygen, carbon, sulfur, ...) lack metallic character. Metals are further divided in industry into ferrous (iron-based) and non-ferrous metals.

Alloys

An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements, engineered to improve strength, hardness, corrosion resistance, or other properties. Important alloys include steel (iron and carbon), stainless steel (with chromium), brass (copper and zinc), bronze (copper and tin), and duralumin (aluminium and copper). Alloys dominate practical metal use: pure metals are often too soft, too reactive, or too expensive for structural purposes.

History and use

Human use of metals stretches from the Copper Age through the Bronze Age and Iron Age — the classic three-age system — to the industrial extraction of aluminium in the nineteenth century and the rare-earth-dependent technologies of the present. Metals are extracted from ores by mining and smelting and are obtained for industry largely by recycling as well as primary production. In astronomy and astrophysics, "metal" denotes any element heavier than hydrogen and helium — a usage unrelated to the materials sense.
