Phosphorylation

Phosphorylation is the attachment of a phosphoryl group (PO3) to a molecule or ion. Together with its counterpart, dephosphorylation, it is critical for many cellular processes, including energy transfer, signal transduction, and the regulation of enzyme activity. Phosphorylation is one of the most common post-translational modifications of proteins.

A kinase transfers a phosphate group from ATP to a substrate protein.

Protein phosphorylation

In eukaryotes, protein phosphorylation typically occurs on serine, threonine, and tyrosine residues. It is catalysed by enzymes called kinases, which transfer the terminal phosphate group of ATP to the substrate. The reverse reaction, dephosphorylation, is performed by phosphatases. The balance between kinase and phosphatase activity acts as a molecular switch controlling cell growth, division, metabolism, and apoptosis.

Oxidative phosphorylation

Oxidative phosphorylation is the metabolic pathway by which cells use enzymes to oxidise nutrients, releasing energy that is used to form ATP. In eukaryotes it takes place in the mitochondria, where the electron transport chain pumps protons across the inner membrane, and the resulting gradient drives ATP synthase. It is the main source of ATP in aerobic organisms.

Substrate-level phosphorylation

Substrate-level phosphorylation generates ATP by the direct transfer of a phosphoryl group to ADP from a reactive intermediate. It occurs in the cytoplasm during glycolysis and in the mitochondrial matrix during the citric acid cycle, and does not require oxygen.

History

The first phosphorylation reaction described was the formation of glucose 6-phosphate in glycolysis. In the 1950s, Edmond Fischer and Edwin Krebs demonstrated reversible protein phosphorylation as a regulatory mechanism in glycogen metabolism, work for which they received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1992.
