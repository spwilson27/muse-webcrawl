Caesar's civil war

Caesar's civil war (49–45 BC) was the conflict between Julius Caesar and the Senatorial faction led by Pompey that destroyed the Roman Republic's governing consensus. Its narrative is known in unusual detail because Caesar himself wrote an account, the Commentarii de Bello Civili, covering the first years of the war.

Period49–45 BC
Principal commandersJulius Caesar; Pompey the Great
Key battlesPharsalus, Thapsus, Munda
OutcomeCaesar becomes dictator

Causes

Historians debate whether the war was driven by constitutional disputes over Caesar's command and immunity from prosecution, by personal rivalry between Caesar and Pompey, or by deeper structural failures of the Republic's institutions. The Senate's declaration of Caesar as a public enemy and his crossing of the Rubicon made war unavoidable from January 49 BC.

Campaigns

The war was fought across the Mediterranean world: Italy, Hispania, Greece, Egypt, Africa, and again Hispania. Caesar's strategy relied on speed, clemency toward defeated opponents, and the loyalty of his veteran legions from the Gallic Wars. Pompey's death in Egypt in 48 BC did not end resistance; his sons and allies fought on until Munda in 45 BC.

Significance

The war ended any realistic prospect of restoring the old senatorial regime. Caesar's dictatorship, his assassination in 44 BC, and the triumviral wars that followed completed the transition from Republic to Empire.
