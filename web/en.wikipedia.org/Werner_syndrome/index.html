Werner syndrome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Werner syndrome (WS), also known as adult progeria, is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterised by the appearance of premature aging beginning in early adulthood. It is named after the German physician Otto Werner, who described it in 1904. The condition is caused by mutations in the WRN gene on chromosome 8, which encodes a DNA helicase involved in DNA repair and maintenance of telomeres.

Signs and symptoms

Affected children develop normally, but typically fail to experience the adolescent growth spurt. From the second and third decades, features of accelerated aging appear: greying and loss of hair, scleroderma-like skin changes, bilateral cataracts, type 2 diabetes, osteoporosis, atherosclerosis, skin ulcers and a high-pitched voice. Patients face an increased risk of cancer, including unusual mesenchymal tumours. Death most commonly results from cardiovascular disease or malignancy, typically in the fifth or sixth decade of life.

Genetics and mechanism

The WRN protein is a member of the RecQ helicase family, with both helicase and exonuclease activity. Its loss causes genomic instability, defective DNA repair and accelerated telomere shortening, driving cells into premature senescence. Werner syndrome is therefore studied as a model of normal human aging. It is more frequently reported in Japan, where founder effects raise its prevalence.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the characteristic clinical picture and is confirmed by genetic testing for biallelic WRN mutations. There is no cure; treatment is directed at complications — cataract extraction, aggressive management of diabetes and cardiovascular risk, care of skin ulcers, and cancer surveillance.
