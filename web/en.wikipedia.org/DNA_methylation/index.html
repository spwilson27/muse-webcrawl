DNA methylation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

DNA methylation is a biological process by which methyl groups are added to the DNA molecule. Methylation can change the activity of a DNA segment without changing its sequence. In mammals it occurs predominantly on cytosine in CpG dinucleotides, producing 5-methylcytosine, and is a fundamental epigenetic mechanism.

Function

DNA methylation is essential for normal development. It is involved in genomic imprinting, X-chromosome inactivation, repression of transposable elements, gene silencing, ageing, and carcinogenesis. Most CpG sites in the genome are methylated, but "CpG islands" near gene promoters are usually unmethylated when the gene is active; promoter hypermethylation is associated with transcriptional silencing.

Enzymes and inheritance

Methylation patterns are established by de novo methyltransferases DNMT3A and DNMT3B and copied through cell division by the maintenance enzyme DNMT1. Demethylation can be passive (failure of maintenance during replication) or active, via TET enzymes that oxidise 5-methylcytosine. Because methylation patterns are largely reset in the early embryo, most acquired marks are not inherited, although some escape reprogramming.

Measurement and significance

Bisulfite sequencing is the standard method for mapping methylation at single-base resolution. In medicine, aberrant methylation is a hallmark of many cancers, and "epigenetic clocks" based on methylation at specific sites accurately estimate biological age.
