The Gulf of Aqaba (Arabic: خليج العقبة) or Gulf of Eilat (Hebrew: מפרץ אילת) is a large gulf at the northern tip of the Red Sea, east of the Sinai Peninsula and west of the Arabian Peninsula. Its shoreline is shared by four countries: Egypt, Israel, Jordan, and Saudi Arabia.

Geography

The gulf is one of two gulfs created by the Sinai Peninsula's split of the northern Red Sea, the other being the Gulf of Suez to the west. Reaching depths of about 1,850 metres, it is far deeper than the shallow Gulf of Suez. Geologically it forms the southern end of the Dead Sea Transform fault system; the region is seismically active, and its deep basins (including the Eilat Deep) were formed by strike-slip motion. At its northern end sit the Jordanian port of Aqaba and the Israeli port and resort city of Eilat, almost side by side; the Egyptian resort of Taba lies just to the southwest.

Straits of Tiran and history

Access to the gulf from the Red Sea is through the Straits of Tiran, guarded by Tiran Island. Egypt's blockade of the straits to Israeli shipping was a central cause of both the Suez Crisis of 1956 and the Six-Day War of 1967. The 1979 Egypt–Israel peace treaty guaranteed freedom of navigation, and a multinational observer force has been stationed in the area since.

Environment

The gulf's clear, warm waters support some of the world's northernmost coral reefs, with marine reserves near Eilat and Aqaba attracting divers. Planned development, including Saudi Arabia's Neom project on the eastern shore, has raised environmental and geopolitical attention.
