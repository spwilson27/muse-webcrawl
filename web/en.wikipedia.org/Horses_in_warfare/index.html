Horses in warfare

Horses in warfare were central to military history for more than four thousand years — as chariot teams, cavalry mounts, draught animals for artillery and supply wagons, and messengers — from their domestication on the Eurasian steppe until mechanisation displaced them in the mid-twentieth century.

Chariots and cavalry

Horses first served in war pulling chariots, which dominated Bronze Age battlefields from Egypt to China by the second millennium BC. True cavalry emerged with steppe peoples and the Assyrians; Alexander the Great's Companion cavalry, medieval armoured knights, Mongol horse archers, and Ottoman sipahis each represented successive peaks of mounted warfare.

Gunpowder era and decline

Firearms gradually eroded the heavy cavalry charge, though mounted troops remained decisive for scouting, raiding, pursuit, and logistics into the First World War, which consumed some eight million horses. The Second World War still saw vast equine use — the German army relied on millions of horses for transport — and one of the last great cavalry charges occurred at Izbushensky in 1942.

Modern use

Horses survive in ceremonial roles, mounted police, and some irregular and mountain warfare; US special forces famously rode with the Northern Alliance in Afghanistan in 2001.
