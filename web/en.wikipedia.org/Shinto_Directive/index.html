Shinto Directive

Shinto Directive
Full titleSCAPIN-448: "Abolition of Governmental Sponsorship, Support, Perpetuation, Control, and Dissemination of State Shinto"

Issued byGeneral Headquarters, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers (SCAP), Occupation of Japan

The Shinto Directive, formally "Abolition of Governmental Sponsorship, Support, Perpetuation, Control, and Dissemination of State Shinto (Kokka Shintō, Jinja Shintō)", was an order issued on 15 December 1945 by the Allied Occupation authorities (SCAP) in Japan. It disestablished the state cult of Shinto, severing the financial, administrative, and ideological ties between the Japanese government and Shinto shrines, and it laid the groundwork for the separation of religion and state and the guarantee of religious freedom in postwar Japan.

Background

From the Meiji Restoration through 1945, Japan's governments had promoted "State Shinto" — a system in which shrines were publicly funded and priests state-appointed, while officially declaring shrine observances "not religion" so that participation could be required of all subjects alongside a nominal freedom of belief. Shrines and rituals exalting the emperor and the imperial line, and militaristic doctrines drawn from texts such as the Kokutai no Hongi, formed part of wartime mobilisation. The Potsdam Declaration required the removal of obstacles to democratic tendencies and the establishment of freedom of religion and thought, and SCAP's Religions Division prepared the directive, with William K. Bunce the principal drafter.

Provisions

The directive abolished government sponsorship, support, perpetuation, control, and dissemination of State Shinto; removed public funding and tax privileges from shrines; banned the use of public buildings and state ceremonies for Shinto observance; prohibited the teaching of State Shinto doctrine in schools and the distribution of state Shinto publications such as Kokutai no Hongi; ended official visits and rites by state representatives in their official capacity; and guaranteed the freedom of private citizens, including the emperor as a private person, to worship. It expressly did not abolish Shinto as a religion: shrines could continue as private religious organisations, and sect Shinto and folk observance remained lawful.

Consequences

The Jinja Honcho (Association of Shinto Shrines) was founded in February 1946 as a private body to represent shrines, and roughly 80,000 shrines reorganised without state support. The directive anticipated Articles 20 and 89 of the 1947 Constitution of Japan, which guarantee religious freedom, bar religious organisations from state privilege, and forbid public money for religious institutions. The emperor's 1946 New Year statement renouncing his official divinity (the Ningen-sengen) was a parallel, though separate, step. Postwar litigation — over public funding of ceremonies such as ground-breaking rites and the status of Yasukuni Shrine, which became a private religious corporation — has continued to test the boundaries the directive established.
