Planned city
Also calledPlanned community, model city, new town

DisciplineUrban planning

A planned city is laid out according to a design prepared before construction.

A planned city (or planned community) is a city whose layout and development have been designed in advance according to an overall plan, rather than growing organically over time. Planning may range from a street grid and zoning scheme to a fully detailed blueprint covering housing, transport, industry and public space.

History

Planned cities are ancient: the Indus Valley city of Mohenjo-daro (c. 2600 BCE) had a grid of streets and sophisticated drainage, and Hippodamus of Miletus was famed in classical Greece for gridiron layouts. Medieval bastides, Renaissance ideal cities, Baroque capitals such as Karlsruhe, and colonial foundations such as Philadelphia and Adelaide extended the tradition. In the 19th century, company towns and garden cities — Ebenezer Howard's influential concept, realised at Letchworth and Welwyn — responded to industrial overcrowding.

Modern planned capitals

Many nations have built capitals from scratch: Washington, D.C., Canberra, New Delhi, Ankara, Brasília (inaugurated 1960 to Lúcio Costa's and Oscar Niemeyer's modernist plan), Chandigarh (designed by Le Corbusier), Islamabad, Abuja, Naypyidaw and Egypt's New Administrative Capital. Post-war "new towns" such as Britain's Milton Keynes and France's villes nouvelles applied planning at regional scale.

Advantages and criticism

Planned cities can provide coherent infrastructure, green space and efficient land use from the outset. Critics argue they can feel sterile, impose rigid zoning that separates functions and stifles spontaneity, and fail to anticipate how residents actually use space. Many successful examples evolved considerably from their original master plans. Contemporary eco-city and smart city projects, such as Masdar City and Songdo, continue the planned tradition with an emphasis on sustainability and data systems.
