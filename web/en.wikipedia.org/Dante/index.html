Dante Alighieri

Born: c. 1265, Florence, Republic of Florence
Died: 14 September 1321, Ravenna
Notable work: Divine Comedy; La Vita Nuova; De Monarchia

Durante degli Alighieri, commonly known as Dante (c. 1265 – 14 September 1321), was an Italian poet, writer and philosopher. His Divine Comedy, originally called Comedìa and later christened Divina by Giovanni Boccaccio, is widely considered one of the most important poems of the Middle Ages and the greatest literary work in the Italian language.

Life

Dante was born in Florence into a family of the lesser nobility aligned with the Guelphs. He was betrothed and married to Gemma di Manetto Donati, and was famously devoted to Beatrice Portinari, whom he celebrated in the Vita Nuova. He fought as a cavalryman at the Battle of Campaldino (1289) and served as one of Florence's priors in 1300. Caught in the conflict between White and Black Guelphs, he was exiled from Florence in 1302 and never returned, wandering between Italian courts before settling in Ravenna under Guido da Polenta.

Works

The Divine Comedy describes Dante's journey through Hell (Inferno), Purgatory (Purgatorio) and Paradise (Paradiso), guided first by the Roman poet Virgil and then by Beatrice. Written in Tuscan vernacular rather than Latin, it helped establish Tuscan as the standard Italian language. Dante's other works include the philosophical treatise Convivio, the Latin political treatise De Monarchia, and De vulgari eloquentia, on the eloquence of the vernacular.

Legacy

Dante is often called the "father of the Italian language" and il Sommo Poeta ("the Supreme Poet"). He died in Ravenna in 1321, shortly after completing the Comedy, and is buried there despite Florence's later claims to his remains.
