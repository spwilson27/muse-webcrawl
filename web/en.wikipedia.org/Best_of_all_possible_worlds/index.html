Best of all possible worlds

The phrase "the best of all possible worlds" (French: le meilleur des mondes possibles) was coined by the German philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in his 1710 work Essais de Théodicée sur la bonté de Dieu, la liberté de l'homme et l'origine du mal ("Essays on Theodicy concerning the goodness of God, the freedom of man and the origin of evil").

Leibniz's argument

Leibniz argued that an omnipotent, omniscient and perfectly good God would necessarily create the best world He could. Since God surveyed all possible worlds and chose to actualise this one, the actual world must be the best of all possible worlds — despite the evil and suffering it contains, which Leibniz held to be necessary consequences of a world containing free creatures and ordered natural laws. The doctrine is a central part of his theodicy, an attempt to reconcile the existence of evil with the goodness of God.

Satire and reception

The idea was famously satirised by Voltaire in his 1759 novella Candide, whose character Dr Pangloss stubbornly repeats that "all is for the best in the best of all possible worlds" in the face of disasters, including the 1755 Lisbon earthquake, which had already shaken European confidence in philosophical optimism. Later philosophers have both defended revised forms of Leibniz's optimism and attacked it as unfalsifiable or morally complacent.
