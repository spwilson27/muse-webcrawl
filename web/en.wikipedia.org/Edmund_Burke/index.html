Edmund Burke (12 January 1729 – 9 July 1797) was an Anglo-Irish statesman, philosopher and writer, born in Dublin, who served as a member of the British Parliament between 1766 and 1794 in the House of Commons of Great Britain with the Whig Party. He is remembered principally for his support of the American colonies in their dispute with Britain, his advocacy of Catholic emancipation and economic reform, his prosecution of Warren Hastings, and above all for his Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790), a foundational text of modern conservatism.

Early life and writings

Burke was educated at Trinity College Dublin and studied law at the Middle Temple in London, which he soon abandoned for literature. His A Vindication of Natural Society (1756) satirised rationalist utopianism, while A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful (1757) was an influential treatise on aesthetics that drew the attention of Kant and Lessing.

Parliamentary career

Entering Parliament in 1766 as member for Wendover, and later for Bristol and Malton, Burke became a leading spokesman of the Rockingham Whigs. He argued for conciliation with the American colonies, defending their resistance to taxation without representation while opposing their independence. In Thoughts on the Cause of the Present Discontents (1770) he gave the first modern defence of political parties. From 1788 he led the impeachment of Warren Hastings, former Governor-General of India, denouncing the abuses of the East India Company.

Reflections on the Revolution in France

Burke's Reflections, published in November 1790, was among the first sustained attacks on the French Revolution. Against abstract claims of the rights of man, Burke defended prescription, tradition, inherited institutions and gradual reform, warning that revolutionary rationalism would end in anarchy and military despotism — a prediction widely seen as vindicated by the Terror and Napoleon. The work provoked famous replies, including Thomas Paine's Rights of Man and Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Men.

Legacy

Burke is regarded as the philosophical founder of conservatism, though his thought also influenced liberals and he is claimed by both traditions. His emphasis on prudence, the partnership between generations, and society as an inheritance rather than a contract remains central to conservative political theory.
