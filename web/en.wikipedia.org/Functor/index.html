Functor

In mathematics, specifically category theory, a functor is a mapping between categories which preserves their structure. Functors can be thought of as homomorphisms between categories, or as morphisms in the category of small categories. They were first considered in algebraic topology and have since become a fundamental tool throughout modern mathematics.

A functor F from a category C to a category D assigns to each object X in C an object F(X) in D, and to each morphism f : X → Y in C a morphism F(f) : F(X) → F(Y) in D, in such a way that identity morphisms and composition of morphisms are preserved.

Definition

Let C and D be categories. A (covariant) functor F from C to D consists of an assignment on objects and an assignment on morphisms satisfying two axioms: F(idX) = idF(X) for every object X, and F(g ∘ f) = F(g) ∘ F(f) whenever the composition g ∘ f is defined.

A contravariant functor reverses the direction of morphisms: it maps f : X → Y to F(f) : F(Y) → F(X), and composition is correspondingly reversed. Contravariant functors may equivalently be viewed as covariant functors from the opposite category Cop to D.

Examples

The forgetful functor from the category of groups to the category of sets assigns to each group its underlying set and to each group homomorphism the corresponding function, "forgetting" the algebraic structure. The free functor, left adjoint to it, assigns to each set the free group generated by it. The power set construction gives a contravariant functor on the category of sets. In algebraic topology, homology and cohomology are functors from categories of topological spaces to categories of abelian groups or modules.

Properties

Functors preserve isomorphisms: if f is an isomorphism in C, then F(f) is an isomorphism in D. However, a functor need not be injective or surjective on objects or morphisms; functors that are (in the appropriate sense) are called faithful, full, or essentially surjective, and a functor with all three properties is part of an equivalence of categories. The study of functors between fixed categories leads to natural transformations, the morphisms between functors, and to functor categories.
