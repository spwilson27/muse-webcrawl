French invasion of Russia

The French invasion of Russia, also known as the Russian campaign (French: Campagne de Russie) and in Russia as the Patriotic War of 1812, began on 24 June 1812 when Napoleon's Grande Armée crossed the Neman River into the Russian Empire. It ended in catastrophe: of more than 600,000 men who entered Russia, only a fraction survived the advance on Moscow and the winter retreat, and the disaster fatally weakened Napoleon's empire.

Background

Napoleon invaded to compel Tsar Alexander I to rejoin the Continental System against Britain and to settle rival claims over Poland. The Grande Armée, the largest army yet assembled in Europe, included troops from France, Poland, Germany, Italy, Austria and Prussia.

The advance

The Russian armies under Barclay de Tolly and later Kutuzov retreated deep into Russia, scorching the earth. After the bloody, indecisive Battle of Borodino (7 September), Napoleon entered a deserted, burning Moscow on 14 September and waited five weeks in vain for Alexander to negotiate.

The retreat

The retreat began on 19 October into early winter cold, starvation and constant Cossack harassment. The crossing of the Berezina River in late November was a final disaster. By December the remnant had left Russian soil; total French and allied losses are estimated at around 400,000–500,000 dead, with some 200,000 taken prisoner. Russian losses were comparable in scale.

Consequences

The campaign destroyed Napoleon's military supremacy and emboldened the Sixth Coalition that defeated him in 1813–14. It remains a defining event of Russian national memory, commemorated in Tchaikovsky's 1812 Overture and Tolstoy's War and Peace; Minard's famous map of the army's losses is a landmark of statistical graphics.
