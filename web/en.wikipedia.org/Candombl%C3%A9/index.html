Candomblé

Candomblé is an African diasporic religion that developed in Brazil during the 19th century. It arose through a process of syncretism between several of the traditional religions of West Africa, especially those of the Yoruba, Bantu and Gbe peoples, coupled with influences from Roman Catholicism. Adherents, called povo do santo ("people of the saint"), venerate deities known as orixás, voduns and inkices.

Beliefs and practice

Candomblé teaches the existence of a supreme creator, Oludumaré/Olorun, served by divinities embodying natural forces and human archetypes. Worship takes place in temples called terreiros, led by priests (babalorixás) and priestesses (ialorixás). Rituals involve drumming, dance, offerings and possession trance, in which a deity is believed to mount an initiated devotee.

History

The religion was formed by enslaved Africans in Bahia, who preserved their traditions under Catholic cover. Persecuted under the Empire and early Republic, Candomblé gained legal recognition in the 20th century and is now an established part of Afro-Brazilian culture, with major "nations" including Ketu, Angola and Jeje.
