Optimal experimental design

In statistics, an optimal experimental design is a design of an experiment that is optimal according to some statistical criterion — typically, that it extracts the maximum information about model parameters from a given number of observations. Optimal designs minimize the variance of estimators by choosing the settings (and replication) of experimental runs in advance, rather than relying on classical standard designs alone.

Criteria

Most criteria are functions of the information matrix of the design. D-optimality maximizes its determinant, minimizing the volume of the confidence region for all parameters jointly. A-optimality minimizes the trace of the inverse information matrix, i.e., the average parameter variance. E-optimality maximizes the smallest eigenvalue, while G-optimality minimizes the maximum prediction variance over the design region. The Kiefer–Wolfowitz equivalence theorem establishes that D- and G-optimality coincide for approximate designs.

History and applications

The subject grew from the work of Kirstine Smith (1918) and was developed systematically by Jack Kiefer and others from the 1950s. Optimal designs are now standard in industrial experimentation, clinical trials — where they raise ethical questions as well as statistical ones — dose-finding studies, and computer experiments. Because optimal designs can depend on the assumed model and unknown parameters, practitioners also use robust, Bayesian, and sequential designs that adapt as data accumulate.
