The cochineal (Dactylopius coccus) is a scale insect in the suborder Sternorrhyncha, from which the natural dye cochineal is derived. A primarily sessile parasite in its native subtropical New World range, it lives on cacti in the genus Opuntia, feeding on plant moisture and nutrients. The insect produces carminic acid, which deters predation by other insects; this acid can be extracted from the body and eggs to make the crimson dye also known as carmine (food additive E120).

History

Cochineal dye was used by the Aztec and Maya peoples of pre-Columbian Mexico, where it was a valuable tribute item. After the Spanish conquest it became one of New Spain's most valuable exports — second only to silver — and coloured the red coats of British soldiers and the textiles of Europe. Spain guarded the secret of its insect origin for centuries. Production later spread to the Canary Islands and elsewhere; Peru is today the largest producer.

Use

About 70,000 insects are needed to make one pound (about 450 g) of dye. Carmine is used in foods, drinks, cosmetics and artists' pigments, and interest in it revived as consumers sought natural colorings to replace synthetic red dyes. The insect's biology also matters to agriculture: related Dactylopius species have been used as biological control agents against invasive prickly pear cacti in Australia and South Africa — a notable example of organismal interactions exploited in pest management.
