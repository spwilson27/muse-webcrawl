Health maintenance organization

A health maintenance organization (HMO) is a type of health insurance plan in the United States that provides coverage through a network of doctors, hospitals and other providers that agree to supply services to members, usually for a fixed prepaid fee. Members generally must choose a primary care physician who coordinates their care and provides referrals to specialists.

Characteristics

HMOs typically charge lower premiums and out-of-pocket costs than preferred provider organizations (PPOs) but restrict members to in-network care, usually covering out-of-network services only in emergencies. Providers may be paid by capitation — a fixed amount per patient — which is intended to reward preventive care and cost control rather than volume of services.

History

The model developed from prepaid group practices such as Kaiser Permanente, founded in the 1940s. Federal support came with the Health Maintenance Organization Act of 1973, which required many employers to offer HMO options and provided development funding. HMOs expanded rapidly in the 1980s and 1990s as a form of managed care, drawing both praise for cost control and criticism for restrictions on choice and referrals.
