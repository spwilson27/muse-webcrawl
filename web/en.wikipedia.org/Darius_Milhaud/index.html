Darius Milhaud

Darius Milhaud, French composer of Les Six

Darius Milhaud (4 September 1892 – 22 June 1974) was a French composer, conductor and teacher. A member of Les Six, the group of young composers promoted by Jean Cocteau and Erik Satie, he was one of the most prolific composers of the twentieth century, with a catalogue of more than 400 works. He is known for his use of polytonality and for absorbing the rhythms of Brazilian music and jazz into classical forms.

Life and career

Born in Marseille to a Jewish family from Aix-en-Provence, Milhaud studied at the Paris Conservatoire with André Gedalge, Charles-Marie Widor and Vincent d'Indy. From 1917 to 1919 he served as secretary to the poet Paul Claudel, then French ambassador to Brazil; the music he heard in Rio de Janeiro shaped the ballet Le Bœuf sur le toit (1919) and the Saudades do Brasil piano pieces. A trip to the United States in 1922, where he heard jazz in Harlem, produced the ballet La Création du monde (1923), one of the earliest classical works to use jazz scoring.

Fleeing the German invasion in 1940, Milhaud emigrated to the United States and taught at Mills College in Oakland, California, where his students included Dave Brubeck and Burt Bacharach. After the war he divided his teaching between Mills and the Paris Conservatoire. His later works include twelve symphonies, operas such as Christophe Colomb and Bolivar, concertos, chamber music and film scores. Rheumatoid arthritis confined him to a wheelchair in later life.

Music

Milhaud's signature technique is polytonality — the simultaneous use of several keys — which gives his music its bright, tangy dissonance. His enormous output varies in character from the festive to the austere, and he wrote fluently for every medium, including eighteen string quartets.
