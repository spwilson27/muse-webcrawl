An epigram is a brief, interesting, memorable, and sometimes surprising or satirical statement. The word derives from the Greek epigramma, "inscription", from epigraphein, "to write on" — reflecting the form's origins in short poems inscribed on monuments, votive offerings and tombs.

History

Greek epigrams, collected in the Greek Anthology, were typically composed in elegiac couplets and covered subjects from epitaphs and dedications to love, drinking and literary criticism; Simonides, Callimachus and Meleager are among the celebrated authors. The Roman poet Martial (1st century CE) gave the epigram its definitive literary character: short, pointed poems, often ending in a witty or stinging turn. Through Martial the word came to suggest brevity combined with a sharp, paradoxical conclusion.

Later development

Renaissance and neoclassical poets revived the form in Latin and the vernaculars. In English, writers such as John Donne, Alexander Pope and Samuel Taylor Coleridge — who defined it in verse ("What is an epigram? a dwarfish whole, / Its body brevity, and wit its soul") — practised it, and Oscar Wilde made the spoken epigram an instrument of paradox and social satire. The term is also applied to prose maxims and aphorisms with a similar pointed concision.

Characteristics

Coleridge's definition captures the standard criteria: extreme brevity, compression of thought, and wit — often achieved through antithesis, paradox or a final unexpected twist. Closely related forms include the epitaph (an inscription for the dead), the aphorism and the motto.
