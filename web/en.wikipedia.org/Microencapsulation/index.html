Microencapsulation

Microencapsulation is a process in which tiny particles or droplets of a core material (solid, liquid or gas) are surrounded by a coating or shell to form small capsules, typically from about one micrometre to several millimetres in size. The resulting products are called microcapsules; larger particles are sometimes termed macrocapsules.

Purposes

Encapsulation can protect a sensitive core from moisture, oxygen, light or reactive chemicals; mask taste or odour; convert liquids into free-flowing powders; prevent volatile ingredients from evaporating; and provide controlled or triggered release of the contents — for example, when a capsule wall is rubbed, heated, dissolved or ruptured by pressure.

Techniques

Physical methods include spray drying (the dominant industrial process), pan coating, fluidised-bed coating, extrusion and centrifugal coextrusion. Physico-chemical methods include coacervation (phase separation of polymers such as gelatin–gum arabic), sol–gel encapsulation, and supercritical fluid processes. Chemical methods include interfacial polymerisation and in-situ polymerisation, used to produce melamine–formaldehyde shells.

Applications

Classic applications include carbonless copy paper (dye capsules broken by pen pressure), developed commercially in the 1950s; scratch-and-sniff perfume strips; detergents and cosmetics; phase-change microcapsules for thermal-regulating textiles; self-healing materials; agrochemicals; and pharmaceuticals, where encapsulation of drugs such as aspirin can reduce stomach irritation and enable timed release. In food science, flavours, oils and probiotics are commonly microencapsulated.
