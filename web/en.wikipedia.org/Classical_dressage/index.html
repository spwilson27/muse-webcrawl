Classical dressage

Classical dressage is the art of training horses according to the traditions developed in the royal riding academies of Europe from the Renaissance onward, culminating in the "airs above the ground" of the Spanish Riding School in Vienna. It emphasizes harmony, lightness, and the horse's willing obedience through refined aids rather than force.

History

The tradition descends from Xenophon's writings on horsemanship and was systematized by Renaissance masters such as Federico Grisone and, above all, Antoine de Pluvinel, riding master to Louis XIII, who advocatede gentler methods. William Cavendish, Duke of Newcastle, and François Robichon de La Guérinière, whose École de cavalerie (1733) introduced the shoulder-in and remains foundational, shaped the French school. In the 19th century François Baucher and James Fillis developed competing methods, while the German tradition emphasized systematic gymnastic development.

The high school and its institutions

The most advanced exercises—the airs above the ground—include the levade, courbette, and capriole, derived from military manoeuvres. Four academies preserve the tradition: the Spanish Riding School (Vienna, Lipizzaners), the Cadre Noir (Saumur, France), the Royal Andalusian School (Jerez, Spain), and the Portuguese School of Equestrian Art. UNESCO inscribed classical horsemanship traditions of these schools on its Intangible Cultural Heritage lists (2015 for Vienna).

Relation to competitive dressage

Modern competitive dressage descends from the classical tradition but is judged in arenas by prescribed tests. Classical purists sometimes criticize competition riding for emphasizing spectacle over lightness, advocating instead the older masters' progressive, force-free schooling.
