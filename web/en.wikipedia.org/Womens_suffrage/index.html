Women's suffrage

Women's suffrage is the right of women to vote in elections. Beginning in the mid-19th century, besides women working for broad-based economic and political equality and for social reforms, women sought to change voting laws to allow them to vote. National and international organisations formed to coordinate efforts toward that objective — most notably the International Woman Suffrage Alliance (founded 1904) — as well as for equal civil rights generally.

Timeline of adoption

New Zealand became the first self-governing country to grant women the vote in national elections, in 1893, followed by the Commonwealth of Australia in 1902 (though Indigenous Australians were largely excluded until 1962). Finland, then a Russian grand duchy, adopted full suffrage with eligibility to stand for office in 1906, electing the world's first women parliamentarians in 1907. Norway (1913), Denmark (1915), Canada, Germany, Austria, Poland, and Russia (1917–18), the Netherlands (1919), and the United States — through the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920 — followed. Britain enfranchised some women in 1918 and all on equal terms in 1928. Some countries came much later: France in 1944, Italy in 1946, Switzerland at the federal level in 1971, and Saudi Arabia in 2015.

Movements

Suffrage campaigns ranged from the constitutional lobbying of suffragists to the militant tactics of the British suffragettes and the American movement led by figures such as Susan B. Anthony, Elizabeth Cady Stanton, and Alice Paul. Opposition drew on claims about women's proper sphere and fears about social order; supporters argued from natural rights, taxation, and women's wartime and civic contributions. Victories were frequently partial at first — limited by age, property, marital status, or race — and full equality often took decades longer.

Legacy

Universal suffrage is now written into international law, including the Convention on the Political Rights of Women (1953) and CEDAW (1979), and women's turnout equals or exceeds men's in many democracies, though women's representation in legislatures worldwide remains well below parity.
