Eyak language

Eyak
Native to: United States
Region: Alaska, Copper River Delta
Ethnicity: Eyak people
Extinct: 2008, with the death of Marie Smith Jones
Family: Na-Dené — Eyak

The Eyak language (Eyak: I·ya·q) is a recently extinct Na-Dené language historically spoken in south-central Alaska, in the area around the mouth of the Copper River, near the present-day town of Cordova. It is the sole member of the Eyak branch of the Na-Dené family, and is most closely related to the Athabaskan languages; more distantly, it is related to Tlingit.

History

The Eyak people's traditional territory extended along the Gulf of Alaska coast from Yakutat to the Copper River. Contact with Russian traders, the introduction of Tlingit as a trade language, and later English-language schooling steadily reduced the number of Eyak speakers through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

The last native speaker, Marie Smith Jones (1918–2008), became a well-known advocate for indigenous language preservation. After her death on 21 January 2008, Eyak was declared extinct as a first language. The language was extensively documented by the linguist Michael E. Krauss, who worked with Jones and other speakers and published a grammar and dictionary materials.

Revival efforts

Since 2010, a revival movement led by learners such as Guillaume Leduey, a French student who taught himself Eyak from archival materials, has sought to bring the language back into use. The Eyak Language Project and the Eyak Preservation Council have produced learning materials and held language camps in Cordova, and a small community of second-language learners now studies and uses the language.

Phonology and grammar

Eyak shares the complex consonant inventory typical of Na-Dené languages, including glottalized (ejective) stops and affricates, and a series of lateral affricates. Like its Athabaskan relatives, Eyak is a polysynthetic, verb-heavy language in which the verb word carries much of the grammatical information of the clause, with elaborate prefixing morphology. Eyak lacks nasal vowels in most positions and developed a distinctive vowel system compared with neighbouring languages.
