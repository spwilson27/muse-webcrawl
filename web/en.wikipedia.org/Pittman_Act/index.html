Pittman Act

The Pittman Act was a United States federal law sponsored by Senator Key Pittman of Nevada and enacted on April 23, 1918. The act authorized the United States Secretary of the Treasury to melt down and sell up to 350 million standard silver dollars as bullion, principally to Great Britain, which needed silver to support the Indian rupee during the First World War.

Provisions

The act provided for the melting of the silver dollars and the sale of the resulting bullion at not less than one dollar per fine ounce. It further directed that when the war emergency passed, the Treasury would purchase American-mined silver at one dollar per ounce and recoin an equal number of silver dollars to replace those melted, thereby restoring the Treasury's stock and supporting the domestic silver-mining industry.

Effects

Under the act, some 270 million silver dollars were melted between 1918 and 1920, the largest destruction of United States coinage in the nation's history. Because so many coins were destroyed, the Morgan dollar was struck again in 1921 to begin replacing them, and the new Peace dollar design, introduced at the end of 1921, was coined in large numbers under the act's replacement provisions. The purchases of domestically mined silver at a fixed price above the market price amounted to a substantial subsidy for silver miners, particularly in Pittman's home state of Nevada.
