Walter Bradford Cannon (19 October 1871 – 1 October 1945) was an American physiologist who spent his career at Harvard Medical School, where he chaired the physiology department for more than three decades. He coined the term homeostasis and developed the concept of the "fight or flight" response.

Research

Cannon began his research career using the newly discovered X-rays with bismuth meals to observe digestion in animals, pioneering radiographic studies of the gastrointestinal tract and publishing The Mechanical Factors of Digestion (1911). During the First World War he studied traumatic shock in wounded soldiers, work summarised in his 1923 book Traumatic Shock.

His mature work concerned the autonomic nervous system and the body's self-regulation. In Bodily Changes in Pain, Hunger, Fear and Rage (1915; revised 1929) he described how adrenaline prepares the body for emergency. In The Wisdom of the Body (1932) he popularised the term "homeostasis" — building on Claude Bernard's milieu intérieur — for the coordinated processes that keep the internal environment stable. With Philip Bard he proposed the Cannon–Bard theory of emotion, arguing that emotional experience and physiological arousal occur simultaneously, in opposition to the earlier James–Lange theory.

Later life

Cannon was also active in public life, defending academic freedom and aiding refugee scholars in the 1930s. He died of leukaemia in 1945, his health having suffered from years of unshielded X-ray experimentation.
