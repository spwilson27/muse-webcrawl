Beneš decrees

The Beneš decrees are a series of laws drafted by the Czechoslovak government-in-exile in London and issued by President Edvard Beneš during and shortly after the Second World War, in the absence of a sitting parliament. The decrees dealt with the restoration of the Czechoslovak state, the punishment of Nazi collaborators, and — most controversially — the confiscation of property and loss of citizenship of ethnic Germans and Hungarians, which underpinned the post-war expulsion of roughly three million ethnic Germans from Czechoslovakia.

Content

More than 140 decrees were issued between 1940 and 1945. Key measures included Decree No. 12/1945 on the confiscation of agricultural property of Germans, Hungarians, and collaborators; Decree No. 108/1945 on general confiscation of "enemy property"; and the constitutional decree on citizenship, which stripped ethnic Germans and Hungarians of Czechoslovak citizenship except those who could prove loyalty to the republic or suffering under Nazi persecution.

Legacy

The decrees remain politically sensitive. Successive Czech governments have maintained their legal validity while expressing regret for excesses committed during the expulsions. The issue was raised during the Czech Republic's accession negotiations with the European Union, which concluded in 2002 that the decrees were not an obstacle to membership.
