Western Regions
Chinese西域 (Xīyù)
AreaCentral Asia, chiefly the Tarim Basin (modern Xinjiang)
PeriodTerm used from the Han dynasty (3rd century BC) to the Ming dynasty

The Western Regions or Xiyu (Chinese: 西域, "Western Regions") was a historical name used in Chinese chronicles between the 3rd century BC and the 8th century AD for the regions west of the Jade Gate (Yumen Pass), including the Tarim Basin and, at times, parts of Central Asia beyond it.

The term entered history with the journeys of Zhang Qian, the Han dynasty envoy sent in 138 BC to seek allies against the Xiongnu. His reports opened the routes later known as the Silk Road, linking China through oasis kingdoms — such as Kashgar, Khotan, Kucha, Turfan and Loulan — with Persia and the Mediterranean world. In 60 BC the Han established the Protectorate of the Western Regions, headquartered near modern Luntai, to administer the Tarim Basin city-states.

Trade and culture

Through the Western Regions flowed silk, jade from Khotan, glassware, horses and crops such as grapes and alfalfa. The routes were also a conduit for Buddhism, which entered China from India and Central Asia; the cave temples of Dunhuang, Kizil and Turfan preserve its art. Control of the region alternated between Chinese dynasties, local kingdoms, the Tibetan Empire and steppe confederations.

Later history

The Tang dynasty re-established control through the Anxi Protectorate in the 7th century, but Chinese influence waned after the Battle of Talas (751) and the An Lushan rebellion. The term Xiyu remained in use into the Ming dynasty, when its scope narrowed largely to the area of present-day Xinjiang.
