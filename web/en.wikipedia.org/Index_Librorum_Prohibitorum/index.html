The Index Librorum Prohibitorum ("List of Prohibited Books") was a list of publications deemed heretical, anti-clerical or lascivious by the Catholic Church, which Catholics were forbidden to read or possess without permission. Published by the Roman Inquisition and later administered by the Sacred Congregation of the Index (established 1571), it was one of the most prominent instruments of censorship in early modern Europe and remained in force for more than four centuries.

History

Lists of forbidden books preceded the printed Index: the University of Paris and various bishops had condemned works in the medieval period, and printed regional lists appeared in the 1540s–1550s at Louvain, Paris and Venice. The first Roman Index was promulgated by Pope Paul IV in 1559 — the severe "Pauline Index", which banned the complete works of more than 550 authors, including all writings of Erasmus. The Council of Trent moderated the scheme: the Tridentine Index of 1564, issued under Pius IV, replaced blanket prohibitions with ten general rules and allowed some works in expurgated form.

Successive editions — some twenty in all — appeared until 1948. Administration passed to the Congregation of the Index and, after 1917, to the Holy Office. On 14 June 1966, in the wake of the Second Vatican Council, the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith announced that the Index no longer had the force of ecclesiastical law, though it retained "moral force"; the list was formally discontinued.

Contents and notable entries

The Index targeted Protestant theology first of all: the works of Luther, Calvin, Zwingli and eventually the Bible in vernacular translation were prohibited or restricted. Over time it encompassed philosophy, science, literature and political thought. Notable listed authors and works included:

  • Copernicus's De revolutionibus (1616, suspended "until corrected") and Galileo's Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems (1633); works of Kepler and Descartes were also listed.
  • Philosophers including Machiavelli, Spinoza, Locke, Berkeley, Hume, Kant and Mill.
  • Writers such as Rabelais, Montaigne, Swift, Voltaire, Rousseau, Hugo (all works, until 1959 registrations lapsed), Dumas, Flaubert (Madame Bovary), and, in the twentieth century, Jean-Paul Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir.

Enforcement and effect

Enforcement varied greatly by country: the Spanish and Portuguese Inquisitions published their own, often stricter, indices; France's Gallican church accepted the Tridentine rules only partially, and the Index had no civil force in much of Europe. Historians debate its practical effect: it plainly constrained Catholic printing and bookselling, drove some scholarship north of the Alps, and chilled scientific publication in Italy, yet prohibited books circulated widely, and listing sometimes increased a work's fame and sales outside Catholic lands — an early example of the publicity value of censorship.
