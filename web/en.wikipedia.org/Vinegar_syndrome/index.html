Vinegar syndrome
AffectsCellulose acetate film stock
CauseHydrolysis of acetate groups releasing acetic acid
SignsVinegar odour, shrinkage, brittleness, buckling
PreventionCool, dry storage; duplication/digitisation

Vinegar syndrome is a chemical degradation process that affects cellulose acetate film, the "safety film" base widely used for motion pictures, still photography and magnetic tape from the mid-20th century. Named for the vinegar smell of the acetic acid it releases, it is one of the most serious threats to film archives.

Chemistry

The process begins when moisture and heat cause the acetate groups in the cellulose acetate base to hydrolyse, freeing acetic acid. The acid catalyses further breakdown in an autocatalytic cycle, so decay, once started, accelerates and can spread to nearby reels stored in the same container or vault. As the base degrades it shrinks, becomes brittle, and the emulsion layer may buckle, channel or separate, making the film unplayable.

Detection and management

Archivists detect vinegar syndrome by smell and by acid-detection (A-D) strips that change colour according to acidity levels. Affected items are segregated from healthy stock, copied or digitised as a priority, and stored in cold, dry conditions — low temperature and controlled relative humidity greatly slow the reaction. Polyester-based film does not suffer from vinegar syndrome.

Significance

Because much of the world's film heritage from roughly 1950 to the 1980s exists only on acetate stock, vinegar syndrome drives large preservation and digitisation programmes at national archives and studios, and informs modern cold-storage vault design.
