The Treaty of Lisbon, officially the Treaty of Lisbon amending the Treaty on European Union and the Treaty establishing the European Community, is an international agreement that amended the two treaties forming the constitutional basis of the European Union (EU). Signed by the EU member states on 13 December 2007 at the Jerónimos Monastery in Lisbon, it entered into force on 1 December 2009.

Background

The treaty was drafted to replace the failed Treaty establishing a Constitution for Europe, which had been rejected in referendums in France and the Netherlands in 2005. It preserved most of the Constitutional Treaty's institutional reforms while dropping its constitutional symbolism, and it was ratified by parliamentary approval in most states. Ireland, required to hold a referendum, initially voted against it in June 2008; after receiving legal guarantees, Irish voters approved it in a second referendum in October 2009.

Main reforms

  • A permanent President of the European Council, serving a two-and-a-half-year renewable term, replaced the rotating presidency for summits.
  • The post of High Representative for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy was strengthened and linked to the European External Action Service.
  • The Charter of Fundamental Rights became legally binding.
  • The European Union gained explicit legal personality, and the "pillar" structure was abolished.
  • The European Parliament's co-decision powers were extended to most legislation; a "double majority" system of qualified-majority voting in the Council was introduced (phased in by 2017).
  • For the first time, a clause (Article 50) formally provided a procedure for a member state to withdraw from the Union — the provision later used by the United Kingdom in Brexit.

Legacy

The Treaty of Lisbon remains the framework under which the EU operates. It streamlined decision-making, enhanced the Union's external representation, and introduced citizens' initiatives and a greater role for national parliaments in policing subsidiarity.
