Free France

Free France (French: France libre) was a political entity claiming to be the legitimate government of France during World War II. Established by Charles de Gaulle in London following his Appeal of 18 June 1940, it opposed the armistice with Nazi Germany and the collaborationist Vichy regime, and organised the Free French Forces to fight alongside the Allies. Territories of the French colonial empire progressively rallied to it, providing a territorial and military base.

The Cross of Lorraine, emblem of the Free French.

From exile government to liberation

De Gaulle's exile forces fought at Dakar, Gabon, Syria, Bir Hakeim (1942), and with the British Eighth Army in North Africa. In 1943 the organisation became the French Committee of National Liberation (CFLN) in Algiers, merging de Gaulle's movement with General Giraud's North African command, and in June 1944 it restyled itself the Provisional Government of the French Republic (GPRF). Free French units — including Leclerc's 2nd Armoured Division — landed in Normandy, liberated Paris in August 1944, and grew by absorbing the internal Resistance (FFI) into an army of over a million by 1945. France was recognised as a victorious great power, receiving occupation zones in Germany and Austria and a permanent seat on the UN Security Council.

Relations and legacy

Relations with Britain and especially the United States were often strained — Roosevelt long refused to recognise de Gaulle's government as representing France. Domestically, Free France embodied the claim that the Republic had never ceased to exist and that Vichy was illegitimate, a position later enshrined in French law and memory ("résistancialisme").
