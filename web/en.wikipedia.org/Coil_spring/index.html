Coil spring

A coil spring is an elastic mechanical device made by winding wire — usually spring steel — into a helix. Coil springs store mechanical energy when deformed and release it when the load is removed. Depending on design, they work in compression, extension (tension), or torsion.

Types

Compression springs shorten under load and are used in vehicle suspensions, mattresses, valves, and countless mechanisms. Extension springs have coils wound tightly together and stretch under load, often with loops or hooks at the ends, as in trampoline and garage-door assemblies. Torsion springs, such as those in clothespins and mousetraps, resist twisting about the coil axis. Springs may be cylindrical, conical, barrel-shaped, or variable-pitch to suit their application.

Behaviour

For moderate deflections, a spring obeys Hooke's law: the force is proportional to the displacement, with the constant of proportionality called the spring rate or spring constant k. The rate depends on the wire diameter, coil diameter, number of active coils, and the shear modulus of the material. Springs fail by fatigue after many cycles, by corrosion, or by permanent set if stressed beyond the elastic limit.

History and manufacture

Helical springs came into use in locks and clocks in the fifteenth century, and coil suspension springs later became standard in railway carriages and automobiles. Modern springs are wound on automatic coiling machines and may be shot-peened, preset, and coated to improve fatigue life.
