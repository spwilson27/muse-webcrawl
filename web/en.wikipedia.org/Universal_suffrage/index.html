Universal suffrage

Universal suffrage is the right of all adult citizens to vote in elections, regardless of wealth, income, gender, social status, race, ethnicity, political stance or any other restriction, subject only to relatively minor exceptions such as age, residency, citizenship, mental capacity and (in some jurisdictions) criminal conviction.

History

Early democracies typically limited the franchise by property, literacy or sex. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries saw gradual extensions: working-class men gained the vote through reforms such as Britain's Reform Acts and the Chartist movement, while organised campaigns for women's suffrage won the vote first in New Zealand (1893) and later across Europe and the Americas, notably the United States with the Nineteenth Amendment (1920) and Britain in 1918 and 1928.

Modern status

Universal suffrage is now regarded as a hallmark of democracy and is proclaimed in Article 21 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Disputes continue over prisoner voting, voting ages (often 18, sometimes 16), and the disenfranchisement effects of identification and registration rules.
