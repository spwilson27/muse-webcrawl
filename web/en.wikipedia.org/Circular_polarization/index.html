Circular polarization

In electrodynamics, circular polarization of an electromagnetic wave is a polarization state in which, at each point, the electromagnetic field of the wave has a constant magnitude and is rotating at a constant rate in a plane perpendicular to the direction of the wave. In a circularly polarized wave, the electric field vector, at a point in space, describes a circle as time progresses; at a single instant, the field along the wave's path traces a helix.

Description

Circular polarization is a limiting case of elliptical polarization. It can be produced by superposing two linearly polarized waves of equal amplitude whose polarization directions are perpendicular, with a phase difference of exactly one quarter of a cycle (90°). Depending on the sense of rotation, the wave is designated right-handed or left-handed circularly polarized. By convention, established in optics and radio engineering respectively with differing handedness conventions, the designation refers to the rotation sense viewed from a specified direction, which can cause confusion between disciplines.

Applications

Circularly polarized antennas are used in satellite communications because reception is relatively insensitive to the satellite's orientation and to Faraday rotation in the ionosphere. Circular polarization is used in 3D cinema systems, where polarizing filters in the glasses separate the left- and right-eye images, and in optical devices such as quarter-wave plates and circular polarizers for photography, which reduce reflections and glare. Many animals, including mantis shrimp and some beetles, can perceive circularly polarized light, and scarab beetles reflect it from their shells.

In nature

Reflected sunlight can become partially circularly polarized. In biology, the cuticle of some scarab beetles reflects circularly polarized light due to a helicoidal arrangement of chitin fibres, producing striking metallic green and gold colours.
