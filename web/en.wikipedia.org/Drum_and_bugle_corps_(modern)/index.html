Drum and bugle corps (modern)

A modern drum and bugle corps is a marching ensemble comprising brass instruments, percussion instruments, and color guard, which performs choreographed shows on football fields in competitions judged on musical and visual execution. Today's corps descend from military and veterans' ensembles of the early 20th century but are now independent youth and all-age arts organizations, most prominently in North America.

Instrumentation

Unlike marching bands, drum corps traditionally used only brass instruments in the key of G, called bugles; since 2000, Drum Corps International (DCI) rules have permitted multi-key brass. A modern corps fields trumpets, mellophones, baritones, euphoniums, and tubas (contras); a battery of snare drums, tenor drums, bass drums, and cymbals; a stationary front ensemble ("pit") of marimbas, vibraphones, timpani, and electronics; and a color guard that spins flags, rifles, and sabres and dances. Electronics and amplification were progressively legalised in the 2000s.

Season and competition

Corps rehearse through the winter and spring, then tour in the summer, performing at shows across the United States and Canada and sleeping on gymnasium floors between travel days. The season culminates in the DCI World Championships each August. Judges score music, visual performance, and general effect. Leading World Class corps have included the Blue Devils, The Cadets, The Cavaliers, Phantom Regiment, Santa Clara Vanguard, Bluecoats, and Carolina Crown.

Participation

DCI World and Open Class corps are limited to members aged 21 or under (22 in some cases), while all-age corps compete under Drum Corps Associates and the later all-age classes. Members pay tour fees and rehearse at an intensity often compared to professional sport, making the activity a notable training ground for music educators and performers.
