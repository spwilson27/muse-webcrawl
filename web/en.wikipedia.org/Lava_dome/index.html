Lava dome

In volcanology, a lava dome or volcanic dome is a roughly circular mound-shaped protrusion resulting from the slow extrusion of viscous lava from a volcano. Dome-building eruptions are common, particularly in subduction-zone volcanoes, and the lava involved is typically rich in silica — rhyolite, dacite or andesite — making it too thick to flow far from the vent. As the lava piles up, it cools and hardens, and the dome grows by expansion from within (endogenous growth) or by lobes of lava breaking out on its surface (exogenous growth).

Characteristics

Because viscous lava does not travel far, it mounds steeply over and around the vent, forming dome-shaped masses that may reach hundreds of metres in height. Cooling at the surface produces a rubbly crust of blocks (talus) around the base. Domes may grow for months or years; the 2004–2008 eruption of Mount St. Helens built a new dome beside the one formed after its 1980 eruption.

Hazards

Lava domes are unstable structures. Partial collapse of a growing dome can generate pyroclastic flows — fast-moving avalanches of hot gas and rock — which are among the most lethal volcanic hazards. Dome collapse at Soufrière Hills on Montserrat destroyed the capital, Plymouth, in 1997, and collapses at Merapi in Indonesia have caused many fatalities. Domes can also plug a vent, allowing pressure to build toward an explosive eruption.

Notable domes

  • The Novarupta dome in Alaska, formed after the 1912 eruption.
  • The domes of the Mono–Inyo Craters and Lassen Peak in California.
  • The growing dome of Chaitén volcano in Chile, which began erupting in 2008 after some 9,000 years of quiet.
