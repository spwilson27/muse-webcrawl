The economy of Zambia is one of the fastest-growing economies in Africa over some periods, yet it remains heavily dependent on copper mining, which historically accounts for roughly 70% of export earnings. Zambia is Africa's second-largest copper producer, and the Copperbelt region in the north of the country has been the centre of its mining industry since the 1920s. The currency is the Zambian kwacha.

Copper mining on the Copperbelt

History

At independence in 1964, Zambia inherited a relatively strong economy oriented toward copper exports. Under President Kenneth Kaunda, the mines were nationalised into the state company ZCCM. Falling copper prices in the 1970s, combined with oil shocks and economic mismanagement, produced two decades of decline. In the 1990s, the government of Frederick Chiluba implemented structural adjustment and privatised the copper mines. High copper prices in the 2000s drove a recovery, with growth averaging over 6% for much of the decade.

Debt crisis

Heavy external borrowing, including Eurobonds issued in 2012–2015 and loans from China for infrastructure, pushed public debt to unsustainable levels. In November 2020, Zambia became the first African country to default on its debt during the COVID-19 pandemic. The government of Hakainde Hichilema, elected in 2021, negotiated a restructuring under the G20 Common Framework and an IMF programme, reaching agreement with official creditors in 2023 and bondholders thereafter.

Sectors

Services account for about half of GDP, industry (mainly mining and construction) for roughly a third, and agriculture — employing the majority of Zambians, largely at subsistence level — for the remainder. Maize is the staple crop. Hydropower from the Kariba and Kafue dams supplies most electricity, though droughts have caused recurring power shortages. Tourism centred on Victoria Falls is a growing source of foreign exchange.
