Digital rights management (DRM) is a set of access-control technologies used by publishers, copyright holders and hardware manufacturers to restrict the use of proprietary digital content and devices after sale. DRM is implemented through encryption, licence verification, copy limits and hardware controls, and is applied to ebooks, music, films, video games and software. Circumventing DRM is restricted by law in many jurisdictions, notably by the anti-circumvention provisions of the US Digital Millennium Copyright Act (1998).

History

Precursors included software copy protection and serial numbers in the 1980s. The DVD format (1996) brought the first mass-market cryptographic DRM, Content Scramble System (CSS), which was cracked in 1999 by the DeCSS program. The early 2000s saw DRM embedded in download stores: Apple's iTunes Store launched in 2003 with FairPlay restrictions, but customer pressure and competition led Apple (2009) and others to drop DRM from music sales. In video, streaming services such as Netflix rely on platform DRM (Widevine, PlayReady, FairPlay) as a licensing condition. Video-game DRM, from SecuROM to Denuvo, has been controversial for performance impacts and for rendering games unplayable when activation servers shut down.

Technical approaches

  • Encryption and licence keys — content is distributed encrypted; licences specify permitted uses (play counts, devices, expiry).
  • Product activation — software validates against a server, binding to hardware fingerprints.
  • Hardware enforcement — HDCP on display links, secure enclaves and trusted execution environments, e-reader and console lockdowns.
  • Watermarking and fingerprinting — forensic (rather than preventive) tracing of leaked copies.

Criticism and debate

Critics — including the Free Software Foundation, which re-dubs DRM "digital restrictions management," and the Electronic Frontier Foundation — argue that DRM restricts lawful uses such as fair use, backup, accessibility conversion, interoperability and preservation; breaks purchases when services close; and treats customers as adversaries. The 2005 Sony BMG CD copy-protection scandal, in which audio CDs installed a hidden rootkit on computers, became a notorious example. Notable industry dissent came from Steve Jobs's 2007 open letter "Thoughts on Music," and many publishers of games and music have since marketed "DRM-free" distribution (e.g. GOG.com) as a selling point. Defenders contend DRM enables rental, subscription and windowing business models and deters casual copying, though empirical evidence for reducing piracy remains disputed.

Legal framework

Anti-circumvention rules under the DMCA and the EU Copyright Directive make it an offence to bypass DRM or traffic in circumvention tools, subject to exemptions that in the US are renewed every three years by the Librarian of Congress — for purposes such as phone unlocking, accessibility for the print-disabled, security research and game preservation.
