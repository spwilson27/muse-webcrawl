Monte Carlo integration

In mathematics, Monte Carlo integration is a technique for numerical integration using random numbers. It belongs to the family of Monte Carlo methods and is especially valuable for high-dimensional integrals, where deterministic grid-based quadrature becomes impractical.

Overview

To estimate the integral of a function f over a domain, Monte Carlo integration samples N random points uniformly in the domain and averages the function values: the integral is approximated by the volume of the domain multiplied by the mean of f at the sample points. By the law of large numbers, the estimate converges to the true integral as N grows.

Convergence and error

The statistical error of the basic method decreases as 1/√N, independent of the dimension of the integral — the key advantage over product quadrature rules, whose cost grows exponentially with dimension. Variance reduction techniques improve the constant: importance sampling concentrates samples where the integrand is large, stratified sampling partitions the domain, and quasi-Monte Carlo methods replace random points with low-discrepancy sequences for faster convergence.

Applications

Monte Carlo integration underlies path tracing in computer graphics (evaluating the rendering equation), particle transport simulations in physics and engineering, Bayesian inference, and quantitative finance, where it prices options depending on many underlying variables.
