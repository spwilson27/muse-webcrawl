Sophus Lie

Marius Sophus Lie (17 December 1842 – 18 February 1899) was a Norwegian mathematician. He largely created the theory of continuous symmetry and applied it to the study of geometry and differential equations. Objects and concepts named after him include Lie groups, Lie algebras, the Lie bracket, and Lie theory.

Lie's key insight was that continuous (now called Lie) groups could be understood by studying their infinitesimal generators — the elements of the corresponding Lie algebra — and linearizing the group action. In collaboration with Felix Klein in Berlin and Paris around 1870, he developed the beginnings of the theory of transformation groups. From 1886 he held a chair at Leipzig, where he worked with Friedrich Engel on the monumental three-volume Theorie der Transformationsgruppen (1888–1893).

Life and work

Born in Nordfjordeid, Norway, Lie initially intended to pursue a military career but turned to mathematics after difficulty with his eyesight made that impossible. His early work concerned contact transformations and the geometry of lines. In 1886 he succeeded Klein at Leipzig; health problems and homesickness led him to accept a specially created chair at the University of Christiania (Oslo) in 1898, but he died the following year of pernicious anemia.

Legacy

Lie's theory unified many areas of mathematics and became fundamental to modern physics, where symmetry groups govern conservation laws and particle classifications. The Hermann Weyl and Élie Cartan developments of his theory completed its assimilation into 20th-century mathematics.
