Thanatosis

A Virginia opossum feigning death

Thanatosis, also known as apparent death, tonic immobility, or "playing possum," is a defensive behaviour in which an animal becomes immobile and appears dead, typically in response to a threat from which escape seems impossible.

Mechanism and examples

The state is an involuntary, reflex-like response rather than conscious deception in most species: heart rate and breathing slow, the body goes limp or rigid, and some animals add odours mimicking decay. The Virginia opossum is the most famous exponent—it may remain in a coma-like state for minutes to hours, emitting a corpse-like smell from its anal glands. Other examples include hognose snakes (which roll onto their backs and gape their mouths), many beetles and other insects, chickens (in "hypnosis"), sharks when inverted, and the lemon shark and some bony fish. In some predator–prey contexts (for example, certain cichlid fish and dragonfly nymphs) feigning death is used offensively or to avoid the attention of more dangerous animals.

Function

Many predators are triggered to attack moving prey or avoid carrion that may carry disease or toxins, so the immobile "corpse" may be abandoned, giving the prey a chance to escape. Thanatosis is one of several last-resort defences, alongside thanatosis-like catatonia observed in humans under extreme threat.
