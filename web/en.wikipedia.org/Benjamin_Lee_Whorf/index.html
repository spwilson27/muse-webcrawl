Benjamin Lee Whorf (April 24, 1897 – July 26, 1941) was an American linguist and fire prevention engineer. He is best known as an advocate of the idea that differences in the structure of human languages shape the way their speakers perceive and think about the world — the principle of linguistic relativity, popularly known as the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis after Whorf and his mentor Edward Sapir.

Life and career

Whorf trained as a chemical engineer at MIT and from 1919 worked for the Hartford Fire Insurance Company, eventually as assistant secretary, investigating fire causes and preventive measures. His linguistic work was pursued outside his profession: from the late 1920s he studied Nahuatl (classical Aztec), Maya hieroglyphs, and other Mesoamerican languages, coming to the attention of Edward Sapir. In 1931, on a grant, he conducted fieldwork in Mexico; when Sapir moved to Yale, Whorf enrolled in graduate study there without ever completing a doctorate, yet lectured on Amerindian linguistics and published influential papers. He died of cancer in 1941, aged 44.

Linguistic relativity

Whorf's famous examples drew on Hopi, which he argued lacked tenses and grammaticalised time as European languages do, organising events instead by validity and aspect; and on his fire-insurance experience, as with workers who treated "empty" gasoline drums as harmless because the word suggested absence, though they held explosive vapour. His key essays — including "Science and Linguistics" (1940) — were collected posthumously in Language, Thought, and Reality (1956), edited by John B. Carroll.

The "strong" version of Whorfianism — that language determines thought — fell from favour after the cognitive revolution and critiques such as Steven Pinker's The Language Instinct, and later fieldwork disputed some of Whorf's claims about Hopi. Since the 1990s, experimental work in cognitive science has revived interest in weaker, testable versions of linguistic relativity: language-specific effects on colour perception, spatial reasoning, number, and time metaphors.

Other work

Whorf also made early attempts to decipher Maya hieroglyphic writing (arguing for a phonetic component, ahead of scholarly consensus), described the grammar of Hopi and Shawnee, and developed interests in theosophy that shaped his view of language and mind.
