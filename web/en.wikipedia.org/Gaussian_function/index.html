Gaussian function

In mathematics, a Gaussian function, often simply called a Gaussian, is a function of the form

f(x) = a · exp( −(x − b)² / (2c²) )

for real constants a, b, and nonzero c. Named after Carl Friedrich Gauss, these functions produce the familiar symmetric "bell curve" graph.

Properties

The parameter a is the height of the peak, b the position of the centre, and c (related to the standard deviation σ) controls the width. The total area under the curve equals a·c·√(2π), and the specific integral of the Gaussian is one of the most famous in mathematics. A Gaussian normalised to unit area is the probability density function of the normal distribution.

Applications

Gaussian functions arise in probability and statistics (normal distribution, diffusion processes), physics (the ground state of the harmonic oscillator, beam profiles), signal and image processing (Gaussian blur and smoothing filters), and neuroscience. They are eigenfunctions of the Fourier transform and saturating cases for the uncertainty principle. In image processing, a two-dimensional Gaussian is applied via the Gaussian blur operation to reduce noise and detail.

Integrals

The Gaussian integral ∫−∞∞ e−x² dx = √π cannot be found by elementary antiderivatives; it is evaluated by squaring the integral and converting to polar coordinates, a trick attributed to Poisson. The related error function erf(x) gives probabilities for normally distributed variables.
