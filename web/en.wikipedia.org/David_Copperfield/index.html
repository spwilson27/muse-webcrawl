David Copperfield is a novel by English author Charles Dickens, narrated by its protagonist, David Copperfield, detailing his adventures and the numerous friends and enemies he meets along his path. Serialised in monthly parts in 1849–1850, it is widely considered Dickens's most autobiographical novel and was his own favourite among his works — "the favourite child", as he wrote in the preface.

Plot summary

David is born in Blunderstone, Suffolk, after his father's death. His gentle mother remarries the cruel Mr Murdstone, whose harsh discipline and the tyranny of his sister drive David to bite his stepfather and be sent to Salem House boarding school under the sadistic Mr Creakle. After his mother's death, David is sent to work in a London bottle factory — drawn from Dickens's own childhood experience — before running away to his great-aunt Betsey Trotwood in Dover, who adopts him.

Schooled at Canterbury, David lodges with the lawyer Mr Wickfield and his daughter Agnes, and encounters the scheming clerk Uriah Heep. He becomes a proctor, falls in love with and marries the childlike Dora Spenlow, and pursues a career as a parliamentary reporter and novelist. The Micawber family — eternally indebted, forever expecting "something to turn up" — drifts through the story, until Mr Micawber exposes Heep's fraud. After Dora's death, David eventually recognises his love for Agnes and marries her, achieving happiness and literary success.

Themes and reception

The novel is a classic bildungsroman charting memory, identity, and moral growth, contrasting disciplined hearts with undisciplined ones. Its characters — Micawber, Heep, Betsey Trotwood, Peggotty — are among Dickens's most enduring creations, and the factory chapters remain a landmark protest against child labour.
