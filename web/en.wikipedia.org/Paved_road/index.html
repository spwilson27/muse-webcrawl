Paved road


An asphalt-paved road

A paved road is a road whose surface has been covered with a durable, hard material — typically asphalt concrete, Portland cement concrete, cobblestones, setts, or paving bricks — as opposed to an unpaved (gravel, dirt, or grass) road. Paving provides a smooth, all-weather running surface that supports higher speeds and heavier loads, reduces dust and mud, and lowers vehicle operating costs.

History

Paved streets existed in ancient cities such as Ur by about 4000 BC, and the Roman Empire built an extensive network of stone-paved roads, including the Appian Way begun in 312 BC. After a long decline, road building revived in the 18th and 19th centuries with the methods of engineers such as Pierre-Marie-Jérôme Trésaguet, Thomas Telford, and John Loudon McAdam, whose "macadam" — compacted layers of graded crushed stone — became the standard of the industrial age. Tar and asphalt surfaces followed in the later 19th century, and concrete pavements in the 20th.

Structure

A modern pavement consists of a surface course (asphalt or concrete), a base course of crushed aggregate, and a sub-base resting on the prepared subgrade. The structure spreads wheel loads to the soil below. Asphalt pavements are flexible and easily repaired, while concrete pavements are more rigid and durable but costlier to construct. Surface markings, drainage, and periodic resurfacing are required to maintain paved roads over their service life.
