Prescribed burn

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Also known asControlled burn, hazard reduction burn
PurposeFuel reduction, ecosystem restoration
Used byLand managers, fire services, Indigenous practitioners

A prescribed burn, also called a controlled burn, is the deliberate application of fire to a predetermined area under specified weather and fuel conditions in order to meet land-management objectives. It is a standard tool of wildfire management and ecological restoration.

Objectives

The main objectives are reducing accumulated fuels (leaf litter, underbrush and dead wood) so that subsequent wildfires burn less intensely; restoring fire-dependent ecosystems whose plants and animals evolved with periodic fire; improving habitat and forage; controlling invasive species and disease; and preparing sites for planting.

Planning and execution

Burns are carried out under a written prescription specifying acceptable wind, humidity, temperature and fuel moisture, with firebreaks, crews and ignition patterns (such as drip torches) used to control spread. Smoke management is a major consideration near populated areas.

Risks

Escaped prescribed burns are uncommon but can be destructive; the 2000 Cerro Grande Fire in New Mexico, which began as a prescribed burn and destroyed hundreds of homes in Los Alamos, led to a national review of US federal burn policy. Air-quality impacts and liability are also persistent concerns.

Cultural burning

Indigenous peoples in North America, Australia and elsewhere practised cultural burning for millennia, and these traditions increasingly inform modern programmes, including "cool burning" mosaics used in northern Australia.
