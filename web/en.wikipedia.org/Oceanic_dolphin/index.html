Oceanic dolphins or Delphinidae are a widely distributed family of dolphins that live in the sea. Close to forty species are extant, including the common bottlenose dolphin, common dolphins, spinner and striped dolphins, pilot whales, and the largest member of the family, the orca (killer whale). Oceanic dolphins are, together with the river dolphins and porpoises, the groups informally called dolphins; the family ranges in size from the small Maui's dolphin to the orca, which can reach about 8 metres.

Characteristics

Delphinids have conical teeth (unlike the spade-shaped teeth of porpoises), a pronounced beak in most species, a single blowhole, and a curved or hooked dorsal fin (absent in a few species). They are fast, social predators that feed on fish, squid, and in the orca's case other marine mammals. Like other toothed whales, they use echolocation, generating sounds with nasal structures and receiving echoes through the jaw and the melon.

Behavior and intelligence

Oceanic dolphins live in social groups (pods) that can number from a few individuals to thousands in feeding aggregations. They are noted for sophisticated communication, signature whistles, cooperative herding of prey, play behavior, and cultural transmission of techniques such as sponge-carrying in Shark Bay bottlenose dolphins.

Conservation

Many species are threatened by bycatch in fishing gear (especially gillnets and tuna purse seines), prey depletion, pollution, and underwater noise. A few, such as the Maui's dolphin, are critically endangered. Dolphin-safe tuna labeling arose from efforts to reduce dolphin mortality in the eastern Pacific tuna fishery.
