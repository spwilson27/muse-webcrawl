The pineal gland (also called the pineal body or epiphysis) is a small endocrine gland in the brain of most vertebrates. Shaped like a tiny pine cone — from which it takes its name — it sits in the epithalamus, near the center of the brain between the two hemispheres. Its principal product is melatonin, a hormone that modulates sleep–wake patterns and circadian rhythms.

Structure

In humans the gland is roughly the size of a grain of rice (about 5–8 mm) and is reddish-grey in color. It consists mainly of pinealocytes, the cells that synthesize melatonin, supported by glial cells. With age the gland often accumulates calcium deposits, sometimes called "brain sand" or corpora arenacea, which make it a useful landmark on skull X-rays.

Function

The pineal gland receives information about light and darkness from the retina via the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus. In darkness it converts the neurotransmitter serotonin into melatonin, which is released into the bloodstream and promotes sleepiness. Light suppresses production, making melatonin secretion a signal of night length that helps entrain seasonal and daily rhythms.

History and philosophy

The gland has attracted outsized philosophical attention. René Descartes famously proposed it as the principal seat of the soul and the point where mind and body interact, partly because he believed it was the brain's only unpaired structure. Modern neuroscience regards it as one component of the circadian system rather than a seat of consciousness.
