The Nyingma school (Tibetan: རྙིང་མ་, "the ancient ones") is the oldest of the four major schools of Tibetan Buddhism. It traces its origins to the first dissemination of Buddhism in Tibet in the 8th century, when the Indian masters Padmasambhava and Śāntarakṣita, invited by King Trisong Detsen, founded Samye, Tibet's first monastery, and oversaw the translation of the scriptures into Tibetan.

Teachings and literature

The Nyingma canon includes the Nyingma Gyubum collection of tantras and relies both on transmitted texts (kama) and on revealed "treasure" texts (terma) said to have been concealed by Padmasambhava and rediscovered by tertöns. Its classification of teachings into nine yānas culminates in Dzogchen (Atiyoga), the "Great Perfection", systematised by Longchenpa (1308–1364) and revived by Jigme Lingpa, whose Longchen Nyingthig cycle is the school's most widely practised Dzogchen lineage. Unlike the later schools, Nyingma historically had no centralised monastic hierarchy; its communities of both monks and lay tantric practitioners (ngakpas) are organised around six "mother" monasteries and their branches.

Modern history

After 1959 many Nyingma masters established monasteries in exile in India and Nepal — notably Mindrolling and Namdroling in India — and the school spread worldwide. Dudjom Rinpoche served as its first formally appointed head in exile; the position has since been held or declined by several senior lamas, and the school continues without a single universally recognised head.
