Polymer chemistry

Polymer chemistry is a sub-discipline of chemistry that focuses on the structures of chemicals, chemical synthesis, and chemical and physical properties of polymers and macromolecules. It combines organic, physical and analytical chemistry and underlies the science of plastics, rubbers, fibres, adhesives and many biological materials.

History

The field emerged in the early twentieth century. Hermann Staudinger proposed in 1920 that materials such as rubber and cellulose consist of very long covalently bonded molecules—macromolecules—rather than aggregates of small molecules, a view that was controversial at first but earned him the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1953. Wallace Carothers at DuPont systematised polymerisation reactions and developed nylon and neoprene in the 1930s; Karl Ziegler and Giulio Natta later introduced catalysts for stereoregular polyolefins (Nobel Prize 1963), and Paul Flory provided the theoretical foundations of polymer physics and statistics (Nobel Prize 1974).

Classification and synthesis

Polymers are classified by origin (natural, such as proteins, DNA and cellulose; synthetic, such as polyethylene and PVC), by structure (linear, branched, cross-linked), and by polymerisation mechanism: chain-growth (addition) polymerisation, including free-radical, ionic and coordination routes, and step-growth (condensation) polymerisation. Controlled techniques such as living polymerisation and ATRP allow precise control of molecular weight and architecture. Important characterisation methods include gel permeation (size-exclusion) chromatography, viscometry, spectroscopy and thermal analysis.

Properties and applications

Polymer properties depend on chain length, branching, crystallinity and intermolecular forces; key concepts include the glass transition temperature and viscoelasticity. Polymers are ubiquitous in packaging, construction, transport, electronics, medicine (drug delivery, implants, hydrogels) and energy technologies, while concerns over plastic waste and microplastics drive research into recycling and biodegradable polymers.
