Silicone

Silicones, also known as polysiloxanes, are polymers composed of repeating siloxane units (−O−SiR2−), where R is an organic group such as methyl. They are typically colourless, heat-resistant, water-repellent and chemically inert materials that occur as oils, greases, rubbers (elastomers), resins and sealants. Not to be confused with the chemical element silicon or with silica and silicates.

History

The chemist Frederick Kipping coined the word "silicone" around 1901 while studying organosilicon compounds; he wrongly supposed they were analogues of ketones. Industrial production began in the 1940s, notably with Eugene Rochow's direct process for methylchlorosilanes at General Electric and the founding of Dow Corning in 1943.

Properties

The silicon–oxygen backbone gives silicones exceptional thermal stability (useful roughly from −100 °C to +250 °C), low surface tension, electrical insulation, gas permeability and resistance to oxidation and weathering. By varying chain length and cross-linking, properties range from flowing fluids to rigid resins.

Uses

  • Sealants, adhesives and caulks (e.g. bathroom sealant)
  • Bakeware, kitchen utensils and oven mitts
  • Lubricants, hydraulic fluids and dielectric greases
  • Medical devices, tubing, catheters and (controversially) breast implants
  • Cosmetics and personal care products (as dimethicone)
  • Waterproofing, mould release agents and foam control

Safety and environment

Silicones are generally considered low in toxicity, though cyclic siloxanes such as D4 and D5 have attracted regulatory scrutiny for persistence in the environment. The safety of silicone-gel breast implants was extensively studied after a 1990s controversy; major reviews found no convincing link to systemic disease, while recognising local complications.
