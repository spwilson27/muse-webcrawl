Obligatory Contour Principle

The Obligatory Contour Principle (OCP) is a hypothesis in autosegmental phonology that states that certain consecutive identical features are prohibited in underlying representations. Originally formulated in the context of tone languages, the principle holds that a sequence of two identical tones (such as two consecutive high tones) on the tonal tier is ill-formed, so that adjacent identical tones merge or one of them is deleted or dissimilated.

History

The principle was first proposed by Mark Leben in his 1973 doctoral dissertation on the phonology of Mende, where he observed that words could not contain two adjacent identical melodies. John Goldsmith incorporated the OCP into autosegmental phonology in 1976, and John McCarthy extended it from tones to other autosegmental tiers, including consonantal and vocalic feature tiers, giving the principle a central role in non-linear phonology.

Effects

Common processes attributed to the OCP include total assimilation blocked by identity, dissimilation of adjacent identical segments, deletion of one of two identical adjacent tones, and fusion of identical tones into a single multiply-linked tone. In many Bantu languages, for example, the OCP explains why a high tone fails to spread onto a syllable already bearing an identical high tone.

Debate

The status of the OCP remains contested. Some researchers treat it as a universal grammatical constraint, for instance within Optimality Theory, while others argue that its effects emerge from more general markedness or processing factors. Apparent counterexamples, in which underlying identical elements surface faithfully, are often analysed as cases of adjacency on separate tiers or of feature underspecification.
