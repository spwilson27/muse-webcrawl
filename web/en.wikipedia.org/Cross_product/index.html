Cross product

In mathematics, the cross product or vector product (occasionally directed area product, to distinguish it from the scalar product) is a binary operation on two vectors in a three-dimensional oriented Euclidean vector space, and is denoted by the symbol ×. Given two linearly independent vectors a and b, the cross product a × b is a vector that is perpendicular to both a and b, and thus "normal" to the plane containing them.

Definition

The cross product of a and b is defined as a vector c that is perpendicular to both a and b, with a direction given by the right-hand rule and a magnitude equal to the area of the parallelogram that the vectors span. Formally, |a × b| = |a| |b| sin θ, where θ is the angle between the vectors. In coordinates, if a = (a₁, a₂, a₃) and b = (b₁, b₂, b₃), then a × b = (a₂b₃ − a₃b₂, a₃b₁ − a₁b₃, a₁b₂ − a₂b₁), conveniently computed as a formal determinant.

Properties

The cross product is anticommutative (a × b = −b × a), distributive over addition, and compatible with scalar multiplication, but it is not associative; instead it satisfies the Jacobi identity. Two non-zero vectors have a zero cross product if and only if they are parallel. The triple product a · (b × c) gives the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the three vectors.

Applications

The cross product is fundamental in physics and engineering: torque is the cross product of lever arm and force, angular momentum is the cross product of position and momentum, and the Lorentz force on a charged particle involves the cross product of velocity and magnetic field. It also underlies computations of surface normals in computer graphics.

History

The modern concept emerged in the 19th century from the work of Hermann Grassmann and William Rowan Hamilton (quaternions), and was separated into dot and cross products by Josiah Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside in the 1880s.
