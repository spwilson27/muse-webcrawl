The Pacific decadal oscillation (PDO) is a robust, recurring pattern of ocean-atmosphere climate variability in the Pacific Ocean poleward of 20°N. It is detected as the leading principal component of monthly sea surface temperature anomalies in the North Pacific and alternates between warm (positive) and cool (negative) phases that typically persist for two to three decades.

Phases and effects

During a warm phase, the western Pacific cools and part of the eastern ocean warms; during a cool phase the reverse pattern occurs. PDO phases modulate the weather of North America: warm phases are associated with warmer, drier conditions in the Pacific Northwest and wetter conditions in the American Southwest, while cool phases tend toward the opposite. The twentieth century saw regime shifts around 1925, 1947, and 1977.

Ecology

The pattern was named by fisheries scientist Steven R. Hare in the 1990s while studying connections between Alaska salmon production and Pacific climate. Salmon catches in Alaska and along the U.S. West Coast vary inversely with PDO phase, and similar effects are seen in other marine ecosystems, zooplankton, and sea ice.

Mechanisms

The PDO is not a single physical mode but reflects the integration of several processes, including El Niño–Southern Oscillation forcing, the Aleutian Low, North Pacific gyre circulation, and oceanic "reddening" of white-noise atmospheric variability, which gives the index its multi-decadal character.
