Ephesus

Ephesus (Greek: Ἔφεσος, Éphesos; Turkish: Efes) was a city in ancient Greece on the coast of Ionia, in present-day İzmir Province, Turkey. It was built in the 10th century BC by Attic and Ionian Greek colonists and became one of the largest and most important cities of the Roman world, famous for the Temple of Artemis — one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World.

History

Ephesus flourished under Roman rule as the capital of the province of Asia, with a population estimated at more than 200,000 in the 1st and 2nd centuries AD. Its great monuments include the Library of Celsus, the enormous theatre that could hold some 25,000 spectators, the Temple of Hadrian, and the terraced houses of its elite. The philosopher Heraclitus was a native of the city.

Christian history

Ephesus figures prominently in early Christianity: the Apostle Paul lived and preached there, and his Epistle to the Ephesians is traditionally addressed to its church; the city is one of the seven churches of Revelation. Tradition holds that the Virgin Mary spent her last years nearby. The Council of Ephesus of AD 431, the third ecumenical council, condemned Nestorianism and proclaimed Mary Theotokos.

Decline and archaeology

The city's harbour gradually silted up from the Cayster River, and Ephesus declined through late antiquity and was largely abandoned by the 15th century. Excavations, conducted mainly by Austrian archaeologists since the 19th century, have made it one of the most complete classical cities in the Mediterranean; it was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2015.
