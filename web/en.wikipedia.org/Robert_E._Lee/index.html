Robert Edward Lee (1807–1870) was a Confederate general during the American Civil War, commanding the Army of Northern Virginia from 1862 until its surrender in 1865. Regarded as a brilliant battlefield tactician, he became the central figure of the Confederacy and, after the war, a contested symbol of the "Lost Cause".

Early life and U.S. Army career

Lee was born at Stratford Hall, Virginia, son of Revolutionary War officer Henry "Light-Horse Harry" Lee. He graduated second in his class at West Point in 1829 without a single demerit, served as an engineer, and distinguished himself on Winfield Scott's staff in the Mexican–American War. He was superintendent of West Point (1852–55) and in 1859 commanded the Marines who captured John Brown at Harpers Ferry.

Civil War

Although he considered secession a betrayal of the Union and was offered command of the Union armies, Lee resigned his commission in April 1861 rather than fight against his home state of Virginia. After early setbacks in western Virginia, he took command of the Army of Northern Virginia in June 1862 and won a string of victories against larger forces — the Seven Days, Second Bull Run, Fredericksburg and Chancellorsville. His two invasions of the North ended at Antietam (1862) and Gettysburg (1863). Ground down by Ulysses S. Grant's Overland Campaign and the siege of Petersburg, he surrendered at Appomattox Court House on 9 April 1865.

Post-war life and legacy

Lee became president of Washington College in Lexington, Virginia, urging sectional reconciliation; the school is now Washington and Lee University. He died in 1870. Statues of Lee erected in the Jim Crow era became focal points of controversy; the massive equestrian statue in Richmond, Virginia, was removed in 2021 following the 2020 protests, and Charlottesville's Lee statue — whose planned removal sparked the 2017 Unite the Right rally — was removed the same year.
