Dionysus is the god of the grape-harvest, winemaking, orchards and fruit, vegetation, fertility, festivity, insanity, ritual madness, religious ecstasy, and theatre in ancient Greek religion and myth. He is also known as Bacchus by the Romans.

His origins are uncertain, and his cults took many forms; some are described by ancient sources as Thracian, others as Greek. He is typically depicted as either an older bearded god or an effeminate, long-haired youth. His thyrsus, a fennel stalk topped with a pine cone and twined with ivy, is a symbol of prosperity, fertility and hedonism.

Mythology

Dionysus was the son of Zeus and the mortal Semele. When Semele, tricked by Hera, asked Zeus to reveal himself in his full glory, she was consumed by his lightning; Zeus sewed the unborn child into his thigh until his birth — hence Dionysus is called "twice-born". As an adult he wandered the earth spreading viticulture, accompanied by satyrs, maenads and Silenus. Euripides' tragedy The Bacchae dramatizes his revenge on Pentheus of Thebes, who denied his divinity.

Cult and festivals

Dionysus was worshipped in mystery rites promising initiates a blessed afterlife. Major Athenian festivals in his honour included the City Dionysia, the Lenaea and the Anthesteria. His female followers, the maenads, were said to achieve ecstatic states through dance, music and wine.

Theatre

Greek tragedy and comedy developed from choral performances at Dionysian festivals; the great Theatre of Dionysus on the south slope of the Acropolis hosted the premieres of Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides. Dionysus is thus regarded as the patron god of the theatre.

Iconography

Common attributes include the drinking cup (kantharos), the thyrsus, ivy wreaths, and big cats such as panthers and leopards. In the Hellenistic period he was often shown as a beautiful youth; Roman art continued both the youthful and bearded types.
