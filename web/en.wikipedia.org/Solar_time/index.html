Solar time

Solar time is a calculation of the passage of time based on the position of the Sun in the sky. The fundamental unit of solar time is the day, based on the synodic rotation period. Two types of solar time are apparent solar time (sundial time) and mean solar time (clock time).

Apparent solar time

Apparent solar time is measured by the actual daily motion of the Sun, as shown by a sundial. Local solar noon occurs when the Sun crosses the meridian and reaches its highest point in the sky. Because Earth's orbit is elliptical and its axis is tilted relative to the orbit, apparent solar time does not advance uniformly through the year.

Mean solar time

Mean solar time smooths out these variations by imagining a fictitious "mean Sun" moving uniformly along the celestial equator. The difference between apparent and mean solar time on a given date is called the equation of time; it ranges from about 16 minutes slow in early November to about 14 minutes fast in February. Before the introduction of time zones, towns kept their own local mean time; standard time zones later defined clock time for wide regions relative to a standard meridian.

Relation to sidereal time

A solar day is about four minutes longer than a sidereal day (Earth's rotation period relative to the distant stars), because Earth must rotate slightly more than 360° to bring the Sun back to the same position as it advances along its orbit. A mean solar day is defined as 24 hours, or 86,400 seconds, and is the basis of civil timekeeping (with leap seconds added to UTC to account for irregularities in Earth's rotation).

History

Sundials, water clocks, and noon marks regulated daily life for millennia. The spread of mechanical clocks and railways in the 19th century drove the adoption of mean time and then standardized time zones. Greenwich Mean Time, based on the mean Sun at Greenwich, became the world standard before being superseded by Coordinated Universal Time.

Solar time on other planets

A solar day on another planet is called a "sol," notably for Mars, whose sol lasts about 24 hours 39 minutes and is used to schedule rover operations.
