The Battle of Naseby took place on 14 June 1645 during the English Civil War, near the village of Naseby in Northamptonshire. The Parliamentarian New Model Army, commanded by Sir Thomas Fairfax with Oliver Cromwell leading the horse, destroyed the main Royalist army of King Charles I. Naseby was the decisive battle of the war: the King never again fielded an army of comparable quality.

Background

In the spring of 1645 Charles's army stormed Leicester while Fairfax besieged Oxford. The King turned south to give battle with inferior numbers, hoping to defeat the newly formed New Model Army before it gained experience. The armies deployed on parallel ridges north of Naseby on the morning of 14 June.

The battle

Prince Rupert's cavalry on the Royalist right charged and broke Henry Ireton's wing, but as so often before, Rupert's troopers galloped on to plunder the Parliamentarian baggage train. On the opposite flank Cromwell defeated the Royalist northern cavalry and—crucially—kept his regiments in hand, wheeling them against the Royalist infantry centre. Fairfax's foot, hard pressed and even driven back at first, rallied; with Cromwell's horse falling on their flank and rear, the King's infantry broke. Some 4,500 Royalists surrendered, and the King's artillery, baggage and his private correspondence were captured.

Aftermath

The captured letters, published by Parliament as The King's Cabinet Opened, exposed Charles's negotiations for Irish and foreign Catholic troops and badly damaged his cause. Remaining Royalist field forces were mopped up at Langport and elsewhere; Oxford fell in 1646, ending the First English Civil War.
