Infrared homing

Infrared homing is a passive weapon guidance system which uses the infrared (IR) light emitted by a target — typically the heat of a jet engine, vehicle exhaust, or other hot surface — to track and follow it. Missiles using infrared homing are often called "heat-seekers". Because they emit no signals of their own, infrared-guided missiles are "fire-and-forget" weapons that are difficult for a target to detect before impact.

History

Early experiments with heat-seeking weapons were conducted during and after the Second World War. The first widely deployed infrared-guided missile was the American AIM-9 Sidewinder, introduced in 1956, whose early versions could only engage targets from behind, where the engine exhaust was visible. Successive generations improved sensitivity and field of view, culminating in modern "all-aspect" seekers able to engage from any direction and imaging infrared seekers that form a picture of the target.

Seeker technology

An infrared seeker contains a detector, usually made of materials such as lead sulfide (early systems) or indium antimonide, often cryogenically cooled to increase sensitivity. Early seekers used rotating reticles to modulate the incoming radiation and derive steering signals; modern imaging seekers use focal-plane arrays. Seekers work mainly in the mid-infrared bands (around 3–5 μm and 8–12 μm atmospheric windows).

Countermeasures

Aircraft defend against infrared missiles with flares — hot decoys ejected to seduce the seeker away — and with directional infrared countermeasures (DIRCM), which dazzle or confuse the seeker with modulated infrared energy. Reducing an aircraft's infrared signature, for example by mixing cool air into the exhaust, also helps.
