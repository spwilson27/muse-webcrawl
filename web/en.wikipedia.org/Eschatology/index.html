Eschatology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Last Judgment is a central image of Christian eschatology.
Eschatology
EtymologyGreek eskhatos, "last", + -logia, "study"
ConcernsDeath, judgment, afterlife, end of the world
Found inMost world religions and mythologies
RelatedApocalypticism, millennialism

Eschatology concerns expectations of the end of the present age, human history, or the world itself. The "end of the world" or "end times" is predicted by several world religions (both Abrahamic and non-Abrahamic), which teach that negative world events will reach a climax.

In many religions, eschatology deals with the destiny of individual souls — death, judgment and the afterlife — as well as the collective fate of humanity and the cosmos. The term comes from the Greek ἔσχατος (eskhatos), meaning "last".

Abrahamic religions

Christian eschatology centres on the Second Coming of Christ, the resurrection of the dead, the Last Judgment, and the renewal of creation; views differ on the millennium and the rapture. Islamic eschatology teaches the Day of Judgment (Yawm al-Qiyāmah), preceded by signs including the coming of the Mahdi and the return of Isa (Jesus), followed by resurrection and judgment. Jewish eschatology anticipates the Messiah, the ingathering of exiles, resurrection and the world to come (Olam Ha-Ba). Zoroastrianism, an early source of many such motifs, teaches the final renovation of the world (Frashokereti).

Dharmic religions

Hindu cosmology frames time as cyclical: the present Kali Yuga will end with the avatar Kalki, before a new cycle begins. Buddhism likewise describes cycles of decline and renewal, with the future Buddha Maitreya, while Jain cosmology posits eternal repeating ages without a final end.

Norse and other traditions

Norse mythology foretells Ragnarök — a great battle, the death of major gods including Odin, the submersion of the world in fire and water, and its re-emergence fertile and green. Various indigenous and new religious movements have their own end-time narratives.

Secular eschatologies

Modern secular analogues include nuclear war scenarios, climate-change catastrophism, pandemic risk, and existential risk from artificial intelligence and other technologies, studied in the academic field of existential risk.
