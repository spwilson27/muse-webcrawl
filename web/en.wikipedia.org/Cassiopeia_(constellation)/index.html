Cassiopeia (constellation)

Cassiopeia is a constellation in the northern sky, named after the vain queen Cassiopeia in Greek mythology, who boasted about her unrivalled beauty. It is one of the 48 constellations listed by the 2nd-century astronomer Ptolemy and remains one of the 88 modern constellations. Cassiopeia is easily recognisable due to its distinctive W shape, formed by five bright stars.

Features

The constellation's five main stars — Alpha (Schedar), Beta (Caph), Gamma, Delta (Ruchbah) and Epsilon Cassiopeiae — trace the seated figure of the queen. Cassiopeia lies in a rich part of the Milky Way and contains a number of open clusters, including M52 and M103, as well as the Heart and Soul Nebulae.

Gamma Cassiopeiae is a variable star and the prototype of the Gamma Cassiopeiae variable class. In 1572, Tycho Brahe's supernova appeared in Cassiopeia and was visible in daylight; the remnant of that explosion, and the younger remnant Cassiopeia A, make the constellation notable in radio astronomy: Cassiopeia A is one of the strongest radio sources in the sky.

Mythology

In Greek myth, Cassiopeia was the wife of King Cepheus of Aethiopia and mother of Andromeda. Her boast that she and her daughter were more beautiful than the Nereids provoked Poseidon, who sent the sea monster Cetus to ravage the coast. To appease the monster, Andromeda was chained to a rock as a sacrifice, but was rescued by the hero Perseus. Cassiopeia was placed in the heavens as punishment, seated on her throne near the pole, spending half the year upside down.
