Series (mathematics)

In mathematics, a series is a description of the operation of adding infinitely many quantities, one after the other, to a given starting quantity. The study of series is a major part of calculus and its generalization, mathematical analysis. Series are used in most areas of mathematics, even for studying finite structures through generating functions, and are widely applied in physics, computer science, statistics, and finance.

Definition

Given an infinite sequence of numbers (a1, a2, a3, …), the series is the sum a1 + a2 + a3 + …, often written with sigma notation as Σan. The series is evaluated by considering the sequence of partial sums; if the partial sums converge to a finite limit, the series is said to converge to that sum. If the partial sums do not approach any limit, the series diverges. A series whose terms' absolute values also form a convergent series is called absolutely convergent; one that converges but not absolutely is conditionally convergent.

Types of series

Important classes include arithmetic and geometric series, the harmonic series (which diverges), alternating series, power series (including Taylor and Maclaurin series), Fourier series, and Dirichlet series such as the one defining the Riemann zeta function. Tests for convergence include the comparison, ratio, root, and integral tests, and the alternating series test.

History

Greek mathematicians, wary of actual infinity, handled proto-series results through the method of exhaustion; Zeno's paradoxes already engaged with infinite sums. Indian mathematician Madhava and his school derived series for π and trigonometric functions in the 14th–15th centuries, and in 17th-century Europe series became central to the new calculus of Newton and Leibniz.
