Epiretinal membrane

Epiretinal membrane
Other names: Macular pucker, preretinal membrane
Specialty: Ophthalmology
OCT scan showing an epiretinal membrane wrinkling the surface of the macula

An epiretinal membrane (ERM), also known as a macular pucker, is a thin sheet of fibrous tissue that can develop on the surface of the macular region of the retina. It causes blurred and distorted vision when it contracts and wrinkles the underlying retina. Epiretinal membranes are most commonly associated with ageing and with posterior vitreous detachment, in which the vitreous gel separates from the retina.

Signs and symptoms

Many epiretinal membranes cause no symptoms and are found incidentally. When symptomatic, patients may notice blurred central vision, distortion of straight lines (metamorphopsia), difficulty reading, and sometimes double vision in one eye. Symptoms are usually slowly progressive.

Causes

The cells that form an epiretinal membrane are thought to arise mainly from glial cells and retinal pigment epithelial cells that gain access to the retinal surface through small breaks, or that are left behind on the retinal surface when the vitreous detaches. Most cases are idiopathic, but membranes can also follow retinal detachment or its repair, diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, uveitis, trauma, or intraocular surgery.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is made by dilated retinal examination and confirmed with optical coherence tomography (OCT), which shows the membrane and the thickening and wrinkling of the macula. Visual acuity testing and an Amsler grid can document the degree of distortion.

Treatment

Mild cases are monitored without intervention. When vision is significantly affected, surgery with pars plana vitrectomy and peeling of the membrane (often together with the internal limiting membrane) can improve or stabilise vision, although recovery may take months and distortion may not resolve completely.
