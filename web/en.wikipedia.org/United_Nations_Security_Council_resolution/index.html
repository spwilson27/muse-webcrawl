United Nations Security Council resolution

A United Nations Security Council resolution is a formal text adopted by the UN Security Council, the fifteen-member body charged with primary responsibility for the maintenance of international peace and security. Resolutions are identified by number and year — the Council passed its 2,700th resolution in the 2020s — and are drafted in a standard format with preambular paragraphs (recalling, noting, expressing concern) followed by numbered operative paragraphs (decides, demands, authorises, calls upon).

Adoption and voting

Under Article 27 of the UN Charter, procedural matters need nine affirmative votes, while substantive decisions require nine votes including the concurring votes of the permanent members — China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States — which gives each permanent member a veto. In practice, since 1946 an abstention by a permanent member has not been treated as a veto. Draft resolutions are usually negotiated informally beforehand, often led by a "penholder" state; a draft that is voted down is said to have been vetoed if a permanent member casts the negative vote.

Legal force

Acting under Chapter VI of the Charter (pacific settlement of disputes), the Council recommends measures and its resolutions are generally not binding. Under Chapter VII — after determining the existence of a threat to the peace, breach of the peace, or act of aggression — the Council may impose binding obligations on all member states, including sanctions, arms embargoes and authorisations of military force, as in Resolution 678 (1990) on Iraq's occupation of Kuwait and Resolution 1973 (2011) on Libya. Member states agree under Article 25 to accept and carry out the Council's decisions.

Landmark resolutions

Notable examples include Resolution 242 (1967), the "land for peace" formula after the Six-Day War; Resolution 338 (1973) calling for a Yom Kippur War ceasefire; Resolution 598 (1987) ending the Iran–Iraq War; Resolution 1325 (2000) on women, peace and security; and Resolution 1441 (2002) giving Iraq a "final opportunity" to disarm. Resolutions also create subsidiary bodies, from peacekeeping missions to the international criminal tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda (Resolutions 827 and 955).
