Uniformitarianism

Uniformitarianism, also known as the doctrine of uniformity or the uniformitarian principle, is the assumption that the same natural laws and processes that operate in our present-day scientific observations have always operated in the universe in the past and apply everywhere in the universe. It holds that geological processes occurring today — erosion, sedimentation, volcanism, mountain building — are the same processes that shaped the Earth over its long history, operating at broadly comparable rates.

History

The principle has ancient roots, but its modern form emerged in the late 18th century. The Scottish geologist James Hutton presented his Theory of the Earth in 1785 and published it in 1788, arguing from field evidence — notably the angular unconformity at Siccar Point — that the Earth must be vastly older than the few thousand years then commonly assumed, famously concluding that in the rock record he found "no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end." Hutton's friend John Playfair restated the theory more clearly in his Illustrations of the Huttonian Theory of the Earth (1802).

Charles Lyell developed and popularised the doctrine in his Principles of Geology (1830–1833), subtitled "an attempt to explain the former changes of the Earth's surface, by reference to causes now in operation." Lyell's work profoundly influenced Charles Darwin, who carried the first volume aboard HMS Beagle. The term "uniformitarianism" itself was coined by the philosopher of science William Whewell in an 1832 review of Lyell's second volume, as a contrast to "catastrophism."

Principles

Lyell's uniformitarianism combined several distinct claims: uniformity of law (natural laws are constant in space and time), uniformity of process (past changes should be explained by causes of the same kind as those acting now), uniformity of rate (change is typically slow, steady and gradual), and uniformity of state (the Earth has no overall directional history). Modern geology retains the first two — methodological uniformitarianism — while abandoning strict uniformity of rate and state: rare catastrophic events such as asteroid impacts and megafloods are now accepted as part of Earth's history.

Relation to catastrophism

Uniformitarianism was historically opposed to catastrophism, associated with Georges Cuvier, which held that Earth's features were shaped by sudden, short-lived, violent events, possibly of supernatural origin. The modern synthesis accepts that gradual processes dominate, punctuated occasionally by catastrophes — a view sometimes called actualism or neocatastrophism.
