Fusiform face area

The fusiform face area (FFA), a part of the human visual system, is a specialized region of the brain that activates in response to faces as compared to other objects. It is located in the inferior temporal cortex (IT), in the fusiform gyrus (Brodmann area 37). The FFA was first described by Sergent et al. in 1992, and later named by Kanwisher et al. in 1997.

Function

The human FFA is usually located in the fusiform gyrus, usually in the right hemisphere. It is thought to specialize in facial recognition, and responds more strongly to faces than to other visual categories. Damage to or disruption of the FFA can produce prosopagnosia, the inability to recognize faces, though debate continues over whether the area is exclusively dedicated to faces or participates in more general expertise-based processing, such as expert recognition of cars or birds in experts of those domains.

Research

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies typically locate the FFA by comparing responses to faces with responses to scrambled faces, houses, or hands. The region shows stronger activation for intact faces than for inverted or scrambled faces. Complementary areas include the occipital face area (OFA) and the face-selective region of the superior temporal sulcus.
