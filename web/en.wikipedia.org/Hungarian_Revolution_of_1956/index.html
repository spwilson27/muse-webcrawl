Hungarian Revolution of 1956

The Hungarian Revolution of 1956 (23 October – 10 November 1956) was a spontaneous nationwide revolt against the Hungarian People's Republic and its Soviet-imposed policies, lasting from 23 October until 10 November 1956. Initially anarchic, it was the first major threat to Soviet control since the Red Army drove Nazi Germany from its territory at the end of World War II in Europe.

Outbreak

On 23 October 1956, students in Budapest marched in solidarity with Poland and presented sixteen demands, including free elections and the withdrawal of Soviet troops. State security police fired on crowds outside the Radio Budapest building, and the protest became an armed uprising. The reformist communist Imre Nagy became prime minister, Soviet forces temporarily withdrew from Budapest, political prisoners were freed, and Nagy announced Hungary's neutrality and withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact on 1 November.

Soviet intervention

On 4 November, the Soviet Union launched Operation Whirlwind, a massive invasion with some 31,500 troops and 1,130 tanks. Organised resistance ended by 10 November after heavy fighting, especially in Budapest's industrial districts. Nagy was arrested despite a promise of safe conduct and was executed in 1958. János Kádár headed the new Soviet-backed government; thousands of Hungarians were imprisoned or executed, and about 200,000 fled the country.

Legacy

The revolution exposed the limits of Western intervention during the Cold War and became a lasting symbol of resistance to Soviet domination. In 1989, Nagy was ceremonially reburied, and 23 October is now a national holiday in Hungary.
