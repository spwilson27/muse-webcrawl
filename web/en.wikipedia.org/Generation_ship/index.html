A generation ship, also called an interstellar ark, is a hypothetical type of interstellar starship built to travel at sub-light speed over very long periods. Because the journey to another star system might take centuries or millennia, the original occupants would grow old and die, leaving their descendants to continue and complete the voyage.

Concept

The idea was explored by rocket pioneer Konstantin Tsiolkovsky and by J. D. Bernal in his 1929 work The World, the Flesh and the Devil, and developed by Robert H. Goddard, who had earlier proposed long-duration interstellar "arks". A generation ship must be a largely self-sufficient closed ecosystem, recycling air, water, and food, with an artificial gravity system (often a rotating habitat) and a population large enough to avoid genetic bottlenecks — studies have suggested minimum populations from the hundreds into the tens of thousands.

Challenges

Proposed obstacles include maintaining the ecosystem and the ship's machinery for centuries, preserving knowledge and social cohesion, genetic diversity, and the psychological effects on descendants who would live and die without ever seeing a planet's surface. There is also the "wait paradox": a ship launched later with faster propulsion might overtake an earlier generation ship.

In fiction

Generation ships are a long-standing staple of science fiction. Notable examples include Robert A. Heinlein's "Universe" (1941), in which the crew has forgotten the ship's purpose; Brian Aldiss's Non-Stop (1958); and Harry Harrison's Captive Universe (1969).
