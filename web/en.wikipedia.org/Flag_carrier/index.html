Flag carrier

A flag carrier is a transport company, such as an airline or shipping company, that, being locally registered in a given sovereign state, enjoys preferential rights or privileges accorded by the government for international operations. Historically, the term referred to airlines owned by a government or national company and associated with the national identity of a country, often enjoying monopoly or favoured status on international routes.

Airlines

In the mid-20th century most international airlines were state-owned flag carriers — for example British Airways (United Kingdom), Air France, Lufthansa (Germany), Qantas (Australia), Air India, and Japan Airlines — and bilateral air service agreements between states typically designated each country's flag carrier for reciprocal routes. From the late 1970s onward, deregulation and privatisation in many countries eroded the legal distinctiveness of flag carriers; many were wholly or partly privatised, and some failed or were re-nationalised. Nevertheless, the term persists in common and journalistic use for a country's principal international airline, and state ownership remains common in parts of Asia, the Middle East and Africa.

Shipping

In maritime usage, related concepts include a ship's flag state — the jurisdiction whose laws govern the vessel — and national fleets once dominated by state-supported shipping lines. The rise of open registries ("flags of convenience") sharply reduced the share of world tonnage sailing under traditional national registers, weakening the older flag-carrier model at sea.

Characteristics

Flag carriers have traditionally been instruments of national prestige and connectivity: they maintain routes to the capital, promote tourism and trade, and serve strategic roles in emergencies and evacuation flights. Critics argue that state support can distort competition, while supporters note the public-service and network benefits of a hub carrier based in the home country.
