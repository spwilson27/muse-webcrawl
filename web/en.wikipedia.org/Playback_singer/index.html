A playback singer is a singer whose singing is pre-recorded for use in movies. Playback singers record songs for soundtracks, and actors lip-sync the songs on screen—often, the playback singer receives more public recognition than actors, and is vital in music-dominated industries. The practice is characteristic of South Asian cinema, where the majority of film music is produced by playback singers, but it has been used occasionally in Hollywood and other industries as well.

History

Playback singing in Indian cinema began in 1935 with Raichand Boral's innovations at New Theatres, Calcutta, enabling songs to be recorded separately from filming rather than sung live on set. The technique freed actors from needing singing voices and allowed professional vocalists to dominate film music. During the mid-20th century, a small number of singers—Lata Mangeshkar, Asha Bhosle, Mohammed Rafi, Kishore Kumar, Mukesh, Manna Dey, and in the South, S. P. Balasubrahmanyam and K. S. Chithra—sang for thousands of films and became cultural icons; Mangeshkar and Bhosle each recorded songs in the thousands over careers spanning decades.

Process

The music director composes a song, a lyricist writes the words, and the playback singer records the vocal in a studio, usually to an orchestral track. On set, the recorded song is played back (hence "playback") while the actor mouths the words; choreography and filming are matched to the recording.

Recognition

India's National Film Awards and Filmfare Awards include playback singing categories, and playback singers headline concerts independently of films. In other industries, uncredited ghost singing for actors—such as Marni Nixon's work for Hollywood musicals—is a related but distinct practice.

Elsewhere

Similar conventions exist in Pakistani, Bangladeshi, and Nepali cinema, and historically in Egyptian and Indonesian film music. In most Western cinemas, actors are usually expected to sing their own parts or songs are performed on screen by the recording artists themselves.
