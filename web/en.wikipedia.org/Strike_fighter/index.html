Strike fighter

In current military parlance, a strike fighter is a multirole combat aircraft designed to operate primarily in the air-to-ground strike role while retaining effective air-to-air fighter capability. The term is used largely in the United States, where it has partly superseded "fighter-bomber" and "light attack aircraft"; well-known examples include the F-15E Strike Eagle and the F/A-18 Hornet and Super Hornet.

Characteristics

Strike fighters combine a fighter's speed, agility, and air-to-air weapons with precision-guided munitions, targeting pods, and the sensors needed for day/night attack in all weather. Unlike dedicated attack aircraft, they are fully capable of self-escort and air combat; unlike air-superiority fighters, their design and training give priority to surface attack missions such as interdiction, close air support, and suppression of enemy air defences.

History

The concept descends from Second World War fighter-bombers such as the P-47 Thunderbolt and F4U Corsair. In the jet age, aircraft like the F-105 Thunderchief and F-4 Phantom II blurred the fighter/attack distinction. The deliberate strike fighter emerged in the 1980s: the F-15E, developed from the F-15 Eagle under the Dual Role Fighter programme, added conformal fuel tanks, a second seat, and ground-attack avionics while keeping the Eagle's air combat performance. The F-35 Lightning II continues the concept with stealth and sensor fusion.
