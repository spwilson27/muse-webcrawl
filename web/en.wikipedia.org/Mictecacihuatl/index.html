Mictecacihuatl (Nahuatl: Mictēcacihuātl, "Lady of Mictlan" or "Lady of the Dead") is the Aztec (Mexica) goddess of the underworld, Mictlan, where she rules jointly with her husband Mictlantecuhtli. Her role was to watch over the bones of the dead and to preside over the festivals of the dead, from which the modern Day of the Dead (Día de Muertos) traditions partly derive.

Mythology

In Mexica belief, the dead traveled for four years through the nine levels of Mictlan before reaching its deepest level, Chicunamictlan, where Mictecacihuatl and Mictlantecuhtli reigned. The goddess guarded the bones of previous generations of humanity; in the creation myth, Quetzalcoatl descended to Mictlan to retrieve those bones so that humankind could be reborn — the death gods attempted to trick him into leaving them behind, and the bones that fell and broke explained why humans are of differing sizes. She is depicted in codices with a skeletal face, a defleshed jaw, and a skirt of serpents or stars.

Cult and modern legacy

The Mexica devoted the months of Tlaxochimaco and Xocotlhuetzi to feasts of the dead, overseen by Mictecacihuatl, during which offerings of food, flowers, and incense were made; under colonial rule these merged with All Saints' and All Souls' Days. Popular accounts sometimes identify her with La Calavera Catrina imagery or Santa Muerte, though historians treat these as distinct later developments influenced by European personifications of death.
