Renaissance architecture is the European architecture of the period from the early 15th to the early 17th century. Developed first in Florence, Italy, it consciously revived elements of ancient Roman and Greek architecture — symmetry, proportion, geometry and the regular use of the classical orders — in deliberate contrast to the complexity of the Gothic buildings that preceded it. The style spread across Europe, carried by treatises, travelling architects and the new technology of printing.

Characteristics

Renaissance buildings are characterised by a clear, rational order: façades are symmetrical, columns and pilasters follow the classical orders (Doric, Ionic, Corinthian, Tuscan and Composite), arches are semicircular rather than pointed, and domes replace spires. Plans and elevations were governed by mathematical proportion, often based on a module such as the diameter of a column. Leon Battista Alberti's treatise De re aedificatoria (1452) provided the theoretical foundation, adapting Vitruvius to contemporary practice.

Early Renaissance

Filippo Brunelleschi is regarded as the founding figure. His dome for Florence Cathedral (completed 1436) — the largest masonry dome of its age, built without conventional scaffolding — demonstrated classical engineering revived. His Foundling Hospital and the churches of San Lorenzo and Santo Spirito in Florence established the style's vocabulary of round arches on slender columns and harmonious, modular spaces.

High Renaissance and Mannerism

In Rome, Donato Bramante's Tempietto (1502) and his designs for the new St Peter's Basilica marked the High Renaissance. Michelangelo's Laurentian Library and the dome of St Peter's introduced the tensions of Mannerism, in which classical rules were deliberately stretched. In the Veneto, Andrea Palladio's villas and churches codified a serene classicism that, through his Four Books of Architecture (1570), profoundly influenced later European and American building — the Palladian style.

Spread and legacy

The style reached France (the châteaux of the Loire), Spain, England (Inigo Jones) and northern Europe, each region blending classical forms with local traditions. Renaissance architecture laid the groundwork for Baroque architecture and, through Palladianism and later neoclassicism, remained a reference point for centuries.
