A water supply network or water supply system is a system of engineered hydrologic and hydraulic components that provide water supply. A typical network comprises the water source (rivers, reservoirs, groundwater), raw-water transmission, a water treatment plant, storage in reservoirs and elevated tanks or water towers, pumping stations, and the distribution system of mains, valves, hydrants and service connections that deliver potable water to consumers and provide water for firefighting.

Design

Distribution systems are laid out as branching (tree), gridiron, ring or radial networks; looped layouts are preferred because they maintain supply and pressure when a section is isolated for repair. Pipes are commonly of ductile iron, PVC, HDPE or steel, sized to meet peak-hour demand plus fire flow while maintaining residual pressure, typically between about 30 and 80 psi. Storage balances diurnal demand, provides emergency reserve and stabilises pressure. Disinfectant residual, usually chlorine or chloramine, is maintained throughout the network to control microbial regrowth.

Operation and problems

Utilities monitor pressure, flow and water quality, and manage leakage — a major loss term known as non-revenue water, which exceeds 50% in some systems worldwide. Other concerns include ageing cast-iron mains and main breaks, corrosion and lead service lines (the subject of major replacement programmes, notably after the Flint water crisis), backflow contamination, and water hammer pressure transients. Modern networks increasingly use SCADA telemetry, smart meters and hydraulic models (digital twins) for control and planning.

History

Pressurised urban water networks developed in the 19th century alongside filtration and chlorination, transforming public health in industrial cities. London, Paris and New York (with the Croton Aqueduct, 1842) were early leaders; the association of municipal water systems with dramatic declines in cholera and typhoid made the networked supply a defining technology of the modern city.
