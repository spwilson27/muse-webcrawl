Gau (territory)

Gau (Dutch: gouw; Old High German: gouwe) is a Germanic term for a region within a country, originally denoting a district or county-like subdivision. In the early Middle Ages a Gau was a territorial unit of the Frankish and later German kingdoms, administered by a count (Gaugraf), and many modern German place-names preserve the names of these districts, such as the Rheingau, Breisgau, and Aargau in Switzerland.

Medieval usage

The Gaue were the basic territorial divisions of the Frankish Empire from about the 8th century. Each Gau was centred on a fortified place or royal estate and was subdivided into smaller units called Zenten or Huntaren. Over the course of the Middle Ages the Gau system was gradually superseded by the growing territorial principalities and duchies, though the word survived in regional names and in Swiss cantonal names such as Thurgau and Aargau.

Nazi Germany

The term was revived by the Nazi Party, which divided Germany and annexed territories into party districts called Gaue, each headed by a Gauleiter. From 1934, and especially during the Second World War, these party Gaue increasingly displaced the traditional states (Länder) as the effective units of administration, and Reichsgaue were created in annexed territories such as Austria and the Sudetenland.
