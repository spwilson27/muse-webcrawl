Parity (physics)

In physics, a parity transformation (also called parity inversion) is the flip in the sign of one spatial coordinate. In three dimensions, it is often described as the simultaneous flip of all three spatial coordinates — a point reflection through the origin — which is equivalent to viewing a system in a mirror. A physical system is said to have parity symmetry (P-symmetry) if its behaviour is unchanged under such a transformation.

Quantum mechanically, states can be assigned an intrinsic parity: even (+1) if the wavefunction is unchanged by inversion, or odd (−1) if it changes sign. Parity is conserved by the electromagnetic and strong interactions, so it serves as a useful quantum number in atomic, nuclear, and particle physics.

Parity violation

Until the 1950s it was assumed parity was conserved by all fundamental interactions. In 1956, Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen Ning Yang proposed that the weak interaction might violate parity, and in 1957 Chien-Shiung Wu's experiment on the beta decay of cobalt-60 confirmed that it does — the weak force distinguishes left from right. The discovery overturned a deep assumption of physics and earned Lee and Yang the Nobel Prize. The combined symmetry CP (charge conjugation plus parity) is also violated slightly, as shown in 1964, while the full CPT symmetry is believed to be exact.
