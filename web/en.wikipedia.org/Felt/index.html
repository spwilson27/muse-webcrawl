Felt

Felt is a textile material produced by matting, condensing, and pressing fibers together. Felt can be made of natural fibers such as wool, or synthetic fibers such as acrylic. Unlike woven cloth, felt is a non-woven fabric: its fibers are interlocked through a combination of moisture, heat, agitation, and pressure rather than by weaving or knitting.

Production

Wool fibers are covered in microscopic scales; when agitated in warm, soapy water, the scales lock together irreversibly, causing the mass to shrink and densify — a process called felting or fulling. Industrial felt is produced with needle-punching machines or large rollers, while handmade felt is still produced by artisans using traditional wet-felting techniques.

History

Feltmaking is among the oldest textile technologies, predating weaving. It has been central to the cultures of Central Asia, where felt yurts, rugs, and garments have been made for millennia. Felt hats — including beaver-fur felt — were a major industry in Europe and North America from the 17th to the 19th century; the phrase "mad as a hatter" is associated with mercury poisoning among hat makers who used mercuric nitrate in felt production.

Uses

Felt is used in clothing and hats, musical instruments (piano hammers, drum beaters), polishing and filtration, gaskets and insulation, crafts, and soundproofing. Its properties — resilience, breathability, and resistance to fraying — make cut edges clean without hemming.
