Insomnia

Insomnia, also known as sleeplessness, is a sleep disorder in which people have trouble sleeping. They may have difficulty falling asleep, or staying asleep for as long as desired, despite adequate opportunity for sleep. Insomnia is typically followed by daytime sleepiness, low energy, irritability, and depressed mood, and is associated with impaired attention, learning and memory.

Insomnia is difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, with daytime consequences.

Types and causes

Insomnia may be acute (days to weeks, often triggered by stress, illness, pain or life events) or chronic (at least three nights a week for three months or more). It can be a primary condition or secondary to medical disorders (such as chronic pain, hyperthyroidism or sleep apnoea), psychiatric conditions (depression, anxiety, PTSD), medications, or substances including caffeine and alcohol. Risk factors include female sex, older age, shift work and irregular schedules. About 10% of adults have chronic insomnia, and up to a third report symptoms at any given time.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is based on the clinical history and sleep diaries; polysomnography is used when other sleep disorders are suspected. The first-line treatment for chronic insomnia is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which includes sleep hygiene, stimulus control, sleep restriction and cognitive therapy, and produces durable benefits. Medications — including benzodiazepine receptor agonists, dual orexin receptor antagonists, and low-dose sedating antidepressants — may be used for short-term or selected cases, weighed against dependence and side effects.
