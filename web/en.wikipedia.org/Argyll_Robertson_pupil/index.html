Argyll Robertson pupil

Argyll Robertson pupils (AR pupils) are small, irregular pupils that constrict briskly when focusing on a near object (accommodation) but do not constrict in response to bright light — a phenomenon called light-near dissociation. The sign is classically associated with neurosyphilis, particularly tabes dorsalis and general paresis.

Characteristics

The pupils are typically bilateral, small (miotic), and irregular in outline, with a somewhat atrophic, depigmented iris. Vision is usually preserved, which distinguishes the condition from afferent pupillary defects. The mnemonic "prostitute's pupil" is sometimes used to recall that the pupil "accommodates but does not react."

Mechanism

The lesion is thought to lie in the dorsal midbrain, in the region of the pretectal nuclei, damaging the interneurons connecting to the Edinger–Westphal nuclei that mediate the light reflex, while sparing the pathways from the visual cortex that mediate the near response arriving from a more ventral route. Because the responsible lesion interrupts light-reflex fibers bilaterally, both pupils are usually affected.

History and causes

The sign is named for the Scottish ophthalmologist Douglas Argyll Robertson, who described the phenomenon in 1869 in patients with spinal disease later recognized as tabes dorsalis. Although strongly linked to tertiary syphilis, similar pupils can occur with other dorsal midbrain lesions, diabetic neuropathy, multiple sclerosis, and midbrain tumours. "Pseudo-Argyll Robertson pupils" refers to pupils with light-near dissociation but without the small size and irregularity of the true sign.
