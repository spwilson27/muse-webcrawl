United States federal executive departments

The United States federal executive departments are the principal units of the executive branch of the federal government of the United States. They are analogous to ministries in other national governments. There are currently 15 executive departments; their heads — the secretaries — are appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate and together constitute the Cabinet of the United States, along with other officials the President chooses to include.

History

The First Congress created the Departments of Foreign Affairs (soon renamed State), Treasury, and War in 1789, and the office of Attorney General. New departments followed the expansion of federal responsibilities: the Navy (1798, later folded into Defense), Interior (1849), Agriculture (1862; Cabinet rank 1889), Commerce and Labor (1903, split in 1913), and the post-war additions culminating in Homeland Security after the September 11 attacks. Former departments include the Post Office Department, a Cabinet agency until 1971, and the Department of War, replaced by Defense in 1949.

Order of precedence and succession

Department heads rank in the presidential line of succession in the order in which their departments were created, beginning with the Secretary of State, after the Vice President, Speaker of the House, and President pro tempore of the Senate.
