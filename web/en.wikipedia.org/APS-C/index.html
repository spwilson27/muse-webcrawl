APS-C

Advanced Photo System type-C (APS-C) is an image sensor format approximately equivalent in size to the "classic" (C) format negative of the Advanced Photo System film format of 25.1 × 16.7 mm, with an aspect ratio of 3:2 and a diagonal of about 30 mm. In digital photography the term denotes a class of sensors smaller than a 35 mm "full frame" sensor (36 × 24 mm).

Dimensions and crop factor

Actual APS-C sensor sizes vary by manufacturer. Canon's version measures about 22.3 × 14.9 mm, giving a crop factor of approximately 1.6× relative to full frame; sensors used by Nikon, Sony, Fujifilm, and Pentax measure about 23.6 × 15.7 mm with a crop factor of approximately 1.5×. Because the sensor captures a smaller portion of the image circle, a lens of a given focal length produces a narrower field of view than on full frame, which is often expressed as a "35 mm equivalent focal length".

Use in cameras

APS-C became the dominant sensor size in consumer and prosumer digital single-lens reflex cameras and later in mirrorless interchangeable-lens cameras, balancing image quality, size, and cost. Dedicated lens lines were designed for the format, including Canon EF-S, Nikon DX, Sony E-mount APS-C, and Fujifilm X-mount lenses. Professional APS-C systems such as Fujifilm's X series and Sony's α6000 series are widely used, while the smaller Micro Four Thirds and 1-inch formats sit below APS-C and full frame and medium format sit above it.

Characteristics

Compared with full frame, APS-C cameras and lenses can be made smaller and lighter, and the crop factor effectively extends the reach of telephoto lenses, which is valued in sports and wildlife photography. Disadvantages include reduced low-light performance at the same pixel count, shallower control over depth of field, and typically wider lenses being needed for equivalent fields of view.
