Southern Uplands Fault

The Southern Uplands Fault is a major geological fault in Scotland that runs from the Rhins of Galloway in the south-west to Dunbar on the North Sea coast in the east. It forms the northern boundary of the Southern Uplands terrane and separates the hills of the Southern Uplands from the low ground of the Midland Valley to the north.

Geology

The fault marks the junction of two crustal blocks that were welded together during the Caledonian orogeny, when the Iapetus Ocean closed in the Silurian and Devonian periods. North of the fault lie the younger sedimentary and volcanic rocks of the Midland Valley; south of it, the hard greywackes and shales of the Southern Uplands — an accretionary prism of deep-sea sediments scraped off the floor of the closing ocean and piled up against the Laurentian continent. The fault zone itself is a complex of steep, near-vertical fractures, repeatedly reactivated over hundreds of millions of years.

Landscape

Although the fault passes through some dramatic country — including the border between the Midland Valley lowlands and the upland plateau around Moffat and the Tweed valley — its expression at the surface is often subtle, picked out by valleys, scarps and changes in rock hardness rather than a single visible crack. The line of the fault is followed in places by roads and rivers, including stretches near Sanquhar and along the upper Tweed.

Significance

Together with the Highland Boundary Fault and the Great Glen Fault, the Southern Uplands Fault is one of the three great terrane boundaries that divide Scotland into distinct geological provinces. It influenced the distribution of mineral deposits — including gold at Wanlockhead, Scotland's highest village, near the fault's line — and continues to guide studies of Scotland's deep crustal structure.
