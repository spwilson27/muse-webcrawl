Relationship between religion and science

The relationship between religion and science concerns the interaction of religious and scientific views, institutions, and methods — historically ranging from conflict and competition to dialogue, integration, and mutual influence. Scholars commonly organise accounts of the relationship using typologies such as Ian Barbour's fourfold scheme of conflict, independence, dialogue, and integration.

Historical outline

In antiquity and the Middle Ages, natural inquiry was often pursued within religious institutions: Islamic scholars advanced optics, astronomy, and medicine; Christian monasteries and universities preserved and extended learning. The popular image of a medieval Church uniformly suppressing science is now regarded by historians as largely a nineteenth-century construction. The Galileo affair (1633), in which Galileo Galilei was tried for advocating heliocentrism, remains the most famous episode of apparent church–science conflict, though historians emphasise its political and personal dimensions as well.

Conflict thesis

The "conflict thesis", popularised by John William Draper's History of the Conflict between Religion and Science (1874) and Andrew Dickson White's A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom (1896), holds that religion and science are inherently at war. Most contemporary historians of science reject the thesis as oversimplified, while noting genuine historical flashpoints, notably debates over Darwinian evolution.

Alternative models

The independence model — echoed in Stephen Jay Gould's idea of "non-overlapping magisteria" — treats science and religion as answering different kinds of questions. Dialogue and integration models, associated with theologians and scientist-theologians such as Barbour, John Polkinghorne, and Arthur Peacocke, explore points of contact and consonance. Surveys show that scientists' personal religiosity varies widely by country and field.

Contemporary issues

Modern debates include evolution and creationism, cosmology and fine-tuning, neuroscience and the soul, bioethics, and the science-and-religion implications of artificial intelligence.
