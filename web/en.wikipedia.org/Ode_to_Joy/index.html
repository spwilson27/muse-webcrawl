"Ode to Joy" (German: "An die Freude") is a poem written in the summer of 1785 by German poet, playwright and historian Friedrich Schiller. It is best known for its use by Ludwig van Beethoven in the final movement of his Ninth Symphony, completed in 1824, and celebrates the universal brotherhood of humanity.

The poem

Schiller wrote the ode in 1785 while staying near Dresden and Leipzig, and published it in his journal Thalia in 1786. Its famous opening — "Freude, schöner Götterfunken" ("Joy, beautiful spark of divinity") — hails joy as the force uniting all people. Schiller later revised the text (the 1803 version), reportedly regarding the poem as of mainly biographical significance, but it had already become widely popular as a song.

Beethoven's setting

Beethoven had admired the poem since the 1790s and set selected verses, with some reordering and an added introductory recitative ("O Freunde, nicht diese Töne!"), in the choral finale of his Symphony No. 9 in D minor, Op. 125, premiered in Vienna on 7 May 1824. The setting made the ode a fixture of concert life and a symbol of freedom and fraternity; Leonard Bernstein famously conducted it in Berlin in December 1989, after the fall of the Berlin Wall, substituting "Freiheit" (freedom) for "Freude".

Anthem of Europe

In 1972 the Council of Europe adopted Beethoven's melody as its anthem, in an arrangement by Herbert von Karajan, and in 1985 it was adopted by the European Community — now the European Union — as its anthem, performed in an instrumental version without official lyrics, symbolising the values of freedom, peace and solidarity.
