Hollywood Sign

The Hollywood Sign is an American landmark and cultural icon overlooking Hollywood, Los Angeles, California. Originally created in 1923 as an advertisement for a local real estate development, the sign — which reads "HOLLYWOOD" in 45-foot-tall (13.7 m) white capital letters — has left the site and become widely identified with the American film industry. It is situated on Mount Lee in the Santa Monica Mountains, in Griffith Park.

History

The sign was erected in 1923 by the Hollywoodland Real Estate Group to advertise a new housing development in the hills above Hollywood. It originally read "HOLLYWOODLAND", its thirteen letters studded with thousands of light bulbs and maintained by a caretaker who lived in a small cabin behind the first "L". Intended to last only about a year and a half, the sign survived the Great Depression and became an emblem of the booming film industry; the "LAND" suffix was dropped in 1949 when the Hollywood Chamber of Commerce took over its care, on the condition that it promote the district rather than the development.

Alterations and pranks

The sign has frequently been altered by pranksters, usually by covering parts of letters — changing the reading to, among others, "HOLLYWeeD" (in 1976 and again in 2017, following changes in California cannabis law), "HOLYWOOD" (1976, for an Easter service), and "HOLLYBOOB" (2021, briefly). Because of its fame and protected status, such alterations are treated as vandalism, and the sign is guarded by security systems and Los Angeles park rangers.

Protection and restoration

By the 1970s the wooden and sheet-metal sign was badly deteriorated — the first "O" had crumbled and an arsonist had burned part of an "L". In 1978 a campaign raised funds from nine donors (including Alice Cooper, Gene Autry and Hugh Hefner), each sponsoring one letter, and the sign was completely rebuilt in steel on concrete foundations, reopened in time for Hollywood's 75th anniversary. In 2010 the land behind and around the sign was purchased for conservation with the help of a public campaign and a contribution from Hefner, protecting it from development.

In popular culture

The sign appears in countless films, television shows and photographs as an establishing symbol of Hollywood and the entertainment industry. Its cultural weight has also taken darker forms: in 1932 the struggling actress Peg Entwistle died by suicide from the top of the letter "H", an event that remains part of the sign's mythology. Parodies and replicas of the sign — from "HOLLYWOOD" style signs in other cities to altered versions in video games — testify to its status as one of the most recognisable landmarks in the world.
