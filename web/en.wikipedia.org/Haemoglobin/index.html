Haemoglobin

Haemoglobin (American English: hemoglobin) is the iron-containing oxygen-transport protein in red blood cells of almost all vertebrates and some invertebrates. It carries oxygen from the lungs to tissues and returns carbon dioxide toward the lungs.

Haemoglobin

Function: oxygen transport
Subunits: 2 alpha, 2 beta (adult HbA)
Contains: 4 haem groups, 4 Fe atoms

Structure

Adult haemoglobin A is a tetramer of two alpha and two beta globin chains, each holding a haem group with an iron atom that reversibly binds one O2 molecule — four per haemoglobin. Binding is cooperative: uptake of oxygen in the lungs promotes further uptake, and release in tissues promotes further release, producing a sigmoid oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve.

Function and regulation

2,3-Bisphosphoglycerate, pH (the Bohr effect), CO2 and temperature shift the curve. Fetal haemoglobin (HbF, α2γ2) binds oxygen more strongly, aiding transfer across the placenta.

Variants and disease

A single point mutation (glutamate to valine in beta-globin) causes sickle-cell disease. Thalassaemias result from reduced globin synthesis. Other forms include HbA2, methaemoglobin and carboxyhaemoglobin.

History

Haemoglobin was described in the 19th century; its structure was solved by Max Perutz using X-ray crystallography, work recognised alongside John Kendrew.
