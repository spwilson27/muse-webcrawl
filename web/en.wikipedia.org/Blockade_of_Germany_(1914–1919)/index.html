Blockade of Germany (1914–1919)

The Blockade of Germany was a naval blockade conducted by the Allied powers during and after the First World War, from 1914 to 1919. The Royal Navy, exploiting Britain's geographical position, cut Germany off from overseas trade in food, raw materials and fertiliser, contributing greatly to the deterioration of the German civilian economy and, ultimately, to Germany's defeat.

Implementation

At the outbreak of war in August 1914, Britain established distant blockade lines between Scotland and Norway and across the Straits of Dover, enforced by the Northern Patrol of cruisers. The list of contraband was progressively expanded until it covered virtually all goods. Neutral shipping was diverted to British ports for inspection, and rationing agreements were negotiated with neutral countries such as the Netherlands, Denmark and Sweden to limit re-export to Germany.

Effects on Germany

Imports of food and nitrates collapsed. Official German estimates later attributed several hundred thousand excess civilian deaths to the blockade's effects, amplified by the failure of the 1916 potato harvest — the “Turnip Winter” of 1916–17 — and by declining coal and transport capacity. Malnutrition, disease and social unrest spread, weakening morale on the home front.

Continuation after the armistice

The blockade was maintained after the Armistice of 11 November 1918 to pressure Germany into signing the Treaty of Versailles, provoking lasting controversy. Limited food shipments were permitted from March 1919, and the blockade was formally lifted in July 1919 after Germany signed the treaty.
