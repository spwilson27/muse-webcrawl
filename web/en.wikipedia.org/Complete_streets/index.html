Complete streets

ConceptTransportation planning and design approach
Term coined2003 (United States)
Users servedPedestrians, cyclists, transit riders, motorists, people of all ages and abilities
Key advocatesNational Complete Streets Coalition (Smart Growth America)

Complete streets is a transportation policy and design approach that requires streets to be planned, designed, operated, and maintained to enable safe, convenient, and comfortable travel and access for users of all ages and abilities — regardless of their mode of transportation — including pedestrians, bicyclists, transit riders, and motorists, as well as people using wheelchairs and other mobility devices.

A street designed for all users and modes

History

The principle that streets should serve more than cars long predates the term; Oregon enacted a "bike bill" in 1971 requiring accommodation of bicycles and pedestrians. The phrase "complete streets" was coined in 2003 by America Bikes (with David Goldberg credited) as a more appealing replacement for "routine accommodation," and the National Complete Streets Coalition was formed in 2004. Since then, well over a thousand US jurisdictions — cities, counties, states, and tribal governments — have adopted complete streets policies, and the concept influenced the US Department of Transportation's guidance and the 2021 Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, which requires states and metropolitan planning organizations to devote a portion of planning funds to complete streets activities.

Elements

There is no single complete streets design; the features depend on context but may include sidewalks, bicycle lanes or protected cycle tracks, bus lanes and shelters, accessible curb ramps and audible signals, safe crossings with refuge islands, narrower travel lanes, street trees, and green stormwater infrastructure. Policies typically apply to new construction and reconstruction projects, often with exceptions for prohibitions of use, excessive cost, or absence of need.

Outcomes and criticism

Studies associate complete streets projects with improved safety, increased walking and cycling, public-health benefits, and support for local retail. Critics have raised concerns about cost, reduced vehicle capacity and parking, and "check-box" policies that are adopted but weakly implemented; advocates respond that evaluation frameworks — rating the strength of policies and measuring outcomes — help close the implementation gap.
