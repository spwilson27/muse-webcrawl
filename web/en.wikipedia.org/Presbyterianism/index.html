Presbyterianism is a branch of Reformed Christianity within Protestantism, distinguished by its system of church government: congregations are governed by assemblies of elders (Greek: presbyteros) rather than by bishops or by the congregation alone.

History

Presbyterian polity developed from the reforms of John Calvin in Geneva and was carried to Scotland by John Knox, who founded the Church of Scotland in 1560. The Westminster Standards, including the Westminster Confession of Faith (1646), became the classic doctrinal statements of Presbyterian churches. Presbyterianism spread to Ireland, North America, and—through mission work in the 19th and 20th centuries—widely in Korea, Africa and Latin America.

Polity

Governance proceeds through graded courts: the session (elders of a local congregation), the presbytery (a regional body), and synods and a general assembly at wider levels. Both teaching elders (ministers) and ruling elders share in government, reflecting an emphasis on representative, conciliar decision-making.

Beliefs and worship

Presbyterian theology is covenantal and Calvinist, stressing the sovereignty of God, the authority of Scripture, and salvation by grace through faith. Worship traditionally centres on preaching, prayer and psalm-singing, though contemporary practice varies considerably between conservative and mainline denominations.
