Public Broadcasting Act of 1967

The Public Broadcasting Act of 1967 (Public Law 90-129) is an Act of the United States Congress that established the Corporation for Public Broadcasting (CPB), a private non-profit corporation charged with developing non-commercial educational radio and television in the United States. Signed into law by President Lyndon B. Johnson on November 7, 1967, it laid the foundation for the Public Broadcasting Service (PBS, founded 1969) and National Public Radio (NPR, founded 1970).

Background and provisions

The Act grew out of the 1967 report of the Carnegie Commission on Educational Television, Public Television: A Program for Action, which recommended federal support insulated from political pressure. The legislation authorized the CPB to receive federal appropriations and to distribute funds to local stations and program producers, but expressly prohibited the corporation itself from operating a network or owning stations. Johnson, signing the bill, described public broadcasting as a resource that would "make our nation a replica of the old Greek marketplace, where public affairs took place in view of all the citizens."

Later history

Congressional appropriations for the CPB have been a recurring subject of political debate, with periodic proposals to eliminate federal funding. The Act has been amended several times, extending its authorization and adjusting the structure of station grants. Despite recurring controversies, the system it created — locally owned stations supported by a mix of federal, state, viewer, and corporate funding — remains the framework of American public media.
