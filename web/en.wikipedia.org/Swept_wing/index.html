Swept wing

A swept wing is a wing planform in which the wing angles backward (or occasionally forward) from its root rather than extending straight out from the fuselage. Sweep is the defining feature of most high-speed jet aircraft, because it delays the onset of transonic drag rise and shock-wave formation.

Aerodynamics

Only the component of airflow perpendicular to the wing's leading edge governs shock formation. Sweeping the wing reduces that effective component, so the wing "feels" a lower Mach number than the aircraft's true speed, postponing compressibility drag. The trade-offs include spanwise airflow, tip stalling (pitch-up), reduced low-speed lift and a heavier structure.

History

German researchers led by Adolf Busemann proposed sweep in 1935, and it was applied to the Messerschmitt Me 262 and the rocket-powered Me 163 late in the Second World War. Captured data fed directly into the first post-war jets such as the F-86 Sabre and Boeing B-47, and sweep became standard on airliners from the Boeing 707 onward.

Variants

Related planforms include the delta wing, the crescent wing, variable-sweep ("swing") wings as on the F-14 Tomcat and B-1 Lancer, forward-swept wings (X-29), and oblique wings. Designers combine sweep with washout, fences, vortex generators and winglets to manage its low-speed penalties.
