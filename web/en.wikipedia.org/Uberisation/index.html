Uberisation

Uberisation (also spelled uberization) is a neologism describing the transformation of an existing industry or market by a digital platform business model, named after the ride-hailing company Uber. The term refers to using smartphone applications, rating systems and networks of nominally independent service providers to match supply and demand directly, bypassing traditional intermediaries, companies and employment structures.

Uberisation
Origin of termNamed after Uber (founded 2009); widespread from c. 2014
Related conceptsGig economy, platform economy, sharing economy, digital disruption
Sectors affectedTransport, food delivery, logistics, domestic services, healthcare, freelancing

Characteristics

Commentators commonly identify several features of uberised markets: an app-based interface that connects customers and providers in real time; dynamic or "surge" pricing; workers classified as independent contractors who supply their own assets (cars, tools, phones); reputation systems based on customer ratings; and a platform firm that controls prices, standards and access while owning few of the underlying assets itself.

Usage and spread

The term spread from discussion of taxis to sectors including food delivery (Deliveroo, DoorDash), parcel and freight services, cleaning, care work and professional freelancing. Start-up pitches frequently described new companies as "Uber for X", making the word a shorthand for on-demand platform business models generally. In France, where ubérisation entered mainstream political debate, it featured prominently in discussion of labour law reform.

Criticism

Critics argue uberisation transfers risk and costs onto workers, erodes collective bargaining, and creates precarious work without the security of employment — issues litigated in cases such as the UK Supreme Court's 2021 ruling that Uber drivers were "workers" entitled to minimum wage and holiday pay. Supporters counter that platforms lower prices and barriers to entry and offer flexible work. The term is often used pejoratively and is associated with broader debates on the gig economy.
