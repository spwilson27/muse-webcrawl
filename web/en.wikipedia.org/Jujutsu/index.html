OriginJapan (feudal period)
FocusGrappling, joint locks, throws, strikes
Literal meaning"Gentle / yielding art"
DescendantsJudo, aikido, Brazilian jiu-jitsu

Jujutsu (Japanese: 柔術, jūjutsu) is a family of Japanese martial arts developed for close combat, especially for situations in which a warrior was unarmed or lightly armed against an armoured opponent. Its techniques include throws, joint locks, chokes, pins, strikes and the use of some small weapons, organized around the principle of using an opponent's force and balance against them rather than meeting force with force — hence the "gentle" or yielding art.

History

Jujutsu schools (ryū) proliferated during Japan's feudal period, particularly under the Tokugawa shogunate, when samurai and later commoners systematized unarmed combat into hundreds of distinct schools such as Takenouchi-ryū and Tenjin Shin'yō-ryū. With the Meiji Restoration and the decline of the samurai class, many schools faded, but Kanō Jigorō drew on several of them to found judo in 1882, emphasizing safe, sportive practice (randori). Jujutsu also fed into aikido and, through Mitsuyo Maeda's teaching in Brazil, into Brazilian jiu-jitsu and modern grappling and mixed martial arts.

Techniques and training

Traditional schools teach formal patterns (kata) alongside practice of breakfalls (ukemi), striking (atemi), throws (nage-waza) and groundwork (ne-waza). Modern traditional jujutsu (often distinguished as "Japanese jiu-jitsu") continues to be practised worldwide, while sport and self-defence variants emphasize sparring and competition.
