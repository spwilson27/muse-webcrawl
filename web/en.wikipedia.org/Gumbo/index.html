Gumbo

Gumbo (Louisiana French: Gombo) is a stew that is popular in the U.S. state of Louisiana and is the official state cuisine. Gumbo consists primarily of a strongly flavored stock, meat or shellfish (or sometimes both), a thickener, and the Creole "holy trinity" of vegetables — celery, bell peppers, and onions. Gumbo is often categorized by the type of thickener used, whether okra or filé powder (dried and ground sassafras leaves).

The dish combines ingredients and culinary practices of several cultures, including African, American, French, Spanish, and German. Gumbo is usually identified by its dark roux, which is made by cooking flour and fat together until deeply browned.

History

Gumbo originated in Louisiana in the 18th century, drawing on West African okra soups (the word "gumbo" likely derives from a Bantu word for okra, ki ngombo), Choctaw filé traditions, French roux-based cooking, and Spanish and German contributions. Over time, distinct Cajun and Louisiana Creole styles developed: Creole gumbos often include tomatoes, while Cajun gumbos generally do not, and Cajun versions tend to use a darker roux.

Variations

Common varieties include seafood gumbo (with shrimp, crab, and sometimes oysters), chicken and andouille sausage gumbo, and gumbo z'herbes, a meatless version traditionally eaten during Lent. Gumbo is almost always served over hot steamed white rice, and many cooks add filé at the table rather than during cooking, since boiling filé can make the gumbo stringy.

Cultural significance

Gumbo is a centerpiece of Louisiana food culture, central to family gatherings, church suppers, and festivals; the town of Bridge City calls itself the "Gumbo Capital of the World", and several Louisiana communities hold annual gumbo cook-offs and festivals.
