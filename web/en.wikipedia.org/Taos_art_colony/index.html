Taos art colony

The Taos art colony was an art colony centred on Taos, New Mexico, founded in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries by artists drawn to the region's high-desert landscape, dramatic light, adobe architecture, and the cultures of Taos Pueblo and the local Hispano communities.

Origins

In 1898, artists Bert Geer Phillips and Ernest L. Blumenschein, on a sketching trip through the West, stopped in Taos — famously after a broken wagon wheel — and decided to stay. Their paintings of the Southwest, sent east, attracted other artists. In 1915, Phillips and Blumenschein joined Joseph Henry Sharp, Oscar E. Berninghaus, E. Irving Couse, and W. Herbert Dunton to found the Taos Society of Artists, which exhibited members' work on national tours and established Taos imagery — Pueblo life, mountain landscapes, and adobe scenes — in American art.

The Mabel Dodge Luhan circle

From 1917, patron and salon hostess Mabel Dodge Luhan drew a wider circle of modernists and writers to Taos, including D. H. Lawrence, Georgia O'Keeffe, Ansel Adams, and Marsden Hartley. Later arrivals, such as the Taos Moderns of the 1940s and 1950s, brought abstraction to the colony. The art colony helped make Taos a lasting centre for Southwestern art, with museums including the Harwood Museum of Art and the Millicent Rogers Museum.

Critique and legacy

The colony's romantic depictions of Native life have been both celebrated and criticised for idealising and appropriating Pueblo culture; relations between the artists and Taos Pueblo were complex, and the pueblo closed itself to some activities over time. Nonetheless, the colony decisively shaped the visual identity of the American Southwest and the region's art market, which thrives in Taos and Santa Fe to this day.
