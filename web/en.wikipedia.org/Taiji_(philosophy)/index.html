Taiji (philosophy)

Taiji (Chinese: 太極; pinyin: tàijí; lit. 'great pole' or 'supreme ultimate') is a central concept in Chinese philosophy, especially in Taoism and Neo-Confucianism, denoting the ultimate source and unity of the universe from which the complementary principles of yin and yang arise. In Chinese cosmology, taiji emerges from the undifferentiated potentiality of wuji (無極, "without pole" or "limitless") and gives rise to the dynamic interplay of yin and yang, from which the myriad things are generated.

The taijitu, the familiar diagram of taiji showing yin and yang in dynamic balance

The concept

The term appears in the Xici Zhuan (Appended Statements) of the I Ching: "In the system of change there is the Taiji, which generates the Two Modes (yin and yang); the Two Modes generate the Four Images; the Four Images generate the Eight Trigrams." Later thinkers such as the Song-dynasty philosopher Zhou Dunyi, in his Taijitu Shuo (Explanation of the Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate), made taiji the metaphysical foundation of cosmology and ethics: the supreme ultimate, through movement and stillness, produces yang and yin, the five phases, and ultimately all beings, and human sages embody its equilibrium.

Neo-Confucian development

Zhu Xi identified taiji with li (principle), holding that taiji is the sum of the principles of all things and yet is wholly present in each individual thing. This made taiji the cornerstone of Neo-Confucian moral metaphysics, linking cosmic order with human self-cultivation.

In martial arts and culture

The internal martial art tai chi (taijiquan, "supreme ultimate fist") takes its name from the concept, expressing the interplay of yielding and firmness drawn from taiji cosmology. The taijitu symbol—the black-and-white swirling diagram—has become one of the most widely recognized emblems of Chinese philosophy worldwide, and even appears on the flag of South Korea in a red-and-blue form.
