Neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) are aggregates of hyperphosphorylated tau protein that accumulate inside neurons. They are one of the two defining pathological hallmarks of Alzheimer's disease, alongside amyloid plaques. Tangles are composed primarily of paired helical filaments and straight filaments of tau, a protein that normally stabilises microtubules in axons.

Formation

Tau normally binds to and stabilises microtubules, the intracellular tracks along which materials are transported within neurons. In disease, tau becomes abnormally phosphorylated—hyperphosphorylated—which reduces its affinity for microtubules. Detached tau misfolds and aggregates into insoluble filaments that pack into tangles within the cell body. The consequent loss of microtubule stability impairs axonal transport and contributes to neuronal dysfunction and death.

Distribution and staging

In Alzheimer's disease, tangles appear in a stereotyped anatomical sequence that forms the basis of Braak staging. They begin in the transentorhinal and entorhinal cortex, spread to limbic structures such as the hippocampus, and eventually involve widespread areas of the neocortex. Tangle burden correlates more closely with the severity of cognitive impairment than amyloid plaque burden does. Tangles are also found in other disorders collectively called tauopathies, including progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal degeneration, Pick's disease, and chronic traumatic encephalopathy.

History

Neurofibrillary tangles were first described by the German psychiatrist and neuropathologist Alois Alzheimer in 1906, in his report of the patient Auguste Deter. Using the Bielschowsky silver stain, Alzheimer observed dense bundles of fibrils within neurons of the cerebral cortex—one of the earliest descriptions of the pathology of the disease that now bears his name.
