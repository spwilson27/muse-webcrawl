Greek government-debt crisis

Anti-austerity protests in Athens at the height of the crisis.

The Greek government-debt crisis was a sovereign debt crisis that struck Greece in late 2009 and became the most severe episode of the wider European debt crisis. Years of high government spending, weak tax collection, and misreported statistics left Greece with public debt far above the eurozone limits; when the newly elected government of George Papandreou revealed in October 2009 that the budget deficit was far larger than previously reported (eventually revised to about 15.4% of GDP), credit rating agencies downgraded Greek bonds and the state lost access to capital markets.

Bailouts and austerity

Between 2010 and 2015 Greece received three rescue packages from the "Troika" — the European Commission, European Central Bank, and International Monetary Fund — totalling roughly €289 billion, conditioned on deep austerity: pension and wage cuts, tax rises, privatisation, and structural reforms. In 2012 private creditors accepted the largest sovereign debt restructuring in history, writing down about €107 billion of Greek bonds. The economy shrank by roughly a quarter from its pre-crisis peak, and unemployment exceeded 27% in 2013, with youth unemployment near 60%.

The 2015 crisis

The election of the left-wing Syriza government under Alexis Tsipras in January 2015 led to confrontation with creditors; a referendum in July 2015 rejected the creditors' terms, banks were closed and capital controls imposed, yet the government ultimately accepted a third bailout. Greece exited the bailout programmes in August 2018, though its public debt — around 180% of GDP, the highest in the eurozone — remained subject to enhanced surveillance.
