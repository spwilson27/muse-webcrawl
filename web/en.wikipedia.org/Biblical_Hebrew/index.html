Biblical Hebrew (also called Classical Hebrew) is an archaic form of the Hebrew language, a language in the Canaanite branch of Semitic languages spoken by the Israelites in the area known as the Land of Israel, roughly west of the Jordan River and east of the Mediterranean Sea. It is the language in which most of the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) was written.

History

Biblical Hebrew is not a single uniform language but spans several centuries of development. Scholars generally distinguish Archaic Biblical Hebrew, found mainly in poetic passages such as the Song of Deborah, from Standard Biblical Hebrew of the pre-exilic period, and Late Biblical Hebrew of the Second Temple period, which shows increasing Aramaic influence.

Grammar

Biblical Hebrew is a typically Semitic language built on triconsonantal roots. Its verb system is organized around aspects and stems (binyanim) such as Qal, Niphal, Piel, Pual, Hiphil, Hophal and Hithpael. The language makes extensive use of the waw-consecutive construction to chain narrative verb sequences.

Writing system

The language was originally written in the Paleo-Hebrew alphabet, derived from Phoenician script. After the Babylonian exile, the square Aramaic-derived script became standard. The Masoretes of the early Middle Ages added vowel points (niqqud) and cantillation marks to preserve the traditional pronunciation.

Legacy

Biblical Hebrew remained a language of liturgy, scholarship and literature for centuries after it ceased to be spoken, and it formed the foundation for the revival of Hebrew as a modern spoken language in the 19th and 20th centuries.
