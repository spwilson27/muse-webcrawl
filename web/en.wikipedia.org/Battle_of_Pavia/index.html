Battle of Pavia

The Battle of Pavia, fought on 24 February 1525, was the decisive engagement of the Italian War of 1521–1526 of the Habsburg–Valois wars. The French army under King Francis I was annihilated by the Imperial-Spanish forces of Emperor Charles V, and Francis himself was captured. The battle marked the ascendancy of Spanish infantry and firearms on the European battlefield and confirmed Habsburg hegemony over Italy.

Background

In October 1524 Francis I invaded Lombardy and laid siege to Pavia, defended by an Imperial garrison under Antonio de Leyva. Over the winter an Imperial relief army, including German landsknechts under Georg von Frundsberg, assembled near the French siege lines in the great walled hunting park of Mirabello north of the city.

The battle

On the night of 23–24 February, Imperial engineers breached the park wall and the relief army entered in darkness and fog. At dawn French heavy cavalry—the gendarmes—charged and initially drove back Spanish horse, but masked their own artillery and became separated from support. Spanish and German arquebusiers under the Marquis of Pescara, fighting from the woods, shot the French men-at-arms down at close range; meanwhile De Leyva's garrison sallied from Pavia into the French rear. The French army collapsed: perhaps 8,000–10,000 French and Swiss troops were killed, including many of France's greatest nobles such as La Trémoille, La Palice and Bonnivet. Francis, unhorsed and wounded, surrendered—famously writing to his mother that "of all things nothing remains to me but honour and life."

Aftermath

Francis was taken to Madrid and released in 1526 under the Treaty of Madrid, which he repudiated on his return, but the battle secured Spanish dominance in Italy for a generation. Militarily, Pavia demonstrated that pike-and-shot infantry with arquebus fire could destroy the finest heavy cavalry in Europe, a lesson that reshaped warfare.
