Self-consciousness

Self-consciousness is a heightened sense of self-awareness. It is a preoccupation with oneself, as opposed to the philosophical concept of self-consciousness, which is an awareness that one exists as an individual being. In the contemporary sense of the term, self-consciousness is a preoccupation with one's own appearance or manner and, in severe forms, can be a manifestation of chronic anxiety or other mental disorders.

An unpleasant feeling of self-consciousness may occur when one realizes that one is being watched or observed, as in the feeling of being "on stage". Some people habitually develop self-conscious feelings as a personality trait, while others experience them only situationally.

Psychology

Psychologists distinguish between private self-consciousness (attention to one's inner thoughts and feelings) and public self-consciousness (awareness of oneself as a social object). The Self-Consciousness Scale, developed by Fenigstein, Scheier, and Buss in 1975, measures these dimensions along with social anxiety. High public self-consciousness is associated with concern about how others evaluate one's appearance and behaviour.

Development

Self-conscious emotions such as embarrassment and shame typically emerge in childhood once children develop self-recognition, often tested by the mirror test. In adolescence, self-consciousness often intensifies, a phenomenon related to what David Elkind called the "imaginary audience"—the adolescent's belief that others are constantly watching and evaluating them.

Philosophical usage

In philosophy, self-consciousness has a different meaning: it refers to the capacity of a subject to be aware of itself as an object of experience. Thinkers from Descartes to Hegel treated self-consciousness as central to the nature of mind and personal identity.
