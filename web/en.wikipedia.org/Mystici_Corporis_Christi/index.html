Mystici Corporis Christi ("Of the Mystical Body of Christ") is a papal encyclical issued by Pope Pius XII on 29 June 1943, during the Second World War. It is regarded as one of the most significant encyclicals of his pontificate, setting out the Catholic doctrine of the Church as the Mystical Body of Christ — a single, visible, and divinely constituted society united to Christ as its head and animated by the Holy Spirit — and identifying that body with the Roman Catholic Church.

Pope Pius XII issued the encyclical on the feast of Saints Peter and Paul, 1943.

Content

Building on scriptural images — above all St Paul's description of the Church as Christ's body (1 Corinthians 12) — the encyclical teaches that Christ is the founder, head, and sustainer of the Church; that the Holy Spirit is its soul; and that membership is constituted by baptism, profession of the true faith, and submission to legitimate authority, including the Roman Pontiff. It rejects two extremes: a rationalistic view that reduces the Church to a merely human federation, and a false mysticism that dissolves the visible Church into a purely spiritual fellowship. The document also addresses the position of those outside full communion, speaking of non-Catholics who relate to the Mystical Body by an "unconscious desire and longing" (inscio quodam desiderio ac voto), and it closes with exhortations to prayer, Eucharistic devotion, and loyalty to the Holy See.

Context and influence

Written amid wartime, the encyclical also condemned racism and the murder of the disabled and other innocents, lamenting the "sad fate" of those killed for their nationality or descent. Its teaching strongly influenced the Second Vatican Council's constitution Lumen Gentium (1964), which adopted the image of the Body of Christ as a central description of the Church — while broadening the language on membership with the phrase "subsists in," a formulation that traces back to Mystici Corporis Christi's identification of the Mystical Body with the Catholic Church. Together with Pius XII's liturgical and biblical encyclicals of the same year, it is seen as a key bridge between pre-conciliar and conciliar Catholic theology.
