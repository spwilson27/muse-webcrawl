Anselm of Canterbury

Anselm of Canterbury (c. 1033 – 21 April 1109), also called Anselm of Aosta after his birthplace and Anselm of Bec after his monastery, was an Italian Benedictine monk, abbot, philosopher and theologian of the Catholic Church, who held the office of Archbishop of Canterbury from 1093 to 1109. As Archbishop, he defended the Church's interests in England amid the Investiture Controversy.

Philosophy and theology

Anselm is famous as the originator of the ontological argument for the existence of God, set out in his Proslogion (1077–78), in which he argued that God, defined as "that than which nothing greater can be conceived", must exist in reality as well as in the understanding. In Cur Deus Homo ("Why God Was Man"), he developed the satisfaction theory of atonement. His motto, fides quaerens intellectum ("faith seeking understanding"), defines his method of rational inquiry grounded in belief, and he is often called the father of scholasticism.

Life

Born in Aosta in the Kingdom of Arles, Anselm entered the Abbey of Bec in Normandy in 1060, succeeding Lanfranc as prior and then abbot. In 1093 he became Archbishop of Canterbury, but conflicts with William II and Henry I over lay investiture drove him twice into exile. He died in Canterbury in 1109 and was canonized in 1494; his feast day is 21 April.
