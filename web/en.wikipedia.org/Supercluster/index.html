Supercluster

[Image placeholder: supercluster map]

A supercluster is a large group of smaller galaxy clusters and galaxy groups, and is among the largest structures known in the observable universe. Galaxies are not distributed uniformly but are arranged in clusters, which in turn cluster into superclusters separated by immense voids nearly devoid of galaxies. The Milky Way galaxy is part of the Local Group, which belongs to the Virgo Cluster, which in turn is part of the Laniakea Supercluster.

Structure

Superclusters typically span hundreds of millions of light-years and contain thousands of galaxies. They form part of the cosmic web: filamentary structures of dark matter and baryonic matter along which galaxies are distributed, surrounding vast cosmic voids. Unlike galaxy clusters, most superclusters are not gravitationally bound as single systems and will be pulled apart by the accelerating expansion of the universe.

Notable superclusters

  • Laniakea Supercluster: defined in 2014 by the flow of galaxies toward the Great Attractor; contains roughly 100,000 galaxies over about 520 million light-years, including the Virgo Cluster and the Local Group.
  • Virgo Supercluster: the older, more concentrated structure centered on the Virgo Cluster, about 110 million light-years across.
  • Shapley Supercluster: one of the most massive concentrations nearby, about 650 million light-years away in Centaurus.
  • Perseus–Pisces Supercluster: a prominent chain of clusters about 250 million light-years away.
  • Coma Supercluster: containing the Coma and Leo clusters, part of the Great Wall filament.
  • Hercules Superclusters: a pair of superclusters in the constellation Hercules.

Observation

Superclusters are mapped through large redshift surveys such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey and the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey, which measure the three-dimensional distribution of galaxies. Their study constrains models of structure formation in the early universe, including the role of dark matter and dark energy.
