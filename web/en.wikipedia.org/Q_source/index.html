The Q source (also called the Q Gospel, Q document, or simply Q, from the German Quelle, "source") is a hypothetical written collection of Jesus's sayings. It is a central component of the two-source hypothesis, the most widely held solution to the Synoptic Problem: the observation that the Gospels of Matthew and Luke share a great deal of material — sayings of Jesus such as the Lord's Prayer, the Beatitudes and much of the Sermon on the Mount — that is absent from Mark, generally held to be the earliest Gospel.

The hypothesis

According to the two-source hypothesis, Matthew and Luke were written independently, each using Mark as a narrative framework and Q as a source of sayings. Q itself, if it existed, was a Greek document of roughly 4,500 words with little narrative structure: it opens with the preaching of John the Baptist, includes Jesus's temptation, the great sermon, instructions to disciples, controversies with opponents, and pronouncements about the coming Son of Man, but contains no account of Jesus's birth, death or resurrection and no explicit narrative of his miracles. Scholars reconstruct its text from the passages where Matthew and Luke agree against Mark, and debate its layers of composition and the theology of the community that produced it.

History and criticism

The idea was developed by 19th-century German scholars including Christian Weisse and Christian Hermann Weisse's successors; the label "Q" became standard in the early 20th century. No manuscript of Q has ever been found — unsurprising if it was copied into the canonical Gospels and ceased to circulate independently. Alternative solutions dispense with Q: the Farrer hypothesis proposes that Luke used Matthew directly, and the Augustinian hypothesis assumes Matthean priority. Debates about Q remain a mainstay of New Testament scholarship, and a critical edition, The Critical Edition of Q (2000), presents the reconstructed text.
