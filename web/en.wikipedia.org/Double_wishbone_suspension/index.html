Double wishbone suspension

A double wishbone suspension showing upper and lower control arms

A double wishbone suspension is an independent suspension design for automobiles using two (occasionally parallel) wishbone-shaped arms to locate the wheel. Each wishbone or arm has two mounting points to the chassis and one joint at the knuckle. The shock absorber and coil spring mount to the wishbones to control vertical movement. Double wishbone designs allow engineers to carefully control the motion of the wheel throughout suspension travel, controlling camber angle, caster angle, toe pattern, roll center height, scrub radius, scrub, and more.

History

Double wishbone suspension was introduced in the 1930s. The French car manufacturer Citroën introduced it in their 1934 Rosalie and Traction Avant models. Packard Motor Car Company in Detroit, Michigan, built the Packard One Twenty with it in 1935 and promoted it as a safety feature. In that era the MacPherson strut was still in development by aircraft engineers and use for automobiles did not appear until the 1950s. After World War II, double wishbone suspension design came to be prominently used on performance and luxury cars because of its superior geometry control, although the simpler MacPherson strut later dominated mass-market vehicles on cost grounds.

Design

The assembly typically includes two control arms per wheel — an upper arm and a lower arm — each pivoting on the chassis at two points and joining at a ball joint on the steering knuckle or upright. The two arms, the knuckle, and the chassis form a four-bar linkage. If the upper arm is shorter than the lower arm, the wheel gains negative camber as the suspension compresses, which helps keep the tire's contact patch flat against the road during cornering roll.

Geometry

Because the lengths and pivot positions of the arms can be chosen independently, the designer can tune camber gain, roll-center migration, and anti-dive and anti-squat characteristics. Spring and damper usually act on the lower arm, though in some racing designs they act on the upper arm or through pushrod or pullrod linkages.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include excellent handling potential, a low tendency for the tire to tilt in corners, and the ability to keep the suspension relatively low and compact vertically. Disadvantages include greater cost and complexity than a MacPherson strut, more ball joints to wear, and the space taken up by the upper arm, which can intrude into engine or luggage compartments.

Applications

Double wishbone suspension is widely used in sports cars, racing cars (including Formula One historically, before pushrod-actuated variants), and premium passenger cars such as earlier Honda Accord models and many Mercedes-Benz and BMW designs. Trucks and SUVs sometimes use it at the front for its strength and adjustability.
