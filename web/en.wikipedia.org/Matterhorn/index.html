Matterhorn

Matterhorn
Elevation: 4,478 m (14,692 ft)
Location: Switzerland–Italy border (Valais / Aosta Valley)
Range: Pennine Alps
First ascent: 14 July 1865, Edward Whymper and party

The Matterhorn (German: Matterhorn, Italian: Monte Cervino, French: Mont Cervin) is a mountain of the Alps, straddling the border between Switzerland and Italy. Its 4,478-metre (14,692 ft) pyramidal peak, formed by glacial erosion, is one of the most recognizable mountains in the world and a symbol of Switzerland. Because of its isolated prominence and forbidding shape, it was among the last major Alpine peaks to be climbed, and its first ascent in 1865 ended in tragedy that marked the close of the golden age of alpinism.

First ascent

After years of attempts, mainly by the English climber Edward Whymper and the Italian guide Jean-Antoine Carrel, two parties raced for the summit in July 1865. Whymper's seven-man party reached the top via the Hörnli ridge on 14 July, but on the descent four climbers — Michel Croz, Douglas Hadow, Charles Hudson and Lord Francis Douglas — fell to their deaths when a rope broke. The disaster became a sensation in Britain and prompted debate about the morality of mountaineering; Whymper's account in Scrambles Amongst the Alps (1871) remains a classic. Three days later Carrel's party completed the Italian (Lion) ridge.

Climbing and tourism

Today roughly 3,000 climbers a year attempt the Matterhorn, chiefly by the Hörnli ridge from Zermatt with the Hörnli hut as base, and more than 500 people in total have died on the mountain. The resorts of Zermatt (Switzerland) and Breuil-Cervinia (Italy) face each other across its flanks, linked by cable cars including the Klein Matterhorn station, among the highest in Europe. The mountain's silhouette famously inspired the Toblerone chocolate bar's shape, and a replica stands at Disneyland in California.
