Iris recognition is an automated method of biometric identification that uses mathematical pattern-recognition techniques on images of the complex patterns within the iris of an individual's eyes. The iris — the coloured muscular diaphragm surrounding the pupil — has an extraordinarily rich texture of furrows, crypts, rings and freckles that forms randomly during gestation and remains stable throughout life; even the two eyes of one person, and those of identical twins, have unrelated patterns.

Technology

Modern iris recognition is based largely on algorithms developed by John Daugman at the University of Cambridge. A camera using near-infrared light (700–900 nm) images the iris; software locates the iris boundaries, unwraps the annulus into a normalised rectangular strip, and encodes its phase structure with two-dimensional Gabor wavelets into a compact "IrisCode" of about 256 bytes. Matching is done by comparing Hamming distances between codes, fast enough to search databases of hundreds of millions of records. False-match rates are extremely low because the code contains roughly 249 independent degrees of freedom.

Applications

Iris recognition is used at border crossings (notably in the United Arab Emirates), in refugee registration by UN agencies, in physical access control, and in national identity programmes — most prominently India's Aadhaar system, which has enrolled the irises of more than a billion residents. It is considered among the most accurate single biometrics, though it requires cooperative subjects, suitable lighting and cameras at short range.
