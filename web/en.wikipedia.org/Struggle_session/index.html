Struggle session

A struggle session was a form of public humiliation used by the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) to shape public opinion and to humiliate, persecute, and sometimes execute political rivals and other enemies of the state. In sessions, which were often held at the workplace or school of the accused, victims were forced to confess their perceived misdeeds in front of crowds.

Origins

The practice grew out of criticism and self-criticism rituals within the Communist Party and was applied on a mass scale during the land reform campaigns of the late 1940s and early 1950s, when landlords were publicly denounced in village meetings. The term "struggle" (鬥爭) drew on the broader Maoist theory of class struggle.

Cultural Revolution

Struggle sessions reached their most violent and widespread form during the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976). Victims — teachers, intellectuals, officials, and alleged "class enemies" — were paraded with placards around their necks, forced into humiliating postures such as the "jet plane" position (arms pulled back and head bowed), beaten, and in many cases driven to suicide or killed outright. Prominent victims included head of state Liu Shaoqi, who was repeatedly struggled against before dying in custody in 1969.

Purpose and effects

The sessions served several functions: breaking the victim's social status and will, demonstrating the fate awaiting nonconformity, and implicating the audience itself in the persecution, thereby binding participants to the regime. Historians regard them as one of the defining instruments of terror and social control in Mao-era China.
