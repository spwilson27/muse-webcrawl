The Calculus of Consent: Logical Foundations of Constitutional Democracy (1962) is a book by economists James M. Buchanan and Gordon Tullock. It is considered one of the foundational works of public choice theory and launched Buchanan's research programme of constitutional economics, for which he received the Nobel Memorial Prize in 1986.

Argument

The book applies economic reasoning — methodological individualism and rational self-interest — to political decision-making, rather than assuming politicians and voters pursue an undefined "public interest". Its central analysis concerns the choice of voting rules: an individual choosing a constitution balances the external costs of collective decisions imposed on them (falling as the required majority grows toward unanimity) against the decision-making costs of reaching agreement (rising toward unanimity). The optimal rule minimises the sum of the two.

Key concepts

The authors analyse logrolling (vote trading), the rationale for bicameral legislatures and qualified majorities, and argue that near-unanimity (following Knut Wicksell) is appropriate at the constitutional stage, when rules themselves are chosen behind a partial veil of uncertainty about one's future position.

Influence

Alongside works by Anthony Downs and Kenneth Arrow, The Calculus of Consent founded modern public choice: the economic study of non-market decision-making, with lasting influence on constitutional design, federalism and the analysis of bureaucracy and rent-seeking.
