The Ring of Fire (also known as the Pacific Ring of Fire, the Rim of Fire, the Girdle of Fire or the Circum-Pacific belt) is a tectonic belt of volcanoes and earthquakes, about 40,000 km (25,000 mi) long and up to about 500 km (310 mi) wide, which surrounds most of the rim of the Pacific Ocean. The Ring of Fire is the direct result of plate tectonics: specifically the movement, collision and destruction of lithospheric plates under and around the Pacific Ocean.

It contains between 750 and 915 active or dormant volcanoes — around two-thirds of the world total — and about 90% of the world's earthquakes occur along its path, including some of the largest ever recorded, such as the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile (magnitude 9.5), the 1964 Alaska earthquake, and the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami in Japan.

Geography

The belt runs from New Zealand northward along the eastern edge of Asia — through the Philippines, Japan and the Aleutian Islands — and then down the western coasts of North and South America, following the Andes. Nearly every segment is associated with a subduction zone, where an oceanic plate sinks beneath a neighbouring plate and melts, feeding chains of volcanoes such as the Cascade Range, the Japanese archipelago and the Indonesian arc.

Hazards

More than 350 million people live within the Ring of Fire. Volcanic eruptions such as Mount St. Helens (1980) and Pinatubo (1991), and megathrust earthquakes with their attendant tsunamis, make it one of the most closely monitored geological regions on Earth. Geothermal resources associated with the belt are exploited in Iceland-like Pacific settings including New Zealand, Indonesia and the western United States.
