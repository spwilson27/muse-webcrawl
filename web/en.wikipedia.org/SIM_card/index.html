SIM card

TypeSmart card / integrated circuit card
Full nameSubscriber Identity Module
Introduced1991 (GSM networks)
StandardISO/IEC 7816; ETSI TS 102 221
Form factorsFull-size, mini-SIM, micro-SIM, nano-SIM, eSIM

A SIM card (Subscriber Identity Module or Subscriber Identification Module) is an integrated circuit card used in mobile devices to securely store the subscriber's identity information, most importantly the International Mobile Subscriber Identity (IMSI) number and its related key, which are used to identify and authenticate subscribers on mobile telephone networks. The SIM also stores network-specific information, and on many cards, the user's contacts and text messages.

The SIM card allows users to change mobile devices simply by removing the card from one device and inserting it into another. Because the subscription identity travels with the card rather than the handset, the same phone number and service follow the user.

History

The SIM card was specified as part of the GSM standard developed by the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI) in the late 1980s. The first SIM cards were issued in 1991 by the German company Giesecke & Devrient to the Finnish network operator Radiolinja. Developed alongside the GSM system by the Groupe Spécial Mobile, the card-based approach contrasted with earlier analogue systems in which the subscriber identity was tied to the handset itself.

With the arrival of 3G UMTS networks, the SIM evolved into the USIM application, and in 4G LTE and 5G networks a single physical card (a UICC, Universal Integrated Circuit Card) can host several applications, including the SIM, USIM and ISIM functions.

Form factors

SIM cards have shrunk steadily over time. The original full-size SIM was the size of a credit card. The mini-SIM (25 × 15 mm) became the familiar standard of the GSM era. The micro-SIM (15 × 12 mm), introduced with devices such as the iPhone 4 in 2010, was followed by the nano-SIM (12.3 × 8.8 mm) in 2012, barely larger than the chip contacts themselves. The embedded SIM, or eSIM, dispenses with a removable card entirely: the chip is soldered into the device and provisioned remotely. A virtual equivalent, the iSIM, integrates the function into the device's main processor.

Data stored

A SIM card contains a unique serial number (the ICCID), the IMSI, a security authentication key (Ki), and temporary information related to the local network. The Ki is never transmitted; instead, the network issues a random challenge and both the card and the network's authentication centre compute a signed response using the A3 algorithm and a session key using A8, allowing mutual verification without exposing the secret.

Cards also store the preferred network list, service provider name, emergency numbers, and — depending on capacity, which ranges from a few kilobytes on early cards to several hundred kilobytes on modern ones — phone book entries and SMS messages.

Security and locking

Access to a SIM can be protected by a personal identification number (PIN); entering the wrong PIN three times locks the card until a longer PIN Unblocking Key (PUK) is supplied. Some operators sell handsets locked to their network, a practice known as SIM locking, which prevents the phone from accepting cards from other carriers until unlocked.
