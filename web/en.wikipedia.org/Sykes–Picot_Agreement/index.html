Signed16 May 1916

SubjectProposed partition of Ottoman Arab provinces after the First World War

The Sykes–Picot Agreement, officially the Asia Minor Agreement, was a secret treaty concluded in May 1916 between the United Kingdom and France, with the assent of the Russian Empire, defining their mutually agreed spheres of influence and control in the Middle East should the Ottoman Empire be defeated in the First World War. It is named after its negotiators, Britain's Sir Mark Sykes and France's François Georges-Picot.

Terms

The agreement allotted direct control of coastal Syria, Lebanon, and Cilicia to France, and of southern Mesopotamia (Iraq) and the ports of Haifa and Acre to Britain. Inland areas were divided into a French sphere of influence in Syria and Mosul (Zone A) and a British sphere in Transjordan and northern Arabia (Zone B), where nominally independent Arab states were envisaged, while Palestine was to be placed under an international administration. The plan conflicted with promises made to Sharif Hussein of Mecca in the McMahon–Hussein Correspondence encouraging an Arab revolt against the Ottomans, and with the Balfour Declaration of 1917 promising a Jewish national home in Palestine.

Disclosure and consequences

The Bolshevik government published the secret treaties in November 1917, causing embarrassment to the Allies and anger in the Arab world. Although the actual post-war settlement — the League of Nations mandates for Syria and Lebanon (French) and for Palestine, Transjordan, and Iraq (British), confirmed at San Remo in 1920 — differed in detail, the modern borders of Iraq, Jordan, Israel, Palestine, Lebanon, and Syria broadly descend from this settlement. The agreement remains a potent symbol of imperial duplicity in the region, famously invoked by the Islamic State group in its propaganda when it bulldozed the Iraq–Syria border berm in 2014.
