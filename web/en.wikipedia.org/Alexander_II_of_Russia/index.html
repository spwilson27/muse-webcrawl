Alexander II (29 April 1818 – 13 March 1881) was Emperor of Russia from 1855 until his assassination in 1881. Known as Alexander the Liberator, he is best remembered for the Emancipation Reform of 1861, which abolished serfdom in Russia, and for a wider programme of modernising reforms affecting the judiciary, local government, the military and education.

Reign and reforms

Alexander came to the throne during the Crimean War, whose defeat exposed the backwardness of the Russian Empire. Concluding the Treaty of Paris in 1856, he embarked on the "Great Reforms". The Emancipation Manifesto of 3 March 1861 freed more than 23 million serfs, though redemption payments and communal land arrangements left many peasants dissatisfied. The zemstvo reform of 1864 created elected local assemblies; the judicial reform of the same year introduced independent courts, trial by jury and a modern legal profession. Military reforms under Dmitry Milyutin replaced 25-year service with universal conscription from 1874.

Foreign policy

Under Alexander, Russia sold Alaska to the United States in 1867, expanded into Central Asia and the Far East, and fought the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78, which led to the liberation of Bulgaria and the independence of Romania, Serbia and Montenegro, though the resulting Treaty of San Stefano was revised at the Congress of Berlin.

Assassination

Alexander survived several assassination attempts by the revolutionary group Narodnaya Volya (People's Will). On 13 March 1881, in Saint Petersburg, he was mortally wounded by bombs thrown at his carriage; the Church of the Savior on Spilled Blood was later built on the site. He was succeeded by his son Alexander III, who reversed many of his father's liberal measures. At the time of his death Alexander was considering a consultative assembly — sometimes called the Loris-Melikov constitution — which was abandoned by his successor.
