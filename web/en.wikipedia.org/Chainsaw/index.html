Chainsaw

A chainsaw is a portable saw powered by electricity, compressed air, hydraulic power, or — most commonly — an internal combustion engine, used in activities such as tree felling, limbing, bucking, pruning, cutting firebreaks in wildland fire suppression, and harvesting firewood. It cuts with a set of teeth attached to a rotating chain that runs along a guide bar.

Construction

A chainsaw consists of an engine (typically a two-stroke petrol engine of 30–120 cc, or an electric motor), a guide bar around which the cutting chain travels, the chain itself with its cutters and rakers (depth gauges), a centrifugal clutch, and safety features including a chain brake and anti-vibration mounts. Chain types include full chisel, semi-chisel and low-profile chains; specialized chainsaws exist for cutting concrete, ice and other materials.

History

The earliest ancestor of the chainsaw was a flexible hand-driven chain saw used by surgeons from the 1780s for cutting bone (the osteotome). For forestry, several inventors developed powered chain saws in the 1920s; Andreas Stihl patented an electric chain saw in 1926 and a petrol model in 1929, founding the company that bears his name. After the Second World War, improved engines and aluminium construction produced the first true one-man chainsaws (such as the McCulloch 3-25 of 1949), transforming logging productivity.

Safety

Chainsaws are among the most dangerous hand tools; the characteristic hazard is kickback, a sudden upward throw of the bar when the chain tip contacts wood. Operators wear protective clothing — chainsaw chaps or trousers with fibres that clog the chain, helmets with visors and ear defenders — and most jurisdictions require training for professional use. Modern saws include chain brakes, throttle interlocks and reduced-kickback bars and chains.
