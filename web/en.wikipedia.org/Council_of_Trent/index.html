The Council of Trent (Latin: Concilium Tridentinum), held between 1545 and 1563 in Trent (now Trento, northern Italy) and briefly in Bologna, was the 19th ecumenical council recognised by the Catholic Church. It was the Church's principal response to the Protestant Reformation and is considered the embodiment of the Counter-Reformation.

Meeting in twenty-five sessions over eighteen years under Popes Paul III, Julius III and Pius IV, the council issued condemnations of what it defined as Protestant heresies and made authoritative statements of Catholic doctrine.

Doctrinal decrees

  • Scripture and tradition: affirmed that divine revelation is contained in both the Bible and unwritten traditions, rejecting sola scriptura.
  • Original sin and justification: reaffirmed justification by grace through faith informed by charity, rejecting justification by faith alone.
  • Sacraments: affirmed seven sacraments and the doctrine of transubstantiation in the Eucharist.
  • Vulgate: declared the Latin Vulgate the authoritative biblical text for doctrine.

Reforms

The council ordered the founding of seminaries in every diocese for the proper training of priests, required bishops to reside in their dioceses, attacked pluralism and simony, and commissioned the Tridentine Catechism, a revised breviary and missal, and an Index of Prohibited Books. Its decrees shaped Catholic doctrine and practice until the Second Vatican Council (1962–65).
