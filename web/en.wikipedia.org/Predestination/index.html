Predestination

Predestination, in theology, is the doctrine that all events have been willed by God, usually with reference to the eventual fate of the individual soul. It is a religious concept involved in questions of whether God knows or determines future events, and it is often associated with the Reformed tradition of Christianity, particularly the teachings of Augustine of Hippo and John Calvin.

Christian theology

Augustine argued that God elects some to salvation by grace, a view developed in controversy with Pelagius. In the Reformation, John Calvin taught double predestination: God has eternally decreed both the election of some to eternal life and the reprobation of others. The Synod of Dort (1618–19) affirmed unconditional election against the Arminians, summarised in the "TULIP" points of Calvinism. Arminian theology, by contrast, teaches conditional election based on God's foreknowledge of faith, a position adopted by Methodism.

Other traditions

In Islam, belief in divine decree (qadar) is one of the six articles of faith, held alongside human responsibility. Debates between schools such as the Qadariyah and Jabriyah concerned the extent of human free will. Similar concepts appear in discussions of karma and fate in other religions, though these differ from the Western theological doctrine.
