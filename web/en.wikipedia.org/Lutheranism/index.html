Lutheranism

Lutheranism
Type: Protestant Christian denomination
Founder figure: Martin Luther (1483–1546)
Origin: 16th century, Wittenberg, Saxony
Scripture: Bible (sola scriptura)
Confessions: Augsburg Confession (1530); Book of Concord (1580)
Adherents: ~77 million worldwide
Largest bodies: Ethiopian Evangelical Church Mekane Yesus; churches of Germany, Scandinavia, Indonesia, Tanzania, USA
Global communion: Lutheran World Federation (founded 1947)

Lutheranism is a major branch of Protestant Christianity that identifies primarily with the theology of Martin Luther, the German monk and reformer who launched the Reformation with his Ninety-five Theses of 1517. With about 77 million adherents, it is the third-largest Protestant tradition after Anglicanism and Pentecostalism, historically dominant in northern Germany and Scandinavia and carried worldwide by migration and missions — today the largest Lutheran churches are in Ethiopia and Tanzania.

Theology

Lutheran doctrine is summarised by the "solas": salvation by grace alone (sola gratia) through faith alone (sola fide), on the authority of Scripture alone (sola scriptura). At its centre stands justification by faith — God declares sinners righteous for Christ's sake, apart from works. Lutherans confess two sacraments, Baptism and the Eucharist, affirming the real presence of Christ's body and blood "in, with and under" the bread and wine (sacramental union). The tradition is confessional: its teachings are collected in the Book of Concord (1580), including the Augsburg Confession (1530), Luther's Small and Large Catechisms, and the Formula of Concord. Other themes include the theology of the cross, the distinction of Law and Gospel, and the priesthood of all believers.

History

The movement spread through the universities and printing presses of the Holy Roman Empire, was presented corporately in the Augsburg Confession of 1530, and gained legal standing in the Peace of Augsburg (1555). Lutheran state churches arose in Denmark-Norway (1536) and Sweden, and the tradition weathered the Thirty Years' War, the orthodox scholastic age, Pietism (Spener, Francke), and the rationalist era. In the 19th century, confessional revival and mass emigration planted Lutheran synods across North America; mission movements reached Africa and Asia, where Lutheran churches now grow fastest. The Lutheran World Federation, founded at Lund in 1947, links most Lutheran churches; its 1999 Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification with the Roman Catholic Church resolved the central doctrinal dispute of the Reformation era.

Worship and practice

Lutheran worship retains much of the historic liturgy — the Mass order, lectionary, hymnody and church year — centred on preaching and the sacrament. Congregational singing, enriched by chorales and the music of J. S. Bach, is a hallmark. Polity ranges from episcopal (Scandinavia) to synodical-congregational (America), and practice varies between confessional bodies (such as the LCMS) and the more ecumenical mainline churches (such as the ELCA and the LWF member churches).
