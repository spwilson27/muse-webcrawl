North Pole

The North Pole, also known as the Geographic North Pole or Terrestrial North Pole, is the point in the Northern Hemisphere where the Earth's axis of rotation meets its surface. It is the northernmost point on Earth and lies in the middle of the Arctic Ocean, amid waters almost permanently covered by constantly shifting sea ice.

Geography

The North Pole is the northernmost point on Earth, lying diametrically opposite the South Pole. Its position is defined as 90 degrees north latitude. Unlike the South Pole, which lies on a continental land mass, the North Pole is located in the middle of the Arctic Ocean amid waters covered by sea ice. The nearest land is usually said to be Kaffeklubben Island, off the northern coast of Greenland, about 700 kilometres (430 mi) away.

At the pole, the Sun is continuously above the horizon during the summer months and continuously below it during the winter, producing one very long "day" and one very long "night" each year. All directions at the North Pole point south.

Exploration

Attempts to reach the pole began in the late 19th century. The American explorer Robert Peary claimed to have reached the pole on 6 April 1909, accompanied by Matthew Henson and four Inuit men; the claim remains disputed. Frederick Cook had earlier claimed to have reached it in 1908. The first undisputed attainment was by air: on 12 May 1926 the airship Norge, carrying Roald Amundsen, Lincoln Ellsworth and Umberto Nobile, flew over the pole. The first verified surface attainment was by Ralph Plaisted's snowmobile expedition in 1968, and the first unsupported dog-sled journey to reach the pole was in 1986.

Climate

The North Pole is substantially warmer than the South Pole because it lies at sea level in the middle of an ocean, which acts as a reservoir of heat. Winter temperatures range from about −43 °C to −26 °C, while summer temperatures hover near the melting point of ice, around 0 °C.
