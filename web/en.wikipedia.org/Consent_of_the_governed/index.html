Consent of the governed

In political philosophy, the phrase consent of the governed refers to the idea that a government's legitimacy and moral right to use state power is justified and lawful only when consented to by the people or society over which that political power is exercised. This theory of consent is opposed to the traditional political philosophy of the divine right of kings and is often signaled in the United States Declaration of Independence.

History

The concept was developed by thinkers such as John Locke, whose Second Treatise of Government (1689) grounded political authority in an original compact among free individuals, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau, who developed the idea through the social contract and the general will. Earlier antecedents appear in the writings of Francisco Suárez and in medieval notions of law requiring communal agreement.

Types of consent

Philosophers distinguish express (or explicit) consent, given openly for example by oath or vote, from tacit consent, implied by remaining within a state's territory or accepting its protection. Critics, notably David Hume, questioned whether most people can meaningfully be said to consent tacitly, given the costs of emigration.

In founding documents

The phrase "deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed" appears in the United States Declaration of Independence (1776). The idea underlies popular sovereignty in democratic constitutions and the right of a people to alter or abolish a government destructive of their rights.
