Crystal field theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Crystal field theory (CFT) describes the breaking of orbital degeneracy in transition metal complexes due to the presence of a static electric field produced by a surrounding set of anionic charges (ligands). CFT successfully accounts for some magnetic properties, colours, hydration enthalpies and spinel structures of transition metal complexes, but it does not attempt to describe bonding.

History

CFT was developed by physicists Hans Bethe and John Hasbrouck van Vleck in the 1930s, and was later combined with molecular orbital theory into the more complete ligand field theory, which accounts for covalency in the metal–ligand bond.

Orbital splitting

In an isolated transition metal ion the five d orbitals are degenerate. When ligands approach — for example at the vertices of an octahedron — the degeneracy is lifted: the orbitals pointing directly at the ligands (dx²−y² and dz², the eg set) are raised in energy, while those pointing between them (dxy, dxz, dyz, the t2g set) are lowered. The energy gap is denoted Δo (or 10 Dq).

High spin and low spin

Whether electrons pair in the lower set or occupy the higher set depends on the size of Δo relative to the pairing energy. Weak-field ligands (small Δo) give high-spin complexes; strong-field ligands (large Δo) give low-spin complexes. Ligands are ordered by field strength in the spectrochemical series, from I− (weak) through H2O and NH3 to CO and CN− (strong). The size of Δo also determines the wavelengths of light absorbed, explaining the colours of transition metal complexes.

Crystal field stabilisation energy

The net stabilisation from occupying the lowered orbitals, the crystal field stabilisation energy (CFSE), helps explain trends in hydration enthalpies, lattice energies, ionic radii and the site preferences of ions in spinel minerals.
