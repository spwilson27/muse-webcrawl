Molecular cloning

Molecular cloning is a set of experimental methods in molecular biology used to assemble recombinant DNA molecules and to direct their replication within host organisms. The use of the word cloning refers to the fact that the method involves the replication of one molecule to produce a population of cells with identical DNA molecules. Molecular cloning generally uses DNA sequences from two different organisms: the species that is the source of the DNA to be cloned, and the species that will serve as the living host for replication of the recombinant DNA.

Procedure

In a typical cloning experiment, the target DNA fragment is first isolated and cut, along with a vector — usually a small circular plasmid — using restriction endonucleases that recognise specific DNA sequences. The fragments are joined with DNA ligase to form a recombinant plasmid. This plasmid is then introduced into host cells, commonly the bacterium Escherichia coli, by transformation. Cells that have taken up the plasmid are identified using selectable markers, such as antibiotic-resistance genes carried by the vector, and a screening step (for example blue–white screening in lacZ vectors) distinguishes colonies carrying an insert. As the bacteria grow, they copy the plasmid, yielding large quantities of the cloned DNA, which can be harvested and analysed by sequencing, restriction mapping or PCR.

Applications

Molecular cloning underpins much of modern biology and biotechnology. Cloned genes are used to produce recombinant proteins such as human insulin and growth hormone, to create genetically modified organisms, to study gene function through mutagenesis and expression analysis, and to assemble DNA libraries — genomic and cDNA libraries — for genome projects. Newer methods, including Gibson assembly and Golden Gate cloning, allow several fragments to be joined in a single reaction and have partly replaced classic restriction-enzyme cloning for complex constructs.

History

The foundations of cloning were laid in the early 1970s with the work of Paul Berg on recombinant DNA, and of Stanley Cohen, Herbert Boyer and Annie Chang, who in 1973 constructed the first plasmid that could be propagated in bacteria. These experiments launched the recombinant DNA revolution and led directly to the founding of the biotechnology industry.
