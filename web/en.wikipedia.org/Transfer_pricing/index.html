Transfer pricing

Transfer pricing is the setting of prices for goods, services, intangible assets and financial transactions exchanged between related entities within a multinational enterprise — for example, between a parent company and its subsidiaries. Because such prices can be used to shift profits between tax jurisdictions, tax authorities in most countries require that they be set on an arm's length basis: the price that would have been charged between independent parties in comparable circumstances.

Methods

The OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines recognise five principal methods for establishing an arm's length price: the comparable uncontrolled price (CUP) method, the resale price method, the cost plus method, the transactional net margin method (TNMM), and the transactional profit split method. Selection of the "most appropriate method" depends on the nature of the transaction, the availability of reliable comparables, and the functions performed, assets used and risks assumed by each party (the FAR analysis).

Regulation and disputes

Many jurisdictions require contemporaneous documentation demonstrating that transfer prices are arm's length, and impose penalties for material misstatements. The OECD's Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) project (2013–2015) strengthened rules on intangibles, risk and capital, and introduced country-by-country reporting for large multinational groups. Large disputes — involving technology, pharmaceutical and consumer goods companies — have led to adjustments of billions of dollars, and taxpayers may seek advance pricing agreements (APAs) or mutual agreement procedures between competent authorities to avoid double taxation.

Economic role

Within firms, transfer prices also serve management accounting purposes: evaluating divisional performance, allocating costs and motivating managers. These internal objectives can conflict with tax-minimisation or customs objectives, so a single transfer price rarely satisfies all purposes.
