Greek comedy

Comic actors of the ancient Greek theatre.

Ancient Greek comedy was a popular and influential form of theatre performed across ancient Greece from the 5th century BCE. Alongside tragedy, it formed one of the two principal genres of Greek drama. Scholars conventionally divide its development into three phases: Old Comedy, Middle Comedy, and New Comedy.

Old Comedy

Old Comedy, exemplified by Aristophanes — whose eleven plays survive complete — was topical, fantastical, and fiercely satirical, attacking politicians, generals, and intellectuals by name. Its structure combined dialogue scenes with choral elements including the agon (formal debate) and the parabasis, in which the chorus addressed the audience directly in the poet's voice. Plays such as The Clouds, The Frogs, and Lysistrata remain widely read and performed.

Middle and New Comedy

Middle Comedy (c. 400–320 BCE), represented by fragmentary authors such as Antiphanes and Alexis, turned away from personal and political satire toward mythological burlesque and stock social types. New Comedy, whose chief author was Menander, focused on domestic life, love intrigues, and recognisable character types — the strict father, the clever slave, the young lover — and strongly influenced Roman comedy through Plautus and Terence and, through them, later European comedy.

Origins

Aristotle traced comedy's origins to the phallic processions and revel songs (kōmos) of Dorian communities and to the iambic satire tradition. Comedy was admitted to the City Dionysia festival at Athens in 486 BCE, later than tragedy, and was also performed at the Lenaea, where it came to be especially favoured.
