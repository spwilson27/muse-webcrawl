Mexican Cession

The Mexican Cession (Cesión mexicana) is the region in the modern-day southwestern United States that Mexico ceded to the United States in the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in 1848, at the end of the Mexican–American War. The territory comprised 529,000 square miles (1,370,000 km²) — about 55% of Mexico's pre-war claimed territory — in exchange for US$15 million and the assumption of up to $3.25 million in claims by American citizens against Mexico.

Territory

The cession included the present-day US states of California, Nevada, and Utah, most of Arizona, the western half of New Mexico, the western quarter of Colorado, and the southwest corner of Wyoming. Texas, including its claimed extension to the Rio Grande, had already been annexed by the United States in 1845.

Consequences

Together with the later Gadsden Purchase (1853), the Cession completed the modern land boundary between Mexico and the United States. The new lands intensified the American debate over the expansion of slavery, contributing to the Compromise of 1850 and the road to the Civil War. The treaty guaranteed property rights and offered US citizenship to Mexican nationals in the ceded lands, though many protections eroded in practice.
