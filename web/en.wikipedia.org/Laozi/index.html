Laozi, also romanised as Lao Tzu, was an ancient Chinese philosopher traditionally credited as the author of the Tao Te Ching and regarded as the founder of philosophical Taoism. Tradition places him in the 6th century BC, as a contemporary of Confucius, but modern scholars debate whether he was a single historical person, a composite figure, or a purely legendary sage; the received text of the Tao Te Ching is generally thought to have been compiled in the 4th–3rd centuries BC.

Traditional biography

The earliest biography, in Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian (c. 91 BC), identifies Laozi with Li Er (also called Lao Dan), a keeper of archives at the Zhou royal court who, weary of the dynasty's decline, rode west into exile. At the Hangu Pass, the gatekeeper Yinxi persuaded him to write down his teaching — the 5,000-character Tao Te Ching — before disappearing into the west. Confucius is said to have consulted him about ritual and compared him to a dragon.

Thought and legacy

The teaching attributed to Laozi centres on the Tao (Dao), the ineffable "Way" underlying the universe, and on wu wei — effortless, non-coercive action in harmony with the natural order — along with simplicity, humility, and softness overcoming hardness ("the softest thing in the world dashes against and overcomes the hardest"). In religious Taoism Laozi was deified as Taishang Laojun, one of the Three Pure Ones, and his book became one of the most translated texts in world literature, deeply shaping Chinese philosophy, religion, and art.
