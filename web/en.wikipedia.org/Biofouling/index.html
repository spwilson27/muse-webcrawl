Biofouling

[Image placeholder: fouled ship hull]

Biofouling or biological fouling is the accumulation of microorganisms, plants, algae, or small animals where it is not wanted on surfaces such as ship hulls, submerged structures, water inlets, pipelines, and medical devices. The phenomenon is divided into microfouling — the formation of biofilm, which begins within minutes to hours of immersion — and macrofouling, the attachment of larger organisms such as barnacles, mussels, tubeworms, and seaweeds, which follows over weeks to months.

Process

When a clean surface is immersed in natural water, dissolved organic molecules first form a conditioning film. Bacteria colonize the surface and produce an extracellular polymeric slime, forming a biofilm. Diatoms and other microalgae follow, creating a visible slime layer. Finally, the larvae of macrofouling organisms settle, metamorphose, and grow into mature communities that can add substantial weight and roughness to a structure.

Impact

On ships, biofouling increases hydrodynamic drag, raising fuel consumption and greenhouse gas emissions substantially; heavy calcareous fouling can increase required shaft power by large margins. Fouling organisms are also a major vector for the spread of invasive aquatic species when they are transported on hulls between ports. In industry, biofilms clog pipelines, contaminate drinking water systems, increase corrosion, and foul heat exchangers; in medicine, biofilm growth on implants and catheters is a persistent cause of infection.

Prevention and control

Antifouling strategies include biocidal coatings that leach copper or other toxicants, foul-release silicone coatings with low surface energy, mechanical cleaning and grooming, ultrasonic systems, and in-water cleaning robots. The once-dominant biocide tributyltin (TBT) was banned globally under an International Maritime Organization convention that took effect in 2008 because of severe effects on non-target marine life. Modern research focuses on non-toxic surface topographies, enzyme-based coatings, and biomimetic materials inspired by shark skin and other naturally fouling-resistant surfaces.
