Solfège

In music, solfège (French: [sɔl.fɛʒ]) or solfeggio (Italian: [solˈfeddʒo]) is a music education method used to teach aural skills, pitch, and sight-reading of Western music. It assigns syllables to the notes of a musical scale: in the most familiar form, do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti (or si), do.

History

The syllables derive from the medieval Latin hymn Ut queant laxis, whose phrases begin on successive scale degrees: Ut, Re, Mi, Fa, Sol, La. The 11th-century monk Guido d'Arezzo used these syllables, together with the "Guidonian hand," to teach singers to sight-read chant. Ut was later replaced by the more singable Do, and a seventh syllable (Si, from Sancte Iohannes, or Ti in English-speaking countries) was added.

Systems

Fixed do

In fixed-do systems, used widely in France, Italy, Spain, and many other countries, do always corresponds to C, re to D, and so on, whatever the key. In these languages the syllables effectively name the notes themselves.

Movable do

In movable-do systems, common in English-speaking countries and in the Kodály method, do is the tonic of whatever key is in use, so the syllables express scale functions. Chromatic alterations are sung with modified syllables (for example, raised do becomes di, lowered ti becomes te). In minor keys, movable-do teaching may begin on la ("la-based minor").

Use in education

Solfège is a core component of ear training and sight-singing curricula in conservatories and school music programs. Related systems include the tonic sol-fa method of John Curwen, which spread widely in Britain and its colonies, and number-based systems used in some countries. Comparable syllabic traditions exist in other musics, such as the sa re ga ma (sargam) of Indian classical music.

In popular culture

The syllables became famous worldwide through the song "Do-Re-Mi" from the 1959 Rodgers and Hammerstein musical The Sound of Music.
