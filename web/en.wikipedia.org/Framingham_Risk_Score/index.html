Framingham Risk Score

The Framingham Risk Score is a sex-specific algorithm used to estimate an individual's 10-year risk of developing cardiovascular disease. It was derived from data collected by the Framingham Heart Study, a long-running cohort study begun in 1948 in Framingham, Massachusetts.

Inputs and calculation

The score incorporates age, sex, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, systolic blood pressure (and whether it is treated), smoking status, and diabetes. Points are assigned for each risk factor, and the total is converted into an estimated percentage risk of a coronary or broader cardiovascular event over the following ten years.

Clinical use

Guidelines have used Framingham-based risk estimates to guide decisions about statin therapy, antihypertensive treatment, and aspirin prophylaxis. Commonly used versions include the 1998 Wilson score for coronary heart disease and the 2008 D'Agostino general cardiovascular risk profile.

Limitations

The score was developed in a largely white, middle-class American population and may over- or under-estimate risk in other ethnic groups and eras with different background event rates. Later tools, such as the pooled cohort equations in the United States and QRISK in the United Kingdom, were developed to recalibrate risk in more diverse populations.
