Arabic poetry (Arabic: الشعر العربي) is one of the oldest and most central literary arts of Arabic culture, with surviving works dating to the pre-Islamic period of the 6th century AD and oral traditions extending earlier. Poetry held an elevated status in Arab society as a record of genealogy, praise, satire, and tribal memory, and it profoundly shaped the Arabic language and its rhetoric.

Pre-Islamic poetry

The classical form was the qasida, a long ode built on strict metres codified later by al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi, typically moving through themes of the abandoned encampment (nasib), the desert journey, and praise or boast. The most celebrated collection is the Mu'allaqat, the "Suspended Odes", attributed to poets including Imru' al-Qais and Labid.

Classical and later development

Under the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates, poetry flourished at court and in cities; al-Mutanabbi is often regarded as the greatest classical Arabic poet. Themes expanded to include love (ghazal), elegy (ritha), satire (hija), wine and ascetic poetry, and mystical verse in the Sufi tradition. In al-Andalus, new strophic forms such as the muwashshah and zajal arose. The 20th century saw the free verse movement led by figures such as Nazik al-Malaika and Badr Shakir al-Sayyab, and the prose poem, while poets like Mahmoud Darwish gained international prominence.
