A breadboard, or protoboard, is a construction base used to build semi-permanent prototypes of electronic circuits. Modern solderless breadboards consist of a perforated plastic block with numerous spring clips beneath the holes; component leads and wires are pushed into the holes to make connections without soldering, allowing circuits to be assembled, tested, and modified quickly.


A 400-point solderless breadboard.

History

The name comes from the early days of radio, when hobbyists mounted components on actual wooden bread boards using screws and nails. In the 1970s the modern solderless plastic breadboard became widely available, and it remains a staple of electronics education and prototyping.

Layout

A typical board has terminal strips in which the holes are connected in groups of five, plus distribution strips (rails) along the sides used for power supply connections. Integrated circuits in dual in-line packages straddle a central ravine so that each pin connects to a separate strip segment.

Limitations

Breadboards are unsuited to high frequencies because of parasitic capacitance and inductance, and their contacts can be unreliable for high currents. Finished designs are usually transferred to a printed circuit board or stripboard.
