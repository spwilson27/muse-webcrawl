Dirty bomb

A dirty bomb (also called a radiological dispersal device) is a speculative radiological weapon that combines radioactive material with conventional explosives. The purpose is to contaminate the area around the dispersal agent or conventional explosion with radioactive material, potentially making buildings uninhabitable and causing public panic; it is not a true nuclear weapon and involves no nuclear explosion.

Likely effects

Analyses by agencies such as the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission conclude that the explosion itself, rather than the radiation, would cause most immediate casualties: the quantity of radioactivity obtainable from accessible sources (such as medical or industrial devices) would disperse over a wide area at generally low levels. The principal effects would likely be economic and psychological — evacuation, decontamination costs, and disruption — leading commentators to describe such a device as a "weapon of mass disruption" rather than mass destruction.

Incidents and concerns

No successful dirty bomb attack has occurred. In 1995, Chechen separatists planted (but did not detonate) a device containing caesium-137 in a Moscow park and alerted the media. Security efforts focus on protecting and tracking high-activity radioactive sources used in medicine and industry, coordinated internationally by the IAEA.

Response

First responders treat a suspected dirty bomb scene like other hazardous-materials incidents: isolation of the area, monitoring for contamination, and decontamination of people and property.
