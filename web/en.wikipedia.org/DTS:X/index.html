DTS:X is an object-based audio codec developed by DTS, Inc., announced in January 2015 and commercially launched in April 2015. Like its principal competitor Dolby Atmos, DTS:X allows sound mixers to place and move individual sound objects — a helicopter, a voice, a rain shower — in three-dimensional space above and around the listener, rather than assigning sounds to fixed speaker channels. The decoder in an AV receiver or soundbar then renders those objects to whatever speaker layout is actually installed.

History

DTS announced DTS:X in January 2015, positioning it as an open, license-friendly alternative to Dolby Atmos for the emerging immersive-audio market. The first DTS:X-capable AV receivers reached the market in mid-2015 through firmware updates and new models from manufacturers including Denon, Marantz, Onkyo, Pioneer, and Yamaha. The first Blu-ray Discs carrying DTS:X soundtracks followed later that year, with early titles including Ex Machina and The Last Witch Hunter. DTS:X support was subsequently built into the Ultra HD Blu-ray ecosystem and into a wide range of soundbars and televisions.

Technology

A DTS:X soundtrack is carried within a DTS-HD Master Audio bitstream. Alongside the conventional channel bed (typically 7.1 channels), the stream carries audio objects with metadata describing each object's position in a three-dimensional space. The DTS:X renderer maps the objects to the listener's actual speaker configuration — which can range from a soundbar using reflected sound, through 5.1.2 and 7.1.4 home-theater layouts with height speakers, up to large commercial cinema installations.

Unlike fixed channel-based formats, DTS:X is designed to be layout-agnostic and is promoted as an open standard without strict mandated speaker placements; the renderer adapts to speakers positioned within a reasonable tolerance of the recommended locations. DTS:X also includes dialogue control features on some content, allowing listeners to raise the level of dialogue objects independently of the rest of the mix. Its headphone-oriented companion technology, DTS Headphone:X, applies the same object rendering binaurally.

Variants

DTS:X Pro, introduced in 2019, extends the format to support up to 30.2 channels of rendering for high-end home theaters and processors, using the Neural:X upmixer to make full use of large speaker counts. DTS Virtual:X is a related post-processing technology that simulates height and surround effects from a small number of speakers, such as a standalone soundbar, without discrete surround or height channels. The DTS:X codec is also used in IMAX Enhanced releases, where a specially mastered DTS:X track reproduces the IMAX theatrical mix at home.

Adoption

DTS:X soundtracks appear on numerous Ultra HD Blu-ray and Blu-ray releases from studios including Universal, Lionsgate, and Paramount, and the format is supported by most mid-range and premium AV receivers and processors sold since 2016, as well as a growing range of televisions and soundbars. In commercial cinema, DTS:X installations compete with Dolby Atmos screens; DTS reported several hundred DTS:X-equipped auditoriums worldwide within a few years of launch.
