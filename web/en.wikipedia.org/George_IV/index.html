George IV (1762–1830) was King of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland and King of Hanover from 1820 until his death. For the preceding nine years he had served as Prince Regent during the mental illness of his father, George III — the period known as the Regency, famed for its distinctive architecture, fashion, and literature.

Early life and the Regency

As Prince of Wales, George was a flamboyant patron of the arts and a leader of fashion, but his extravagance, debts, and political intrigues made him unpopular. In 1785 he secretly married the Catholic widow Maria Fitzherbert, a union invalid under the Royal Marriages Act; his legal marriage in 1795 to Caroline of Brunswick was a notorious failure, and as king he unsuccessfully sought to divorce her through the Pains and Penalties Bill of 1820.

Reign

George's coronation in 1821 was a spectacle of unprecedented lavishness. His reign saw Catholic emancipation conceded in 1829 under pressure from Wellington and Peel. He gave his name to an era of elegance: the Brighton Pavilion, remodelled by John Nash, Buckingham Palace, and the Regency terraces of London reflect his taste.

Assessment

George's only legitimate child, Princess Charlotte, died in 1817, so he was succeeded by his brother William IV. Historians remember him as indolent and self-indulgent yet a genuine connoisseur whose patronage permanently shaped British art and architecture.
