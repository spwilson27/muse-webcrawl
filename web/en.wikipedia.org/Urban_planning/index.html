Urban planning

Urban planning, also known as town planning, city planning, regional planning or rural planning, is the technical and political process of developing and designing land use and the built environment, including air, water and the infrastructure passing into and out of urban areas, such as transportation, communications and distribution networks, and their accessibility. Traditionally, urban planning followed a top-down approach in master planning the physical layout of human settlements, with the primary concern being the public welfare, which included considerations of efficiency, sanitation, protection, use of the environment, effects of economic activities and effects on the natural environment.

Urban planning answers questions about how people will live, work and play in a given area and thus guides orderly development in urban, suburban and rural areas. It is concerned with the allocation of resources, the location of housing, industry, transport and public services, and the protection of landscapes and historic fabric. Modern planning is a technical and political profession that brings together engineering, architecture, geography, economics, sociology, environmental science and law.

History

Planned settlements are as old as urbanisation itself. The cities of the Indus Valley, such as Mohenjo-daro and Harappa (c. 2600 BCE), were laid out on grids with drainage systems. In classical antiquity Hippodamus of Miletus was credited with the orthogonal grid plan. Roman towns, Chinese capitals such as Chang'an, and Islamic cities all embodied formal planning principles, as did the Laws of the Indies that governed Spanish colonial towns in the Americas.

The modern discipline emerged in response to the industrial city of the 19th century. Overcrowding, cholera and pollution prompted public-health legislation, model dwellings and, in Britain, the Town Planning Act 1909. Ebenezer Howard's Garden Cities of To-Morrow (1898) proposed self-contained communities surrounded by greenbelts and inspired Letchworth and Welwyn Garden City. In the United States, the City Beautiful movement, the 1909 Plan of Chicago by Daniel Burnham and Edward Bennett, and the adoption of zoning after New York's 1916 Zoning Resolution shaped practice.

After the Second World War, comprehensive planning, new towns, urban renewal and large-scale highway building dominated, drawing criticism from writers such as Jane Jacobs, whose The Death and Life of Great American Cities (1961) defended mixed uses, short blocks and lively streets. From the 1970s, advocacy planning, public participation, environmental assessment and heritage conservation became central, followed by new urbanism, transit-oriented development and, in the 21st century, smart-city technologies and climate adaptation.

Theory and approaches

Planning theory debates whether plans should be comprehensive and rational, incremental, or negotiated among competing interests. Major approaches include the rational comprehensive model, advocacy planning associated with Paul Davidoff, communicative and collaborative planning, and radical planning. In practice most systems combine statutory land-use plans, development control, and strategic spatial plans at regional and national scales.

Practice

Land use and zoning

Zoning ordinances divide land into districts — residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural and mixed-use — and regulate density, height, setbacks and permitted activities. Alternatives include form-based codes, performance standards and incentive zoning.

Transportation planning

Transportation planning seeks to move people and goods efficiently and safely, balancing roads, public transit, cycling and walking. Concepts include the hierarchy of streets, complete streets, transit-oriented development around stations, and demand management through pricing and parking policy.

Environmental and climate planning

Planners protect watersheds, floodplains and ecosystems, require environmental impact assessment, and increasingly plan for climate change mitigation and adaptation: reducing emissions through compact, transit-served development, and managing risks from heat, flooding and sea-level rise through green infrastructure and resilient design.

Urban design

Urban design works at the scale between architecture and planning — streets, squares, parks and districts — concerned with legibility, enclosure, human scale and place-making, as analysed by Kevin Lynch in The Image of the City (1960).

Planning systems

Systems vary by country. The United Kingdom uses a discretionary, plan-led system under the Town and Country Planning Acts; most of continental Europe uses codified plans; the United States leaves planning largely to local governments under state enabling law, with the Standard State Zoning Enabling Act of 1922 as a model. India, China, and many developing countries operate formal master-plan systems alongside large informal settlements, a central challenge for contemporary planning.

Criticism and debates

Critics have charged planning with social engineering, displacement through urban renewal and gentrification, automobile dependence produced by single-use zoning and minimum parking requirements, and failure to house growing populations affordably. Defenders argue that markets alone cannot coordinate infrastructure, protect public health and the environment, or provide public goods such as parks and transit.
