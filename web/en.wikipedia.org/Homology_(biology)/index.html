Homology (biology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In biology, homology is the existence of shared ancestry between a pair of structures, or genes, in different taxa. A common example is the forelimbs of vertebrates, where the wings of bats, the arms of primates, the front flippers of whales, and the forelegs of four-legged animals such as dogs and crocodiles are all derived from the same ancestral tetrapod structure. Evolutionary biology explains homologous structures as having adapted to different purposes as a result of descent with modification from a common ancestor.

The term was coined by anatomist Richard Owen in 1843, defined as "the same organ in different animals under every variety of form and function," in contrast to analogy, "the same function in different organs." Homology thus predates Darwin, but evolutionary theory gave Owen's formal distinction a causal explanation: structures are homologous because they are inherited, with modification, from a common ancestor. The concept contrasts with homoplasy — similarity acquired independently by convergent evolution, parallelism, or reversal, as in the wings of insects and of birds.

Levels of homology

Homology applies at many levels. Serial homology describes repeated structures within one organism, such as vertebrae or the segments of an arthropod. At the molecular level, homologous genes are those descended from a common ancestral gene: orthologs diverge by speciation, while paralogs diverge by gene duplication within a lineage. Developmental biology adds "deep homology" — the sharing of ancient genetic toolkits, such as Hox and Pax6 genes, underlying structures (insect and vertebrate eyes, for instance) that are not themselves homologous as organs.

Criteria and evidence

Homology hypotheses are tested by congruence with phylogenies built from many characters, by detailed correspondence of structure and position (Remane's criteria), by embryological development, and, for sequences, by statistical similarity beyond chance. Homologous similarities are the primary evidence used in cladistics to reconstruct the tree of life; mistaking homoplasy for homology is a principal source of error in systematics.
