Flood basalt

A flood basalt (or plateau basalt) is the result of a giant volcanic eruption or series of eruptions that covers large stretches of land or the ocean floor with basalt lava. Flood basalts form the main part of large igneous provinces, in which volumes of basalt exceeding a million cubic kilometres may be erupted in geologically short intervals, typically a few million years or less.

Formation

Flood basalt provinces are thought to be caused by mantle plumes — upwellings of hot material from deep in the Earth — that arrive beneath the lithosphere, cause melting, and feed enormous volumes of low-viscosity basaltic magma to the surface through fissure systems. Individual flows can travel hundreds of kilometres, and successive flows stack into stepped plateaus (the name "traps", from the Swedish trappa, "stairs", is given to such provinces). Continental flood basalts include the Siberian Traps (~252 million years ago), the Deccan Traps of India (~66 million years ago), the Paraná–Etendeka Traps, the Karoo–Ferrar province, and the Columbia River Basalt Group in the northwestern United States (~16 million years ago), the youngest and smallest major example.

Environmental effects

Because of their scale, flood basalt eruptions release vast quantities of carbon dioxide and sulfur gases and are strongly implicated in mass extinctions. The Siberian Traps coincide with the end-Permian mass extinction, the largest known; the Deccan Traps erupted across the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary and may have contributed to the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs alongside the Chicxulub impact; and the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province is associated with the end-Triassic extinction.
