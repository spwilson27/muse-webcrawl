Silent Spring is an environmental science book by Rachel Carson. Published on September 27, 1962, the book documented the environmental harm caused by the indiscriminate use of pesticides, particularly DDT. Carson accused the chemical industry of spreading disinformation and public officials of accepting the industry's marketing claims unquestioningly.

Content

The book opens with "A Fable for Tomorrow", imagining a town in which birdsong had ceased — the "silent spring" of the title. Carson then documents how chlorinated hydrocarbon and organophosphate insecticides persist in the environment, accumulate in fatty tissues, thin the eggshells of birds of prey, and threaten human health. She did not call for an outright ban on all pesticides but for restraint, research, and biological alternatives.

Reaction

Chemical companies mounted a vigorous countercampaign, attacking Carson's scientific credentials and character. Nevertheless, the book became a bestseller, serialized first in The New Yorker. President John F. Kennedy's Science Advisory Committee reviewed and largely vindicated her findings in 1963.

Legacy

Silent Spring is widely credited with launching the modern environmental movement in the United States, inspiring grassroots activism that led to the first Earth Day in 1970, the creation of the Environmental Protection Agency, and the nationwide ban on agricultural use of DDT in 1972.
