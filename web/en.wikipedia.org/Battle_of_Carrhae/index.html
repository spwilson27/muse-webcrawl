Battle of Carrhae

Date53 BC (probably 9 June)
LocationNear Carrhae (Harran), northern Mesopotamia, modern Turkey
ResultDecisive Parthian victory
BelligerentsRoman Republic vs. Parthian Empire
CommandersMarcus Licinius Crassus (killed), Cassius Longinus; Surena (Parthia)

The Battle of Carrhae was fought in 53 BC between the Roman Republic and the Parthian Empire near the ancient town of Carrhae (modern Harran, Turkey). The Roman army, commanded by the triumvir Marcus Licinius Crassus, invaded Mesopotamia seeking glory and plunder to rival his partners Pompey and Caesar. It met a Parthian force under the young general Surena composed largely of horse archers and cataphracts, and suffered one of the worst defeats in Roman history: some 20,000 Romans were killed and 10,000 captured, and Crassus himself died during or after the fighting.

The campaign

Crassus crossed the Euphrates with about seven legions — roughly 35,000–40,000 heavy infantry with 4,000 cavalry and light troops — and, guided onto the open desert plain, met Surena's approximately 10,000 horsemen. The Parthian horse archers, supplied with fresh arrows by a train of a thousand camels, could shoot while retreating (the origin of the phrase "Parthian shot") and their composite bows pierced Roman shields and armour. Roman attempts to close with the enemy failed, and a detached force led by Crassus's son Publius was lured out, surrounded, and destroyed; Publius's head was displayed to the main army on a spear.

Aftermath

The surviving Romans retreated by night to Carrhae and then toward the Armenian hills, abandoning their wounded; Cassius Longinus escaped to Syria with about 10,000 men. Crassus was killed during a parley or in its confused aftermath. The loss of the legionary standards (aquilae) was a lasting humiliation, only recovered decades later by Augustus through diplomacy. Carrhae shattered the First Triumvirate's balance and fixed the Euphrates as the effective frontier between Rome and Persia for centuries.
