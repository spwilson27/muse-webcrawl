Biwa

The biwa (Japanese: 琵琶) is a Japanese short-necked wooden lute, traditionally played with a large triangular plectrum called a bachi. It descends from the Chinese pipa, which reached Japan via the Silk Road during the Nara period (710–794), and ultimately from lute traditions of Central and West Asia. The biwa is strongly associated with narrative storytelling, above all the recitation of The Tale of the Heike by blind monk-musicians known as biwa hōshi.

Construction and playing

The instrument has a shallow, pear-shaped body carved from hardwoods such as mulberry or rosewood, four (occasionally five) strings of wound silk, and tall frets that let the player press the string down between them to bend pitch. The oversized plectrum produces a percussive attack, and players exploit a characteristic buzzing tone (sawari) shared with other Japanese instruments. Because notes decay quickly, performance interleaves short vocal narrative passages with instrumental interludes.

Types

Several types are distinguished: the gagaku biwa, heavy and used in court music; the mōsō biwa of the blind monks; the Heike biwa, used to accompany the Heike epic; the Satsuma biwa, developed in Satsuma domain with a vigorous, martial style; and the smaller, more melodic Chikuzen biwa of the Meiji era, often played by women performers. Each type differs in size, stringing, and repertoire.

History and legacy

Biwa-accompanied storytelling flourished in the medieval period, when biwa hōshi travelled the country reciting war tales and performing rituals to pacify spirits of the dead. The instrument declined with modernisation but survived through dedicated schools and was revitalised in the 20th century, when players such as Kinshi Tsuruta — whose collaborations included Tōru Takemitsu's November Steps (1967) for biwa, shakuhachi, and orchestra — brought it to international audiences. Japan's largest lake, Lake Biwa, is named for its resemblance to the instrument's shape.
