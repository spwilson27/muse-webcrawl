Prostacyclin

Prostacyclin (also called prostaglandin I2, PGI2) is a prostaglandin member of the eicosanoid family of lipid molecules. Produced by endothelial cells lining blood vessels, it is a potent vasodilator and the most powerful endogenous inhibitor of platelet aggregation, playing a central role in vascular homeostasis.

Synthesis and action

Prostacyclin is synthesized from arachidonic acid via prostaglandin H2 by the enzyme prostacyclin synthase. It acts on IP receptors to raise intracellular cyclic AMP in platelets (preventing clumping) and in vascular smooth muscle (causing relaxation and widening of vessels). Its effects oppose those of thromboxane A2, and the balance between the two helps regulate clot formation. Prostacyclin is chemically unstable, with a half-life of only a few minutes.

Clinical use

Synthetic prostacyclin (epoprostenol) and stable analogues such as iloprost, treprostinil, and the receptor agonist selexipag are used to treat pulmonary arterial hypertension, where they improve exercise capacity and survival. Prostacyclin was discovered in 1976 by a group led by John Vane.
