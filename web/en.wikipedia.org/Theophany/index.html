Theophany

EtymologyGreek θεοφάνεια (theopháneia), "appearance of a god"
Related conceptsEpiphany, Christophany, hierophany, divine revelation
FeastTheophany/Epiphany, 6 January (Eastern Orthodox)

Theophany (from Ancient Greek θεοφάνεια, theopháneia, meaning "appearance of a deity") is an encounter with a deity that manifests in an observable and tangible form. The term has been used to refer to appearances of the gods in ancient Greek and Near Eastern religions, and in Christian theology it denotes the visible manifestations of God to humankind, especially those recorded in the Bible.

In the Hebrew Bible

Biblical theophanies include God's appearance to Abraham at Mamre, the burning bush from which God speaks to Moses on Mount Horeb, the giving of the Law amid thunder, lightning, and cloud on Mount Sinai, the pillar of cloud and fire guiding the Exodus, and the visions of Isaiah and Ezekiel. Such appearances are often mediated — by fire, cloud, storm, or the "angel of the LORD" — and typically inspire awe and fear; Jacob, after wrestling with a mysterious figure at Peniel, declares that he has seen God "face to face" and survived.

In Christianity

Christian writers have debated whether Old Testament theophanies were appearances of God the Father or pre-incarnate appearances of Christ, the latter being termed Christophanies. In Eastern Orthodox tradition, Theophany is also the name of one of the Great Feasts, celebrated on 6 January, commemorating the baptism of Jesus in the Jordan, at which the Holy Trinity was made manifest: the voice of the Father, the Son in the water, and the Spirit descending as a dove. The feast is marked by the Great Blessing of Waters.

Other traditions

Theophanic encounters appear across religions: Greek gods descending in Homer's epics, Krishna's universal form revealed to Arjuna in the Bhagavad Gita, and the descent of the Quran held in Islamic tradition to be revelation conveyed through the angel Jibril rather than a direct manifestation. The academic study of religion uses related terms such as hierophany, coined by Mircea Eliade for manifestations of the sacred generally.
