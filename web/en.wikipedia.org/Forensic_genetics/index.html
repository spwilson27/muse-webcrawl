Forensic genetics

An electropherogram, the standard readout of a forensic DNA profile.

Forensic genetics is the branch of genetics concerned with the application of genetic knowledge and DNA analysis to legal issues. It is primarily used to identify individuals from biological evidence such as blood, semen, saliva, hair and skin cells left at crime scenes, to establish paternity and other family relationships, and to identify human remains in mass disasters and missing-person cases.

History

The field began in 1984, when Alec Jeffreys at the University of Leicester discovered that certain regions of DNA contain repeated sequences that vary greatly between individuals. His technique of DNA fingerprinting was first used forensically in the investigation of the murders of two teenagers in Leicestershire, England, exonerating an innocent suspect and identifying Colin Pitchfork as the killer. With the development of the polymerase chain reaction (PCR), analysis shifted to short tandem repeats (STRs), which could be amplified from minute or degraded samples.

Methods

Modern forensic DNA profiling examines a standard panel of STR loci — 20 core loci in the expanded CODIS set used in the United States. The resulting profile is compared with reference samples and searched against offender databases such as CODIS in the US and the National DNA Database in the United Kingdom, the first of its kind, established in 1995. Mitochondrial DNA sequencing is used for highly degraded samples such as hair shafts and old bone, and Y-chromosome STRs are used in sexual-assault evidence and lineage testing.

Newer techniques

Forensic DNA phenotyping predicts externally visible characteristics such as eye, hair and skin colour from DNA. Investigative genetic genealogy, which searches consumer genealogy databases for relatives of an unknown donor, gained prominence after it was used to identify the Golden State Killer in 2018. Rapid DNA instruments can produce a profile from a cheek swab in about ninety minutes.

Controversies

Debate surrounds the retention of profiles from arrested but unconvicted persons, familial searching, the risk of contamination and transfer, and the privacy implications of genealogy databases. Low-template DNA evidence has also been challenged because of stochastic effects such as allele drop-out.
