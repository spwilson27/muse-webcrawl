Glaze (painting technique)

A glaze is a thin, transparent or semi-transparent layer of paint applied over a dried layer of paint in oil painting and other media. Glazes modify the appearance of the underlying layer, deepening colour, increasing luminosity, and creating optical mixtures that cannot be achieved by physically mixing pigments on a palette.

Technique

The glaze is made by mixing a pigment with a large proportion of medium — oil, resin, or varnish — so that light passes through the film and reflects from the layers below. Because each layer must be dry before the next is applied, glazing is a slow, deliberate process; painters observe the rule of "fat over lean" to prevent cracking. Warm glazes over cool underpainting (or the reverse) produce subtle shifts of temperature used to model flesh and drapery.

History

Glazing was highly developed by the Early Netherlandish painters of the 15th century, such as Jan van Eyck, whose layered oil glazes achieved jewel-like colour. The technique was central to the work of Titian, Rembrandt, and Johannes Vermeer, and related methods appear in tempera painting, where thin layers were hatched or veiled over opaque underpainting (velatura). Acrylic glazing is common in contemporary practice.
