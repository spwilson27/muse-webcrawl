Bicameralism

Bicameralism is a type of legislature that is divided into two separate assemblies, chambers, or houses, known as a bicameral legislature. Bicameralism is distinct from unicameralism, in which all members deliberate and vote as a single group. About 60% of the world's national legislatures are unicameral and 40% bicameral, although the two chambers rarely have equal power.

A legislative chamber of the kind used by upper and lower houses in bicameral systems.

Often, the members of the two chambers are chosen by different methods. Primary legislation typically requires a concurrent majority, the approval of both chambers, to become law. Although the terms "enactment" and "adoption" are not exact, bicameralism is the most important method used by many parliamentary and semi-presidential systems. Some unions of states, including Australia, Belgium, Brazil, Canada, Germany, India, Malaysia, Mexico, Nigeria, Pakistan, Russia, Switzerland, and the United States, are federations with bicameral legislatures.

History

The British Parliament is often described as the original bicameral legislature, having existed in two chambers since the 14th century: the House of Commons and the House of Lords, the latter originally representing the nobility and clergy. Other European countries adopted the model over the following centuries. The United States Constitution created a bicameral Congress, the Senate and the House of Representatives, as a compromise between large and small states at the Constitutional Convention of 1787.

Rationale

Advocates argue that a second chamber provides a check on hasty legislation, represents different interests (such as states or provinces within a federation), and adds expertise or a longer-term perspective. Critics respond that bicameralism can create gridlock, duplicate effort, and give disproportionate power to indirectly elected or appointed bodies.

Types

In federal systems the upper house usually represents constituent states or provinces, often with equal representation regardless of population, as in the United States Senate or the Australian Senate. In unitary systems upper houses are typically weaker, serving as revising chambers; examples include the British House of Lords and the French Senate. Some upper houses are fully elected, others partly elected and partly appointed, and some wholly appointed.
