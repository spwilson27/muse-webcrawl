Bath stone is an oolitic limestone from the Great Oolite Group of the Middle Jurassic, quarried around Bath in Somerset, England. Its warm, honey colour gives the Georgian city of Bath its distinctive appearance and has made it one of Britain's most famous building stones.

Geology and quarrying

The stone formed about 166 million years ago in a shallow tropical sea, from countless tiny spherical grains (ooids) of calcium carbonate. It has been extracted since Roman times, but the great era of quarrying came in the 18th century, driven by entrepreneur Ralph Allen, who promoted the stone and profited from the building boom in Bath. Later extraction was largely underground, in mines such as those at Combe Down and Corsham, the latter also worked for munitions storage and government facilities in the 20th century.

Characteristics

Bath stone is a freestone: soft and easily sawn when freshly quarried ("green"), hardening on exposure to air. It weathers to a pleasing mellow tone but is vulnerable to pollution and frost in some exposures, requiring careful conservation of Bath's Georgian terraces and crescents.

Uses

Beyond Bath itself — where it faces the Royal Crescent, the Circus and Pulteney Bridge — Bath stone has been used for churches, country houses and civic buildings across southern England.
