Calcium metabolism

Calcium metabolism is the set of mechanisms by which the body maintains adequate levels of calcium ions in the blood and tissues. Calcium is essential for bone structure, muscle contraction, neurotransmission, blood clotting, and intracellular signalling. About 99% of the body's roughly 1 kg of calcium is stored in the skeleton as hydroxyapatite; the remaining 1% circulates in blood and extracellular fluid, where its concentration is tightly regulated, normally around 8.5–10.5 mg/dL in total serum calcium.

Regulation

Three hormones dominate regulation. Parathyroid hormone (PTH), secreted by the parathyroid glands in response to low blood calcium, raises calcium by increasing bone resorption, renal reabsorption, and activating vitamin D. Calcitriol (active vitamin D) increases intestinal absorption of calcium and phosphate. Calcitonin, from the thyroid's C cells, mildly lowers calcium, though its role in adult humans is minor.

Compartments

Calcium enters the body through the diet, absorbed in the intestine, and leaves through urine, faeces, and sweat. Bone is in constant turnover: osteoblasts deposit calcium during formation and osteoclasts release it during resorption, exchanging roughly 500 mg per day in adults.

Disorders

Abnormally low blood calcium (hypocalcemia) can cause tetany, paresthesia, and seizures, and may result from hypoparathyroidism or vitamin D deficiency. Hypercalcemia causes fatigue, kidney stones, and confusion, most often from primary hyperparathyroidism or malignancy. Chronic imbalance contributes to osteoporosis and rickets.
