Memetics

Memetics is the study of information and culture based on an analogy with Darwinian evolution. It proposes that ideas, behaviours and styles — called memes — spread from person to person within a culture in a manner analogous to genes, undergoing variation, selection and replication. The field takes its name from the "meme", coined by Richard Dawkins in his 1976 book The Selfish Gene.

History

Dawkins coined "meme" from the Greek mimeme ("imitated thing"), shortening it to echo "gene". Examples he gave included tunes, catchphrases, fashions and religious ideas. The term was taken up by writers such as Daniel Dennett, who used memes in his theory of consciousness, and Susan Blackmore, whose 1999 book The Meme Machine developed the idea that humans are hosts for meme replication. The Journal of Memetics was published from 1997 to 2005.

Concepts

Memeticists argue that memes, like genes, vary in their "fitness": ideas that are memorable, simple and emotionally salient tend to be copied more faithfully and widely. Groups of co-adapted memes are sometimes called memeplexes. The theory has been applied to the spread of religions, fads, technologies and, with the rise of social media, internet memes.

Criticism

Critics object that the gene–meme analogy lacks a clear unit of cultural replication, that cultural transmission is often intentional and "Lamarckian" rather than blind, and that memetics has produced few testable predictions. The field declined as an academic discipline in the 2000s, though the meme concept remains influential in popular discussion of online culture.
