A soviet (Russian: совет, sovet, literally "council") was a workers' council that emerged during the Russian Revolutions and became the basic unit of government in the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics. The word also gave the Soviet Union its name.

Origins

Soviets first appeared during the Russian Revolution of 1905, when strike committees in Ivanovo and St. Petersburg organised themselves as councils of workers' deputies. The St. Petersburg Soviet, briefly chaired by Leon Trotsky, coordinated strikes and published the newspaper Izvestia.

The revolutions of 1917

After the February Revolution of 1917, soviets of workers', soldiers' and peasants' deputies sprang up across the Russian Empire, most importantly the Petrograd Soviet. Its Order No. 1 asserted soldiers' allegiance to the soviet over the Provisional Government, creating a situation of "dual power" (dvoevlastie). Under the slogan "All Power to the Soviets!", the Bolsheviks won majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow soviets in the autumn of 1917 and seized power in the October Revolution in the name of the Second All-Russian Congress of Soviets.

Soviets in the USSR

In the Soviet state, soviets were formally the organs of government at every level, from village and factory soviets up to the Supreme Soviet of the USSR. Elections were nominally universal, but in practice candidates were vetted by the Communist Party, and the soviets largely ratified decisions taken elsewhere. The 1936 and 1977 constitutions described the USSR as a state of "soviets of people's deputies".

Elsewhere

Workers' councils modelled on the soviet appeared in Germany (Räte) during the revolution of 1918–19, in Hungary in 1919 and 1956, and in other revolutionary situations; the English word "soviet" is sometimes used generically for such councils.
