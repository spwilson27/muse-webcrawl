Old Norse literature refers to the vernacular literature of Scandinavia written in Old Norse during the Middle Ages, overwhelmingly preserved in Iceland. Although composed in writing mainly between the 12th and 14th centuries, much of it drew on older oral traditions reaching back to the Viking Age. It comprises the sagas, Eddic and skaldic poetry, and a body of learned, religious, and legal writing.

Eddic poetry

The poems of the Poetic Edda, preserved chiefly in the Codex Regius manuscript (c. 1270), include mythological poems such as Völuspá (the prophecy of a seeress), Hávamál (sayings attributed to Odin), and Þrymskviða, as well as heroic lays about Sigurd the dragon-slayer, Brynhild, and Attila. Their terse alliterative verse preserves much of what is known of Norse mythology.

Skaldic poetry

Skaldic verse, composed by named court poets (skalds) from the 9th century onward, used intricate metres such as dróttkvætt and dense kennings — metaphorical compound expressions. Skaldic stanzas are embedded throughout the sagas and treatises as historical evidence, and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda (c. 1220) was written as a handbook explaining this poetic tradition.

The sagas

The sagas are prose narratives unique to medieval Iceland. The Íslendingasögur (Sagas of Icelanders), such as Njáls saga, Egils saga, and Laxdæla saga, recount the lives, feuds, and voyages of settlers of the 9th–11th centuries in a laconic, realistic style. The konungasögur (kings' sagas), including Snorri's Heimskringla, chronicle Scandinavian kings; the fornaldarsögur ("sagas of ancient times") are legendary tales of heroes in a mythic past. There are also bishops' sagas, chivalric sagas (riddarasögur), and contemporary sagas such as the Sturlunga compilation.

Legacy

Old Norse literature shaped modern fantasy: J. R. R. Tolkien drew heavily on the Eddas and sagas, and the literature remains central to Icelandic national identity. Manuscripts are preserved chiefly by the Árni Magnússon Institute in Reykjavík and the Royal Library in Copenhagen.
