Fjord

A fjord or fiord is a long, narrow sea inlet with steep sides or cliffs, created by a glacier. Fjords exist on the coasts of Antarctica, the Arctic and surrounding landmasses, and are found where past or present glaciation extended below current sea level.

Formation

A fjord forms when a glacier cuts a U-shaped valley by ice segregation and abrasion of the bedrock. When the glacier melts, the valley is flooded by the sea. Many fjords are far deeper than the adjacent sea — Norway's Sognefjord reaches 1,308 metres below sea level — because glaciers can erode well below the waterline, and they often have a shallow sill or threshold at their mouth where the ice met greater resistance.

Distribution

The longest fjords are in Greenland, Norway, Chile, Canada, Alaska and New Zealand. Norway's coastline, famous for fjords such as Geirangerfjord and Nærøyfjord — both UNESCO World Heritage Sites — would be far shorter if the fjords were not counted.

Freshwater fjords

Some inland glacial lakes in steep valleys are also called fjords, including Norwegian lakes such as Mjøsa in some usages, and the fjord-lakes of British Columbia.

Ecology and human use

Fjord waters, with their mix of fresh surface water and saline deep water, support distinctive ecosystems and important fisheries. Fjords provide sheltered harbours and anchorages, and their scenery is a major draw for tourism, while their deep, calm waters are used for aquaculture and, in Norway, for hydroelectric power development in surrounding valleys.
