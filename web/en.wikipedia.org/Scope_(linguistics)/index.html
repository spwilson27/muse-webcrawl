Scope (linguistics)

In semantics, linguistics and philosophy, the scope of a quantifier or other logical operator is the part of a sentence or formula that it applies to. An expression takes scope over another expression when its interpretation determines, or is determined by, the interpretation of the other.

Quantifier scope

Quantifier scope is a classic source of ambiguity. A sentence such as "Every student read a book" has two readings: one in which a single book was read by all students (the universal quantifier takes wide scope over the existential), and one in which each student may have read a different book (the existential takes wide scope). Theories of logical form explain such readings through operations such as quantifier raising.

Scope and negation

The interaction of negation with quantifiers and modals also produces scope ambiguities. "All the students didn't pass" can mean that none of them passed or that not all of them passed, depending on whether negation takes scope over or under the universal quantifier.

Scope in formal semantics

In formal semantics, scope relations are typically represented at a level of syntactic structure where a quantifier c-commands the material in its scope. Scope is also central to the analysis of intensional contexts, where substitution of co-referential expressions can fail, a phenomenon known since Frege and discussed extensively by Quine.
