Slalom skiing

Slalom is an alpine skiing and alpine snowboarding discipline involving skiing between poles or gates. These are spaced more closely than in giant slalom, super giant slalom or downhill, necessitating shorter and quicker turns. Internationally, the sport is contested at the FIS Alpine World Ski Championships and in the Winter Olympics. The term "slalom" comes from the Morgedal/Seljord dialect of Norwegian slalåm: sla, meaning "slightly inclining hillside," and låm, meaning "track after skis."

Course and rules

A slalom course is set with alternating red and blue gates, each consisting of two poles between which the skier must pass with both ski tips and feet. Men's courses typically have 55 to 75 gates and women's 45 to 65, over a vertical drop of roughly 180 to 220 metres for men and 140 to 200 metres for women. Competition consists of two runs on different courses, with the times combined; the fastest total wins, and missing a gate results in disqualification unless the skier hikes back up to pass it correctly. Slalom is the slowest of the alpine disciplines in absolute speed but the fastest in terms of turn frequency.

Technique and equipment

Slalom racers strike the flexible spring-loaded poles with their shins, hands and poles to clear the racing line — a technique called cross-blocking — and wear protective equipment including shin guards, hand guards and face protection. Slalom skis are the shortest of the alpine racing skis, with a tight turn radius, allowing the rapid edge-to-edge transitions the discipline requires. Modern technique emphasizes carved, skidded turns with strong angulation and pressure control on steep, often artificially hardened or icy surfaces.

History

Slalom was developed in Norway in the nineteenth century as a variation of Telemark racing near Oslo and in Morgedal. Organized alpine slalom racing took shape in the Alps in the 1920s under Arnold Lunn, who set the first slalom race at Mürren in 1922, and the discipline entered the Winter Olympics at Garmisch-Partenkirchen in 1936 as part of the combined event, becoming a standalone Olympic medal event in 1948. Legendary slalom racers include Ingemar Stenmark of Sweden, whose 86 World Cup victories, most of them in slalom and giant slalom, stood as a record for decades.
