Zuni (Zuni: Shiwiʼma) is a language of the Zuni people, an indigenous people of the southwestern United States. The language has no known relatives and is classified as a language isolate. It is spoken primarily in the Zuni Pueblo in western New Mexico, with additional speakers elsewhere.

Unlike many endangered indigenous languages of the Americas, Zuni is still acquired by children and used in everyday life; a majority of the Zuni population speaks it. Nevertheless, the language is considered vulnerable, particularly as English has become the dominant language of instruction and media.

Classification

Zuni has been proposed as part of several larger groupings — most notably Edward Sapir's Penutian hypothesis — but none has been demonstrated to general acceptance, and Zuni is conventionally treated as an isolate. Contact with neighbouring Keresan, Tanoan, Hopi and Athabaskan languages has produced loanwords and areal features, including the absence of initial vowels being resolved by prothetic glottal stops in some forms.

Writing system

Zuni uses a Latin-based practical orthography developed by linguists and the Zuni tribe, employing letters such as ʼ for the glottal stop and doubled vowels for length. The Zuni Public School District and tribal programmes teach the language, and materials including a Zuni–English dictionary have been published.
