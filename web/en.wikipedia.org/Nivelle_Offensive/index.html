Nivelle Offensive

Date16 April – 9 May 1917
LocationNorthern France
ResultGerman defensive success
BelligerentsFrance, Russia vs. German Empire

The Nivelle Offensive (April–May 1917) was a Franco-British operation on the Western Front in the First World War, named after its planner, General Robert Nivelle, commander-in-chief of the French armies. Nivelle promised a decisive breakthrough within 48 hours using massive artillery preparation and creeping barrages.

Course of the battle

The main French assault began on 16 April 1917 along the Chemin des Dames ridge between Soissons and Reims. German forces, warned by captured plans and a compromised timetable, had withdrawn to the fortified Hindenburg Line and prepared elastic defences in depth. French gains were limited and casualties were heavy, commonly estimated at well over 100,000 in the first days.

Mutinies and aftermath

The failure of the offensive and the scale of losses triggered widespread mutinies in the French Army in the spring of 1917, in which soldiers refused orders to attack while generally holding defensive positions. Nivelle was replaced by Philippe Pétain, who restored morale through improved leave, rest, and limited offensives. The British diversionary attack at Arras, including the Canadian victory at Vimy Ridge, is generally judged more successful than the main French effort.
