Carcinisation

In evolutionary biology, carcinisation is an example of convergent evolution in which a crustacean evolves into a crab-like form from a non-crab-like form. The term was introduced by L. A. Borradaile, who described it as "one of the many attempts of Nature to evolve a crab". Carcinisation has occurred independently in several groups of decapod crustaceans, most strikingly among the king crabs, which evolved from hermit crab ancestors, and among porcelain crabs, which are more closely related to squat lobsters than to true crabs.

Features of the crab-like form

Carcinisation involves a characteristic suite of changes: the carapace broadens and flattens; the abdomen (pleon) shortens, curves and folds flat beneath the cephalothorax, losing its role in swimming; the fifth pair of walking legs is often reduced; and the body becomes wide and armoured. The result trades the elongate, muscular tail of a shrimp- or lobster-shaped ancestor for a compact, protected body plan suited to walking on the sea floor and sheltering in crevices.

Examples

True crabs (Brachyura) are the model. King crabs (Lithodidae) evolved from hermit-crab-like ancestors — their asymmetrical abdomen, inherited from life in a borrowed shell, betrays this ancestry — and the hairy stone crab shows an intermediate condition. Porcelain crabs (Porcellanidae) are small, flattened anomurans that mimic true crabs. The process also has a documented reverse: some lineages have "decarcinised", re-elongating the body.

Why crabs?

Proposed advantages of the crab form include better protection of the vulnerable abdomen, improved manoeuvrability in all directions (including the sideways walk of true crabs), freed claws for feeding and defence, and access to new habitats such as rocky shores. The phenomenon has attracted wide popular attention as an apparent case of evolution repeatedly arriving at the same solution — sometimes summarised in the internet-era quip that "everything becomes a crab" — while researchers debate how many independent origins there have been and whether carcinisation is a single phenomenon or several parallel ones.
