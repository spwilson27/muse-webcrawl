Verdun

Verdun is a commune in the Meuse department in Grand Est in north-eastern France. It is the largest city in Meuse, although not the prefecture, and lies on the river Meuse. The city is chiefly known as the site of the Battle of Verdun in 1916, the longest battle of the First World War.

History

Verdun was founded by the Gauls and was an important Roman town (Verdunum). The Treaty of Verdun of 843, concluded here, divided the Carolingian Empire of Charlemagne's heirs into three kingdoms, a landmark in the emergence of France and Germany. A fortress city on France's eastern frontier, Verdun was ringed with forts, including Fort Douaumont and Fort Vaux.

Battle of Verdun

From 21 February to 18 December 1916 the German army attempted to bleed France white at Verdun. Some 300 days of fighting caused roughly 700,000 or more casualties. The French under Philippe Pétain held the city along the supply route later named the Voie Sacrée. Verdun became the enduring symbol of French determination — "Ils ne passeront pas" ("They shall not pass").

Memorials

The battlefield preserves cratered ground, the Douaumont Ossuary (containing the remains of some 130,000 unidentified soldiers), and the Verdun Memorial museum. In 1984, President Mitterrand and Chancellor Kohl famously held hands at Douaumont in a gesture of Franco-German reconciliation.
