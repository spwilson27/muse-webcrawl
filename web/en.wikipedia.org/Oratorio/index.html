Oratorio

Oratorio
Origins: Early 17th century, Italy
Forces: Soloists, chorus and orchestra
Typical subjects: Biblical narratives

An oratorio is a large musical composition for orchestra, choir and soloists. Unlike an opera, an oratorio is performed in concert without scenery, costumes or dramatic action. Like an opera, it includes the use of a choir, soloists, ensembles, various distinguishable characters and arias, often linked by narrative recitative. Oratorios traditionally take sacred subjects, most often stories from the Bible.

History

The oratorio arose in Rome in the early 17th century, growing out of the musical devotions held in the prayer halls (oratori) of St Philip Neri's Congregation of the Oratory, which give the genre its name. Early examples include Cavalieri's Rappresentatione di Anima, et di Corpo (1600). Giacomo Carissimi established the characteristic Latin oratorio in the mid-1600s.

The genre reached its most celebrated form in the English oratorios of George Frideric Handel, including Messiah (1741), Samson and Israel in Egypt. Haydn's The Creation (1798) and Mendelssohn's Elijah (1846) extended the tradition into the following centuries.

Structure

A typical oratorio is divided into two or three parts or acts. It alternates choruses, solo arias and ensembles with recitative that advances the narrative. A narrator (sometimes called the testo or evangelist, as in Bach's Passions) may carry the storyline. Related sacred forms include the Passion setting and the Italian tradition of the oratorio volgare.
