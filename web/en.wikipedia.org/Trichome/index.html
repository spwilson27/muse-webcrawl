Trichome

Trichomes (from the Greek tríchōma, "hair") are fine outgrowths or appendages on plants, algae, lichens, and certain protists. They are of diverse structure and function. Examples include hairs, glandular hairs, scales, and papillae. A plant covered in dense trichomes may be described as "pubescent" or "hairy".

Types

Trichomes vary considerably in morphology. They may be unicellular or multicellular, branched or unbranched, and glandular or non-glandular. Glandular trichomes secrete substances such as oils, resins, or toxins, while non-glandular trichomes serve primarily protective roles. Stinging hairs, such as those of the stinging nettle, inject irritating compounds into animals that touch them.

Functions

Trichomes serve many adaptive functions. They can deter herbivores physically or chemically, reduce water loss by trapping a layer of still air near the leaf surface, reflect excess sunlight, and protect against frost. In carnivorous plants, trichomes may help trap or digest prey. In some species, such as soybean, trichomes affect the plant's resistance to insect pests.

Development

In the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, trichome development is controlled by a well-studied network of transcription factors, including GLABRA1, GLABRA3 and TRANSPARENT TESTA GLABRA1, which form an activator complex, opposed by small single-repeat proteins that inhibit trichome formation in neighbouring cells.

Economic importance

Several commercially important substances are produced in glandular trichomes, including the cannabinoids and terpenes of cannabis, the essential oils of mint and lavender, and artemisinin from Artemisia annua. Cotton fibres are extremely elongated single-celled trichomes arising from the seed coat.
