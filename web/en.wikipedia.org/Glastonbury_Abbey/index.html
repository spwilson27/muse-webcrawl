Glastonbury Abbey

Glastonbury Abbey was a monastery in Glastonbury, Somerset, England, once one of the richest and most powerful abbeys in the kingdom. Founded in the 7th century and enlarged in the 10th century by Abbot Dunstan, the abbey church was largely destroyed by fire in 1184 and rebuilt in the Early English Gothic style. The abbey was suppressed in 1539 during the Dissolution of the Monasteries under Henry VIII, and its last abbot, Richard Whiting, was executed on Glastonbury Tor.

Legends

Glastonbury is enveloped in legend. Medieval tradition claimed the site was founded by Joseph of Arimathea and associated it with the Holy Grail and with Avalon, the resting place of King Arthur. In 1191 the monks announced the discovery of the grave of Arthur and Guinevere, marked by a lead cross — an announcement now generally regarded as a fundraising fabrication following the fire. The Glastonbury Thorn, a hawthorn said to descend from Joseph's staff, is another famous tradition.

History

Under a succession of powerful abbots the community amassed vast estates across the West Country. The surviving buildings include the Abbot's Kitchen (14th century), one of the best-preserved medieval kitchens in Europe, the gatehouse, and the ruins of the great church and Lady Chapel. After the Dissolution, the site was quarried for stone and passed through private hands; it was bought in 1908 for the Church of England and is now held by the Diocese of Bath and Wells.

Today

The ruins, set in 36 acres of parkland, are a Grade I listed monument and a place of pilgrimage for Anglican, Catholic, and Orthodox Christians, as well as a focus for the town's wider Arthurian and New Age associations. Annual pilgrimages are held each summer.
