Time dilation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Minkowski diagram illustrating time dilation between moving reference frames]

Time dilation is the difference in elapsed time as measured by two clocks, either because of a relative velocity between them (special relativistic time dilation) or a difference in gravitational potential between their locations (gravitational time dilation). After compensating for varying signal delays due to the changing distance between an observer and a moving clock (the Doppler effect), the observer will measure the moving clock as ticking slower than a clock at rest in the observer's own reference frame.

Velocity time dilation

In special relativity, a clock moving relative to an observer ticks more slowly. The relationship is given by the Lorentz factor:

Δt′ = Δt / √(1 − v²/c²)

where Δt is the time interval measured in the rest frame, v is the relative velocity, and c is the speed of light. The effect is negligible at everyday speeds but becomes dramatic as v approaches c. It has been confirmed by experiments with atomic clocks flown around the world (the Hafele–Keating experiment of 1971) and by the observed lifetimes of fast-moving muons produced by cosmic rays, which survive far longer than their rest-frame half-lives would allow because their internal clocks run slow relative to Earth.

Gravitational time dilation

General relativity predicts that clocks deeper in a gravitational well tick more slowly than clocks higher up. This was measured directly by the Pound–Rebka experiment (1959) using the Mössbauer effect over a height of only 22.5 metres, and it is a daily engineering concern: the clocks aboard GPS satellites run faster than ground clocks by a net of about 38 microseconds per day once both effects are combined, and the system would accumulate navigational errors of roughly 10 km per day if the corrections were not applied.

The twin paradox

The famous twin paradox imagines one twin travelling at relativistic speed while the other stays on Earth; on reunion the travelling twin is younger. The asymmetry — the travelling twin must accelerate to turn around — resolves the apparent paradox within special relativity.
