Vladimir Yakovlevich Propp (Russian: Владимир Яковлевич Пропп; 29 April 1895 – 22 August 1970) was a Soviet folklorist and scholar who analysed the basic structural components of Russian folk tales to identify their simplest irreducible narrative elements. His work laid the foundations of structural narratology.

Morphology of the Folktale

Propp's best-known work, Morphology of the Folktale (Morfologija skazki), was published in Russian in 1928 but became widely known in the West only after its English translation in 1958. Studying a corpus of Russian fairy tales collected by Alexander Afanasyev, Propp argued that all the tales he examined could be reduced to a sequence of 31 narrative functions — events such as "absentation", "interdiction", "villainy", "departure", "the hero's reaction", "struggle", "victory" and "wedding" — performed by characters who fall into seven broad roles: hero, villain, donor, helper, princess (or sought-for person) and her father, dispatcher, and false hero.

Influence

Propp's method influenced structuralism and narratology, including the work of Claude Lévi-Strauss — who criticised Propp's formalism, provoking Propp's later reply — and later narrative theorists such as Tzvetan Todorov and A. J. Greimas. His scheme has been applied, and adapted, to film and popular fiction analysis, including screenwriting manuals.

Other works

His later books include Historical Roots of the Wonder Tale (1946), Russian Heroic Epic Poetry (1955), and Russian Agrarian Festivals (1963). Propp taught German philology and folklore at Leningrad University for most of his career.
