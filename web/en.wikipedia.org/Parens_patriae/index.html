Parens patriae

Parens patriae is Latin for "parent of the fatherland" (or "parent of the country"). In law, it is the doctrine that the state, acting as guardian, may intervene to protect those unable to protect themselves, such as children and incapacitated persons, and may litigate on behalf of the general welfare of its citizens.

Origins

The doctrine descends from English common law, where the King, through the Court of Chancery, held protective authority over wards and persons lacking legal capacity. It was received into the law of the United States and other common-law jurisdictions.

Applications

Parens patriae underlies juvenile courts and child-protection proceedings, where the state may remove children from neglectful homes or act in a child's best interests. U.S. state attorneys general also invoke the doctrine to sue on behalf of citizens collectively — for example in consumer protection, environmental, and antitrust actions, including the litigation that produced the tobacco Master Settlement Agreement of 1998.

Limits

Courts have held that a state cannot use parens patriae to sue the federal government on citizens' behalf, and the doctrine does not override constitutional rights of parents in ordinary circumstances.
