Lev Landau

Lev Davidovich Landau (Russian: Лев Дави́дович Ланда́у; 22 January 1908 – 1 April 1968) was a Soviet physicist who made fundamental contributions to many areas of theoretical physics. His accomplishments include the independent co-invention of the density matrix method in quantum mechanics, the quantum mechanical theory of diamagnetism, the theory of superfluidity, the theory of second-order phase transitions, the Ginzburg–Landau theory of superconductivity, the theory of Fermi liquid, the explanation of Landau damping in plasma physics, the Landau pole in quantum electrodynamics, the two-component theory of neutrinos, and the S-matrix theory of singularities. He received the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physics for his development of a mathematical theory of superfluidity that accounts for the properties of liquid helium II.

Life

Landau was born in Baku and graduated from Leningrad University at nineteen, having published his first papers as a teenager. On a Rockefeller fellowship he visited Europe and worked with Niels Bohr in Copenhagen, whom he considered his teacher. In 1932 he became head of the theoretical division of the Ukrainian Physico-Technical Institute in Kharkov, building a famous school of theoretical physics. In 1938, during the Great Purge, he was arrested for alleged anti-Soviet activity and spent a year in prison before Pyotr Kapitsa secured his release. He then worked at Kapitsa's Institute for Physical Problems in Moscow. In January 1962 a car accident left him gravely injured and ended his scientific career; he died in 1968.

Scientific work

Landau's theory of superfluidity (1941) explained liquid helium II through a spectrum of quasiparticle excitations — phonons and rotons. With Vitaly Ginzburg he formulated the phenomenological Ginzburg–Landau theory of superconductivity (1950), and his Fermi-liquid theory (1956–58) remains a cornerstone of condensed matter physics. With Evgeny Lifshitz he wrote the monumental ten-volume Course of Theoretical Physics, a standard training for generations of physicists. His "theoretical minimum" examinations were legendary for their difficulty; only 43 candidates passed them over three decades.

Legacy

The Landau Institute for Theoretical Physics in Chernogolovka bears his name, as do a lunar crater and the minor planet 2142 Landau. His ranking scale of physicists, and anecdotes of his sharp wit, remain part of physics folklore.
