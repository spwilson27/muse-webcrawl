History of Greenland

The history of Greenland is the story of successive waves of Inuit peoples arriving from the North American Arctic, of a medieval Norse settlement that flourished and vanished, and of Greenland's centuries as a Danish colony and its gradual movement toward self-government within the Kingdom of Denmark.

Early Inuit cultures

Paleo-Inuit peoples first settled northern Greenland around 2500 BCE, arriving from Canada. Successive cultures — Independence I and II, Saqqaq, and the Dorset culture — occupied the coasts for millennia, hunting seal, walrus and musk ox. The Thule culture, ancestors of the modern Inuit (Kalaallit), spread across Greenland from around 1200 CE, bringing dog sleds, large boats and sophisticated harpoon technology, and displaced or absorbed the Dorset.

Norse settlement

Around 985 CE Erik the Red, exiled from Iceland, founded Norse settlements in the south-west: the Eastern Settlement near modern Qaqortoq and the Western Settlement near modern Nuuk. At their height the settlements may have held several thousand people, farming cattle and sheep and exporting walrus ivory, narwhal tusks and furs. They accepted Norwegian overlordship in 1261 and later came under the Danish crown. Contact with Europe faded in the fifteenth century; the Western Settlement was abandoned by about 1350 and the Eastern Settlement disappeared by about 1450, probably owing to a cooling climate, soil erosion, conflict or competition with the Thule Inuit, and the collapse of the ivory trade.

Danish colonisation

In 1721 the missionary Hans Egede re-established European presence under Denmark–Norway, seeking both the lost Norse colonists and souls to convert; finding only Inuit, he founded Godthåb (now Nuuk). Greenland became a closed Danish trading colony administered by the Royal Greenland Trading Department, whose monopoly lasted until 1950. During the Second World War, with Denmark occupied by Germany, Greenland was protected by the United States, which built air bases there; the American Thule Air Base (now Pituffik Space Base) remains.

Home rule and self-government

In 1953 Greenland's colonial status ended and it became a county of Denmark, followed by rapid modernisation and urbanisation. Home rule was granted in 1979 after a referendum, giving Greenland its own parliament and government. In 1985 Greenland left the European Economic Community following a referendum, chiefly over fishing rights. The Self-Government Act of 2009 recognised Greenlanders as a people under international law, transferred further powers, made Greenlandic the official language, and set out a path by which Greenland may choose independence.
