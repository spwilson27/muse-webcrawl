Old Turkic alphabet

Old Turkic script
Type: Alphabet
Languages: Old Turkic
Period: 7th–13th centuries
Direction: Right-to-left

The Old Turkic alphabet (also called the Orkhon script, Orkhon–Yenisei script, or Turkic runes) was the alphabet used by the Göktürks and other early Turkic khanates during the 7th to 10th centuries to write Old Turkic. It takes its name from the Orkhon Valley in Mongolia, where the most famous inscriptions were discovered.

Discovery and decipherment

The script was deciphered in 1893 by the Danish linguist Vilhelm Thomsen, working from the Orkhon inscriptions, which include the monuments erected for the prince Kül Tigin (732 AD) and the emperor Bilge Khagan (735 AD). A rival expedition under Vasily Radlov published competing readings, and the decipherment race became a famous episode in Oriental studies.

Characteristics

The alphabet typically consists of about 38–40 signs. Most consonants have two forms, one used with front vowels and one with back vowels, reflecting Turkic vowel harmony. Writing normally runs right to left, letters are generally not joined, and a colon-like sign separates words. Regional variants include the Yenisei, Talas and Volga varieties.

Legacy

The angular shapes, adapted to carving in wood and stone, resemble Germanic runes, though no direct connection is established; the script probably derives ultimately from Aramaic via Sogdian intermediaries. The Old Hungarian (Székely) script shows notable similarities. Today the Orkhon script is a symbol of Turkic heritage and appears on coins, monuments and in revived usage.
