Holstein Friesian cattle

Holstein Friesian
Conservation status: Not at risk
Country of origin: Netherlands and Germany
Use: Dairy
Coat: Black and white (or red and white) piebald

Holstein Friesians are a breed of dairy cattle originating from the Dutch provinces of North Holland and Friesland and the Schleswig-Holstein region of northern Germany. They are the world's highest-producing dairy animals and the dominant breed in industrialised dairy production, found in more than 160 countries. The breed is known for its distinctive black-and-white (occasionally red-and-white) piebald markings and its exceptional milk yield.

History

The breed descends from cattle kept by Frisian and Batavian settlers in the Rhine delta about 2,000 years ago. Dutch breeders developed large, productive black-and-white cattle on the rich polders, and North German breeders in Holstein pursued similar stock. Cattle were exported to North America from the 1850s; American breeders, who called the breed "Holstein", bred almost exclusively for milk, while European "Friesian" lines retained more dual-purpose character. In the later 20th century the North American type was re-imported worldwide, and the two strains merged into one global breed.

Characteristics

Holsteins are large cattle: a mature cow weighs about 680–770 kg and stands roughly 147 cm at the shoulder. The typical coat pattern is a sharply defined black-and-white patchwork, unique to each animal; a recessive gene produces a red-and-white variant. Bulls can be aggressive and are managed with caution. The breed's frame is angular and "dairy" in type, with a large udder adapted to high production.

Production

Holstein Friesians produce more milk than any other breed: well-managed herds in North America and Europe average over 10,000 kg of milk per cow per year, and elite cows exceed 20,000 kg in a 305-day lactation. Record-holding cows have produced more than 35,000 kg in a year. The milk has a relatively modest butterfat content — typically around 3.7 per cent — lower than that of Jersey or Guernsey cattle, reflecting selection concentrated on volume. Intensive selection for yield, combined with artificial insemination and genomic testing, has brought concerns about fertility, longevity and inbreeding depression, which modern breeding programmes increasingly address with health and fitness traits.

Distribution and use

The breed dominates dairying in the United States, Canada, much of Europe, Israel, Japan, Australia and New Zealand, and its genetics are used to upgrade local cattle across Asia, Africa and Latin America. Holstein genetics account for the great majority of the world's recorded dairy cows, and the breed has profoundly shaped the global milk industry; spent cows also contribute substantially to beef production.
