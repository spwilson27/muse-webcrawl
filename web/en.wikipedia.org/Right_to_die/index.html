Right to die

The right to die is a concept based on the belief that individuals have a right to end their own lives or undergo voluntary euthanasia, grounded in bodily autonomy and freedom from unwanted medical treatment. Legal frameworks vary widely: some jurisdictions permit physician-assisted dying under strict conditions, while others prohibit any form of assisted death.

Definitions and distinctions

The debate distinguishes several practices: refusal or withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, which is broadly recognised in law; voluntary euthanasia, in which another person directly causes death at the patient's request; and assisted dying or assisted suicide, in which a patient is provided the means to end their own life, often restricted to those with terminal illness.

Legal status

Euthanasia or assisted dying is legal under specified conditions in countries including the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, Canada, Spain, New Zealand and Colombia, and in some states of the United States (beginning with Oregon's Death with Dignity Act of 1994) and Australia. Landmark cases, such as Karen Ann Quinlan and Nancy Cruzan in the United States, helped establish rights to refuse treatment, while cases involving Diane Pretty in the United Kingdom and Rodriguez and Carter in Canada tested claims to a positive right to assistance.

Arguments

Supporters emphasise autonomy, relief of suffering and the equivalence of refusing treatment and seeking assistance. Opponents raise concerns about the sanctity of life, the risk of coercion of vulnerable people, the "slippery slope" toward involuntary cases, and the role of physicians. Religious traditions differ in their teachings on the subject.
