Robert Jay Lifton (born 16 May 1926) is an American psychiatrist and author, chiefly known for his studies of the psychological causes and effects of wars and political violence and for his theory of thought reform. He was an early proponent of the techniques of psychohistory.

Work

Serving as an Air Force psychiatrist in Japan and Korea in the 1950s, Lifton interviewed American prisoners of war returning from China and Chinese refugees from the mainland. From this research came Thought Reform and the Psychology of Totalism (1961), which identified eight psychological themes of coercive persuasion or "brainwashing", including milieu control, the demand for purity, and the dispensing of existence; these criteria remain widely cited in the study of cults and coercive groups.

His later books include Death in Life: Survivors of Hiroshima (1967), which won the National Book Award; Home from the War (1973), on Vietnam veterans and what became post-traumatic stress disorder; and The Nazi Doctors (1986), which introduced the concept of "doubling" to explain how physicians participated in the Holocaust. Lifton has also written extensively on nuclear weapons, apocalyptic violence—including the Aum Shinrikyo cult—and climate change psychology.
