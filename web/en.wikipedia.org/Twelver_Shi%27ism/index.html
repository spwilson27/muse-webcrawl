Twelver Shi'ism (Ithna 'Ashariyya)

Twelver Shi'ism is the largest branch of Shia Islam, comprising the majority of Shia Muslims. Twelvers believe in twelve divinely ordained Imams, beginning with Ali and continuing through his descendants by Fatimah, whose line includes Hasan and Husayn. The twelfth, Muhammad al-Mahdi, is believed to be in occultation (ghayba) since 874 and to return as the Mahdi.

Doctrine

Core tenets include divine justice ('adl), prophethood, and the Imamate — sinless, divinely guided leadership of the community by the Imams — alongside monotheism and resurrection. The occultation is divided into the Minor Occultation (874–941), when four deputies communicated with the Hidden Imam, and the Major Occultation since. In the Imam's absence, jurists (marja' al-taqlid) provide religious guidance through ijtihad; the Usuli school predominates over the Akhbari.

History

Twelver identity crystallised in the 9th–10th centuries in Iraq and Iran. The Buyids patronised Shia scholarship; under the Safavids (from 1501) Twelver Shi'ism became Iran's state religion, shaping Iranian identity. Seminaries at Najaf and Qom became centres of learning. The 1979 Iranian Revolution, invoking guardianship of the jurist (velayat-e faqih) developed by Ruhollah Khomeini, gave Twelver doctrine a modern political form.

Practices

Distinctive practices include mourning for Husayn during Muharram after the Battle of Karbala, pilgrimage (ziyara) to shrines in Najaf, Karbala, Mashhad and Qom, temporary marriage (mut'a), and combining some daily prayers. Twelvers differ from Ismailis and Zaydis mainly over the succession and number of Imams.
