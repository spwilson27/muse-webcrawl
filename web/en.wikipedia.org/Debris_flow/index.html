Debris flow

A debris flow is a fast-moving form of mass wasting in which a slurry of water and loose material — soil, rock fragments, and organic matter — flows downslope under gravity, behaving much like wet concrete. Debris flows are among the most dangerous types of landslide: they can travel tens of kilometres per hour, bury settlements, and carry boulders many metres across.

Formation

Debris flows typically begin when intense rainfall, rapid snowmelt, or the failure of a natural dam saturates loose sediment on steep slopes, often in recently burned or deforested terrain. The mixture becomes fluidized and moves down gullies and stream channels, growing as it entrains more material. Volcanic eruptions can generate related flows called lahars.

Notable events

Debris flows from the Nevado del Ruiz eruption in Colombia destroyed the town of Armero in 1985, killing about 23,000 people. In December 1999, extreme rainfall triggered massive debris flows in Vargas State, Venezuela, killing tens of thousands. Recurrent flows also threaten communities on alluvial fans worldwide, from the Swiss and Japanese mountains to the San Gabriel Mountains of California.

Mitigation

Engineered defenses include check dams (sabo dams in Japan), debris basins, deflection walls, and flexible barrier nets, together with land-use zoning, rainfall monitoring, and early-warning systems in high-risk watersheds.
