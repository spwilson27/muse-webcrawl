Moons of Saturn

The moons of Saturn are numerous and diverse, ranging from tiny moonlets less than 1 kilometre across to the giant Titan, which is larger than the planet Mercury. As of 2025, Saturn has 274 confirmed moons with well-determined orbits — the most of any planet in the Solar System — following the announcement of 128 additional moons in March 2025. Only a small fraction have names; 24 are regular satellites in near-circular prograde orbits, while the rest are mostly small irregular satellites.

Discovery

Christiaan Huygens discovered Titan in 1655. Giovanni Domenico Cassini added Iapetus, Rhea, Tethys, and Dione between 1671 and 1684. William Herschel found Mimas and Enceladus in 1789. The irregular moon Phoebe, discovered photographically by William Henry Pickering in 1898, was the first irregular satellite found. The Cassini–Huygens spacecraft (2004–2017) revolutionised knowledge of the system and discovered several small moons.

Saturn's largest moons range from cratered Mimas to orange, hazy Titan.

Major moons

Titan is the largest moon, with a thick nitrogen atmosphere and lakes of liquid methane and ethane. Enceladus hides a global salty ocean beneath its icy crust and sprays water plumes from its south pole, making it a prime target in the search for life. Iapetus is two-toned, with one dark and one bright hemisphere. Mimas bears the giant Herschel crater, giving it a "Death Star" appearance. Rhea, Dione, and Tethys are icy mid-sized worlds.

Groups

The irregular satellites are divided into the Inuit, Norse, and Gallic groups based on their orbital inclinations and distances; the Norse group orbits retrograde and is thought to derive from the breakup of a larger captured body, with Phoebe as its largest member.
