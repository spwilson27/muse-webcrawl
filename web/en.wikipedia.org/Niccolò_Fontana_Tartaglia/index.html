Niccolò Fontana Tartaglia (c. 1499/1500 – 13 December 1557) was an Italian mathematician, engineer, and bookkeeper from the Republic of Venice. He is best known for discovering a method to solve cubic equations, which he disclosed in confidence to Gerolamo Cardano and whose subsequent publication by Cardano in the Ars Magna (1545) provoked one of the most famous priority disputes in the history of mathematics. "Tartaglia" ("the stammerer") was a nickname arising from a speech impediment caused by wounds suffered as a boy.

Life

Born Niccolò Fontana in Brescia, he was left poor by his father's early death. During the French sack of Brescia in 1512, the young Niccolò survived by hiding in the cathedral but received severe sabre wounds to his jaw and palate that left him with a lifelong stammer. Largely self-taught in mathematics, he worked as a teacher of abacus mathematics in Verona and Venice, earning a reputation in public mathematical contests.

The cubic equation

In 1535 Tartaglia won a celebrated contest against Antonio Maria Fiore by solving cubic equations of the form x³ + px = q, a method he had discovered that Fiore could not match. Cardano persuaded Tartaglia to reveal the method in 1539 under an oath of secrecy, but published it in 1545 after learning that Scipione del Ferro had found a solution earlier. Tartaglia bitterly protested; the dispute culminated in a public contest in Milan in 1548 between Tartaglia and Cardano's student Lodovico Ferrari, which Tartaglia lost. The general solution is still known as the Cardano–Tartaglia formula.

Other work

His Nova scientia (1537) was the first published work applying mathematics to artillery, founding the science of ballistics and (incorrectly, but influentially) analysing projectile trajectories. Tartaglia produced the first Italian translation of Euclid's Elements (1543), published an edition of Archimedes, studied the binomial coefficients arranged in "Tartaglia's triangle" (Pascal's triangle), and gave a formula for the volume of a tetrahedron in terms of its edges (Tartaglia's formula). His General trattato di numeri et misure (1556–60) was a major encyclopaedia of practical arithmetic.
