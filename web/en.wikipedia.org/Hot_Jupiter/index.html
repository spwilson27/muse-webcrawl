Hot Jupiter

A hot Jupiter is a class of exoplanet similar in mass to Jupiter but orbiting extremely close to its star — typically within 0.1 astronomical units, with periods of a few days. Their proximity heats them to temperatures of 1,000 K or more and makes them easy to detect, so they dominated early exoplanet discoveries.

Discovery

The first exoplanet found around a Sun-like star, 51 Pegasi b (announced in 1995), was a hot Jupiter — a shock, since giant planets were expected to form only far from their stars, beyond the frost line where ices are abundant. Their existence forced theorists to develop models of planetary migration, in which a giant forms far out and spirals inward through the protoplanetary disc, or is thrown inward by gravitational scattering and later circularised by tides.

Characteristics

Hot Jupiters are often "inflated," with radii larger than Jupiter's despite similar mass, owing to their intense irradiation. Many orbit in planes misaligned with their star's spin, evidence for violent dynamical histories. The most extreme examples, ultra-hot Jupiters such as KELT-9b, exceed 4,000 K on their daysides, hot enough to vaporise iron. Atmospheric studies of hot Jupiters by transit spectroscopy have detected water, carbon monoxide, and escaping hydrogen.
