The sociology of religion is the study of the beliefs, practices, and organizational forms of religion using the tools and methods of sociology. It examines religion's role in social life — how it creates solidarity, legitimates authority, shapes values, and changes with the rest of society — without adjudicating the truth of religious claims.

Classical theorists

Émile Durkheim, in The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life (1912), defined religion as a unified system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things that unites adherents into a moral community, arguing that religious ritual generates social cohesion. Max Weber studied how religious ideas shape economic and political conduct, most famously in The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1905), and compared the world religions' paths of rationalization. Karl Marx treated religion as an expression of social suffering and a legitimating ideology — the "opium of the people" — expected to wither as its social causes disappeared.

Key concepts

Sociologists distinguish churches, denominations, sects, and new religious movements along a continuum of tension with surrounding society; analyse ritual, religious experience, and conversion; and study religious economies and fundamentalism. The secularization thesis — that modernization erodes religion's social authority — was long dominant but has been revised as religion has remained publicly influential in many societies; scholars now speak of desecularization, privatization, and multiple modernities.

Contemporary research

Current work addresses religion and migration, religious nationalism, the rise of the religiously unaffiliated ("nones"), religion in digital spaces, and the relationship between religiosity and health, fertility, and politics, using international survey programmes and comparative data.
