Lake Baikal (Russian: озеро Байкал) is a rift lake in southern Siberia, Russia. It is the deepest lake in the world, with a maximum depth of 1,642 metres, and the largest freshwater lake by volume, holding roughly a fifth of the world's fresh surface water — more than all the North American Great Lakes combined. Formed in an active continental rift zone some 25–30 million years ago, it is also the oldest lake in existence, and was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1996.

Geography and geology

The crescent-shaped lake stretches 636 km from southwest to northeast, fed by more than 300 rivers and streams, of which the largest is the Selenga. Its only outflow is the Angara River near Listvyanka. The Baikal Rift Zone is still widening by about 2 cm per year, and the lake floor holds sediments up to 7 km thick. Olkhon Island, the largest island in the lake, is a centre of Buryat shamanic tradition.

Biodiversity

Isolation over millions of years has made Baikal an evolutionary hotspot: of its more than 2,500 described species and subspecies, a large majority are found nowhere else. Notable endemics include the nerpa (Pusa sibirica), one of the only exclusively freshwater seal species; the omul, a whitefish central to local fisheries; the transparent, viviparous golomyanka oilfish of the deep zone; and vast beds of freshwater sponges. The tiny crustacean Epischura baikalensis filters the water and helps keep Baikal exceptionally clear.

Environmental concerns

A pulp and paper mill at Baikalsk, long the focus of protest, closed in 2013. Current threats include shoreline algal blooms fed by inadequately treated sewage, tourism pressure, and climate-driven warming of the surface waters, which may favour non-endemic algae over the endemic diatoms adapted to the lake's ice-covered winters.
