Rapid sequence induction

Rapid sequence induction (RSI) is a special process for endotracheal intubation that is used where the patient is at a high risk of pulmonary aspiration — for example in emergency surgery, in patients who are not fasted, or in critical illness. It differs from induction of general anaesthesia in that it aims to secure the airway with a cuffed tube in the shortest possible time, minimising the interval during which the airway is unprotected.

Technique

The classic sequence involves thorough preoxygenation; administration of a predetermined dose of an intravenous induction agent immediately followed by a rapidly acting neuromuscular blocking drug (historically suxamethonium, now often rocuronium); application of cricoid pressure (Sellick manoeuvre) by an assistant; avoidance of bag-mask ventilation; and intubation with a cuffed endotracheal tube as soon as muscle relaxation is achieved. Correct tube placement is confirmed, typically by capnography, before cricoid pressure is released.

Indications

RSI is standard for emergency intubation in the emergency department and intensive care, for patients with bowel obstruction or recent food intake, in late pregnancy, and in trauma. Variants described as "modified RSI" permit gentle mask ventilation or omit cricoid pressure, whose benefit remains debated.

Complications

Potential complications include hypoxia during the apnoeic period, hypotension from induction drugs, failed intubation, and aspiration if the technique fails. A rehearsed failed-intubation plan is considered an essential part of the procedure.
