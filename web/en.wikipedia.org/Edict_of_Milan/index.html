Edict of Milan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

DateFebruary 313 AD
AuthorsConstantine I and Licinius
PurposeReligious toleration in the Roman Empire

The Edict of Milan was the February 313 AD agreement between the Roman emperors Constantine the Great and Licinius, reached when they met in Milan (Mediolanum), to treat Christians benevolently within the Roman Empire. It proclaimed religious tolerance for all religions, effectively ending the persecution of Christians begun under Diocletian.

Terms

The agreement was not a single surviving edict but a policy promulgated through letters to provincial governors, preserved by Lactantius and Eusebius. It granted everyone freedom to follow the religion of their choice, ordered that confiscated Christian meeting places and property be restored without charge, and extended the same restitution to other religions. It built on Galerius's Edict of Toleration of 311, which had first legalised Christianity in the East.

Significance

The edict transformed the position of Christianity, allowing the Church to own property, organise openly and receive imperial patronage. Within decades, under Theodosius I, Christianity became the state religion of the empire. The Edict of Milan is often cited as a landmark in the history of religious freedom, though it did not establish a modern separation of church and state: Constantine continued to involve himself in church affairs, presiding over councils and favouring the Christian God.
