British Expeditionary Force (World War I)

British Expeditionary Force
Active: August 1914 – 1918 (as designation)
Country: United Kingdom / British Empire
First commander: Field Marshal Sir John French
Nickname: The "Old Contemptibles"

The British Expeditionary Force (BEF) was the name given to the British Army forces sent to France at the outbreak of the First World War in August 1914, and thereafter to the army on the Western Front generally. Plans for such a force had been developed under Secretary of State for War Richard Haldane and Director of Military Operations Henry Wilson, in cooperation with the French, in the years before the war.

The force of 1914

The original BEF consisted of six infantry divisions and a cavalry division, some 120,000–150,000 men, almost all long-service regulars and reservists of the pre-war professional army. Commanded by Field Marshal Sir John French, it crossed to France in August 1914 and first engaged the German First Army at the Battle of Mons on 23 August. Forced to retreat by superior numbers, it fought a rearguard action at Le Cateau and then retreated almost to Paris before advancing again in the Allied victory on the Marne in September.

In the "Race to the Sea" and the First Battle of Ypres (October–November 1914) the BEF held the northern flank of the Allied line; the regular army suffered such heavy losses that it effectively ceased to exist as it had been. Its survivors proudly adopted the name "Old Contemptibles", from a supposed German order of the day by Kaiser Wilhelm II calling the force a "contemptible little army" — a phrase of doubtful authenticity but lasting fame.

Expansion and after

As the war continued, the force in France grew to include Kitchener's volunteer New Armies, Territorial divisions and large contingents from across the British Empire. The designation "BEF" remained in use for the whole British force on the Western Front, commanded after December 1915 by Sir Douglas Haig. A second, separate force of the same name was sent to France in 1939–40 in the Second World War and evacuated from Dunkirk.
