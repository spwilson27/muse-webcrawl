Guest star (astronomy)

Chinese court astronomers recorded "guest stars" for more than two thousand years.

In astronomy, a guest star is a star which has suddenly appeared in a place where no star had recently been observed and which becomes invisible again after some time. The term is a calque of the Chinese kèxīng (客星), used in ancient Chinese astronomical records for transient celestial objects. Guest stars are now understood to have been mostly supernovae, but the category also included novae and possibly comets whose motion was not noticed.

Historical records

Chinese, Korean, Japanese and Arab astronomers recorded guest stars from at least 185 CE, when a bright object appeared near Alpha Centauri and is now identified as the supernova remnant RCW 86 — the earliest recorded supernova. Other famous guest stars include the events of 1006 (the brightest supernova in recorded history), 1054 (which produced the Crab Nebula), 1181, 1572 (Tycho's supernova) and 1604 (Kepler's supernova). These records remain valuable to modern astrophysics for dating supernova remnants and studying long-term changes in variable stars.
