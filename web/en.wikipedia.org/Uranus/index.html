Uranus

Uranus is the seventh planet from the Sun. It is a gaseous cyan-coloured ice giant. Most of the planet is made of water, ammonia, and methane in a supercritical phase of matter, which astronomy calls "ice" or volatiles. The planet's atmosphere has a complex layered cloud structure and has the lowest minimum temperature (49 K, −224 °C) of all the Solar System's planets. It has a marked axial tilt of 97.77° with a retrograde rotation period of 17 hours and 14 minutes. This means that in an 84-Earth-year orbital period around the Sun, its poles get around 42 years of continuous sunlight, followed by 42 years of continuous darkness.

Uranus has the third-largest planetary radius and the fourth-largest planetary mass in the Solar System. It has a planetary ring system and numerous moons. It is similar in composition to Neptune, and both have bulk chemical compositions which differ from those of the other two giant planets, Jupiter and Saturn. For this reason, scientists often classify Uranus and Neptune as "ice giants" to distinguish them from gas giants.

As with gas giants, ice giants lack a well-defined solid surface. Uranus's atmosphere is similar to Jupiter's and Saturn's in its primary composition of hydrogen and helium, but it contains more "ices" such as water, ammonia, and methane, along with traces of other hydrocarbons. One of the most striking features of Uranus is its extreme axial tilt: the planet rotates on its side, so its poles lie roughly where other planets' equators are.

Discovery and naming

Uranus was discovered on 13 March 1781 by the astronomer William Herschel, although it had been observed earlier and mistaken for a star. It was the first planet discovered with a telescope. Herschel originally wished to name it Georgium Sidus ("George's Star") after King George III; the name Uranus, after the Greek god of the sky and father of Cronus (Saturn), was proposed by Johann Elert Bode and eventually prevailed.

Orbit and rotation

Uranus orbits the Sun once every 84 Earth years at an average distance of about 19.2 astronomical units. Its rotational period (day) is about 17 hours 14 minutes, and its rotation is retrograde relative to most planets. Because of the 97.77° tilt, each pole experiences about 21 years of continuous sunlight and 21 years of darkness during the planet's long year, with additional seasonal extremes.

Internal structure

Models suggest Uranus consists of a rocky core of roughly 0.5 Earth masses, surrounded by a mantle of water, ammonia, and methane ices under extreme pressure, and an outer envelope of hydrogen and helium. Unlike Neptune, Uranus radiates little excess internal heat, a long-standing puzzle.

Moons and rings

Uranus has 28 known moons, named mainly after characters from the works of William Shakespeare and Alexander Pope. The five largest are Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania, and Oberon. The planet also has a system of 13 faint, narrow rings, discovered in 1977.

Exploration

The only spacecraft to visit Uranus is NASA's Voyager 2, which flew by in January 1986, returning images of the planet, its rings, and its moons, and discovering ten new moons. A dedicated Uranus orbiter has been recommended as a flagship priority for future decades.
