A proton is a stable subatomic particle, symbol p or p+, with a positive electric charge of +1 e (elementary charge). Its mass is slightly less than the mass of a neutron and 1,836 times the mass of an electron (the proton-to-electron mass ratio). Protons and neutrons, each with masses of approximately one atomic mass unit, are jointly referred to as "nucleons" (particles present in atomic nuclei).

One or more protons are present in the nucleus of every atom; they are a fundamental part of the nucleus. The number of protons in the nucleus is the defining property of an element, and is referred to as the atomic number (represented by the symbol Z). Since each element has a unique number of protons, each element has its own unique atomic number, which determines the chemical behaviour of the atom.

Structure

Protons are spin-1/2 fermions composed of three valence quarks: two up quarks of charge +2/3 e and one down quark of charge −1/3 e, bound together by the strong interaction mediated by gluons. Quantum chromodynamics shows that most of the proton's mass arises from the kinetic and binding energy of its constituent quarks and gluons rather than from the quarks' rest masses. The proton's charge radius is about 0.84 femtometres. The distribution of charge and magnetism inside the proton is mapped through electron-scattering experiments and deep inelastic scattering.

History

Ernest Rutherford is credited with the discovery of the proton. In experiments between 1917 and 1920 he showed that bombarding nitrogen with alpha particles produced hydrogen nuclei, and he proposed that the hydrogen nucleus is a fundamental particle—the proton, from the Greek πρῶτον (prōton), "first". Earlier, Eugen Goldstein's work with canal rays and Wilhelm Wien's measurements had suggested a positively charged particle of roughly the hydrogen mass.

Stability and occurrence

No decay of a free proton has ever been observed; experimental lower limits on its half-life exceed 1034 years, making it effectively stable and placing strong constraints on grand unified theories that predict proton decay. Protons are abundant in the universe: hydrogen, consisting of one proton and one electron, is the most common element. Accelerated proton beams are used in particle physics (the Large Hadron Collider collides protons), in proton therapy for cancer, and proton screens and NMR spectroscopy exploit the proton's magnetic moment.
