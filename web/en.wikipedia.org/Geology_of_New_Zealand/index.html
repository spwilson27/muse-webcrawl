The geology of New Zealand is shaped by its position astride the boundary between the Pacific Plate and the Australian Plate. New Zealand is the emergent portion of Zealandia, a largely submerged continental fragment that rifted away from Gondwana beginning about 85 million years ago. The active plate boundary produces frequent earthquakes, volcanism, and rapid mountain building.

Basement rocks

The oldest rocks, of Cambrian age, are found in northwest Nelson. Much of the country is underlain by Palaeozoic and Mesozoic "basement" terranes — greywacke sandstones, argillites, schists, and granitic intrusions — accreted to the Gondwana margin before breakup. These terranes are divided by major faults into the Western and Eastern provinces.

The plate boundary

In the north, the Pacific Plate subducts westward beneath the North Island along the Hikurangi Trough, feeding the active volcanoes of the Taupō Volcanic Zone, including Ruapehu, Tongariro, and the caldera lakes Taupō and Rotorua; the 1886 eruption of Tarawera was New Zealand's largest historic eruption. Through the South Island, the boundary is the Alpine Fault, a major strike-slip fault that has offset basement rocks by hundreds of kilometres and drives the uplift of the Southern Alps, whose highest peak, Aoraki / Mount Cook, reaches 3,724 m. South of New Zealand, the Australian Plate subducts beneath the Pacific Plate at the Puysegur Trench.

Hazards and resources

New Zealand faces significant earthquake, volcanic, tsunami, and landslide hazards, illustrated by the Canterbury earthquakes of 2010–2011 and the Kaikōura earthquake of 2016. Geothermal energy from the Taupō Volcanic Zone supplies a notable share of the country's electricity, and the country also produces coal, gold, and offshore petroleum.
