The Muslim conquest of the Iberian Peninsula (711–720s) was the invasion and conquest of the Visigothic Kingdom of Hispania by the Umayyad Caliphate, which established the Muslim territory known as al-Andalus. Within a few years, most of the peninsula — modern Spain and Portugal — came under Muslim rule, where it would remain, in part, for nearly eight centuries.

The invasion

In April 711 the Berber commander Tariq ibn Ziyad crossed the strait from North Africa with a largely Berber army, landing near the rock that still bears his name (Jabal Tariq — Gibraltar). He defeated and killed the Visigothic king Roderic at the Battle of Guadalete. Tariq advanced to Toledo, and in 712 his superior, Musa ibn Nusayr, arrived with a larger, predominantly Arab force, taking Seville, Mérida and other strongholds. By about 716 most of the peninsula was under Umayyad control; Muslim armies also pushed into southern Gaul until their defeat near Tours in 732.

Aftermath

Al-Andalus was first governed as a province of the caliphate, then from 756 as the independent Emirate of Córdoba under Abd al-Rahman I, later the Caliphate of Córdoba. Christian polities survived in the northern mountains, beginning the long process of Christian expansion known as the Reconquista, completed only in 1492 with the fall of Granada.
