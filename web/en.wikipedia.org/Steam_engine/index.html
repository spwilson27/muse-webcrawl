Steam engine

Type: External combustion heat engine
Working fluid: Water (steam)
Key inventors: Thomas Newcomen, James Watt
Era: 18th–20th centuries

A steam engine is a heat engine that performs mechanical work using steam as its working fluid. The steam engine was used to pump water out of mines, drive machinery in factories, and power locomotives and ships; it was the dominant source of mechanical power during the Industrial Revolution.

History

The first commercially successful engine, built by Thomas Newcomen in 1712, used atmospheric pressure acting on a piston after steam in the cylinder was condensed. James Watt's improvements from the 1760s and 1770s — above all the separate condenser — greatly improved efficiency and, with Matthew Boulton, his firm supplied engines to mines and factories. Later developments included high-pressure engines by Richard Trevithick, compound engines, and the steam turbine of Charles Parsons (1884).

How it works

Water is boiled in a boiler to produce steam, which expands against a piston in a cylinder or drives the blades of a turbine. In a reciprocating engine the piston's linear motion is converted to rotary motion by a connecting rod and flywheel; the steam is then condensed and the cycle repeats. Steam engines are external combustion engines, separating the working fluid from the combustion products.

Legacy

Steam engines were progressively displaced by internal combustion engines and electric motors during the 20th century, although steam turbines remain central to electricity generation in fossil-fuel and nuclear power stations.
