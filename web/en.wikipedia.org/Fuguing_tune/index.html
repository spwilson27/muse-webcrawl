Fuguing tune

A fuguing tune (also spelled fuging tune) is a variety of Anglo-American vernacular choral music, characterised by counterpoint in which the voices enter successively with the same phrase, in imitation of a fugue. Fuguing tunes were common in 18th-century New England singing-school repertory and survive in shape note traditions such as the Sacred Harp.

Structure

A typical fuguing tune has two sections: an opening section in block chords (homophony), in which all voices sing together, followed by the "fuguing" section, where the parts enter one by one — usually bass, treble, counter, and tenor — overlapping the text in lively counterpoint before rejoining.

History

The form was imported from England, where composers such as William Tans'ur published fuguing psalm tunes. In America, William Billings became its best-known composer, publishing tunes like "Chester" and "Africa". The tradition persists in Sacred Harp singings in the American South.
