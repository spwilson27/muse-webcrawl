Epigraphy (from Greek ἐπιγραφή, "inscription") is the study of inscriptions, or epigraphs, as writing; it is the science of identifying graphemes, clarifying their meanings, classifying their uses according to dates and cultural contexts, and drawing conclusions about the writing and the writers. Inscriptions are typically texts carved, incised, or painted on durable materials such as stone or metal.

Scope and methods

Epigraphers record inscriptions using photography, squeezes (paper impressions), and increasingly 3D scanning and reflectance transformation imaging. They transcribe the text using standard editorial conventions (the Leiden system), restore damaged passages, and date inscriptions by letter forms, language, formulas, and archaeological context. Epigraphy is distinguished from palaeography, which studies handwriting in manuscripts, though the fields overlap.

Importance for history

Inscriptions provide primary evidence often absent from literary sources: laws and decrees, treaties, tax records, building dedications, honorific monuments, funerary epitaphs, graffiti, and milestones. Famous examples include the Rosetta Stone, the Behistun Inscription, the Res Gestae Divi Augusti, and the Athenian tribute lists.

Major traditions

Subfields include Greek and Latin epigraphy, Egyptian hieroglyphic studies (closely linked to Egyptology), Semitic epigraphy, and the study of runic and Maya inscriptions. Since the 19th century, inscriptions have been collected in great corpora such as Theodor Mommsen's Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum.
