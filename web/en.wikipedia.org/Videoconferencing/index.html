Videoconferencing

A videoconference, or videoconferencing, is the conduct of a videotelephony session in which participants at two or more locations interact with one another in real time through two-way video and audio transmissions. Modern videoconferencing is typically carried out over broadband internet connections using dedicated room systems, desktop computers, or mobile devices, and software platforms such as Zoom, Microsoft Teams, and Webex.

History

The concept of two-way video communication dates to the earliest days of television. AT&T demonstrated the Picturephone at the 1964 New York World's Fair, and a commercial Picturephone service launched in Pittsburgh in 1970, but high cost and limited demand kept adoption low. Through the 1980s and 1990s, videoconferencing was dominated by expensive room-based systems from companies such as PictureTel and Polycom, running over ISDN lines using the H.320 standard, and later over IP networks using H.323.

The spread of broadband internet, cheap webcams, and free services such as Skype (launched 2003) brought videoconferencing to consumers. The COVID-19 pandemic beginning in 2020 caused an explosive increase in use, as remote work and remote learning became widespread; Zoom in particular became a household name, and "video call" entered everyday vocabulary.

Technology

A videoconferencing system generally comprises a camera, microphone, display, speakers, a codec that compresses and decompresses the audio and video streams, and software for call management, screen sharing, and chat. Compression standards such as H.264 and its successors reduce the bandwidth required, while protocols such as the Session Initiation Protocol (SIP) and WebRTC handle session setup and media transport. Multipoint control units (MCUs) and selective forwarding units (SFUs) bridge together participants in group calls. Echo cancellation, noise suppression, and automatic gain control improve audio quality, which users consistently rate as more important than video quality.

Applications

Videoconferencing is used in business meetings, telemedicine consultations, distance education, legal depositions and remote court hearings, job interviews, and personal communication between family and friends. Telepresence systems, with large life-size displays and carefully matched room furnishings, attempt to create the impression that remote participants are in the same room.

Impact and criticism

Studies of "Zoom fatigue" have identified causes including excessive eye contact at close range, the cognitive load of interpreting limited nonverbal cues, and seeing one's own image continuously. Videoconferencing also raises privacy and security concerns, illustrated by "Zoombombing" incidents early in the pandemic, and it depends on reliable broadband access, which is unevenly distributed.
