Equal Protection Clause
SourceFourteenth Amendment, Section 1 (ratified 1868)
Text (excerpt)"…nor shall any State … deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws."

The Equal Protection Clause is part of the first section of the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution, adopted in 1868 during Reconstruction. The clause provides that no state shall deny to any person within its jurisdiction "the equal protection of the laws." It has been the basis for many Supreme Court decisions regarding discrimination and civil rights.

History

The Fourteenth Amendment was drafted after the Civil War to secure the legal status of formerly enslaved people and to nullify the Black Codes of the Southern states. Early interpretations narrowed its reach — the Slaughter-House Cases (1873) and Plessy v. Ferguson (1896), which upheld "separate but equal" segregation — but the clause was later revived as a central instrument of civil-rights law.

Doctrine

Modern equal protection analysis asks whether a law classifies people and, if so, applies a tier of scrutiny: strict scrutiny for suspect classifications such as race and for infringements of fundamental rights; intermediate scrutiny for sex-based classifications; and rational-basis review for most other classifications. Key decisions include Brown v. Board of Education (1954), which declared school segregation unconstitutional; Loving v. Virginia (1967), striking down bans on interracial marriage; Reed v. Reed (1971) on sex discrimination; and Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) on same-sex marriage. Through Bolling v. Sharpe (1954), equal-protection principles also apply to the federal government via the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause ("reverse incorporation").

Debates

Scholars and courts continue to debate the clause's original meaning, its application to affirmative action (addressed in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard, 2023), voting rights, and the treatment of noncitizens, who are protected as "persons" though with some limitations.
