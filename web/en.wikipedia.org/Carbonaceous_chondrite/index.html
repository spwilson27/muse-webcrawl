Carbonaceous chondrite

Carbonaceous chondrites or C chondrites are a class of chondritic meteorites comprising at least 8 known groups and many ungrouped meteorites. They include some of the most primitive known meteorites. C chondrites represent only a small proportion (4.6%) of meteorite falls, but they are important because the material from which they formed has not been modified due to melting or differentiation of the parent body.

[Image: A dark fragment of the Murchison meteorite, a well-known carbonaceous chondrite]

Characteristics

Carbonaceous chondrites are among the most primitive materials in the Solar System. They contain up to 22% carbon by some measures in the form of organic compounds, including amino acids, as well as water bound in hydrated minerals. Because volatiles were not driven off by heating, their composition closely matches that of the solar nebula from which the Sun and planets accreted. Some famous examples are the Murchison meteorite, which fell in Australia in 1969, and the Allende meteorite, which fell in Mexico the same year.

Classification

The carbonaceous chondrite groups include CI, CM, CO, CV, CK, CR, CH and CB. The letters after the "C" generally designate the group's type locality: for instance, CI chondrites are named after Ivuna, Tanzania, and are the most chemically primitive, matching the composition of the solar photosphere more closely than any other type of chondrite. CV chondrites, named after Vigarano, Italy, are rich in calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions (CAIs), among the oldest dated solids in the Solar System.

Significance

Because carbonaceous chondrites contain organic matter and water, they are candidates for having delivered volatiles and prebiotic compounds to the early Earth. Samples returned by missions such as Japan's Hayabusa2 from asteroid Ryugu and NASA's OSIRIS-REx from asteroid Bennu have confirmed close links between these asteroids and carbonaceous chondrite material.
