Scale (music)

In music theory, a scale is any set of musical notes ordered by fundamental frequency or pitch. A scale ordered by increasing pitch is an ascending scale, and one ordered by decreasing pitch is a descending scale; the two often differ, as in the melodic minor scale. Scales are typically listed from a tonic (the first, lowest note) through one octave to the tonic an octave above.

Types of scale

Scales are classified by the number of notes they contain and the intervals between them. The most familiar in Western music are the seven-note diatonic scales — the major scale (whole–whole–half–whole–whole–whole–half steps) and the natural minor scale — from which the church modes (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian and others) are derived by starting on different degrees. The chromatic scale contains all twelve pitches of the octave; five-note pentatonic scales are found in folk traditions worldwide; and other systems include the whole-tone and octatonic scales, the blues scale, and the ragas of Indian classical music and maqamat of Arabic music, which are melodic frameworks comparable in function to scales.

Function

A scale provides the pitch material for melody and harmony: in tonal music, chords are built from the notes of the governing scale, and movement between the tonic and other degrees creates the sense of departure and return. Musicians practise scales to internalise key patterns and develop technique, and scales underpin improvisation in jazz and other genres, where particular scales are matched to particular chords.

Non-Western scales

Many musical cultures divide the octave differently from the Western twelve equal semitones: Indian theory recognises microtonal shruti divisions; Indonesian gamelan uses the roughly five-note slendro and seven-note pelog tunings; and Arabic and Turkish theory employ intervals of three-quarter tones and other neutral intervals unknown to the diatonic system.
