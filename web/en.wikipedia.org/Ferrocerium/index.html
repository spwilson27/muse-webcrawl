Ferrocerium
TypePyrophoric alloy (misch metal with iron oxide)
Main componentsCerium, lanthanum, other rare earths, iron
Spark temperatureAbout 3,000 °C (3,270 K)
Invented1903, by Carl Auer von Welsbach

Ferrocerium (also known as Auermetall, after its inventor) is a synthetic pyrophoric alloy of misch metal — mainly cerium, lanthanum, and small amounts of other rare-earth elements — hardened with iron and iron oxide. When scraped or struck, it shaves off fine particles that ignite spontaneously in air, producing sparks of about 3,000 °C.

History

The Austrian chemist Carl Auer von Welsbach patented ferrocerium in 1903, deriving it from his work on rare-earth gas mantles. Replacing flint as the sparking element in lighters, it rapidly became the standard "flint" in cigarette lighters and remained so throughout the 20th century.

Uses

Ferrocerium is found in lighter flints, gas-welding igniters ("spark lighters"), and survival fire starters. Larger ferrocerium rods, often sold with a steel striker and sometimes a magnesium shaving block, are a staple of bushcraft kits because they work when wet and produce thousands of strikes. The material is also used industrially for pyrotechnic initiation effects.

Properties

The alloy typically contains about 70% misch metal and 30% iron. Cerium's low ignition temperature (about 150–180 °C) makes finely divided particles incandesce on oxidation. Commercial "flints" are small cylinders of the alloy; survival rods are larger and purer in rare-earth content, yielding longer-lived molten droplets.
