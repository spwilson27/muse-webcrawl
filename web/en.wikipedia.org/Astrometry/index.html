Astrometry

Astrometry is a branch of astronomy that involves precise measurements of the positions and movements of stars and other celestial bodies. It provides the kinematics and physical origin of the Solar System and the Milky Way galaxy.

Parallax: a nearby star appears to shift against distant stars as Earth orbits the Sun.

Methods

Astrometric measurements include celestial coordinates (right ascension and declination), proper motion across the sky, and parallax, from which distance is derived. Historically these were made with meridian circles and photographic plates; modern work uses CCD detectors, very-long-baseline interferometry, and space observatories that avoid atmospheric distortion.

Space missions

The ESA satellite Hipparcos (1989–1993) measured over 100,000 stars to milliarcsecond precision. Its successor Gaia, launched in 2013, has charted nearly two billion sources with microarcsecond-level precision for the brightest, transforming studies of galactic structure, stellar populations, and the cosmic distance scale. Astrometry is also used to detect exoplanets by measuring the wobble they induce in their host stars.

Applications

Astrometry underpins celestial reference frames used for navigation, telescope pointing, timekeeping, and tests of general relativity through measurements of light deflection and frame dragging.
