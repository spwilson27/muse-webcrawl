Pareto efficiency

Pareto efficiency or Pareto optimality is a situation where no action or allocation can be taken to make one individual better off without making at least one other individual worse off. The concept is named after the Italian economist and political scientist Vilfredo Pareto (1848–1923) and is a central concept in economics, game theory and welfare analysis.

Definitions

A change from one allocation to another is a Pareto improvement if at least one person is made better off and nobody is made worse off. An allocation is Pareto optimal (or Pareto efficient) when no further Pareto improvements are possible. The set of all Pareto optimal allocations forms the Pareto frontier. A weaker notion, weak Pareto optimality, requires that no alternative make everyone strictly better off.

Use in economics

The two fundamental theorems of welfare economics connect Pareto efficiency with competitive markets: under ideal conditions, every competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient (first theorem), and any Pareto efficient allocation can be achieved as an equilibrium given suitable redistribution of initial endowments (second theorem). Pareto efficiency is deliberately silent about equity: an allocation in which one person holds nearly everything can be Pareto optimal if taking anything away makes that person worse off.

Use in engineering and decision-making

In multi-objective optimization — engineering design, logistics, policy analysis — candidate solutions are ranked by Pareto dominance: a solution is dominated if another is at least as good on every objective and better on one. The non-dominated set (the Pareto frontier) is presented to decision-makers as the menu of unavoidable trade-offs.

Criticism and limitations

Critics note that real-world changes almost always create both winners and losers, so strict Pareto comparisons rarely apply directly; cost–benefit practice instead uses the Kaldor–Hicks criterion of potential compensation. Pareto efficiency also says nothing about fairness, rights, or the desirability of the initial distribution of resources, and it takes individual preferences as given, even when they are spiteful or ill-informed.
