Salmonella is a genus of rod-shaped, Gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic bacteria of the family Enterobacteriaceae. The genus comprises two species, Salmonella enterica and Salmonella bongori, subdivided into more than 2,600 serotypes. Salmonellae are among the most common causes of foodborne illness worldwide and also include the agents of typhoid and paratyphoid fever.

Biology

Most salmonellae are motile by means of peritrichous flagella and are non-spore-forming. They survive well outside a host, tolerate drying, and can persist in foods and animal faeces. The genus was named after Daniel Elmer Salmon, under whose direction Theobald Smith first isolated the type organism (from pigs, in 1885), then believed to cause hog cholera.

Disease

Non-typhoidal serotypes such as S. Typhimurium and S. Enteritidis typically cause self-limiting gastroenteritis (salmonellosis): diarrhoea, fever and abdominal cramps arising 6–72 hours after eating contaminated food — commonly poultry, eggs, unpasteurised milk or produce — or contact with reptiles and other carrier animals. Invasive disease is a major killer in sub-Saharan Africa. Typhoidal serotypes (S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi) spread only between humans, usually via contaminated water, and cause the systemic illness typhoid fever; chronic carriers, famously "Typhoid Mary", can shed bacteria for years.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention rests on cooking food thoroughly, pasteurisation, clean water and hand hygiene. Most gastroenteritis needs only fluids; antibiotics are reserved for invasive infection, and rising drug resistance is a concern. Vaccines exist against typhoid fever.
