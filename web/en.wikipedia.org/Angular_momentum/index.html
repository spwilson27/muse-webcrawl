In physics, angular momentum is the rotational analogue of linear momentum. For a particle it is defined as the cross product of its position vector r (relative to some origin) and its linear momentum p = mv:

L = r × p

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, angular momentum is the product of the moment of inertia I and the angular velocity ω: L = Iω. Angular momentum is a vector quantity, measured in kilogram square metres per second, and its direction follows the right-hand rule along the axis of rotation.

Conservation

The rate of change of angular momentum equals the net external torque applied to the system (dL/dt = τ), so where the net torque is zero, angular momentum is conserved. This law, a consequence of the rotational symmetry of space via Noether's theorem, explains many familiar phenomena: a figure skater spins faster when pulling in the arms (reducing I, so ω rises to keep L constant); a falling cat can reorient itself without violating conservation; gyroscopes resist changes in orientation, stabilising ships, aircraft instruments and spacecraft; and a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times (Kepler's second law) because the Sun's gravity exerts no torque about itself on the planet.

Orbital and spin angular momentum

In astronomy, orbital angular momentum dominates the solar system: the Sun holds most of the mass but the planets, above all Jupiter, hold most of the angular momentum — a key constraint on theories of the system's formation. In quantum mechanics, angular momentum is quantised in units of the reduced Planck constant ħ: particles possess orbital angular momentum from their motion and an intrinsic spin angular momentum, which for electrons is ħ/2. Total angular momentum J = L + S is the quantity conserved in atomic and nuclear processes, governing spectroscopic selection rules and the fine structure of spectral lines.
