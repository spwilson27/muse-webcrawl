Black and Tans

Active1920–1922
CountryUnited Kingdom (Ireland)
RoleParamilitary police reserve
Strength~9,500

The Black and Tans (Irish: Dúchrónaigh) were constables recruited into the Royal Irish Constabulary (RIC) as reinforcements during the Irish War of Independence (1919–1921). More than 10,000 were recruited, mainly unemployed former British soldiers who had fought in the First World War.

Name

Because of a shortage of RIC uniforms, recruits were issued a mixture of dark RIC tunics and khaki army trousers — a combination that, together with a famous pack of foxhounds of the same colours, gave them their nickname.

Conduct and legacy

The Black and Tans became notorious for reprisal attacks on civilians and property, including the burning of Cork city centre in December 1920 and the events of Bloody Sunday. Their reputation remains a byword in Ireland for the brutality of the conflict; the force was disbanded in 1922 following the Anglo-Irish Treaty.
