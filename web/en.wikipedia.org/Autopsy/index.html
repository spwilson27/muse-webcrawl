Autopsy

SynonymsPost-mortem examination, necropsy, autopsia cadaverum, obduction
SpecialtyPathology, forensic pathology
PurposeDetermine cause and manner of death; evaluate disease and injury

An autopsy (from Greek autopsia, "seeing for oneself"), also called a post-mortem examination, is a surgical procedure that consists of a thorough examination of a corpse by dissection to determine the cause, mode, and manner of death; or the exam may be performed to evaluate any disease or injury that may be present for research or educational purposes. (The term "necropsy" is generally reserved for non-human animals.)

Types

Forensic (medico-legal) autopsies are carried out, usually under the authority of a coroner or medical examiner, when death is sudden, suspicious, violent or unexplained; they seek to establish the cause and manner of death — natural, accident, suicide, homicide or undetermined — for legal purposes. Clinical (academic) autopsies are performed in hospitals, generally with the consent of the next of kin, to confirm diagnoses, assess treatment and further medical knowledge.

Procedure

The examination begins with an external inspection of the body, noting clothing, injuries and identifying features. The internal examination typically uses a large Y-shaped incision from behind both ears (or from the shoulders) to the sternum and pubic bone. Organs are removed — either en masse (the Rokitansky/Letulle technique) or organ by organ (in situ dissection, associated with Virchow) — weighed, inspected and sectioned. Samples are taken for histology, toxicology and microbiology. The brain is examined after removal of the skull cap. Findings are correlated with the clinical history before a final report is issued.

History

Human dissection was practised in Hellenistic Alexandria, and systematic post-mortem study underlies Giovanni Battista Morgagni's De Sedibus (1761), a foundation of pathology. Karl von Rokitansky in 19th-century Vienna standardised autopsy technique, and Rudolf Virchow added cellular pathology. Autopsy rates in hospitals have declined sharply across the developed world since the mid-20th century, despite studies showing that major diagnostic discrepancies are still found in a significant minority of cases; imaging-based "virtopsy" is an emerging adjunct.
