Instant camera

An instant camera is a camera which uses self-developing film to create a chemically developed print shortly after taking the picture. The best-known instant camera was invented by Edwin H. Land, who founded the Polaroid Corporation; the first commercial model, the Polaroid Land Camera Model 95, went on sale in 1948.

History

The idea for instant photography is often traced to Land's daughter, who asked in 1943 why she could not immediately see a photograph he had taken of her. Land developed the diffusion transfer process, in which exposed negative material is brought into contact with a positive receiving sheet and a reagent pod spreads developer between them, producing a finished print in about a minute.

Polaroid dominated the market for decades with pack film, peel-apart film and integral film systems such as SX-70 (1972) and the 600 series. After Polaroid ceased film production in 2008, the Impossible Project (later Polaroid Originals, then Polaroid) restarted production of integral film.

Other manufacturers

Fujifilm's Instax line, launched in 1998, became the market leader in the 2010s, and Kodak, Leica and others have sold instant cameras and printers. Instant cameras experienced a strong revival in the 2010s driven by nostalgia and social sharing.
