Behaviourism is a theory of learning and a methodological approach in psychology based on the idea that all behaviours are acquired through conditioning and interaction with the environment. Behaviourists held that psychology should study only observable behaviour, not unobservable mental states.

History

John B. Watson launched the school with his 1913 manifesto "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It" and his controversial Little Albert experiment on conditioned fear. Ivan Pavlov's work on classical conditioning with dogs provided a model of associative learning, and Edward Thorndike formulated the law of effect. B. F. Skinner developed radical behaviourism and the analysis of operant conditioning, describing schedules of reinforcement and inventing the Skinner box and teaching machines.

Decline and legacy

From the 1950s the cognitive revolution challenged behaviourism's exclusion of mental processes, and Noam Chomsky's critique of Skinner's account of language was influential. Behaviourist principles nevertheless persist in behaviour therapy, applied behaviour analysis, education and animal training.
