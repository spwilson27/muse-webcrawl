Quasi-Monte Carlo method

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In numerical analysis, the quasi-Monte Carlo method is a method for numerical integration and solving other problems that uses low-discrepancy sequences (also called quasi-random sequences) instead of the purely random numbers used in the plain Monte Carlo method. Because the points of such sequences fill space more uniformly, quasi-Monte Carlo methods can converge considerably faster.

Theory

The Koksma–Hlawka inequality bounds the integration error by the product of the variation of the integrand and the star discrepancy of the point set. Good sequences — including the Halton, Sobol′, and Faure sequences and lattice rules — achieve discrepancies of order (log N)s/N in dimension s, giving an error close to O(1/N) rather than the O(1/√N) of random sampling, although the logarithmic factor grows with dimension and can erode the advantage in very high dimensions.

Applications

Quasi-Monte Carlo methods are used heavily in mathematical finance for pricing derivatives and computing risk measures, where Sobol′ sequences are standard, as well as in computer graphics (sampling for rendering), statistics, and experimental design. Randomized quasi-Monte Carlo, which scrambles deterministic sequences, restores the ability to estimate errors statistically while retaining much of the convergence benefit.
