Bayes' theorem

FieldProbability theory, statistics
Named afterThomas Bayes (1701–1761)

In probability theory and statistics, Bayes' theorem (also Bayes' law or Bayes' rule) describes the probability of an event based on prior knowledge of conditions that might be related to the event. It is named after Thomas Bayes, who first used conditional probabilities to provide an algorithm to update probabilities with new evidence.

The theorem states that the posterior probability P(A|B) equals the likelihood P(B|A) times the prior probability P(A), divided by the marginal probability P(B): P(A|B) = P(B|A)·P(A) / P(B).

Applications

Bayes' theorem is applied in medicine (interpreting test results), finance, machine learning (naive Bayes classifiers, Bayesian inference), law, and spam filtering. It underpins the Bayesian interpretation of probability, in which probability measures a degree of belief that is rationally updated as evidence accumulates.

History

Bayes's work was published posthumously in 1763 by Richard Price. Pierre-Simon Laplace independently rediscovered and generalised the theorem in the early 19th century.
