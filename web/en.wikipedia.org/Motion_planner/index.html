Motion planning

Field: Robotics, computational geometry
Also known as: The "piano mover's problem"
Key concepts: Configuration space, sampling-based planning, PRM, RRT

Motion planning (also known as the navigation problem or the piano mover's problem) is a computational problem in robotics: given a start state and a goal state, find a valid sequence of movements that moves a robot (or animated character, vehicle, or object) from start to goal while avoiding collisions with obstacles and respecting the robot's constraints.

Concepts

The standard formalism is the configuration space (C-space), in which each point represents a complete configuration of the robot—positions of all its joints or its pose—and obstacles are mapped into forbidden regions of this space. Planning then becomes the problem of finding a path for a point through the free configuration space. Exact algorithms exist for low-dimensional problems, but general motion planning is computationally hard (PSPACE-hard in the classic formulation).

Algorithms

Approaches include grid-based search (such as A*), potential field methods, exact cell decomposition, and roadmap methods. In practice, sampling-based planners dominate: the Probabilistic Roadmap (PRM) builds a graph of randomly sampled collision-free configurations, while tree-based methods such as the Rapidly-exploring Random Tree (RRT) and its optimal variant RRT* grow a search tree from the start state. These methods are probabilistically complete and scale to high-dimensional problems such as multi-joint robot arms.

Applications

Motion planning is used in industrial and surgical robotics, autonomous vehicles, computer animation and video games, computational biology (for example, protein folding and molecular docking), and digital prototyping and assembly verification.
