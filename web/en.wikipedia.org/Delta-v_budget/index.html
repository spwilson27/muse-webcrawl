Delta-v budget

A delta-v budget is an estimate of the total delta-v — the total change in velocity — required for a space mission. It is calculated as the sum of the delta-v required for all propulsive manoeuvres in the mission, from launch through orbital insertions, transfers, rendezvous and landing. Because a spacecraft's achievable delta-v is fixed by the rocket equation, the budget is a fundamental constraint on mission design.

Calculation

The budget adds together the individual manoeuvres of a mission. For example, reaching low Earth orbit from Earth's surface requires roughly 9.3–10 km/s once gravity and drag losses are included; from low Earth orbit, trans-lunar injection requires about 3.2 km/s, and entering low lunar orbit a further 0.7 km/s or so. Hohmann transfer values are commonly used as a starting point, and mission planners add margins for gravity losses, steering losses and contingencies.

Uses

Delta-v budgets allow different destinations and trajectories to be compared on a common scale: a destination with a lower total budget is "closer" in propulsive terms, regardless of its physical distance. Astrodynamicists use "delta-v maps" of the Solar System (sometimes called "porkchop plots" in the case of departure windows) to plan interplanetary missions and assess concepts such as aerocapture, which can substitute atmospheric drag for propulsive delta-v.
