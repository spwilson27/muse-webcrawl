CMYK color model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (recreated from memory)

The CMYK color model is a subtractive color model used in color printing, describing the printing process itself. CMYK refers to the four ink plates used in most color printing: cyan, magenta, yellow, and key (black). It works by partially or entirely masking colors on a lighter — usually white — background; the inks reduce the light that would otherwise be reflected.

How it works

In subtractive mixing, cyan absorbs red light, magenta absorbs green, and yellow absorbs blue. In theory, combining all three at full strength produces black, but real inks are imperfect and give a muddy dark brown, so a separate black ink is added: it increases density and shadow detail, sharpens text, and reduces the total ink required (undercolor removal and gray component replacement). Halftone dots of each ink, printed in overlapping rosette patterns, create the illusion of continuous-tone color.

Comparison with RGB

Displays use the additive RGB model, whose gamut includes bright, saturated colors that CMYK printing cannot reproduce; conversely, some printable colors fall outside older RGB spaces. Converting from RGB to CMYK for print therefore requires color management (typically ICC profiles) and often compresses out-of-gamut colors. Standard printing conditions are defined by specifications such as SWOP, FOGRA, and Japan Color.

History

Three-color printing traces to early experiments by Jacob Christoph Le Blon in the 18th century; the modern four-color process matured with chromolithography and, in the 20th century, offset lithography, becoming the universal standard for magazines, packaging, and commercial print.
