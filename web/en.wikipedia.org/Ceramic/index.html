Ceramic

Ceramic
TypeInorganic, non-metallic material
FormationShaping and high-temperature firing
ExamplesEarthenware, stoneware, porcelain, brick, tile
PropertiesHard, brittle, heat- and corrosion-resistant

A ceramic is any of the various hard, brittle, heat-resistant, and corrosion-resistant materials made by shaping and then firing an inorganic, nonmetallic material, such as clay, at a high temperature. Common examples are earthenware, porcelain, and brick.

The earliest ceramics made by humans were pottery objects, including 27,000-year-old figurines, made from clay, either by itself or mixed with other materials like silica, hardened and sintered in fire. Later, ceramics were glazed and fired to create smooth, colored surfaces, decreasing porosity through the use of glassy, amorphous ceramic coatings on top of crystalline ceramic substrates.

History

Human artifacts made of ceramic have been found dating back at least 26,000 years. The Venus of Dolní Věstonice, a ceramic figurine from the Czech Republic, is among the oldest known ceramic objects. Pottery developed independently in many parts of the world, including East Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, and the Americas. Fired clay bricks were used as early as 4500 BCE, and glazed ceramics appeared in Mesopotamia and Egypt.

In the 20th century, new ceramic materials were developed for use in advanced engineering, such as semiconductors. Advanced ceramics now include carbides, oxides, and nitrides engineered for extreme environments.

Types

Earthenware is fired at relatively low temperatures and remains porous unless glazed. Stoneware is vitrified and non-porous, fired at higher temperatures. Porcelain, made from kaolin clay, is translucent and highly durable, and was first developed in China. Terracotta is an unglazed earthenware used for sculpture and architectural ornament. Technical or advanced ceramics include alumina, silicon carbide, and zirconia, used in electronics, armor, and medical implants.

Properties

Ceramic materials are generally hard and strong in compression, but brittle, with low tensile strength and little ductility. They are electrical and thermal insulators (though some are superconductors or semiconductors), resist chemical erosion, and withstand very high temperatures — some advanced ceramics remain stable above 2,000 °C. Their brittleness arises from ionic and covalent bonding that resists dislocation movement.

Applications

Traditional applications include pottery, tiles, bricks, sanitary ware, and tableware. Modern applications include spark plug insulators, catalytic converter substrates, cutting tools, dental crowns, hip-joint replacements, heat shields for spacecraft, and substrates for electronic circuits.
