Mucolytic

Mucolytics are a class of drugs that break down and thin mucus, making it less viscous and easier to clear from the airways. They are used in respiratory diseases characterised by excessive or thick secretions, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), bronchiectasis and cystic fibrosis. They differ from expectorants, which aim to increase the volume or hydration of secretions, and from mucoregulators.

Common agents

N-acetylcysteine (NAC) cleaves disulfide bonds in mucus glycoproteins and also acts as an antioxidant and as the antidote for paracetamol poisoning; it is given orally, by inhalation, or intravenously. Carbocisteine and erdosteine are oral mucoregulators used in COPD. Dornase alfa, a recombinant human DNase delivered by nebuliser, digests the DNA released by neutrophils in cystic fibrosis sputum. Hypertonic saline and mannitol act osmotically to hydrate airway mucus.

Evidence

In COPD, regular mucolytic therapy modestly reduces exacerbations in some patients, particularly those not using inhaled corticosteroids. Evidence for acute bronchitis and the common cold is weak, and guidelines generally do not recommend mucolytics for acute cough. Side effects are usually mild; inhaled agents can provoke bronchospasm.
