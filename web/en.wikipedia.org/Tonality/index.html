Tonality

Tonality is the arrangement of pitches and/or chords of a musical work in a hierarchy of perceived relations, stabilities, attractions and directionality. In this hierarchy, the single pitch or triadic chord with the greatest stability is called the tonic. The root of the tonic chord forms the name given to the key; so in the key of C major, the note C is both the tonic of the scale and the root of the tonic chord (which is composed of the notes C, E, and G). Simple folk music songs, as well as more complex pieces, are usually in a specific key.

Characteristics

Tonal music is characterised by the use of functional harmony — chords relate to one another by their function relative to the key's tonic, most prominently through the dominant–tonic relationship and the cadences that articulate phrases. Although tonality is most often associated with Western art music of the common practice period (roughly 1650–1900), tonal principles also underpin most popular music, film scores and much non-Western-influenced music worldwide.

History

Tonality developed gradually from the modal system of Renaissance polyphony. By the Baroque era, composers such as Arcangelo Corelli had established the major–minor key system and the circle of fifths as organising principles. Jean-Philippe Rameau's Treatise on Harmony (1722) gave the system its first systematic theoretical foundation. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, composers including Wagner and Debussy stretched tonal relationships, leading some — notably Arnold Schoenberg — toward atonality and the twelve-tone technique.
