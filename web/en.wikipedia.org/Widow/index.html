Widow

A widow (female) or widower (male) is a person whose spouse has died. The state of being a widow or widower is called widowhood, and the treatment of widows around the world varies widely by culture, religion and law. Surviving spouses may experience grief, economic hardship and social stigma; research also documents a modestly elevated mortality risk in the period following bereavement, known as the widowhood effect.

Cultural and religious treatment

In many historical societies, widows were expected to observe extended mourning dress and seclusion; in Victorian Britain, mourning etiquette prescribed years of black dress. Practices such as sati — the immolation of widows in parts of India — have been prohibited since the 19th century. Levirate marriage, in which a widow marries her late husband's brother, appears in the Hebrew Bible and other traditions. In some communities, widows face accusations of witchcraft or property dispossession, which international bodies such as the United Nations and widows' charities campaign against; International Widows Day is observed on 23 June.

Legal and economic position

Modern legal systems typically grant widows and widowers inheritance rights and survivors' benefits, such as Social Security survivor benefits in the United States. Remarriage generally ends the status, though terminology and benefit rules vary by jurisdiction.
