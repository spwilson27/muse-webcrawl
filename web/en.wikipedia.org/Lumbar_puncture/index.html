Lumbar puncture

Lumbar puncture
Other name: spinal tap
Purpose: collect cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for diagnosis; measure CSF pressure; administer spinal anaesthesia or drugs
Site: L3/L4 or L4/L5 intervertebral space
Common indication: suspected meningitis
Key risks: post-dural-puncture headache, bleeding, infection, cerebral herniation (rare)

A lumbar puncture (LP), also known as a spinal tap, is a medical procedure in which a needle is inserted into the spinal canal, most commonly to collect cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for diagnostic testing. It can also be used to measure intracranial pressure, inject medications or anaesthetic agents (spinal anaesthesia), or relieve pressure in conditions such as idiopathic intracranial hypertension. The procedure was first described in its modern form by Heinrich Quincke in 1891.

Lumbar puncture performed with the patient in the lateral recumbent position

Technique

The patient lies on their side (lateral recumbent, allowing pressure measurement) or sits leaning forward, with the back flexed to open the intervertebral spaces. Under sterile conditions and local anaesthesia, a fine spinal needle is advanced in the midline at the L3/L4 or L4/L5 level — below the termination of the spinal cord (the conus medullaris, usually at L1/L2 in adults) so only the mobile roots of the cauda equina are at risk. Once CSF flows, the opening pressure may be measured with a manometer and fluid collected into sequential tubes for cell counts, protein and glucose, Gram stain and culture, and other studies. Atraumatic (pencil-point) needles and smaller gauges reduce complications.

Indications and contraindications

The classic diagnostic indication is suspected meningitis or encephalitis; LP is also used in suspected subarachnoid haemorrhage when CT is negative, and in evaluating demyelinating disease, Guillain–Barré syndrome and leptomeningeal malignancy. Important contraindications include signs of raised intracranial pressure with risk of herniation, coagulopathy, and infection at the puncture site; imaging (CT) precedes LP in patients with focal neurology, seizures, papilloedema or reduced consciousness.

Complications

The most common complication is post-dural-puncture headache, caused by continued CSF leakage, typically positional and self-limiting but occasionally treated with an epidural blood patch. Serious complications — spinal haematoma, infection, and cerebral herniation — are rare.
