Valentinian III reigned for thirty years, one of the longest reigns of any Roman emperor, yet real power lay with his mother and his generals.

Placidus Valentinianus (2 July 419 – 16 March 455), known as Valentinian III, was Western Roman emperor from 425 to 455. Made emperor as a small child, he spent most of his long reign in Ravenna under the regency of his mother Galla Placidia and then under the dominance of the generalissimo Flavius Aetius. His reign saw the loss of North Africa to the Vandals, the Hunnic invasions of Attila, and the shrinking of imperial authority to Italy and parts of Gaul and the Balkans.

Accession

Valentinian was the son of Constantius III (briefly co-emperor in 421) and Galla Placidia, daughter of Theodosius I. When his uncle Honorius died in 423 and the usurper Joannes seized the West, the Eastern emperor Theodosius II backed the child's claim. An Eastern expedition defeated Joannes, and on 23 October 425, aged six, Valentinian was proclaimed Augustus at Rome. He was betrothed — and in 437 married — to Licinia Eudoxia, Theodosius II's daughter, sealing the union of the two imperial lines.

Regency and the rule of Aetius

Galla Placidia governed as regent until the mid-430s, balancing rival generals — Felix, Bonifatius and Aetius — whose civil wars fatally damaged the empire: the recall and war against Bonifatius opened Africa to the Vandals, who took Carthage in 439 and were recognized in a treaty of 442 that robbed the West of its richest tax base and grain supply. Aetius, victor of the generals' contest after 433, became patrician and effective ruler, conducting diplomacy and war with the empire's remaining resources and Hunnic auxiliaries.

Attila and the 450s

When Attila turned west in 451 — prompted, legend says, by Valentinian's sister Honoria sending him her ring — Aetius assembled a coalition of Romans, Visigoths and others that checked the Huns at the Battle of the Catalaunian Plains (Châlons). Attila invaded Italy in 452, sacking Aquileia and Milan, and withdrew after famine, disease and negotiation (including the famous meeting with Pope Leo I) — not before Valentinian's court had fled to Rome.

Assassination

In September 454, in a fit of suspicion reportedly inflamed by the eunuch Heraclius and resentment of Aetius's ambition, Valentinian personally murdered Aetius in the palace at Ravenna — prompting the famous rebuke that he had cut off his right hand with his left. Six months later, on 16 March 455, two of Aetius's former guards, Optila and Thraustila, assassinated Valentinian on the Campus Martius in Rome. He left no son; his widow and daughters were carried off when the Vandals sacked Rome weeks later under his successor Petronius Maximus.

Assessment

Contemporaries and historians have generally judged Valentinian weak and idle, interested in religion and amusements while others governed. Yet his reign's dynastic legitimacy held the Western state together for three decades; his death, ending the Theodosian line in the West, opened the final twenty years of rapid imperial turnover before 476.
