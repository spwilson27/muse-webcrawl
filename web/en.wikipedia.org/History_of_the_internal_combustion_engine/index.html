History of the internal combustion engine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The internal combustion engine powered the transport revolutions of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

The history of the internal combustion engine stretches from seventeenth-century experiments with gunpowder engines, such as Christiaan Huygens' 1673 design, to the engines that powered automobiles, aircraft and industry. Early practical attempts included Samuel Brown's gas engine of the 1820s and the non-compression engine of Étienne Lenoir (1860), which ran on coal gas and was sold commercially.

The Otto cycle and the automobile

In 1876 Nikolaus Otto built the first commercially successful four-stroke engine, applying the cycle described by Alphonse Beau de Rochas in 1862. Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach developed high-speed petrol engines in the 1880s, and Karl Benz built his three-wheeled Patent-Motorwagen in 1885, generally regarded as the first practical automobile. The Diesel engine, patented by Rudolf Diesel in 1892 and working by 1897, used compression ignition and offered greater efficiency, finding use in ships, locomotives, trucks and eventually cars.

Twentieth-century development

Mass production, pioneered by Henry Ford, made engine-driven cars affordable. Two world wars drove advances in aero engines, supercharging and fuels. Felix Wankel's rotary engine (1957) offered an alternative configuration but saw limited adoption. Frank Whittle and Hans von Ohain independently developed the turbojet — a form of internal combustion gas turbine — in the late 1930s, transforming aviation.

Emissions and the modern era

From the 1970s, concerns over air pollution and oil supply drove catalytic converters, electronic fuel injection and engine management, large gains in efficiency, and later turbocharged downsizing and hybrid powertrains. In the twenty-first century, battery-electric vehicles have begun displacing internal combustion in passenger cars, and several governments have announced future bans on new petrol and diesel car sales, though the engine remains dominant in shipping, aviation, agriculture and heavy transport.
