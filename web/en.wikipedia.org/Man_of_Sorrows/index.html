Man of Sorrows

The Man of Sorrows is a devotional image-type in Christian art depicting Jesus Christ after the Crucifixion — alive yet bearing the wounds of the Passion, often wearing the Crown of Thorns, with hands crossed or raised to display the nail wounds, and sometimes supported by angels. The title derives from the "Suffering Servant" song of Isaiah 53:3 in the Old Testament ("a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief"), which Christians read as a prophecy of Christ.

Development

The image developed in Byzantine art from the 12th century (the Akra Tapeinosis, "Utmost Humiliation") and spread to the West, where it became one of the most popular devotional images (Andachtsbilder) of the late Middle Ages, closely related to the Mass of Saint Gregory legend and to indulgences granted to prayers said before such images. It is distinct from narrative scenes such as the Ecce homo or the Pietà, presenting Christ out of narrative context for direct meditation on his suffering.

Examples

Famous examples include works by the Master Francke, Geertgen tot Sint Jans, Albrecht Dürer (who returned to the subject repeatedly in prints and paintings), and the sorrowful Christ figures of Northern Renaissance and Baroque art.
