Rouen (French: [ʁwɑ̃]) is a city on the River Seine in northern France. It is the prefecture of the region of Normandy and the department of Seine-Maritime. Formerly one of the largest and most prosperous cities of medieval Europe, Rouen was the seat of the Exchequer of Normandy and one of the capitals of the Anglo-Norman dynasties, which ruled both England and parts of France from the 11th to the 15th centuries.

History

Founded by the Gaulish Veliocasses as Ratumacos and known to the Romans as Rotomagus, Rouen became a Viking settlement in the 9th century; in 911 its Norse leader Rollo received the surrounding territory from the Frankish king, creating what became the Duchy of Normandy. Under Norman and English rule the city flourished as a trading port.

Rouen is indelibly linked with Joan of Arc, who was tried there for heresy and burned at the stake in the Place du Vieux-Marché on 30 May 1431. France recovered the city in 1449. The textile trade drove prosperity in the early modern period, and the city suffered heavy bombing in 1944 during the Normandy campaign.

Landmarks

Rouen Cathedral (Notre-Dame de Rouen), begun in the 12th century in Gothic style, has the tallest church spire in France and was painted repeatedly by Claude Monet in his Rouen Cathedrals series. Other notable monuments include the Gros Horloge astronomical clock, the Church of Saint-Maclou, the Palais de Justice, and the half-timbered streets of the old town — Rouen has some 2,000 half-timbered houses.
