Dionysia

The Dionysia (Greek: Διονύσια) was a large festival in ancient Athens in honor of the god Dionysus, the central events of which were theatrical performances of dramatic tragedies and, from 487 BC, comedies. It was held annually in the month of Elaphebolion (roughly late March to early April) and was the second-most important festival after the Panathenaea.

The City Dionysia (or Great Dionysia) is famous above all as the birthplace of Greek theatre. A separate, older celebration, the Rural Dionysia, was held in winter in the demes of Attica, and the Lenaea festival also honoured Dionysus with dramatic contests.

Origins

Tradition credits the tyrant Pisistratus with establishing the City Dionysia around 534 BC, when the actor Thespis is said to have first stepped out from the chorus — the legendary origin of tragedy. The festival began with a procession (pompē) carrying a wooden statue of Dionysus to the theatre, accompanied by sacrifices, choruses, and citizens bearing phallic symbols in honour of the god of wine and fertility.

The dramatic competitions

Three tragedians each presented three tragedies and a satyr play over successive days; comic poets presented one comedy each. Wealthy citizens called choregoi financed the productions as a public liturgy, and prizes were awarded by citizen judges. The great surviving plays of Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, and Aristophanes were all written for these festivals and performed in the Theatre of Dionysus on the south slope of the Acropolis before audiences of up to 15,000.

Political role

The festival displayed Athens' wealth and power to visiting allies: the tribute of the Delian League was displayed in the theatre, war orphans raised by the state were presented in armour, and public honours were proclaimed — making the Dionysia as much a civic and imperial spectacle as a religious one.
