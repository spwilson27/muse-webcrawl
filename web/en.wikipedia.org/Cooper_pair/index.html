Cooper pair

In condensed matter physics, a Cooper pair is a bound pair of two electrons (or other fermions) formed through an attractive interaction, named after Leon Cooper, who showed in 1956 that an arbitrarily weak attraction between electrons in a metal at low temperature produces a bound state. Cooper pairs are the microscopic basis of conventional superconductivity in the BCS theory developed by John Bardeen, Leon Cooper and John Robert Schrieffer in 1957.

Formation

Although electrons repel one another by Coulomb repulsion, an electron moving through a crystal lattice attracts the positive ions, slightly deforming the lattice and leaving a region of increased positive charge that attracts a second electron. This phonon-mediated interaction can overcome the screened Coulomb repulsion for electrons near the Fermi surface with opposite momenta and spins, binding them into a pair with total spin zero.

Role in superconductivity

At the critical temperature, enormous numbers of overlapping Cooper pairs condense into a single macroscopic quantum state. The condensation opens an energy gap at the Fermi level; breaking a pair costs at least twice the gap energy, which suppresses scattering and allows electric current to flow without resistance. The pairs behave as composite bosons and can occupy the same ground state, giving rise to phenomena such as flux quantisation in units of h/2e — direct evidence that the charge carriers have charge 2e.

Extent and variations

In conventional superconductors a Cooper pair extends over hundreds of nanometres, encompassing many other pairs. In high-temperature and heavy-fermion superconductors the pairing is tighter and the "glue" may involve magnetic rather than phonon interactions, but the formation of two-electron pairs remains the central mechanism of superconductivity.
