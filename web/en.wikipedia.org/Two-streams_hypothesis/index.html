Two-streams hypothesis

The dorsal (where/how) and ventral (what) pathways of the visual system.

The two-streams hypothesis is a model of the neural processing of vision proposed by David Milner and Melvyn Goodale in 1992, building on earlier work by Leslie Ungerleider and Mortimer Mishkin. It proposes that visual information leaving the occipital lobe follows two main pathways: a ventral stream projecting to the temporal lobe, and a dorsal stream projecting to the parietal lobe.

The two streams

The ventral stream — the "what" pathway — is involved in object recognition and form representation, supporting conscious perception. The dorsal stream — characterised by Ungerleider and Mishkin as the "where" pathway and reframed by Milner and Goodale as the "how" pathway — guides visually directed actions such as reaching and grasping, largely outside conscious awareness.

Evidence

The model is supported by a double dissociation in patients. Patient D.F., with damage to the ventral stream (visual form agnosia), cannot recognise objects or judge their orientation yet can accurately "post" a card through a rotated slot and scale her grip when grasping. Conversely, patients with optic ataxia from dorsal-stream damage can recognise objects but cannot use vision to guide their movements toward them. Some illusions, such as the Ebbinghaus illusion, likewise affect perception more than action, though this remains debated.

Analogous models

A similar dual-route division has been proposed for audition (the auditory "what" and "where" pathways) and the two-streams framework has influenced theories of motor control, attention and consciousness. Later versions emphasise extensive interaction between the streams rather than strict independence.
