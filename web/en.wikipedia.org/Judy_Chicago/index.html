Judy Chicago (born Judith Sylvia Cohen, 20 July 1939) is an American feminist artist, art educator and writer. A founder of the feminist art movement in the United States, she is best known for The Dinner Party (1974–1979), a monumental installation celebrating women's history that is permanently housed at the Brooklyn Museum.

Early life and feminist art

Chicago studied art at UCLA, graduating in 1962 and earning a master's degree in 1964. In the male-dominated Los Angeles art world of the 1960s she made minimalist sculpture and fireworks "atmospheres," and in 1970 — having changed her name from Judy Gerowitz (born Cohen) to Judy Chicago — she founded the first feminist art programme in the United States, at Fresno State College. With Miriam Schapiro she then established the Feminist Art Program at CalArts, whose students produced the landmark 1972 installation Womanhouse.

The Dinner Party

Produced over five years with hundreds of volunteers, The Dinner Party consists of a triangular table set for 39 mythical and historical women — from Sappho and Elizabeth I to Virginia Woolf and Georgia O'Keeffe — with painted and embroidered place settings whose butterfly and floral imagery celebrate female experience, set atop a porcelain "Heritage Floor" inscribed with the names of 999 more women. After years without a permanent home, the work was installed in the Elizabeth A. Sackler Center for Feminist Art at the Brooklyn Museum in 2007.

Later work

Chicago's subsequent collaborative projects include The Birth Project (1980–1985), needlework images of childbirth executed with needleworkers across the United States; Powerplay, on masculinity; and the Holocaust Project (1985–1993), made with her husband, photographer Donald Woodman. Her autobiography is titled Through the Flower. She founded the non-profit organisation Through the Flower and lives and works in Belen, New Mexico.
