Actinide

The actinide or actinoid series encompasses the 15 metallic chemical elements with atomic numbers from 89 to 103, actinium through lawrencium. The series derives its name from the first element, actinium. All actinides are radioactive, and the heavier members are synthetic, produced in nuclear reactors or particle accelerators.

Properties

Actinides are characterised by the gradual filling of the 5f electron shell. They are electropositive, silvery metals that tarnish in air and are pyrophoric when finely divided. Unlike the lanthanides, actinides show a wide range of oxidation states — uranium commonly forms +3 to +6, and plutonium can exhibit +3 through +7 — making their chemistry richer and more varied, particularly for the early members of the series.

Occurrence and uses

Only thorium and uranium occur in nature in quantity; protactinium and actinium appear in trace amounts in uranium ores, and minute quantities of neptunium and plutonium form in uranium deposits by neutron capture. Uranium and plutonium are the principal fuels and explosives of nuclear technology; thorium is a potential reactor fuel, and americium is used in smoke detectors and neutron sources. Handling actinides requires care because of their radioactivity and heavy-metal toxicity.

History

The concept of the actinide series was proposed by Glenn T. Seaborg in 1944, restructuring the periodic table to place the heavy elements in an f-block analogous to the lanthanides — a prediction that guided the discovery of elements 95–103 at Berkeley.
