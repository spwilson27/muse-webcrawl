Sumatra is one of the Sunda Islands of western Indonesia. It is the largest island entirely within Indonesia (Borneo being shared) and the sixth-largest island in the world, at about 473,481 square kilometres including adjacent islands. It stretches some 1,800 km northwest–southeast across the equator, divided by the volcanic Barisan Mountains, and faces the Indian Ocean to the west and the Strait of Malacca to the east.

History

Sumatra's position on the maritime trade route between India and China made it wealthy early: the Buddhist empire of Srivijaya, centred near Palembang, dominated regional trade from the 7th to the 13th century. Marco Polo visited in 1292; Ibn Battuta recorded the Islamic sultanate of Samudera Pasai, the island's first Muslim state. From the 16th century the Sultanate of Aceh grew powerful on the pepper trade, resisting Portuguese and then Dutch expansion. The Dutch gradually subjugated the island over the 19th and early 20th centuries, culminating in the long Aceh War (1873–1904). Sumatra became part of the Republic of Indonesia in 1945–49; a special autonomy settlement ended the Acehnese insurgency in 2005.

Geography and nature

The island's geology — on the Sunda megathrust — produces frequent great earthquakes, including the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami, which devastated Aceh, and the caldera of Lake Toba, site of the largest known eruptive event of the last two million years. Sumatra's rainforests shelter Sumatran tigers, elephants, rhinoceroses and orangutans, but are severely threatened by deforestation for oil palm plantations, making the island a global conservation priority.

Economy

Sumatra produces much of Indonesia's oil and natural gas, palm oil, rubber, coffee (including kopi luwak) and pulpwood. Its ports — Belawan (Medan), Dumai, Palembang — serve the busy Malacca Strait.
