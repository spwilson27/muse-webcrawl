Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants

Signed22 May 2001, Stockholm, Sweden
Effective17 May 2004
PartiesOver 180

The Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants is an international environmental treaty, adopted in 2001 and entering into force in 2004, that aims to eliminate or restrict the production and use of persistent organic pollutants (POPs) — chemicals that persist in the environment, bioaccumulate through the food web, and pose risks to human health and ecosystems.

Scope

The Convention initially listed twelve chemicals, the so-called "dirty dozen", including DDT, aldrin, dieldrin, endrin, chlordane, hexachlorobenzene, mirex, toxaphene, heptachlor and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), along with dioxins and furans as unintentional by-products. Additional substances have since been added through review by the Persistent Organic Pollutants Review Committee.

Implementation

Parties must develop national implementation plans, restrict or eliminate listed chemicals, manage stockpiles and wastes, and reduce releases of unintentionally produced POPs. The Global Environment Facility serves as the financial mechanism supporting implementation in developing countries.
