Illuminated manuscript

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An illuminated manuscript is a formally prepared document where the text is decorated with flourishes such as borders, illuminated initials, and miniature illustrations. Often used in the Roman Catholic Church for prayers and religious texts, the term is today most commonly used for medieval manuscripts, although many illuminated manuscripts were produced in Islamic, Persian, Armenian, and other traditions as well as in the Renaissance.

The term "illumination" refers to the use of gold or silver leaf, which reflected light and made the page appear to glow. Manuscripts were written on parchment or vellum (prepared animal skin) and, later, paper.

History

Illuminated manuscripts survive from late antiquity; among the earliest are the Vergilius Vaticanus and the Rossano Gospels. The tradition flourished in the Insular art of early medieval Britain and Ireland, producing masterpieces such as the Lindisfarne Gospels (c. 715–720) and the Book of Kells (c. 800). Carolingian, Ottonian, Romanesque, and Gothic workshops continued the art, with professional lay artists increasingly involved in the later Middle Ages.

Techniques

Production was a collaborative craft: a scribe wrote the text with quill and ink, leaving spaces for decoration; rubricators added red headings; illuminators painted miniatures and initials, applying gesso, laying gold leaf, burnishing it, and adding pigments derived from minerals, plants, and even precious lapis lazuli (ultramarine). Common decorative elements included historiated initials (containing a scene), inhabited initials, marginalia, and drolleries — whimsical figures in the borders.

Famous examples

Celebrated illuminated manuscripts include the Book of Kells, the Lindisfarne Gospels, the Bedford Hours, the Très Riches Heures du Duc de Berry (illuminated by the Limbourg brothers in the early 15th century), and the Codex Manesse. Outside Europe, richly decorated Qur'ans, Persian epics such as the Great Mongol Shahnameh, and Mughal manuscripts represent parallel traditions.

Decline

The arrival of the printing press in the mid-15th century gradually displaced manuscript production, though hand illumination was sometimes added to early printed books. Deluxe manuscripts continued to be commissioned into the 16th century.
