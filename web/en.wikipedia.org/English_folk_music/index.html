English folk music

English folk music is the traditional music of England, encompassing folk songs, ballads, dance tunes (such as jigs, reels, and hornpipes), morris dancing music, sea shanties, and seasonal customs including wassailing and mummers' plays. It has been collected, revived, and reinterpreted repeatedly since the late 19th century, and has strongly influenced British folk rock and folk music worldwide.

History

Traditional English music was transmitted orally and through cheap printed broadside ballads from the 16th century onward. Cecil Sharp, who began collecting songs in Somerset in 1903, founded the English Folk Dance Society and, with collectors such as Ralph Vaughan Williams, documented thousands of songs and dances that might otherwise have been lost; Vaughan Williams and Gustav Holst wove folk melodies into classical compositions.

A second folk revival after the Second World War, led by figures such as A. L. Lloyd and Ewan MacColl, linked folk song to left-wing politics and industrial traditions. In the late 1960s bands such as Fairport Convention and Steeleye Span pioneered British folk rock, electrifying traditional material. A continuing scene of folk clubs, festivals, and sessions supports performers from Martin Carthy and Shirley Collins to a younger generation of traditional singers and instrumentalists.

Characteristics

English folk song often uses modal melodies and narrative forms — murder ballads, love songs, and songs of work, war, and rural life. Dance traditions include morris dancing with bells and sticks, sword dances in the north, and social country dancing. Instruments commonly include fiddle, melodeon, concertina, guitar, and accordion.
