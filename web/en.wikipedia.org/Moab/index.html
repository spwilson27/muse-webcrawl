Moab

Moab was an ancient Levantine kingdom whose territory is today part of Jordan, located in the highlands east of the Dead Sea between the Zered (Wadi al-Hasa) and the Arnon (Wadi Mujib) rivers. The Moabites spoke a Canaanite language closely related to Hebrew, and their chief god was Chemosh.

History

Moab appears in Egyptian and biblical sources from the Late Bronze and Iron Ages. The Hebrew Bible portrays the Moabites as descendants of Lot and as frequent adversaries of Israel, though also connected by kinship: Ruth, the great-grandmother of David, was a Moabite. The Mesha Stele (Moabite Stone), erected by King Mesha around 840 BC, is the most important Moabite inscription; written in the Moabite language, it records Mesha's wars against the kingdom of Israel and the building projects of his reign.

Moab flourished in the 9th–8th centuries BC, alternately independent and tributary to Israel, Judah, and Assyria. It came under Babylonian and then Persian control, and by the Hellenistic period the distinct Moabite identity had faded, the region becoming part of Nabataean and later Roman territory.

Geography and society

The kingdom's heartland, the Ard al-Karak plateau, receives enough rain for cereal farming and supported towns such as Dibon (the capital under Mesha), Kir of Moab (al-Karak), and Madaba. Transhumant herding of sheep and goats supplemented agriculture. Biblical references to Moabite wine, and the region's position along the King's Highway trade route, point to its commercial importance.
