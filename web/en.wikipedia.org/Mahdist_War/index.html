Mahdist War

The Mahdist War (1881–1899), also known as the Anglo-Sudan War or the Sudanese Mahdist Revolt, was a conflict between the Mahdist movement of Muhammad Ahmad in Sudan and, successively, the forces of the Khedivate of Egypt and the British Empire. The Mahdists overthrew Turco-Egyptian rule, captured Khartoum, and established the Mahdist state, which Britain and Egypt reconquered in 1896–1899 under General Herbert Kitchener.

The Battle of Omdurman (1898) was the decisive engagement of the reconquest.

Origins

Sudan had been ruled by Egypt since the 1820s, with a corrupt and extractive administration, heavy taxation, and — under European pressure — suppression of the lucrative slave trade, which alienated powerful trading interests. In June 1881 Muhammad Ahmad, a Sufi religious leader from Dongola, proclaimed himself the Mahdi and called for jihad against the Turco-Egyptian regime. His first clashes with government troops on Aba Island were Mahdist victories, and the movement spread rapidly westward.

The Mahdist ascendancy

In November 1883 a 10,000-strong Egyptian force under the retired Indian Army officer William Hicks was annihilated near El Obeid. Britain, unwilling to garrison Sudan, ordered evacuation and sent General Charles George Gordon to Khartoum to organise it; instead Gordon fortified the city and was besieged for ten months. Khartoum fell on 26 January 1885, two days before a British relief column arrived, and Gordon was killed — a national scandal in Britain. The Mahdi died that June, but his successor, the Khalifa Abdallahi, held the state through wars with Ethiopia and Egypt, including the Mahdist defeat by British-officered Egyptian troops at Toski in 1889.

The reconquest

Concerned by French and Italian ambitions on the Nile, Britain authorised reconquest in 1896. Kitchener built a railway across the Nubian Desert, advanced methodically with gunboat support, and won at the Atbara (April 1898) and at Omdurman (2 September 1898), where modern rifles, artillery, and Maxim guns inflicted devastating losses on the ansar. The young Winston Churchill rode in the 21st Lancers' charge there and wrote a famous account, The River War (1899). The Khalifa was killed in November 1899. The 1899 condominium agreement then ruled Sudan jointly for Britain and Egypt until independence in 1956.
