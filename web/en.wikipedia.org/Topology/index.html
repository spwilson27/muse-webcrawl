Topology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Topology (from the Greek tópos, "place," and lógos, "study") is the branch of mathematics concerned with the properties of geometric objects that are preserved under continuous deformations such as stretching, twisting, and crumpling, but not tearing or gluing. It is sometimes called "rubber-sheet geometry." A classical illustration of the subject's spirit is that, to a topologist, a coffee cup and a doughnut (torus) have the same shape, since each has exactly one hole and one can be continuously deformed into the other.

History

Topology grew out of geometry and set theory. Leonhard Euler's 1736 solution to the Seven Bridges of Königsberg problem and his polyhedron formula are seen as founding results. Georg Cantor's work on point sets, Henri Poincaré's Analysis Situs (1895), and the axiomatisation of the topological space by Felix Hausdorff in 1914 laid the modern foundations. The 20th century saw the development of algebraic topology by Emmy Noether, Heinz Hopf, and others, and the field was transformed by category-theoretic methods.

Main branches and concepts

General topology (point-set topology) studies the abstract notion of a topological space: a set equipped with a collection of "open sets" satisfying simple axioms, from which concepts like continuity, connectedness, compactness, and convergence are defined. Algebraic topology assigns algebraic invariants — fundamental groups, homology, and cohomology groups — to spaces, allowing them to be distinguished up to homeomorphism. Differential topology studies smooth manifolds and maps, while geometric topology focuses on low-dimensional manifolds. The celebrated Poincaré conjecture, which characterises the 3-sphere, was proved by Grigori Perelman in 2002–2003 using Ricci flow.

Applications

Topological ideas underpin much of modern mathematics, analysis, and geometry, and are applied in physics (topological phases of matter, string theory), data analysis (topological data analysis), robotics (configuration spaces), and computer science (network topology, semantics of computation).
