Economic equilibrium

In economics, economic equilibrium is a situation in which economic forces such as supply and demand are balanced and, in the absence of external influences, the values of economic variables will not change. In a competitive market, equilibrium occurs at the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.

Economic equilibrium
ConditionQuantity supplied = quantity demanded; no agent wishes to change behaviour
Key modelsPartial equilibrium, general equilibrium (Arrow–Debreu)
Related conceptNash equilibrium (game theory)

Partial equilibrium

Partial equilibrium analyses a single market in isolation, holding other prices constant. If price lies above equilibrium, a surplus pushes it down; if below, a shortage pushes it up — the process Alfred Marshall illustrated with supply and demand curves.

General equilibrium

General equilibrium theory, developed by Léon Walras and formalised by Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu in 1954, studies simultaneous equilibrium across all markets. The Arrow–Debreu model proves that, under convexity and other assumptions, a set of prices exists at which every market clears.

Stability and disequilibrium

Not all equilibria are stable or unique. Keynesian economics stresses that economies can settle at underemployment equilibria, while disequilibrium analysis studies rationing and adjustment when prices are sticky.
