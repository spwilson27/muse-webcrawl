Realism (arts)

Realism in the arts is the accurate, detailed and unembellished depiction of nature or of contemporary life. Realism rejects imaginative idealization in favour of a close observation of outward appearances. As such, realism in its broad sense has comprised many artistic currents in different civilizations, from antiquity to the present.

History

The Realist movement began in France in the 1840s, in the aftermath of the Romantic period. Realists sought to depict people of all social classes in situations that arise in ordinary life, and accounted for the rise of the working classes in the wake of the Industrial Revolution. Gustave Courbet's large canvases such as A Burial at Ornans (1849–50) and The Stone Breakers (1850) caused a sensation by treating humble provincial subjects with the monumental scale previously reserved for history painting.

Characteristics

Realist works typically avoid the picturesque, the supernatural and the melodramatic. Subjects are painted as they appear, without beautification, and often with an emphasis on squalor or banality as a form of social critique. In literature, realism is exemplified by the novels of Honoré de Balzac, George Eliot and Gustave Flaubert; in painting by the Barbizon school and the Russian Peredvizhniki (Wanderers).

In literature

Literary realism became the dominant mode of the nineteenth-century novel. Writers such as Charles Dickens, Leo Tolstoy and Mark Twain rendered ordinary experience in dense social detail. Later developments include naturalism, associated with Émile Zola, and twentieth-century currents such as social realism and magical realism.

Legacy

Realism's insistence on observation shaped later movements including Impressionism, and remains a reference point for figurative art and narrative fiction today.
