Rock-cut tomb

A rock-cut tomb is a burial chamber that is cut into an existing rock formation or, loosely, a chamber tomb hewn from stone. Rock-cut tombs were a form of burial and interment chamber used in many cultures throughout prehistory and history, especially in the ancient Near East and Mediterranean world.

Egypt

In ancient Egypt, rock-cut tombs were used extensively in the Middle and New Kingdoms. The nobles' tombs at Beni Hasan, the royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings and Valley of the Queens at Thebes, and the great temples-tombs at Abu Simbel are among the best-known examples. Tomb walls were decorated with paintings and reliefs depicting the life of the deceased and scenes of the afterlife.

Anatolia and the Levant

The Lycian tombs of south-western Turkey, such as those at Myra and Dalyan, imitate wooden house and temple facades in the living rock. Phrygian tombs, the royal tombs of Pontus at Amasya, and the Nabataean tombs at Petra in Jordan also belong to this tradition. In Jerusalem, the so-called Tomb of Absalom and other Kidron Valley monuments are rock-cut funerary structures of the Second Temple period.

Other regions

Rock-cut tombs occur in Etruria (the necropolises of Cerveteri and Tarquinia), in the Jewish necropolis of Beth She'arim in Galilee, in the cliff tombs of Sichuan in China, and in the megalithic cultures of Sardinia, where they are known as domus de janas.
