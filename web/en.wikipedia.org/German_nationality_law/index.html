German nationality law

German nationality law details the conditions under which a person holds German nationality (citizenship). The primary law is the Nationality Act (Staatsangehörigkeitsgesetz), originally enacted in 1913 as the Reich and State Citizenship Act, which remains — extensively amended — the basis of the modern law. German citizenship is also citizenship of the European Union.

Acquisition by descent and birth

German nationality has traditionally been acquired principally by descent (jus sanguinis): a child of a German parent is German regardless of place of birth. Since 1 January 2000, children born in Germany to foreign parents also acquire German citizenship at birth (jus soli) if at least one parent has been legally resident in Germany for a qualifying period (eight years originally, reduced to five by the 2024 reform) and holds a permanent right of residence. For many years such dual nationals were required under the "option model" to choose one citizenship in early adulthood; this obligation was progressively relaxed and abolished for most cases in 2014.

Naturalisation

Foreign residents may naturalise after a period of legal residence — eight years under the older law, reduced by the reform that entered into force on 27 June 2024 to five years, and to three years in cases of special integration achievement. Applicants must generally support themselves, pass a citizenship test on the German legal and social order, demonstrate German language proficiency (usually level B1), and commit to the free democratic basic order, including, since 2024, recognition of Germany's special historical responsibility and the protection of Jewish life. The 2024 reform also generally permits naturalisation without renouncing the previous citizenship, and removes the general requirement that German citizens obtain permission to keep German nationality when acquiring another.

Ethnic German resettlers

Article 116 of the Basic Law and the Federal Expellees Law entitled ethnic Germans from Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union (Aussiedler and Spätaussiedler) to recognition as German citizens upon admission — a channel through which several million people, especially in the late 1980s and 1990s, migrated to Germany.

Loss and historical notes

Citizenship can be lost by voluntary acquisition of a foreign nationality without permission (in cases where permission is still required), by renunciation, or, since 2019, by taking part in combat operations abroad for a terrorist militia if the person holds another nationality. During the Nazi period, Jews and others were stripped of citizenship by decrees such as the Nuremberg Laws; Article 116(2) of the Basic Law provides for restoration to those deprived on political, racial or religious grounds between 1933 and 1945 and their descendants, a right clarified and extended by legislation in 2021.
