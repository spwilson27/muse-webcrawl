Hamiltonian (quantum mechanics)

In quantum mechanics, the Hamiltonian of a system is an operator corresponding to the total energy of that system, including both kinetic energy and potential energy. Its spectrum is the set of possible outcomes when the total energy is measured. The Hamiltonian is named after William Rowan Hamilton, who developed Hamiltonian mechanics in classical physics.

Role in the Schrödinger equation

For a single particle moving in a potential V, the Hamiltonian takes the form

Ĥ = p̂²/2m + V(r̂) = −(ℏ²/2m)∇² + V(r),

where p̂ is the momentum operator and m is the particle's mass. The Hamiltonian generates the time evolution of quantum states through the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, iℏ ∂Ψ/∂t = ĤΨ. States of definite energy satisfy the time-independent eigenvalue equation Ĥψ = Eψ; the corresponding eigenvalues E are the quantised energy levels of the system.

Properties

The Hamiltonian is Hermitian (self-adjoint), which guarantees that energy eigenvalues are real and that time evolution is unitary, conserving probability. For a closed system whose Hamiltonian does not depend explicitly on time, the total energy is conserved. Symmetries of the Hamiltonian correspond, via Noether-type arguments, to conserved quantities: for example, translational invariance implies conservation of momentum.

Examples

  • The hydrogen atom: kinetic energy plus Coulomb potential of the electron in the field of the proton; solving its eigenvalue problem yields the Bohr energy levels.
  • The quantum harmonic oscillator: Ĥ = p̂²/2m + ½mω²x̂², with equally spaced levels E = ℏω(n + ½).
  • Spin and many-body Hamiltonians, such as the Heisenberg and Hubbard models used in condensed-matter physics.
