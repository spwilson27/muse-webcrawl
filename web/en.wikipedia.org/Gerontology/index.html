Gerontology studies the biological, psychological and social dimensions of aging.

Gerontology is the scientific study of aging — the biological, psychological and social processes by which organisms grow old — and of the problems and conditions associated with old age. It is a multidisciplinary field drawing on biology, medicine, psychology, sociology, demography, economics and public policy. Gerontology is distinguished from geriatrics, the branch of medicine concerned with treating disease in older people, although the two fields overlap considerably.

History

The term was coined in 1903 by the Russian biologist and Nobel laureate Ilya Ilyich Mechnikov, from the Greek geron (old man) and logia (study). Interest in the science of aging grew through the twentieth century with rising life expectancy; the Gerontological Society of America was founded in 1945. Modern gerontology expanded rapidly from the 1970s onward as population aging became a central demographic trend in developed countries.

Subfields

Biogerontology

Biogerontology studies the biological mechanisms of aging, including cellular senescence, telomere shortening, accumulated DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction and chronic inflammation. Researchers in this area investigate model organisms — yeast, the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, fruit flies and mice — and search for interventions, such as caloric restriction mimetics and senolytic drugs, that might extend healthspan.

Social gerontology

Social gerontologists examine how aging is shaped by social structures and how older people's roles change over the life course, addressing retirement, pensions, elder care, ageism, family relationships and social isolation. Theories in this tradition include disengagement theory, activity theory and continuity theory.

Other branches

Environmental gerontology studies the fit between older people and their physical surroundings, including housing and age-friendly cities. Financial gerontology concerns retirement income security. Geroscience, an emerging interdisciplinary approach, targets the biology of aging itself as the common driver of chronic diseases of later life.

Population aging

The number of people aged 65 and over worldwide passed 700 million in the early 2020s and is projected by the United Nations to roughly double by 2050, when about one in six people globally will be over 65. This demographic shift drives gerontological research on compressing morbidity — shortening the period of ill health at the end of life — and on sustaining the wellbeing and independence of older populations.
