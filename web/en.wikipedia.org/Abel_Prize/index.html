The Abel Prize is a prize awarded annually by the Norwegian Academy of Science and Letters to one or more outstanding mathematicians. Named after the Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel (1802–1829) and modelled on the Nobel Prizes, it has been awarded since 2003 and is widely regarded, together with the Fields Medal, as one of the highest honours in mathematics — often described as the "Nobel Prize of mathematics".

History

The idea of an international mathematics prize in Abel's name was first proposed in 1899 by Sophus Lie, but was shelved after the dissolution of the union between Sweden and Norway. In 2001 the Norwegian government announced the creation of a new Abel fund with an initial capital of 200 million kroner, timed for the bicentenary of Abel's birth in 2002. The first prize was awarded in 2003 to Jean-Pierre Serre "for playing a key role in shaping the modern form of many parts of mathematics".

Selection and ceremony

The laureate is chosen by an international committee appointed by the Norwegian Academy of Science and Letters, typically containing five leading mathematicians, on the recommendation of bodies such as the International Mathematical Union and the European Mathematical Society. The prize, currently 7.5 million Norwegian kroner, is presented by the King of Norway at a ceremony in Oslo each spring.

Laureates

Laureates include Jean-Pierre Serre (2003), Michael Atiyah and Isadore Singer (2004, for the index theorem), John G. Thompson and Jacques Tits (2008), Mikhail Gromov (2009), John Nash and Louis Nirenberg (2015), Andrew Wiles (2016, for his proof of Fermat's Last Theorem), Karen Uhlenbeck (2019, the first woman to receive the prize), Dennis Sullivan (2022), Luis Caffarelli (2023), Michel Talagrand (2024) and Masaki Kashiwara (2025).
