Plate armour is a type of personal armour made from large metal plates, worn over a padded garment and typically supplemented by mail at the joints. Although partial plate defences existed in the ancient world — Greek bronze cuirasses and Roman lorica segmentata — full plate armour ("white harness") developed in Europe in the late Middle Ages and reached its peak in the 15th and early 16th centuries.

Development

From the 13th century, small plates reinforced mail at the knees, elbows and torso; by about 1420 armourers in Milan and south Germany could produce complete articulated harnesses of hardened steel. Italian (Milanese) armour favoured smooth, rounded surfaces to glance blows, while German Gothic armour used slender, fluted forms; early 16th-century Maximilian armour combined fluting with great strength. Famous centres included Milan, Augsburg, Nuremberg, Innsbruck and the royal workshops at Greenwich.

Components

A full harness comprised a helmet (great bascinet, sallet, armet or close helm), gorget, pauldrons, rerebraces, couters, vambraces and gauntlets for the arms; a cuirass of breastplate and backplate with faulds and tassets; and cuisses, poleyns, greaves and sabatons for the legs. Contrary to popular myth, a man in well-fitted plate armour — weighing some 20–25 kg distributed over the body — could run, mount a horse and even do exercises on the ground; cranes for lifting knights are a modern invention of fiction.

Decline

Plate armour resisted swords and most arrows but was increasingly challenged by crossbows, longbows at close range, and above all firearms. Armourers responded with thicker, proofed breastplates, but by the later 17th century full armour had shrunk to the cuirass and helmet of heavy cavalry, surviving vestigially into the First World War era in ceremonial and cuirassier use. Tournament armour, heavier and highly specialised, developed in parallel.
