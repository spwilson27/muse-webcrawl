Interest

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In finance and economics, interest is payment from a debtor or deposit-taking financial institution to a lender or depositor of an amount above repayment of the principal sum, at a particular rate. It is the price paid for the use of borrowed money, or money earned by deposited funds. Interest may be simple, calculated only on the principal, or compound, calculated on the principal plus accumulated interest.

Calculation

Simple interest is I = P × r × t, where P is principal, r the interest rate per period, and t the number of periods. Compound interest grows according to A = P(1 + r)n; with continuous compounding, A = Pert. The rule of 72 approximates the time for a sum to double at a fixed rate. Rates are quoted as nominal annual rates, annual percentage rates (APR), or effective annual yields, which differ in how fees and compounding frequency are treated.

Determinants and types

Interest rates reflect the time value of money, inflation expectations, credit risk, and monetary policy. Central banks set short-term policy rates (such as the U.S. federal funds rate), influencing market rates across maturities — the pattern known as the yield curve. Interest is charged on loans, mortgages, credit cards, and bonds, and paid on savings accounts and deposits; in Islamic finance, interest (riba) is prohibited and replaced by profit-sharing arrangements.

History

Lending at interest is documented in ancient Mesopotamia, and compound interest calculations appear in Babylonian tablets. Charging interest was condemned as usury by medieval Christian and Islamic teaching, and usury laws capped rates in many countries into the modern era. Interest remains central to modern credit markets, government debt, and investment appraisal (for example, net present value uses an interest or discount rate).
