Partial oxidation

Partial oxidation (often abbreviated POX) is a type of chemical reaction in which a hydrocarbon fuel is reacted with a sub-stoichiometric amount of oxygen — less than would be needed for complete combustion — to produce a mixture of hydrogen and carbon monoxide known as synthesis gas, or syngas. The reaction is mildly exothermic and typically runs at high temperature, around 1,300–1,500 °C, either without a catalyst (thermal partial oxidation) or over a catalyst at lower temperatures (catalytic partial oxidation).

A non-catalytic partial oxidation reactor: fuel and oxygen enter at top, syngas leaves below.

Reaction

For methane, the idealised reaction is

CH4 + ½O2 → CO + 2H2

Heavier hydrocarbon feeds give correspondingly more complex product distributions. The hydrogen-to-carbon-monoxide ratio of the syngas depends on the feedstock: it is about 2 for natural gas and lower, often near 1, for heavy oils and coal-derived feeds, and it is adjusted downstream (for example by the water–gas shift reaction) to suit the intended product.

Technology and uses

Thermal partial oxidation is a workhorse of large-scale syngas production, able to handle feedstocks from refinery residues and asphalt to natural gas; commercial processes include the Texaco and Shell gasification processes. The syngas is used to make hydrogen for refineries and ammonia synthesis, methanol, and synthetic liquid fuels via Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. Compared with steam reforming, partial oxidation needs no external heat supply and handles heavy feeds, but it requires an expensive oxygen plant and generally yields a lower H2/CO ratio. Combined with gasification of coal, biomass, or wastes, the same chemistry underlies integrated gasification combined-cycle power generation.
