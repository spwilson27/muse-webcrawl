Apophenia

Apophenia (/æpoʊˈfiːniə/) is the tendency to perceive meaningful connections or patterns between unrelated things. The term (German: Apophänie, from the Greek ἀποφαίνειν, "to make appear") was coined by psychiatrist Klaus Conrad in his 1958 publication on the beginning stages of schizophrenia. He defined it as the "unmotivated seeing of connections [accompanied by] a specific feeling of abnormal meaningfulness," but it has come to represent the human propensity to seek patterns in random information in general, such as gambling.

Perceiving a face in random stimuli is a common form of apophenia (pareidolia)

Overview

Apophenia is related to pareidolia, the perception of images or patterns — most famously faces — in vague or random stimuli. In statistics, apophenia is associated with Type I errors: concluding that a pattern exists when it does not. The tendency is thought to be an evolved byproduct of the brain's pattern-recognition machinery; seeing a pattern where none exists is often less costly than missing a real one.

Examples

Common examples include seeing faces in clouds or in the Man in the Moon, conspiracy theories built on coincidences, gamblers perceiving "hot hands" or runs of luck in games of chance, and perceiving hidden messages in reversed audio recordings. Manifestations of apophenia have been linked to creative thinking as well as to psychotic disorders, where excessive apophenia (sometimes termed "apophany") can produce delusions of reference.

In culture

The concept appears in works exploring perception and coincidence, and it has been invoked in discussions of divination, synchronicity (as proposed by Carl Jung), and the psychology of superstition. Michael Shermer popularized the related term "patternicity" in The Believing Brain (2011).
