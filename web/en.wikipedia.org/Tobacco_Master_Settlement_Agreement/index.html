The Tobacco Master Settlement Agreement (MSA) was entered into in November 1998 between the attorneys general of 46 US states, Washington, D.C., Puerto Rico and the US territories, and the four largest American tobacco companies: Philip Morris Inc., R. J. Reynolds, Brown & Williamson and Lorillard. It settled the states' lawsuits seeking recovery of Medicaid costs for treating smoking-related illness, exempted the companies from further state claims of that kind, and imposed sweeping restrictions on cigarette advertising and marketing in exchange for payments of at least $206 billion over the agreement's first 25 years, with payments continuing in perpetuity based on cigarette sales volume.

Background

In the 1990s, US states began suing the tobacco industry to recover public health-care costs, beginning with Mississippi in 1994 under attorney general Mike Moore. Leaked and litigated industry documents revealed that companies had long known of smoking's dangers and nicotine's addictiveness while publicly denying them. Four states — Mississippi, Florida, Texas and Minnesota — settled individually for a combined total of about $40 billion before the national agreement was reached; a broader proposed global settlement requiring congressional action collapsed in 1998, leading to the MSA.

Terms

Beyond the payments, the agreement prohibited tobacco advertising aimed at youth, banned cartoon characters in cigarette advertising (ending Joe Camel), restricted billboard and transit advertising, limited brand sponsorships and the distribution of free samples and branded merchandise, and required the dissolution of industry bodies such as the Tobacco Institute and the Center for Indoor Air Research. It also funded the American Legacy Foundation (now the Truth Initiative), which ran the "truth" anti-smoking youth campaign, and established the National Association of Attorneys General as the agreement's coordinating body.

Effects and criticism

Cigarette prices rose as manufacturers passed settlement costs to consumers, and US smoking rates continued their decline. Critics have argued that states spent only a small fraction of MSA funds on tobacco prevention, that many states securitised their payment streams into "tobacco bonds", and that the agreement's structure — tying state revenue to cigarette sales and imposing cost burdens that disadvantage smaller non-participating manufacturers — entrenched the major companies' market position. The MSA nevertheless remains the largest civil settlement in American history and a landmark in tobacco regulation, preceding the FDA's statutory authority over tobacco granted by the Family Smoking Prevention and Tobacco Control Act of 2009.
