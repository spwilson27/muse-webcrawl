A blood bank is a center where blood gathered as a result of blood donation is stored and preserved for later use in blood transfusion. The term also refers to a hospital division where blood products are stored and compatibility testing is performed.

Operations

Donated whole blood is typically separated by centrifugation into components — red blood cells, plasma, and platelets — so that each donation can benefit several patients. Every unit is typed for the ABO and Rh systems and screened for transfusion-transmitted infections including HIV, hepatitis B and C, and syphilis. Red cells are refrigerated for up to 42 days, plasma is frozen for up to a year, and platelets are kept at room temperature with agitation for about 5–7 days.

History

Bernard Fantus established the first hospital blood bank in the United States at Cook County Hospital in Chicago in 1937, coining the term itself. Large-scale blood banking expanded rapidly during the Second World War, and organizations such as the Red Cross built national collection systems that remain the backbone of transfusion medicine.
