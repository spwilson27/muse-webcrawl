Amortization

Amortization is an accounting and finance term with two principal meanings. In lending, amortization is the practice of paying off a debt over time through regular, scheduled payments, each of which covers both interest and principal. In accounting, amortization refers to allocating the cost of an intangible asset over its useful life, analogous to depreciation for tangible assets.

Amortizing loans

With an amortizing loan such as a typical fixed-rate mortgage, each monthly payment is the same amount, but the proportions change: early payments are mostly interest, while later payments are mostly principal. An amortization schedule is a table detailing each periodic payment, the amount applied to interest, the amount applied to principal, and the remaining balance. In the earliest years of a long mortgage the borrower builds equity slowly; the balance declines ever faster as the term progresses.

Negative amortization

Negative amortization occurs when the scheduled payment is smaller than the interest due, so the unpaid interest is added to the principal and the loan balance grows. Some adjustable-rate mortgages permitted this, and the practice contributed to borrower distress during the 2008 financial crisis.

Accounting amortization

In accounting, the cost of intangible assets — patents, copyrights, franchises and similar rights — is amortized over the periods in which they are expected to provide benefit. Goodwill is generally not amortized under modern standards but is instead tested periodically for impairment.
