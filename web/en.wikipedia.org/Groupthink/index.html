Groupthink

Groupthink is a psychological phenomenon that occurs within a group of people in which the desire for harmony or conformity in the group results in an irrational or dysfunctional decision-making outcome. Cohesiveness, or the desire for cohesiveness, in such a group may produce a tendency among its members to agree at all costs. This causes the group to minimize conflict and reach a consensus decision without critical evaluation.

The term was coined by social psychologist Irving Janis in 1972, drawing on case studies of United States foreign policy decisions, including the failure to anticipate the attack on Pearl Harbor, the Korean War escalation, the Bay of Pigs Invasion, and the escalation of the Vietnam War.

Symptoms

Janis identified eight symptoms of groupthink: an illusion of invulnerability; collective rationalization; belief in the inherent morality of the group; stereotyped views of out-groups; direct pressure on dissenters; self-censorship; an illusion of unanimity; and self-appointed "mindguards" who shield the group from dissenting information. Antecedent conditions include high group cohesiveness, insulation from outside information, directive leadership, and high stress from external threats.

Prevention

Janis proposed remedies including assigning a devil's advocate, encouraging leaders to withhold their opinions initially, consulting outside experts, holding "second-chance" meetings, and subdividing the group for independent deliberation. These techniques have been adopted in organizational decision-making and intelligence analysis, including structured analytic techniques used by intelligence agencies.

Criticism and research

Subsequent research has found the full groupthink model only partially supported; cohesiveness alone does not reliably produce defective decisions, and researchers have proposed revisions emphasizing leadership style, group identity, and political positioning. Nevertheless, "groupthink" remains a widely used shorthand for failures of collective critical thinking.
