NotPetya

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

NotPetya was a destructive computer malware attack launched on 27 June 2017, initially targeting organizations in Ukraine before spreading globally. Although it resembled the Petya family of ransomware and displayed a ransom note demanding payment in bitcoin, security researchers quickly determined that it was not genuine ransomware: the malware irreversibly overwrote victims' master boot records and was designed to destroy data rather than to extort money. It is widely considered one of the most costly cyberattacks in history, causing an estimated $10 billion in damages.

The attack

NotPetya was seeded through a compromised update server for M.E.Doc, a Ukrainian tax accounting software package widely used by companies doing business in Ukraine. From infected machines it spread rapidly within corporate networks using multiple propagation methods, including the EternalBlue exploit of the Windows SMB protocol (also used weeks earlier by the WannaCry worm), credential theft with Mimikatz, and legitimate Windows administrative tools such as PsExec and WMIC.

Major victims included the shipping conglomerate Maersk, the pharmaceutical company Merck, FedEx's European subsidiary TNT Express, the advertising group WPP, and Ukrainian banks, ministries, metro systems, and the Chornobyl nuclear plant's radiation monitoring systems. The attack disrupted ports, logistics, and manufacturing worldwide for days or weeks.

Attribution

In February 2018 the United Kingdom and the United States formally attributed NotPetya to the Russian military, describing it as part of Russia's ongoing campaign against Ukraine and the most destructive and costly cyberattack in history at that time. The US later indicted six officers of the GRU's Unit 74455, known as Sandworm, in connection with NotPetya and other attacks. Russia denied involvement.

Technical details

NotPetya overwrote the master boot record with a malicious loader that mimicked Petya's encryption routine, displayed a skull-and-crossbones screen and ransom note, and generated an "installation ID" that was random data rather than a usable decryption key — making recovery by payment impossible even in principle. It also encrypted individual files and, on many systems, simply destroyed the file system structures.
