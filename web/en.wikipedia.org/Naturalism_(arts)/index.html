Naturalism (arts)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Naturalism in art refers to the depiction of realistic objects in a natural setting. The term arose in the mid-19th century, particularly in France, for art that sought to reproduce nature and everyday life as objectively as possible, including subjects—peasants, workers, the poor—previously considered unworthy of art. It is closely related to, and often used interchangeably with, Realism, though naturalism is sometimes distinguished as Realism informed by scientific determinism.

Origins

Precursors include the Dutch Golden Age painting of everyday life and the Barbizon school of landscape painters. Gustave Courbet's Realist manifesto of the 1850s paved the way for painters such as Jean-François Millet and Jules Breton, whose unidealized scenes of rural labour embodied naturalist aims.

Literary naturalism

In literature, naturalism developed from realism into a movement that applied ideas of scientific determinism—heredity and environment, in the work of Hippolyte Taine and the example of Charles Darwin and Claude Bernard—to fiction. Émile Zola, its chief theorist, argued in The Experimental Novel (1880) that the novelist should observe characters as a scientist observes phenomena. His Rougon-Macquart cycle (1871–1893) chronicles generations of a family shaped by heredity and milieu. Naturalist writing spread internationally through authors such as Stephen Crane, Frank Norris, Theodore Dreiser, Thomas Hardy, and Gerhart Hauptmann in drama.

Theatre

Naturalist theatre pursued the illusion of real life through detailed sets, colloquial dialogue and ensemble acting, as in the plays of Henrik Ibsen and Anton Chekhov, André Antoine's Théâtre Libre in Paris, and the theories of Konstantin Stanislavski.
