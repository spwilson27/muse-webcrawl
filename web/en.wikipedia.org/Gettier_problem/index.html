Gettier problem
FieldEpistemology
Introduced byEdmund Gettier, 1963
Source paper"Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?", Analysis 23(6)
TargetThe justified true belief (JTB) account of knowledge

Gettier cases present justified true beliefs that intuitively are not knowledge.

The Gettier problem is a landmark challenge in epistemology to the traditional definition of knowledge as justified true belief. In a three-page paper published in the journal Analysis in 1963, the American philosopher Edmund Gettier presented counterexamples — now called Gettier cases — in which a person has a belief that is both true and supported by good evidence, yet the truth of the belief depends on luck in a way that makes it intuitively fall short of knowledge.

Background

Since Plato's Theaetetus, philosophers had commonly analysed knowledge as justified true belief: a subject S knows that p if and only if p is true, S believes that p, and S is justified in believing that p. Gettier argued these three conditions, though perhaps necessary, are not sufficient.

Gettier's cases

The job and the coins

Smith and Jones have applied for a job. Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get the job, and also knows that Jones has ten coins in his pocket. Smith therefore infers: "The man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket." Unknown to Smith, he himself will get the job — and, by coincidence, he too has ten coins in his pocket. Smith's belief is true and justified, but it seems to be true only by luck, so Smith does not know it.

The Ford and Barcelona

Smith has strong evidence that his friend Jones owns a Ford, and infers the disjunction "Either Jones owns a Ford, or Brown is in Barcelona," choosing Barcelona at random. In fact Jones no longer owns a Ford — but Brown, by sheer coincidence, happens to be in Barcelona. Again the belief is true and justified, yet not knowledge.

Responses

Gettier's paper transformed epistemology and generated a large literature. Proposed repairs to the JTB account include adding a "no false lemmas" condition (the belief must not be inferred from any false premise), defeasibility theories (the justification must not be undermined by any true proposition), reliabilism (the belief must be produced by a reliable cognitive process), and causal theories. Alvin Goldman's "fake barn" cases extended the problem to perceptual knowledge. Other philosophers, such as Timothy Williamson, argue that knowledge is a primitive state that cannot be analysed into belief plus further conditions at all ("knowledge first" epistemology). Experimental philosophers have also tested whether Gettier intuitions are shared across cultures, with some studies suggesting variation.

Significance

Although only three pages long, Gettier's article is among the most influential philosophy papers of the twentieth century. The problem remains a standard test case: any adequate theory of knowledge must explain why Gettier subjects fail to know.
