The Argonaute protein family plays a central role in RNA silencing processes, as the essential catalytic components of the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). Argonaute proteins bind small non-coding RNAs — microRNAs (miRNAs), small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), and Piwi-interacting RNAs (piRNAs) — and use them as guides to find complementary target messenger RNAs, which they silence by endonucleolytic cleavage, translational repression, or deadenylation and decay.

Structure and mechanism

Argonaute proteins (about 100 kDa) have a bilobed architecture: an N-terminal lobe containing the N and PAZ domains, which anchor the 3′ end of the guide RNA, and a C-terminal lobe containing the MID domain, which binds the guide's 5′ phosphate, and the PIWI domain, an RNase H-like fold that in "slicer-competent" proteins cleaves the target strand. Humans have eight Argonaute genes; Ago2 is the principal slicer. The family was named after the leaf phenotype of an Arabidopsis mutant, which resembled a squid (Argonauta).

Subfamilies

Eukaryotic Argonautes divide into the Ago subfamily (ubiquitous, binds miRNAs and siRNAs) and the Piwi subfamily (mainly in the germline, binds piRNAs and defends against transposons). Argonaute-like proteins also occur in many bacteria and archaea, where they are implicated in defence against foreign nucleic acids and are being developed as programmable DNA/RNA-guided tools.
