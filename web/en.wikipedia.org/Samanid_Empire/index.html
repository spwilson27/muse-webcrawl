Samanid Empire

Existence: 819–999 CE

Capital: Bukhara

Languages: Persian (court and literary), Arabic (administration and scholarship)

Religion: Sunni Islam

Notable rulers: Ismail Samani, Nasr II, Nuh II

The Samanid Empire (Persian: سامانیان, Sāmāniyān) was a Persian Sunni Muslim empire that ruled over much of Central Asia and Greater Khorasan from 819 to 999 CE. At its height it stretched from central Persia to the Indus valley, encompassing modern Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Afghanistan and parts of Iran, Kazakhstan and Pakistan. The dynasty is named after its eponymous ancestor, Saman Khuda, a landowner from the village of Saman near Balkh who converted from Zoroastrianism to Islam.

History

The four grandsons of Saman Khuda were rewarded with governorships in Ferghana, Samarkand, Shash (Tashkent) and Herat for their service to the Abbasid caliph al-Ma’mun. From these appanages the family steadily consolidated power in Transoxiana. In 892 Ismail Samani united the Samanid lands, defeating his brother Nasr I and then crushing the Saffarids at Balkh in 900, bringing Khorasan under Samanid rule.

Under Ismail and his successors, above all Nasr II (914–943), the empire enjoyed stability, a flourishing Silk Road trade and a remarkably efficient administration modelled on Baghdad but run largely in Persian. Bukhara became one of the great intellectual centres of the Islamic world, rivalling Baghdad.

From the mid-10th century the empire weakened through succession disputes, the growing autonomy of Turkish military commanders (ghulams) such as Alp Tigin, and pressure from the Turkic Karakhanids to the east. In 999 the Karakhanids occupied Bukhara, ending Samanid rule; the last emir, Isma’il Muntasir, was assassinated in 1005 while trying to restore the dynasty.

Culture and legacy

The Samanid period is remembered as a renaissance of Persian language and culture within an Islamic framework. New Persian, written in the Arabic script, emerged as a literary language at the Samanid court. The poet Rudaki, often called the father of Persian poetry, flourished under Nasr II; the epic tradition that culminated in Ferdowsi’s Shahnameh was nurtured under Samanid patronage, and the physician-philosopher Ibn Sina (Avicenna) was educated in Bukhara and served the Samanid court. Samanid Bukhara also produced celebrated ceramics and the exquisite brickwork of the Samanid mausoleum.

The Samanids are central to the national histories of Tajikistan and Uzbekistan; Tajikistan’s currency, the somoni, is named after Ismail Samani.
