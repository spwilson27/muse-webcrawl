Electron paramagnetic resonance

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) or electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy is a method for studying materials that have unpaired electrons. In a magnetic field, the energy levels of an unpaired electron split (the Zeeman effect), and the electron absorbs microwave radiation at a characteristic resonance frequency.

EPR spectra reveal the local environment of unpaired electrons, such as free radicals and transition-metal ions.

Principle

An electron has spin 1/2 and an associated magnetic moment. In an external magnetic field the two spin states differ in energy; microwave photons matching the energy gap are absorbed. Most instruments operate at X-band (around 9.5 GHz, field ~0.35 tesla for a free electron, g ≈ 2.0023). Deviations of the g-factor and splitting by nearby magnetic nuclei (hyperfine coupling) provide chemical information.

History and applications

EPR was first observed by Yevgeny Zavoisky in 1944 at Kazan State University. It is applied in chemistry and materials science to free radicals, in biology to metalloproteins, spin labels, and radiation dosimetry, and in archaeology and geology for dating (e.g., of tooth enamel and quartz). Unlike NMR, which probes nuclear spins, EPR probes electron spins and requires paramagnetic species.
