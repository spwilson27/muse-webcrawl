The Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops aduncus) is a species of bottlenose dolphin inhabiting the coastal waters of the Indian Ocean, the western and central Pacific, and the seas around Australia, Japan, and East Africa. It was recognised as a species distinct from the common bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops truncatus) in 1998 on the basis of genetic and morphological evidence.

Description

Adults generally measure about 2.6 metres (8.5 ft) and weigh up to 230 kilograms. Compared with the common bottlenose dolphin, the Indo-Pacific species has a longer, more slender beak, a more triangular dorsal fin, and — in adults — dark speckling on the belly and flanks. It lives almost exclusively in shallow coastal water less than about 30 metres deep, over reefs and sandy bays.

Behaviour and ecology

These dolphins live in fission–fusion societies of typically 5–25 individuals. They feed on a wide variety of reef and schooling fish and squid, and exhibit regionally distinctive foraging techniques, including "sponging" — carrying a marine sponge on the beak while probing the sea floor — documented in Shark Bay, Australia, and transmitted culturally from mother to calf. Males form long-term alliances to compete for access to females.

Conservation

The IUCN lists the species as Near Threatened. Its inshore habits expose it to gillnet bycatch, shark-control nets, coastal development, pollution, and drive fisheries in some regions. Coastal populations are often small and resident, making local depletion a serious risk.
