Devised by: Charles F. Richter (with Beno Gutenberg)

Richter scale

The Richter scale — more precisely the Richter magnitude scale, or local magnitude scale (ML) — is a measure of the strength of earthquakes, developed by Charles F. Richter in collaboration with Beno Gutenberg and presented in 1935.

Definition

The magnitude is calculated from the logarithm of the amplitude of waves recorded by seismographs, adjusted for the distance between the seismograph and the earthquake's epicentre. The scale is logarithmic: each whole-number increase corresponds to roughly a tenfold increase in measured amplitude and about 31.6 times more energy released.

Use and successors

Originally designed for shallow, moderate earthquakes in Southern California, the Richter scale saturates for very large earthquakes and has largely been superseded for scientific purposes by the moment magnitude scale (Mw), which better measures total energy. "Richter scale" remains the term most often used by news media for earthquake magnitudes generally.

Typical effects

Magnitudes below 2.0 are usually not felt; 4.0 can cause moderate damage near the epicentre; 7.0 and above are major earthquakes capable of widespread destruction.
