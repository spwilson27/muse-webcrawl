V(D)J recombination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

V(D)J recombination assembles antibody and T-cell receptor genes from separate V, D and J segments.

V(D)J recombination is the unique mechanism of genetic recombination that occurs only in developing lymphocytes during the early stages of T and B cell maturation. It results in the highly diverse repertoire of antibodies/immunoglobulins and T cell receptors (TCRs) found on B cells and T cells, respectively. The process is a defining feature of the adaptive immune system, which evolved in jawed vertebrates roughly 500 million years ago.

The newly formed lymphocyte exits the bone marrow (for B cells) or thymus (for T cells) with a unique antigen receptor generated by randomly selecting and combining variable (V), diversity (D) and joining (J) gene segments. Combinatorial and junctional diversity together can generate on the order of 1011 or more distinct receptor specificities from a modest set of gene segments.

Mechanism

Recombination signal sequences

Each coding segment is flanked by a recombination signal sequence (RSS) consisting of a conserved heptamer and nonamer separated by a spacer of 12 or 23 base pairs. Recombination occurs only between a 12-RSS and a 23-RSS (the "12/23 rule"), which enforces the correct order of assembly — for example, preventing direct V-to-J joining in loci where a D segment must be included.

Cutting and joining

The lymphoid-specific proteins RAG1 and RAG2 (recombination-activating genes) bind the RSSs and introduce double-strand breaks at the coding ends, forming hairpin intermediates. The hairpins are opened — often asymmetrically, generating short palindromic "P" nucleotides — by the Artemis nuclease in complex with DNA-PKcs. Exonucleases trim the ends, and the enzyme terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) adds random non-templated "N" nucleotides. The general non-homologous end-joining (NHEJ) machinery (Ku70/80, XRCC4, DNA ligase IV, XLF) then ligates the coding ends to form the coding joint, while the signal ends are joined precisely into a signal joint, usually on an excised circular piece of DNA.

Diversity generation

  • Combinatorial diversity: random choice among multiple V, D and J segments at each locus, and pairing of independently rearranged heavy/light (or α/β) chains.
  • Junctional diversity: imprecise joining — P nucleotides, exonuclease trimming and N-nucleotide addition — concentrated in the CDR3 region that most directly contacts antigen.

After antigen encounter, B cells further diversify their receptors by somatic hypermutation and class-switch recombination, which are separate processes.

Immunoglobulin and TCR loci

In humans the immunoglobulin heavy-chain locus (chromosome 14) contains roughly 40 functional VH, 23 D and 6 JH segments; the κ and λ light-chain loci contain V and J segments but no D segments, so light chains rearrange by V–J joining only. Heavy chains rearrange first (D→J, then V→DJ); a successful, non-autoreactive product signals allelic exclusion, ensuring each lymphocyte expresses a single specificity. T-cell receptor β, γ and δ loci use all three segment types; the α locus uses V and J.

Defects

Mutations in RAG1, RAG2, Artemis (DCLRE1C) and other pathway components cause severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID), because neither B nor T cells can mature; hypomorphic RAG mutations cause Omenn syndrome, with oligoclonal, autoreactive T cells. Aberrant recombination can also contribute to lymphoid malignancies by placing proto-oncogenes under immunoglobulin-locus enhancers.

History

The "two genes, one polypeptide" hypothesis of Dreyer and Bennett (1965) anticipated gene rearrangement. Susumu Tonegawa demonstrated somatic rearrangement of immunoglobulin genes in 1976, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1987.
