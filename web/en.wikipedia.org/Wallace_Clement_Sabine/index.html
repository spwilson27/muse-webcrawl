Wallace Clement Sabine


Born: 13 June 1868, Richmond, Indiana
Died: 10 January 1919, Boston, Massachusetts
Field: Physics (acoustics)
Institution: Harvard University
Known for: Architectural acoustics; Sabine's law; the sabin unit

Wallace Clement Ware Sabine (13 June 1868 – 10 January 1919) was an American physicist who founded the field of architectural acoustics. His investigations into the acoustics of Harvard's Fogg Lecture Hall and his consulting work on Boston Symphony Hall established reverberation time as the central measurable quantity in the design of concert halls and lecture rooms.

Reverberation research

In 1895 Sabine, then a young Harvard instructor, was asked to fix the nearly unusable acoustics of the lecture theatre in the new Fogg Art Museum. Working at night, he moved seat cushions in and out of the hall as absorbers and timed how long an organ-pipe tone remained audible after it stopped, using a stopwatch and his own ears. From thousands of such measurements he derived Sabine's law: the reverberation time of a room is proportional to its volume and inversely proportional to the total sound absorption within it,

T = 0.161 V / A (in metric units)

where T is the time for a sound to decay by 60 decibels, V the room volume and A the total absorption. The unit of absorption, the sabin — one square foot of perfectly absorbing material — is named after him.

Career and legacy

Applying his formula as consultant for Symphony Hall, Boston (opened 1900), Sabine helped produce one of the most admired concert halls in the world. His collected papers were published posthumously as Collected Papers on Acoustics (1922). Harvard's acoustics laboratory was absorbed into what became the Riverbank Laboratories in Illinois, where his cousin Paul Sabine continued the work. Sabine is regarded as the founder of a quantitative, scientific approach to the sound of buildings.
