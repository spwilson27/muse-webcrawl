Due process

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Due process requires fair procedures before the state may deprive anyone of liberty or property.

Due process is the legal requirement that the state must respect all legal rights owed to a person. It balances the power of the state and protects individuals from arbitrary or abusive exercises of power. The phrase originates in the Magna Carta (1215), which promised "no free man shall be seized or imprisoned... except by the lawful judgment of his equals or by the law of the land."

In the United States, due process is guaranteed by the Fifth Amendment (against the federal government) and the Fourteenth Amendment (against the states). Courts distinguish procedural due process — fair procedures — from substantive due process — limits on what the government may regulate at all.

Procedural due process

Procedural due process requires, at a minimum: adequate notice of the government's intended action, an opportunity to be heard before an impartial tribunal, the right to present evidence and confront witnesses, and a decision based on the record. What process is "due" depends on the interests at stake, under the balancing test of Mathews v. Eldridge (1976).

Substantive due process

Substantive due process protects certain fundamental rights from government interference regardless of the procedures used. Under this doctrine the Supreme Court has recognized rights including privacy, contraception (Griswold v. Connecticut), abortion (Roe v. Wade, later limited by Dobbs v. Jackson), and same-sex marriage (Obergefell v. Hodges). The doctrine remains controversial, with critics arguing it lets judges constitutionalize policy preferences.

History

The phrase "due process of law" first appeared in a 1354 English statute interpreting Magna Carta. It crossed into American law through colonial charters and the Bill of Rights, and the Fourteenth Amendment (1868) extended its protections against state governments, becoming a foundation for the incorporation of most of the Bill of Rights against the states.

Outside the United States

Analogous principles exist worldwide: the right to a fair trial under Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights, natural justice in Commonwealth law, and constitutional guarantees of legal procedure in civil-law systems.
