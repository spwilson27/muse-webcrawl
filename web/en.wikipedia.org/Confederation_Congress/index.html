Confederation Congress

The Confederation Congress, formally styled the Congress of the Confederation and, in its earlier sessions, the Continental Congress, was the governing body of the United States of America during the Confederation period, from 1 March 1781 until 4 March 1789, under the Articles of Confederation. It was a unicameral body in which each state delegation had one vote.

Powers and limits

The Congress could declare war, conduct foreign affairs, coin money, and settle disputes between states, but it lacked the power to levy taxes or regulate commerce, and amendments to the Articles required unanimous state consent. It depended on requisitions from the states, which were frequently unpaid. Notable achievements included the Treaty of Paris (1783), which ended the Revolutionary War, and the Northwest Ordinance of 1787.

Decline and replacement

Weaknesses exposed by events such as Shays' Rebellion led to the Philadelphia Convention of 1787, which drafted the United States Constitution. After the Constitution was ratified by the requisite nine states, the Confederation Congress set the timetable for the new government and met for the last time in 1789; its presiding officer was styled the President of the United States in Congress Assembled.
