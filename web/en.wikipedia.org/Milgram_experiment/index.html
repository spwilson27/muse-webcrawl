Milgram experiment

The Milgram experiment was a series of social psychology experiments conducted by Yale University psychologist Stanley Milgram beginning in 1961. The experiments measured the willingness of participants to obey an authority figure who instructed them to perform acts — administering what appeared to be increasingly dangerous electric shocks to another person — that conflicted with their personal conscience.

[Image: The shock generator used in Milgram's obedience experiments]

Procedure

Each session involved a volunteer "teacher", an experimenter in a lab coat, and a "learner" (a confederate) strapped to a chair in another room. The teacher was told to deliver a shock for every wrong answer, escalating from 15 volts to a labelled maximum of 450 volts. The learner's protests were pre-recorded; no real shocks were given. If the teacher hesitated, the experimenter applied a series of standardised verbal prods such as "The experiment requires that you continue."

Results

In the best-known baseline condition, 65 per cent (26 of 40) of participants continued to the maximum 450 volts, and all participants continued to at least 300 volts. Obedience fell when the experiment moved away from Yale, when the victim was in the same room, or when peers disobeyed. Milgram interpreted the results through "agentic state" theory: people in hierarchical situations shift responsibility to the authority figure.

Criticism and legacy

The study provoked lasting debate about research ethics — deception, distress and the adequacy of debriefing — and helped spur modern institutional review standards. Replications and partial replications in several countries have generally found comparably high obedience. The experiments are routinely cited in discussions of how ordinary people participate in harmful institutional acts.
