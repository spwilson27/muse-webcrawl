Integral domain


Integral domains sit between commutative rings and fields in the hierarchy of ring-like structures.

In algebra, an integral domain is a non-zero commutative ring in which the product of any two non-zero elements is non-zero — equivalently, a commutative ring with no zero divisors other than zero itself. The defining property gives a cancellation law: if ab = ac and a ≠ 0, then b = c. The name reflects the prototypical example, the ring of integers ℤ, which behaves in many respects like the integers even though most integral domains are much larger.

Examples

Every field is an integral domain, as is every subring of a field. Examples include the integers, the Gaussian integers ℤ[i], polynomial rings over a domain (such as ℝ[x]), and rings of algebraic integers. The ring ℤ/nℤ of integers modulo n is an integral domain exactly when n is prime, in which case it is a field. Rings with zero divisors, such as ℤ/6ℤ (where 2·3 = 0) or rings of matrices, are not domains.

Field of fractions

Every integral domain can be embedded in a field, its field of fractions (or quotient field), just as ℤ embeds in the rationals ℚ. This construction allows division by any non-zero element and underlies much of commutative algebra and algebraic geometry. Related classes of domains include unique factorisation domains, principal ideal domains and Euclidean domains, each imposing stronger arithmetic properties; every Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain, and every principal ideal domain has unique factorisation.
