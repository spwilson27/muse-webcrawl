New-age music

New-age music is a genre of music intended to create artistic inspiration, relaxation, and optimism. It is used by listeners for yoga, massage, meditation, and reading as a method of stress management or to create a peaceful atmosphere. New-age music is characterised by gentle, layered sounds — acoustic instruments such as piano, flute and harp, or electronic textures from synthesisers — often combined with nature sounds, and by slow tempos, consonant harmonies and a lack of abrupt changes.

History

The genre emerged in the 1960s and 1970s from the confluence of ambient music, minimalism, jazz fusion and the countercultural New Age movement. Early figures included Tony Scott, whose Music for Zen Meditation (1964) is often cited as a precursor, and artists such as Iasos, Steven Halpern and Deuter. In the late 1970s and 1980s, Windham Hill Records and labels such as Narada popularised acoustic and electronic new age respectively. Artists including George Winston, Kitarō, Vangelis, Andreas Vollenweider and Enya brought the style to mass audiences; Enya's Orinoco Flow (1988) became a worldwide hit. The Grammy Award for Best New Age Album was introduced in 1987.

Characteristics and reception

New-age music tends to be melodic and consonant, built on modal harmonies and repetitive motifs, and frequently instrumental. It overlaps with ambient, world, smooth jazz and contemporary instrumental music, and its boundaries are disputed. Critics have sometimes dismissed it as bland background music, while its defenders and the wellness industry have made it a durable commercial category, widely used in spas, therapy and film soundtracks.
