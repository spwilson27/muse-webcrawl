Afterimage

An afterimage is an image that continues to appear in the eyes after exposure to the original image has ceased. Afterimages occur because photochemical activity in the retina continues even when the stimulus is removed, and they are a normal phenomenon of the visual system, exploited in many optical illusions.

Negative afterimages

A negative afterimage appears in complementary colours after staring at a brightly coloured object for tens of seconds and then looking at a plain white or grey surface. The effect is explained by fatigue of the photoreceptors: the cone cells most stimulated by the original colour become less sensitive, so the white surface — which reflects all colours equally — is perceived with the fatigued colour subtracted, leaving its complement. An American flag rendered in green, black, and yellow thus produces a ghostly red, white, and blue afterimage.

Positive afterimages

Positive afterimages retain the original colours and last only briefly; they are caused by the persistence of neural activity after a bright flash. They underlie persistence of vision, the phenomenon that allows a rapid sequence of still images to be perceived as continuous motion in film and animation.

Palinopsia

When afterimages become prolonged, unusually persistent, or appear without a strong stimulus, the condition is called palinopsia. Pathological palinopsia can result from lesions of the occipital or parietal lobes, migraine, seizures, or certain medications and hallucinogens, and is clinically distinguished from the brief physiological afterimage.
