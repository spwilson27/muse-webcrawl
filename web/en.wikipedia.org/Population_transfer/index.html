Population transfer
DefinitionLarge-scale, usually state-organized movement of a population group
Status in lawForcible transfer prohibited by the Fourth Geneva Convention (1949)
Related termsEthnic cleansing, deportation, population exchange

Population transfer is the large-scale, usually state-organized movement of a group of people from one region to another, often motivated by the desire to create ethnically homogeneous states or to remove groups regarded as disloyal or undesirable. Unlike individual migration, population transfers are directed by governments and are frequently compulsory. In the twentieth century they affected tens of millions of people.

Historical examples

The 1923 Greek–Turkish population exchange under the Treaty of Lausanne — some 1.6 million people — was the first internationally sanctioned compulsory exchange and was cited as a precedent for later transfers. After the Second World War, the Potsdam Agreement authorized the "orderly and humane" transfer of German populations from Poland, Czechoslovakia and Hungary; between 12 and 14 million Germans fled or were expelled westward. The 1947 Partition of India displaced 10–20 million people amid mass violence. Other major cases include the Soviet deportations of entire nationalities (Crimean Tatars, Chechens, Volga Germans and others), the population exchanges between Bulgaria and Turkey, and the displacements accompanying the creation of Israel and the Palestinian exodus of 1948.

Types

Transfers may take the form of population exchange (reciprocal movement between two states), deportation, internal resettlement, or expulsion. They differ from ethnic cleansing in stated intent mainly by emphasis: ethnic cleansing aims to remove a group from a territory, whereas a transfer may be framed as relocation; in practice the two often overlap, and forced transfers commonly involve violence and heavy mortality.

Legal status

Article 49 of the Fourth Geneva Convention of 1949 prohibits individual or mass forcible transfers of protected persons from occupied territory. The Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court lists deportation or forcible transfer of population as a crime against humanity. Despite this prohibition, forced displacement continued after 1949, including in the Yugoslav Wars of the 1990s, and international policy has shifted toward guaranteeing displaced persons a right of return.
