The rondeau (plural rondeaux) is one of the three formes fixes (fixed forms) of medieval and Renaissance French poetry and music, alongside the ballade and the virelai. It is built around a refrain whose opening words or lines return, in whole or in part (rentrement), between stanzas, and it uses only two rhyme sounds throughout.

Poetic form

The classic later rondeau, codified in the 15th and 16th centuries, has 13 to 15 lines in three stanzas, using two rhymes; the first one or two lines reappear as a refrain at the end of the second and third stanzas. Earlier, shorter forms appeared in the 13th century in the works of Adam de la Halle. In its musical setting, the form is conventionally notated ABaAabAB, where capital letters denote the full refrain (text and music) and lowercase letters new text sung to the refrain's music.

Music

The rondeau was the most favoured of the formes fixes for polyphonic chansons in the 14th and 15th centuries. Guillaume de Machaut composed 21 rondeaux, including the famous "Ma fin est mon commencement", which is also a retrograde canon. Guillaume Du Fay, Gilles Binchois and Johannes Ockeghem wrote many rondeaux for the Burgundian court. The form declined in the 16th century as through-composed and Italian forms gained favour, though the term survived in the Baroque instrumental rondeau (rondo) movement.

Later revivals

Nineteenth-century French poets such as Théodore de Banville revived the rondeau, and it passed into English verse, most famously in John McCrae's "In Flanders Fields" (1915), a rondeau whose refrain is the poem's first line.
