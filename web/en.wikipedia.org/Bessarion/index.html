Bessarion (Greek: Βησσαρίων; born Basilios; 2 January 1403 – 18 November 1472) was a Byzantine Greek scholar, Catholic cardinal, and one of the famed guests of the Council of Ferrara–Florence. He was Metropolitan of Nicaea, became a leading advocate of the union of the Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic churches, and after the council remained in Italy, where he became a cardinal and a central figure of the early Renaissance — a bridge between Byzantine and Italian humanism. Pope Sixtus IV made him Dean of the College of Cardinals, and he was twice considered a serious candidate for the papacy.

Early life and the council

Born in Trebizond, Bessarion was educated in Constantinople, became a monk (taking the name of the Egyptian anchorite Bessarion), and studied Platonist philosophy under Gemistus Pletho at Mistra. Consecrated Metropolitan of Nicaea in 1437, he accompanied the emperor John VIII to Italy for the Council of Ferrara–Florence (1438–39), where his learning and oratory made him the Greek delegation's most effective spokesman for union. His reading of the Latin and Greek fathers convinced him the two traditions could be reconciled.

Cardinal and scholar

Created cardinal by Pope Eugene IV in 1439, Bessarion settled in Rome, where his household (the Accademia Bessarionea) became a salon of Greek émigré scholars and Italian humanists. He defended Plato against the Aristotelian George of Trebizond in his treatise In calumniatorem Platonis (1469), commissioned translations and copies of Greek manuscripts, and acted as protector of Greek learning in the West after the fall of Constantinople in 1453, while ceaselessly lobbying — with little success — for a crusade to recover it. He served as papal legate in Bologna and on missions to France and Germany, and was made Latin Patriarch of Constantinople in 1463.

Library and legacy

In 1468 Bessarion donated his library — some 750 codices, including about 480 Greek manuscripts, many unique survivals of ancient literature — to the Republic of Venice, in gratitude for its hospitality to Greek exiles. The gift formed the founding collection of the Biblioteca Marciana in Venice, one of the great manuscript libraries of Europe. Bessarion died at Ravenna in 1472 while returning from a legation to France, and is buried in the basilica of Santi Apostoli in Rome. His career exemplifies the transmission of Greek learning that helped fuel the Renaissance, and his library remains his enduring monument.
