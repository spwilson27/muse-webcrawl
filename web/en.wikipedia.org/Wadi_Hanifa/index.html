Wadi Hanifa

Wadi Hanifa (Arabic: وادي حنيفة) is a valley (wadi) in the Najd region of central Saudi Arabia, running for roughly 120 kilometres from northwest to southeast through the capital, Riyadh. Named after the Banu Hanifa, the Arab tribe that inhabited the area in pre-Islamic times, the valley was the site of the ancient settlement of Hajr, the predecessor of Riyadh, and of Diriyah, the original home of the Saudi royal family.

Geography and history

The wadi forms the principal natural drainage of the Riyadh region, its bed seasonally filled by flash floods after rain. Settlements along its fertile banks — including Diriyah, Irqah, and the older towns of the valley — made it the agricultural heartland of the area, famous for date palms and grain. Diriyah, on the valley's western bank, was the capital of the first Saudi state (1744–1818) and its ruins at At-Turaif are a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Environmental restoration

During Riyadh's rapid growth in the later twentieth century, parts of the valley were used for waste disposal and became heavily polluted. A major restoration programme led by the Royal Commission for Riyadh City (formerly the Arriyadh Development Authority), with landscape architects Moriyama & Teshima and Buro Happold, re-naturalised the watercourse, using bio-remediation cells to treat wastewater flows, removing tonnes of waste, and creating parks, trails, and picnic areas along a continuous green corridor. The Wadi Hanifa restoration won the Aga Khan Award for Architecture in 2010.
