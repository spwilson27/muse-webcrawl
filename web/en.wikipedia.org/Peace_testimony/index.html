Peace testimony

The peace testimony is the best-known of the testimonies of the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers): a corporate witness against war and violence, and for the peaceful resolution of conflict. Quakers understand testimonies not as rules but as outward expressions of inward spiritual conviction. The peace testimony has led most Quakers to refuse military service as conscientious objectors, and motivates Quaker work in mediation, disarmament, and relief of war's victims.

Origins

The testimony is rooted in the experience of the early Friends in 17th-century England, who taught that the Light of Christ within every person leaves no room for killing them. Its classic statement is the declaration presented to King Charles II in 1660, drafted after the restoration of the monarchy when Quakers were suspected of plotting: "We utterly deny all outward wars and strife and fightings with outward weapons, for any end or under any pretence whatsoever; and this is our testimony to the whole world." The declaration, associated with George Fox and other early leaders including Margaret Fell, also affirmed Friends' innocence of political conspiracy.

Practice through history

During the American Revolutionary War and the American Civil War, many Friends who fought or paid war taxes were disowned by their meetings, though others served as medics or in relief work. In both World Wars, Quaker conscientious objectors served in the Friends' Ambulance Unit and in alternative service; in the United States, many worked through Civilian Public Service. The shared award of the Nobel Peace Prize in 1947 to the Friends Service Council (London) and the American Friends Service Committee, "for their pioneering work in the international peace movement and compassionate effort to relieve human suffering," recognised this tradition fifty years after the organisations' relief work began.

Modern interpretation

Contemporary Friends read the testimony broadly: beyond refusing war, it calls for addressing the causes of violence—poverty, injustice, and environmental destruction—and for practising peacemaking in families, communities, and international affairs. Quaker organisations such as the Quaker United Nations Offices (established 1948–49) conduct quiet diplomacy with diplomats and officials. Not all individual Quakers are absolute pacifists—notable exceptions include U.S. President Richard Nixon, raised a Quaker—and yearly meetings differ in emphasis, but the peace testimony remains a defining public witness of the Society of Friends.
