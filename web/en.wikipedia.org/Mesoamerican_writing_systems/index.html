The writing systems of Mesoamerica comprise the several scripts independently developed by pre-Columbian cultures of Mexico and Central America. Mesoamerica is one of the few regions of the world where writing arose independently. The best known is Maya script, a fully developed logosyllabic system capable of recording complete spoken language; other traditions include Zapotec writing, the Epi-Olmec (Isthmian) script, and the pictographic-logographic systems of the Mixtecs and Aztecs used in painted screenfold books (codices).

Origins

The earliest generally accepted Mesoamerican writing appears in the Zapotec region of Oaxaca around 600–500 BCE, on monuments such as San José Mogote's Monument 3, apparently recording the name of a sacrificed captive with a day sign from the 260-day calendar — writing and calendrics appear linked from the start. The Cascajal block (c. 900 BCE), attributed to the Olmec, may be older still, though its status as writing is debated. The Epi-Olmec script, known from the La Mojarra stela (2nd century CE), has been partially deciphered and shares features with later Maya writing.

Maya script

Maya writing combined logograms with a syllabary of CV (consonant–vowel) signs, arranged in paired glyph blocks read in double columns. It recorded dynastic histories, astronomical tables, and ritual texts in stone inscriptions, ceramics, and bark-paper codices. Suppressed during the Spanish conquest — most notoriously in Bishop Diego de Landa's 1562 auto-da-fé at Maní — the script was progressively deciphered in the 20th century through the work of Yuri Knorozov, Tatiana Proskouriakoff, and others, and is now substantially readable; contemporary Maya communities promote its revival.

Central Mexican systems

Mixtec and Aztec (Nahuatl) writing relied predominantly on pictorial conventions with logographic and phonetic (rebus) elements, especially for place names and personal names. Post-conquest codices blended these conventions with European formats and alphabetic glosses, preserving genealogies, tribute records, and histories. Whether these systems encoded full sentences remains debated, but they formed part of a rich scribal tradition administered by trained specialists (tlacuiloque).
