Oedipus at Colonus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Oedipus at Colonus (Greek: Οἰδίπους ἐπὶ Κολωνῷ) is the second of the three Theban plays of the Athenian tragedian Sophocles, though it was the last he wrote. Composed shortly before the playwright's death in 406/405 BC, it was first produced posthumously by his grandson Sophocles the Younger in 401 BC. Chronologically it follows Oedipus Rex and precedes Antigone in the story's internal timeline.

Plot

The blind, exiled Oedipus, guided by his daughter Antigone, arrives at Colonus, a village near Athens sacred to the Eumenides, where an oracle has foretold he will die. The chorus of elders initially urges him to leave, but Theseus, king of Athens, grants him protection. Ismene brings news that his sons Eteocles and Polynices are at war over Thebes; Creon tries to seize Oedipus and his daughters, but Theseus rescues them. Polynices begs his father's support and is cursed instead. At last, summoned by thunder, Oedipus leads Theseus alone to a secret place and vanishes mysteriously; his hidden grave becomes a blessing and safeguard for Athens.

Themes

Written in Sophocles' extreme old age, the play meditates on suffering, endurance and the acceptance of death, and presents Athens as a city of justice and refuge. Its choral ode praising Colonus — the playwright's own birthplace — is among the most famous passages in Greek tragedy. See also Oedipus Rex, Antigone and Greek tragedy.
