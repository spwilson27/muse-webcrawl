Lynching in the United States
Peak period: 1880s–1930s
Documented victims (1877–1950): more than 4,400 racial terror lynchings (EJI)
Principal victims: African Americans in the South

Lynching was an extrajudicial killing by a mob, practised in the United States from the late eighteenth century into the twentieth. After Reconstruction it became a tool of racial terror directed overwhelmingly at African Americans, especially in the former Confederate states.

History

The Tuskegee Institute recorded 4,743 lynchings between 1882 and 1968, of whom 3,446 victims were Black. The Equal Justice Initiative has documented more than 4,400 racial terror lynchings between 1877 and 1950. Lynchings were often public spectacles attended by crowds, and perpetrators were rarely prosecuted.

Anti-lynching movement

Journalist Ida B. Wells exposed the practice in the 1890s, and the NAACP campaigned for federal legislation; anti-lynching bills repeatedly passed the House but were blocked in the Senate. Lynching declined after the 1930s under the pressure of activism, migration and changing public opinion.

Legacy

The National Memorial for Peace and Justice opened in Montgomery, Alabama in 2018. In 2022 the Emmett Till Antilynching Act made lynching a federal hate crime.
