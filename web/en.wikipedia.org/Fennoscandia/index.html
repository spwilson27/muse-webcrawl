Fennoscandia

Fennoscandia (Finnish, Swedish and Norwegian: Fennoskandia) is the geographical peninsula in north-western Europe comprising the Scandinavian Peninsula, Finland, Karelia, and the Kola Peninsula. The term is used in geography, geology, and ecology to describe the region delimited by the Norwegian Sea, the Barents Sea, the White Sea, and the Gulfs of Bothnia and Finland, rather than by political borders.

Name and usage

The term was coined by the Finnish geologist Wilhelm Ramsay in 1898, from the Latin words Fennia (Finland) and Scandia (Scandinavia). It was originally a geological concept, denoting the area underlain by the ancient Baltic Shield, but has since been extended to cultural, ecological, and economic contexts. In cultural and political usage, Fennoscandia sometimes loosely overlaps with the Nordic countries, though Denmark and Iceland lie outside the Fennoscandian landmass.

Geology

The region rests largely on the Baltic (Fennoscandian) Shield, one of the oldest parts of the European continental crust, composed chiefly of Archaean and Proterozoic gneisses and granites. The shield was repeatedly scoured by ice sheets during the Pleistocene, and post-glacial rebound continues today — land around the Gulf of Bothnia is rising by up to about a centimetre per year.

Geography and ecology

Fennoscandia includes the Scandes mountain range along Norway's western coast, extensive boreal forests (taiga), and tens of thousands of lakes, especially in Finland. Its ecosystems form part of the Palearctic realm, and the region is noted for large predators such as wolves, bears, lynx, and wolverines.
