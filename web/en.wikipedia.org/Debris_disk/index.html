A debris disk is a circumstellar disk of dust and debris in orbit around a star. The dust is generated by collisions among planetesimals — asteroids, comets and Kuiper Belt-like objects left over from planet formation — and is continually replenished as larger bodies grind each other down, since the tiny grains are quickly removed by radiation pressure and stellar wind.

Discovery and examples

The first debris disk was discovered in 1984 around the star Vega, when the IRAS satellite detected excess infrared emission. Hundreds are now known, including the spectacular disks around Beta Pictoris, Fomalhaut and HR 8799, some of which also host directly imaged exoplanets. The Solar System's own Kuiper Belt is a faint debris disk; the bright disks observed around other stars are typically far more massive and dusty.

Significance

Debris disks trace the outcome of planet formation and the architecture of planetary systems: gaps, warps and asymmetries in a disk can betray the gravitational influence of unseen planets. Studying them helps astronomers understand the history of bombardment in planetary systems, including our own.
