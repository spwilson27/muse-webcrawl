Motor Carrier Act of 1980

The Motor Carrier Act of 1980 is a United States federal law that deregulated the trucking industry. It was signed into law by President Jimmy Carter on July 1, 1980, as part of a broader wave of transport deregulation that also included the airlines and railroads.

Background

Since the Motor Carrier Act of 1935, interstate trucking had been regulated by the Interstate Commerce Commission (ICC), which controlled entry into the industry, the routes carriers could serve, and the rates they could charge. Operating certificates were scarce and valuable; critics argued the system raised freight costs and protected inefficient carriers.

Provisions and effects

The 1980 act eased entry requirements, shifting the burden of proof to those opposing a new carrier's application; permitted carriers to set rates more freely within a "zone of reasonableness"; and prohibited many forms of collective rate-making. The number of licensed carriers more than doubled within a few years, real freight rates fell, and logistics practices such as just-in-time delivery expanded. Critics noted increased pressure on driver wages and the decline of unionized carriers. The ICC itself was abolished in 1995, its remaining functions passing to the Surface Transportation Board.
