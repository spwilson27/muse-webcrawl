Marine pollution occurs when substances used or spread by humans, such as industrial, agricultural and residential waste, particles, noise, excess carbon dioxide or invasive organisms, enter the ocean and cause harmful effects there. The majority of this waste comes from land-based activities, although shipping litter and airborne pollutants also play a significant part. Many potentially toxic chemicals adhere to tiny particles which are taken up by plankton and benthic animals, most of which are deposit feeders or filter feeders; in this way the toxins are concentrated upward within ocean food chains.

Types and sources

Nutrient pollution from fertilizer runoff and sewage causes eutrophication and hypoxic "dead zones". Plastic pollution, including macroplastics and microplastics, accumulates in ocean gyres such as the Great Pacific garbage patch and is ingested by wildlife. Oil pollution arises from tanker spills, offshore drilling accidents such as the Deepwater Horizon blowout of 2010, and routine discharges. Other major categories include toxic metals, persistent organic pollutants, radioactive waste, underwater noise, ocean acidification driven by absorbed CO2, and the introduction of invasive species via ballast water.

Effects

Marine pollution harms ecosystems and human health: toxins accumulate in seafood, coral reefs are degraded by sedimentation and warming-related stress, and coastal economies dependent on fisheries and tourism suffer losses. Eutrophication-driven algal blooms can produce toxins that poison shellfish and marine mammals.

Mitigation and law

International controls include the MARPOL convention regulating discharges from ships, the London Convention on ocean dumping, and Part XII of the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. In the 2020s, states negotiated a global plastics treaty under UN auspices. Point-source controls, wastewater treatment, and reduced plastic production are widely advocated as the most effective remedies.
