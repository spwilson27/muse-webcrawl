Lymphedema

Lymphedema causes chronic swelling, usually of a limb, when lymph drainage fails.

Lymphedema is a long-term condition in which excess fluid (lymph) collects in tissues because the lymphatic system cannot drain it adequately, causing chronic swelling, most often in an arm or leg. It must be distinguished from ordinary oedema of cardiac, renal or venous origin, though the conditions can coexist.

Causes

Primary lymphedema results from developmental abnormalities of the lymph vessels and may be present at birth (congenital, including Milroy disease), appear at puberty (lymphedema praecox, including Meige disease), or develop later in adult life. Secondary lymphedema is far more common and follows damage or obstruction of the lymphatics: worldwide the leading cause is lymphatic filariasis, a parasitic infection affecting tens of millions of people in tropical regions. In high-income countries the most common cause is cancer treatment — surgical removal of lymph nodes and radiotherapy, particularly after breast cancer treatment, where it affects a substantial minority of patients who undergo axillary node clearance.

Signs and course

Swelling typically begins softly and pits on pressure but becomes firmer and non-pitting over time as tissues fibrose; the skin may thicken and develop a peau d'orange texture. A positive Stemmer sign (inability to pinch a fold of skin at the base of a toe or finger) is characteristic. Affected limbs are prone to recurrent bacterial infections such as cellulitis, each episode further damaging the lymphatics. In the most severe form, elephantiasis, swelling is massive and the skin grossly thickened.

Treatment

There is no cure, but complete decongestive therapy — combining manual lymphatic drainage, multi-layer compression bandaging and then compression garments, exercise and meticulous skin care — can control swelling and prevent complications. Weight management and prompt antibiotic treatment of infections are important. Selected patients benefit from microsurgical procedures such as lymphaticovenular anastomosis or vascularised lymph node transfer; liposuction is used for late-stage, fat-dominated swelling.
