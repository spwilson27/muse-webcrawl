Ancient Mesopotamian religion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ancient Mesopotamian religion encompasses the religious beliefs and practices of the peoples of ancient Mesopotamia — the Sumerians, Akkadians, Babylonians and Assyrians — over more than three millennia, from the 4th millennium BC until the early centuries AD. It was a polytheistic system in which hundreds of gods governed the forces of nature and the institutions of society, and it profoundly influenced later religions of the Near East, including Judaism.

Pantheon

At the head of the pantheon stood Anu, god of the sky; Enlil, god of wind and executive power among the gods; and Enki (Akkadian Ea), god of fresh waters, wisdom and creation. Other major deities included Utu (Shamash), the sun god and god of justice; Nanna (Sin), the moon god; Inanna (Ishtar), goddess of love and war; and Ninhursag, the mother goddess. In the first millennium BC, Marduk of Babylon and Ashur of Assyria rose to supremacy within their respective states.

Cosmology and myth

Mesopotamians imagined the universe as a flat disc of earth floating on the freshwater ocean (the Apsu), beneath a vaulted heaven, with the underworld — Kur, the "land of no return" — below. Humanity, in texts such as the Atrahasis epic and the creation epic Enūma Eliš, was created from clay mixed with the blood of a slain god to serve the gods and relieve them of labour. The Epic of Gilgamesh includes a famous flood narrative closely paralleling the biblical story of Noah.

Cult and priesthood

Each city was the earthly estate of its patron god, whose temple — dominated by a stepped ziggurat — was staffed by priests who fed, clothed and entertained the cult statue with daily offerings. Great festivals, above all the Babylonian New Year (Akitu) festival, re-enacted divine victories and ritually renewed the king's mandate. Divination, by extispicy (reading livers), astrology and dream interpretation, was a major priestly science, and Mesopotamian omen literature strongly shaped later Hellenistic astrology.

Afterlife

The dead descended to a gloomy underworld where they ate dust and clay, regardless of conduct in life; funerary offerings by descendants could ease their condition. Because the afterlife offered little hope, Mesopotamian religion centred on securing health, prosperity and divine favour in this world.
