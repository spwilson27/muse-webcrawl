Altitude acclimatisation

Climbers acclimatise by ascending in stages and sleeping progressively higher.

Altitude acclimatisation is the process by which the body adjusts to decreasing oxygen availability at high altitude. Because the partial pressure of oxygen falls with altitude, an unacclimatised person rapidly flown or driven to high elevations risks altitude sickness, while a staged ascent allows physiological adaptations that improve oxygen delivery to the tissues.

Physiological changes

The immediate response is hyperventilation, triggered by the carotid bodies sensing low arterial oxygen, which raises alveolar oxygen but causes respiratory alkalosis that the kidneys correct over several days by excreting bicarbonate. Over days to weeks the kidneys release erythropoietin (EPO), increasing red blood cell production and haemoglobin concentration; 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate rises in red cells, capillarity and myoglobin increase, and pulmonary artery pressure rises as the lung vessels constrict in response to hypoxia.

Practice

Mountaineers follow the rule "climb high, sleep low", limit sleeping-altitude gains to roughly 300–500 metres per day above 3,000 m, and take rest days. Acclimatisation gained is lost within weeks of returning to low altitude. The drug acetazolamide can speed the process.

Limits

Above about 5,500 m (the limit of permanent human habitation) the body progressively deteriorates despite acclimatisation, and above 8,000 m — the "death zone" — survival time is short without supplemental oxygen.
