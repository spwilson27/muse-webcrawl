Nuclear weapon

A nuclear weapon is an explosive device that derives its destructive force from nuclear reactions, either nuclear fission (splitting heavy atomic nuclei) or a combination of fission and nuclear fusion. A relatively small weapon can destroy a city through blast, fire, and ionizing radiation, and the largest thermonuclear weapons are thousands of times more powerful than the bombs used in 1945. Nuclear weapons are considered weapons of mass destruction, and their use and possession are restricted by international treaties.

Types

Fission weapons ("atomic bombs") release energy by splitting nuclei of uranium-235 or plutonium-239 in a runaway chain reaction. Two assembly designs were used in 1945: the gun-type device and the implosion device. Thermonuclear weapons ("hydrogen bombs") use a fission primary to ignite a fusion secondary, in the Teller–Ulam configuration, burning isotopes of hydrogen such as deuterium and tritium. Their yields are not inherently limited and are measured in megatons.

Effects

A nuclear explosion produces a blast wave, intense thermal radiation capable of igniting fires and causing severe burns, prompt ionizing radiation, an electromagnetic pulse, and radioactive fallout. Firestorms may develop in cities. Large-scale exchanges have been modelled as producing a "nuclear winter", in which soot injected into the stratosphere cools the global climate.

History

Nuclear physics discoveries of the 1930s, including the discovery of fission by Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann and its explanation by Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch, made atomic weapons conceivable. During the Second World War, the United States, with British and Canadian support, pursued the Manhattan Project under J. Robert Oppenheimer's scientific leadership. The first test, Trinity, took place on 16 July 1945 in New Mexico. Atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima (6 August 1945) and Nagasaki (9 August 1945), the only uses of nuclear weapons in warfare.

The Soviet Union tested its first device in 1949, beginning an arms race. The United Kingdom, France, and China followed. The Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 brought the superpowers close to nuclear war and spurred arms-control efforts.

Delivery systems and strategy

Nuclear warheads are delivered by ballistic missiles (land-based ICBMs and submarine-launched SLBMs), strategic bombers, and shorter-range tactical systems. The combination of land, sea, and air delivery forms the "nuclear triad". Cold War strategy centred on deterrence and the doctrine of mutually assured destruction (MAD), holding that a secure second-strike capability prevents nuclear attack.

Proliferation and treaties

The Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT, 1968) recognises five nuclear-weapon states — the United States, Russia, the United Kingdom, France, and China — and commits parties to preventing wider spread and pursuing disarmament. India, Pakistan, and North Korea have tested weapons outside or after withdrawing from the treaty framework; Israel is widely believed to possess them without declaring so. Other key agreements include the Partial Test Ban Treaty (1963), the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (1996, not in force), and bilateral U.S.–Russian arms-reduction treaties such as New START. The Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons entered into force in 2021, though no nuclear-armed state has joined it.
