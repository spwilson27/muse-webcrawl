Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 296–298 – 2 May 373), also called Athanasius the Great and Athanasius the Confessor, was a Christian theologian and the 20th Patriarch of Alexandria. He is remembered as the chief defender of Trinitarian orthodoxy against Arianism, a stance that made him the target of emperors and led to five exiles totalling about 17 of his 45 years as bishop — giving rise to the phrase Athanasius contra mundum, "Athanasius against the world".

Career

As a young deacon he accompanied Bishop Alexander to the First Council of Nicaea in 325, which condemned Arius, and succeeded him in 328. His episcopate was consumed by the Arian controversy: successive emperors, favouring compromise formulas, deposed and exiled him to Trier, Rome and the Egyptian desert, where he was sheltered by the desert monks whose life he promoted in his Life of Anthony.

Writings and legacy

Major works include On the Incarnation, Against the Heathen, the Orations against the Arians, and the Festal Letters; Letter 39 (367) contains the earliest surviving list of exactly the 27 books of the New Testament canon. Athanasius is counted among the four great Eastern Doctors of the Church and is regarded by many as the "Father of Orthodoxy". His name was traditionally attached to the Athanasian Creed, though modern scholars date that text after his lifetime.
