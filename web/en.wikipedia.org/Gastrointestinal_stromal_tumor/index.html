Gastrointestinal stromal tumor

Gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST)
SpecialtyOncology, gastroenterology
Cell of originInterstitial cells of Cajal
Frequent sitesStomach (~60%), small intestine (~30%)
Key markersKIT (CD117), DOG1
TreatmentSurgery; imatinib and other tyrosine kinase inhibitors

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common mesenchymal neoplasms of the gastrointestinal tract. They are thought to arise from the interstitial cells of Cajal, the pacemaker cells of gut motility, or their precursors. GISTs occur most often in the stomach and small intestine but can arise anywhere along the digestive tract and, rarely, elsewhere in the abdomen.

Molecular biology

Most GISTs are driven by activating mutations in the receptor tyrosine kinase KIT (about 75–80% of cases) or in the related receptor PDGFRA (about 5–10%). These mutations cause constant signalling that drives cell growth. Gastrointestinal stromal tumors characteristically stain positive for KIT (CD117) on immunohistochemistry, and for the marker DOG1; these tests distinguish them from true smooth-muscle tumors (leiomyomas and leiomyosarcomas), with which they were historically confused.

Signs and symptoms

Many GISTs are small and found incidentally. Larger tumors may cause abdominal pain, a palpable mass, gastrointestinal bleeding, anaemia, or obstruction. Unlike carcinomas, GISTs rarely spread to lymph nodes; metastasis, when it occurs, is usually to the liver or peritoneum. Risk of malignant behaviour is estimated from tumor size, mitotic rate and anatomical site.

Treatment

Localised tumors are treated by complete surgical resection. The introduction of the tyrosine kinase inhibitor imatinib in the early 2000s transformed the outlook for advanced GIST, converting a chemotherapy-resistant cancer into one often controlled for years. Later-line agents include sunitinib, regorafenib, ripretinib and, for PDGFRA D842V-mutant tumors, avapritinib. Adjuvant imatinib is given after surgery for patients at significant risk of recurrence.

Epidemiology

GISTs are uncommon, with an annual incidence of roughly 10–15 cases per million people, typically diagnosed in patients in their sixties. A small proportion occur in inherited syndromes such as neurofibromatosis type 1 or the Carney–Stratakis syndrome.
