Rashi script

Rashi script (Hebrew: כתב רש״י) is a semi-cursive form of the Hebrew alphabet used since the early centuries of Hebrew printing to set rabbinic commentary, distinguishing it typographically from the biblical or Talmudic text, which is printed in the square ("Assyrian") script.

History

The typeface is based on 15th-century Sephardic scribal handwriting, not on the hand of Rashi (1040–1105) himself, the great French commentator for whom it is named. The earliest Hebrew printed books, such as the 1475 Reggio di Calabria edition of Rashi's commentary on the Torah, used the style; the Soncino and later Bomberg presses standardised it in editions of the Talmud and Bible commentaries, a layout convention that persists in traditional editions today.

Characteristics

Rashi script modifies several letterforms substantially — for example aleph, lamed, and final mem differ markedly from their square-script counterparts — so readers must learn it as a second alphabet, though it uses the same 22 letters and final forms. It remains in daily use in yeshiva study and traditional printing.
