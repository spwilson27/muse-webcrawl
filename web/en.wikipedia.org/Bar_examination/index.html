Bar examination

Bar examination
PurposeLicensing assessment for admission to practise law
Administered byJurisdictional licensing bodies (e.g. US state bars)
Typical componentsMultiple-choice questions, essays, performance tests
FrequencyUsually twice yearly (February and July in the US)

A bar examination is an examination administered by the bar association of a jurisdiction that a lawyer must pass in order to be admitted to the bar of that jurisdiction and licensed to practise law there. Such exams test knowledge of general legal principles, jurisdiction-specific law, and practical legal skills, and passing is normally one of several requirements for admission alongside education and character-and-fitness review.

United States

In the United States, bar admission is regulated state by state. Most jurisdictions use components prepared by the National Conference of Bar Examiners: the Multistate Bar Examination (MBE), a 200-question multiple-choice test covering subjects such as contracts, torts, constitutional law, criminal law and procedure, evidence, property and civil procedure; the Multistate Essay Examination; and the Multistate Performance Test, which assesses practical lawyering tasks. Many states have adopted the Uniform Bar Examination (UBE), whose scores are portable between participating jurisdictions. The Multistate Professional Responsibility Examination (MPRE) separately tests legal ethics. Exams are held over two days, twice a year, and overall first-time pass rates vary widely by state and school — historically from under half to over 90 percent of candidates.

Other jurisdictions

Other countries use different licensing routes: in England and Wales, aspiring barristers complete the Bar training course and a period of pupillage; solicitors qualify through the Solicitors Qualifying Examination. In Japan, the national bar examination is famously selective and is followed by training at the Legal Training and Research Institute. Despite these differences, the function is the same: a gatekeeping assessment before a candidate may represent clients as a member of the legal profession.
