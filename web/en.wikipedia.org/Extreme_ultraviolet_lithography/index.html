Extreme ultraviolet lithography

Wavelength13.5 nm
Light sourceLaser-produced tin plasma
Principal manufacturerASML
Introduced in production2019 (7 nm-class nodes)

Extreme ultraviolet lithography (EUVL) is an optical lithography technology used in semiconductor device fabrication to make integrated circuits. It uses extreme ultraviolet light with a wavelength of 13.5 nm, allowing the patterning of much finer features than deep ultraviolet (193 nm) immersion lithography in a single exposure.

Technology

Because EUV light is absorbed by virtually all materials, including air, the entire optical path must operate in vacuum and use reflective optics: multilayer mirrors coated with alternating molybdenum and silicon layers that reflect about 70% of incident EUV light. The light source is a laser-produced plasma in which molten tin droplets are struck by a high-power CO2 laser, emitting at 13.5 nm. The photomasks are also reflective.

History

Research on EUV lithography began in the 1980s and 1990s, including the EUV LLC consortium of Intel, AMD, Motorola, and US national laboratories. Dutch company ASML became the sole supplier of EUV scanners; its NXE series entered high-volume manufacturing with TSMC and Samsung around 2019 for 7 nm and 5 nm process nodes. The newer High-NA EUV systems (numerical aperture 0.55, up from 0.33) began shipping in the mid-2020s.

Challenges

Key challenges include source power and uptime, pellicle and mask defects, photoresist sensitivity versus resolution and line-edge roughness (the "RLS trade-off"), and stochastic printing failures that become significant at very small feature sizes.
