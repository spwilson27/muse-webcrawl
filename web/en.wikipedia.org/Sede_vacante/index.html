Sede vacante

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

During a sede vacante, the arms of the Holy See bear the umbraculum over the crossed keys.

Sede vacante (Latin for "the seat being vacant") is the state, in the Canon Law of the Catholic Church, in which a diocese or the Holy See is without a bishop or pope. The term is most widely used for the period between the death or resignation of a pope and the election of his successor by the College of Cardinals in a conclave.

The papal interregnum

When a pope dies or resigns, the Holy See becomes vacant. The Camerlengo of the Holy Roman Church verifies the pope's death and administers the temporal goods of the Holy See. The general rule is sede vacante nihil innovetur — "nothing shall be altered during the vacancy". Most officials of the Roman Curia cease from their functions, and governance is limited to ordinary business; the College of Cardinals, and in particular the particular congregations of cardinals, handles routine administration but cannot make decisions reserved to the pope.

The apostolic constitution Universi Dominici gregis (1996), promulgated by John Paul II, governs the vacancy and election. The conclave begins 15 to 20 days after the vacancy begins. During the vacancy, Vatican City issues distinctive sede vacante coins and stamps eagerly collected by numismatists.

Notable vacancies

Recent vacancies include those following the deaths of John Paul II in 2005 and Benedict XVI's unprecedented resignation in 2013 — the first papal resignation in nearly 600 years, ending his pontificate on 28 February 2013 and leading to the election of Pope Francis. Historically long vacancies include the nearly three-year vacancy of 1268–1271, ended by the election of Gregory X, which prompted the formal conclave rules.

Diocesan usage

For a diocese, a vacancy arises on the death, resignation, transfer, or removal of the diocesan bishop; a diocesan administrator is then elected by the college of consultors to govern until a new bishop takes possession.
