The Antigonid dynasty was a Hellenistic dynasty of Dorian Greek provenance, descended from Antigonus I Monophthalmus ("the One-Eyed"), one of the Diadochi—successors of Alexander the Great. After Antigonus I's death at the Battle of Ipsus in 301 BC, his descendants eventually secured the throne of Macedon, which the dynasty held until its defeat by the Roman Republic in the Third Macedonian War.

Rulers of Macedon

Antigonus II Gonatas, grandson of the founder, established the dynasty securely on the Macedonian throne in 276 BC and ruled for nearly four decades, defeating invading Galatians and consolidating the kingdom. His successors included Demetrius II, Antigonus III Doson, and Philip V, whose expansion in Greece and alliance with Hannibal drew Rome into two wars. Philip's son Perseus was defeated by Lucius Aemilius Paullus at the Battle of Pydna in 168 BC, ending the dynasty and Macedonian independence; Macedon was divided into four republics, and Perseus died a prisoner in Italy.

Legacy

The Antigonid kingdom was the most stable and conservative of the Hellenistic great powers, centred on Macedon and exercising hegemony over mainland Greece through garrisons (the "Fetters of Greece") at Corinth, Chalcis, and Demetrias. Its long conflict with Rome marked the beginning of Roman domination of the Greek world.
