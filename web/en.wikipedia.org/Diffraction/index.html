Diffraction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Diffraction is the deviation of waves from straight-line propagation without any change in their energy due to an obstacle or through an aperture. The diffracting object or aperture effectively becomes a secondary source of the propagating wave. The phenomenon is the result of interference between different components of a wave that have travelled along different paths. Diffraction occurs with all waves, including sound waves, water waves, and electromagnetic waves such as visible light, X-rays and radio waves. Since sub-atomic particles also exhibit wave properties, diffraction is also a phenomenon studied in quantum mechanics.

History

The effects of diffraction were first carefully noted and characterised by Francesco Maria Grimaldi, who coined the word in the 17th century. Thomas Young's double-slit experiment of 1801 demonstrated the wave nature of light through diffraction and interference. Augustin-Jean Fresnel developed the mathematical theory of diffraction based on the Huygens–Fresnel principle, every point on a wavefront acting as a source of secondary wavelets; his predictions, including the bright spot at the centre of a circular shadow (the Arago or Poisson spot), were famously confirmed by experiment. Joseph von Fraunhofer studied the far-field case, while Gustav Kirchhoff gave the theory a firmer mathematical basis.

Principles

Diffraction is most pronounced when the size of an aperture or obstacle is comparable to the wavelength of the wave. For a single slit of width a, the minima of the far-field pattern occur at angles satisfying a sin θ = mλ for integer m. A diffraction grating — a surface with many regularly spaced slits — produces sharp maxima in directions determined by the grating spacing, and is used to disperse light into spectra. Circular apertures produce an Airy pattern, whose central disk size sets the resolution limit of telescopes and microscopes (the Rayleigh criterion).

Examples and applications

Everyday examples include the coloured patterns on CDs and DVDs, the spreading of sound around corners, and the halo seen around the Moon through thin cloud (caused by diffraction by water droplets). X-ray diffraction by crystals, advanced by Max von Laue and William and Lawrence Bragg, reveals atomic structure; electron diffraction demonstrates the wave nature of matter; and neutron diffraction is used in materials science. Diffraction limits the resolution of all optical instruments and the capacity of optical data storage.
