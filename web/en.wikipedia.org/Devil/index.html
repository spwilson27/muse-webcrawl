Devil

The Devil is a figure of evil in many religions and cultures, typically the personification of evil and the adversary of God and humanity. In the Abrahamic traditions the Devil is identified with Satan, a fallen angel who rebelled against God and now tempts human beings into sin. The English word derives from Old English dēofol, from Greek diábolos ("slanderer, accuser"), the term used in the Septuagint and New Testament where Hebrew śāṭān ("adversary") appears.

In the Bible and Judaism

In the Hebrew Bible, Satan appears as a member of the divine council who tests the righteous, as in the Book of Job, rather than as an independent power of evil. In Second Temple Judaism and the New Testament the figure darkens: Satan tempts Jesus in the wilderness, is called the "ruler of this world", and in Revelation is the dragon, the "ancient serpent", cast into the lake of fire. Christian tradition connected the Devil with Lucifer (from Isaiah 14 read as a fall narrative), with the serpent of Eden, and with demons, of which he is prince.

In Islam

In Islam the corresponding figure is Iblis, a jinn who refused God's command to bow before Adam and was expelled, thereafter misleading humanity as al-Shayṭān until the Day of Judgment. Islamic theology preserves strict monotheism: Iblis can tempt but has no power over God's willing servants.

Depiction and folklore

Medieval and early modern art gave the Devil horns, cloven hooves, bat wings and a tail — partly borrowed from the Greek god Pan and underworld imagery — shaping the popular image ever since. Folklore worldwide tells of pacts with the Devil, from the legend of Faust to crossroads tales in the American South. The figure anchors theological debates about the problem of evil, free will and dualism, and remains a potent character in literature from Dante and Milton's Paradise Lost to Bulgakov's The Master and Margarita.
