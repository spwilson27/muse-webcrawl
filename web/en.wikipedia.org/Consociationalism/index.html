Consociationalism

Consociationalism is an academic political concept and also a form of governmental power-sharing in a state with diverse, conflicting social groups, typically ethnic, linguistic, or religious divisions, to manage societal division in order to ensure social harmony. Consociational governments are often contrasted with majoritarian governments.

Arend Lijphart's model

The theory was developed by political scientist Arend Lijphart, drawing on the experience of the Netherlands ("pillarization") and other divided societies. Lijphart identified four key features: (1) a grand coalition government including leaders of all significant segments; (2) a mutual veto for minorities on vital issues; (3) proportionality in representation, civil service appointments, and allocation of public funds; and (4) segmental autonomy, allowing each group to run its own internal affairs, for example in education.

Examples

Classic examples include the Netherlands from 1917 to 1967, Belgium, Austria after 1945, Lebanon's National Pact system, Bosnia and Herzegovina under the Dayton Agreement, and Northern Ireland under the Good Friday Agreement, which mandates power-sharing between unionist and nationalist parties.

Criticism

Critics argue consociational arrangements can entrench divisions, freeze group identities, reward intransigent parties, and produce weak or deadlocked governments — as illustrated by recurring political crises in Lebanon and Bosnia. Supporters respond that in deeply divided societies it may be the only workable alternative to violence.
