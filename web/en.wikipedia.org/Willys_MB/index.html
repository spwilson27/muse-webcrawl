Willys MB

The Willys MB and the Ford GPW were two nearly identical United States military light utility vehicles produced from 1941 to 1945 for the Second World War. Together with the earlier Bantam BRC-40, they are the vehicles from which the universal "jeep" concept — and ultimately the Jeep brand — developed. Over 600,000 units were built, making the vehicle one of the most produced military vehicles of the war.

Development

In 1940 the U.S. Army sought a light reconnaissance vehicle. American Bantam delivered the first prototype, but lacking production capacity, the contract went to Willys-Overland, whose design — powered by the durable 60 hp "Go Devil" four-cylinder engine — was standardised as the MB. Ford built the same design as the GPW to meet demand. Features included four-wheel drive, a fold-down windshield, a payload of about 800 lb (360 kg), and the ability to be fitted with tools, radios, and weapons mounts.

Service

The jeep served in every theatre of the war with U.S. and Allied forces, used for reconnaissance, liaison, ambulance, and light transport duties. General George C. Marshall called it "America's greatest contribution to modern warfare," and it was widely supplied under Lend-Lease to Britain and the Soviet Union.

Legacy

After the war the design evolved into the civilian CJ (Civilian Jeep) series, founding a line of vehicles that continues today. The Willys MB remains one of the most recognisable military vehicles ever built.
