Homunculus

A homunculus (Latin for "little person"; plural homunculi) is a miniature, fully formed human, once believed in the preformationist theory of development to be present in germ cells and merely to grow during gestation. The term is most commonly used in alchemy and in pre-modern theories of generation, and appears in works such as Paracelsus's writings, where a recipe describes creating a tiny human artificially. The homunculus features prominently in Goethe's Faust, Part Two, where Wagner creates one in a vial.

In neuroscience

In modern usage the term survives in neuroscience as the cortical homunculus: a distorted representation of the human body mapped onto the primary motor and somatosensory cortices, based on Wilder Penfield's studies. Body parts with finer motor control or denser sensory innervation — the hands, lips, and tongue — are drawn disproportionately large, reflecting the cortical area devoted to them.

In philosophy of mind

The "homunculus argument" criticizes theories that explain cognition by positing a little inner agent who perceives or decides, since that agent's own cognition would then need explaining, generating an infinite regress. Daniel Dennett has argued that such homunculi are acceptable in an explanation if they are progressively "discharged" by decomposition into simpler mechanisms.
