The Northeast Passage (abbreviated as NEP) is the shipping route between the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans along the Arctic coasts of Norway and Russia. The western part of the route, from the Kara Sea to the Bering Strait, is administered by Russia as the Northern Sea Route. The passage offers a much shorter link between Northern Europe and northeastern Asia than the routes through the Suez Canal or around the Cape of Good Hope.

History

European interest in a northeast route to Asia dates to the 16th century. English expeditions under Hugh Willoughby and Richard Chancellor in 1553 opened trade with Russia, and Dutch navigator Willem Barentsz died in 1597 after wintering on Novaya Zemlya. The Great Northern Expedition of Vitus Bering in the 18th century charted much of the Siberian coast. The first complete transit was achieved by the Swedish explorer Adolf Erik Nordenskiöld aboard the Vega in 1878–79.

Climate change and shipping

Declining Arctic sea ice has made seasonal transit increasingly feasible. Russia has invested in nuclear icebreakers and ports to develop the route for exports of liquefied natural gas and other cargo. Transit times between northern Europe and East Asia can be up to 40 percent shorter than via Suez, though shallow straits limit vessel size, insurance and icebreaker escort costs are high, and environmental concerns persist about traffic in remote Arctic waters.

Comparison

The Northeast Passage is the eastern counterpart of the Northwest Passage through the Canadian Arctic Archipelago. Both routes have become more navigable as the Arctic warms, raising questions of sovereignty, safety, and environmental protection.
