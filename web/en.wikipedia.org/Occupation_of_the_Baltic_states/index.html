Occupation of the Baltic states

Image placeholder: The Baltic Way human chain, 1989
The Baltic Way, a 1989 human chain across the three Baltic states protesting the occupation

The occupation of the Baltic states refers to the military occupation of the three Baltic states — Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania — by the Soviet Union in 1940 under the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact and their subsequent annexation, the occupation by Nazi Germany from 1941 to 1944, and the re-occupation by the Soviet Union from 1944 until the restoration of independence in 1991.

Background and occupation of 1940

The secret protocols of the 1939 Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact and the German–Soviet Boundary and Friendship Treaty assigned the Baltic states to the Soviet sphere. In autumn 1939 the USSR imposed mutual-assistance pacts and military bases. In June 1940, ultimatums backed by overwhelming force led to full occupation; communist-controlled governments arranged rigged elections and petitioned for incorporation into the USSR, completed in August 1940.

Non-recognition

Most Western governments never recognized the annexation de jure. The United States applied the Stimson Doctrine (the Welles Declaration of July 1940), and Baltic legations continued to function in Washington and elsewhere throughout the Cold War. The state-continuity doctrine holds that the Baltic states existed de jure throughout the occupation, so that in 1991 they restored, rather than newly gained, independence.

German occupation and second Soviet occupation

From 1941 to 1944 the Baltic states were administered within the Reichskommissariat Ostland; the Holocaust destroyed most of the region's Jewish population, and the Baltic populations were drawn into both German and Soviet war efforts. Soviet re-occupation in 1944 was followed by mass deportations (notably in 1941 and 1949), forced collectivization, industrialization, large-scale immigration from other Soviet republics, and armed resistance by the Forest Brothers into the early 1950s.

Restoration of independence

The Singing Revolution of 1987–1991, symbolized by the Baltic Way human chain of August 1989, culminated in declarations restoring independence — Lithuania on 11 March 1990, and Estonia and Latvia in 1990–91 — with international recognition following the failed August 1991 Moscow coup. Russian troops withdrew from Lithuania in 1993 and from Estonia and Latvia in 1994. All three states joined NATO and the European Union in 2004.

Divergent positions

The Baltic states, the European Court of Human Rights, the European Parliament, and many historians describe the events as an illegal occupation and annexation. The Russian Federation's official position has resisted characterizing the incorporation as an occupation, a stance the Baltic states and their partners reject. Specific aspects are covered in the separate article on the occupation of Latvia and counterpart articles for Estonia and Lithuania.
