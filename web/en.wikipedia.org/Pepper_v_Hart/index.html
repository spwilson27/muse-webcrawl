Pepper v Hart

Pepper (Inspector of Taxes) v Hart [1993] AC 593 is a landmark decision of the House of Lords on statutory interpretation in English law. The case established that courts may refer to statements made in Parliament — recorded in Hansard — as an aid to interpreting legislation where the statutory wording is ambiguous, obscure, or leads to an absurdity.

Facts

The case concerned teachers at Malvern College who, under a concessionary scheme, paid only one-fifth of the normal fees for their sons to attend the school. The Inland Revenue assessed the benefit to income tax on the full value of the places under the Finance Act 1976, while the teachers argued it should be based only on the marginal cost to the college. The statutory phrase "the cost of the benefit" was ambiguous, and during the passage of the Act the Financial Secretary to the Treasury had made statements in the House of Commons supporting the marginal-cost interpretation.

Decision

A seven-judge committee of the House of Lords, led by Lord Browne-Wilkinson, relaxed the long-standing rule excluding reference to parliamentary debates. Lord Browne-Wilkinson held that reference to Hansard is permissible where (a) the legislation is ambiguous or obscure, or its literal meaning leads to absurdity; (b) the material relied on consists of statements by a minister or other promoter of the Bill; and (c) those statements are clear. Applying the ministerial statements, the House held for the teachers: the benefit was to be valued at its marginal cost to the employer.

Significance

Pepper v Hart is one of the most important modern authorities on the use of extrinsic aids to interpretation and marked a shift toward purposive construction in English law. Its use has since been qualified in later cases, which caution against routine resort to Hansard and emphasise that the ministerial statement must be clear and directed to the point in issue; it has rarely changed the outcome of subsequent litigation.
