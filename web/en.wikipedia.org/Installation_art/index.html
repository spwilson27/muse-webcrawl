Installation art

Installation art is an artistic genre of three-dimensional works that are often site-specific and designed to transform the perception of a space. Generally, the term is applied to interior spaces, whereas exterior interventions are often called public art or land art; however, the boundaries between these terms overlap. Installations may be temporary or permanent, and invite viewers to walk through or around them rather than merely look at them.

Installation art transforms an entire space into an environment the viewer inhabits.

History

Precursors include Marcel Duchamp's readymades and environments, Kurt Schwitters's Merzbau, and the Surrealist exhibition designs of the 1930s. Installation emerged as a named practice in the 1970s, drawing on minimalism, conceptual art, performance and land art; important early practitioners include Allan Kaprow (environments and happenings), Ilya Kabakov ("total installations"), and James Turrell and Robert Irwin, whose light works made perception itself the medium.

Characteristics

Installations commonly combine sculpture, found objects, sound, video, light and architecture; many are immersive or interactive, engaging senses beyond sight. Because works are often site-specific and ephemeral, they challenge the art market's focus on portable objects, though documentation, artist instructions and re-fabrication have allowed major installations to enter museum collections. Large-scale examples — such as Yayoi Kusama's Infinity Mirror Rooms, Olafur Eliasson's The Weather Project (2003) at Tate Modern, and Christo and Jeanne-Claude's wrapped buildings — have become popular landmarks of contemporary art.
