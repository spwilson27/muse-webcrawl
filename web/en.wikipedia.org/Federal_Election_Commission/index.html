The Federal Election Commission (FEC) is an independent agency of the United States government that administers and enforces federal campaign finance law. It was created by the 1974 amendments to the Federal Election Campaign Act and opened its doors in 1975, in the wake of the Watergate scandal.

Structure

The commission consists of six members appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate; by law no more than three may belong to the same political party, and at least four votes are required for official action, a design intended to encourage bipartisanship but which frequently produces deadlocks on enforcement matters. Commissioners serve staggered six-year terms.

Duties

The FEC discloses campaign finance information filed by candidates, parties and political action committees; enforces contribution limits and prohibitions (such as the ban on contributions from corporations' general treasuries to candidates and on foreign national contributions); and administers the public funding system for presidential elections. Its authority covers elections for the U.S. House, Senate and presidency, but not state or local races. Supreme Court decisions such as Citizens United v. FEC (2010) have reshaped the regulatory landscape the commission oversees, permitting unlimited independent expenditures by corporations and unions through super PACs.
