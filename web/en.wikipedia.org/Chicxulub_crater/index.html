The Chicxulub crater is an impact crater buried underneath the Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico. Its center is offshore, near the community of Chicxulub, after which it is named. It was formed slightly over 66 million years ago when a large asteroid, about 10 to 15 kilometres (6.2 to 9.3 miles) in diameter, struck the Earth. The crater is estimated to be 180 kilometres (110 miles) in diameter and 20 kilometres (12 miles) deep, the third-largest confirmed impact structure on Earth. The impact has been identified as the cause of the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event, which killed off the non-avian dinosaurs and about 75% of all species on Earth.

Discovery

In 1978, geophysicists Glen Penfield and Antonio Camargo, working for the Mexican state oil company Pemex, detected a giant semicircular arc of gravity and magnetic anomalies while surveying the Yucatán. Penfield suspected an impact origin but confidentiality rules delayed publication. In 1980, physicist Luis Alvarez, his son Walter Alvarez, and colleagues published the hypothesis that a worldwide layer of iridium-rich clay at the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary recorded a giant impact. By 1990 the Chicxulub structure was connected to that evidence, and in 2010 an international panel of 41 scientists confirmed the impact as the extinction's cause.

The impact event

The asteroid struck shallow tropical seas at an estimated 20 km/s, releasing energy equivalent to billions of Hiroshima bombs. It generated mega-tsunamis, global wildfires, earthquakes far exceeding magnitude 9, and injected sulphur aerosols, soot, and dust into the stratosphere, plunging the planet into an "impact winter" that shut down photosynthesis for years. Evidence includes the K–Pg boundary clay, shocked quartz, tektites and tsunami deposits around the Gulf of Mexico, and the crater's peak ring, drilled in 2016 by IODP–ICDP Expedition 364.

Effects on life

Non-avian dinosaurs, pterosaurs, ammonites, and most marine reptiles perished. Survivors included birds (avian dinosaurs), small mammals, crocodilians, and many freshwater and detritus-feeding organisms. The extinction cleared ecological space for the subsequent radiation of mammals, eventually including humans.
