Earthquake

Earthquakes release energy as seismic waves along faults

An earthquake — also called a quake, tremor or temblor — is the shaking of the surface of the Earth resulting from a sudden release of energy in the Earth's lithosphere that creates seismic waves. Earthquakes can range in intensity from those so weak they cannot be felt to those violent enough to propel objects into the air, damage critical infrastructure, and trigger tsunamis, landslides and liquefaction.

Causes

Most earthquakes occur along geological faults, where accumulated elastic strain — largely from the motion of tectonic plates — is released in a rupture. Other causes include volcanic activity, landslides, mine collapses, nuclear tests and reservoir-induced seismicity. The point of initial rupture is the hypocenter (focus); the point directly above it on the surface is the epicenter. Elastic rebound theory, developed after the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, explains the stick–slip cycle of fault loading and failure.

Measurement

Seismographs record seismic waves, distinguishing fast compressional P-waves from slower shear S-waves and surface waves. Earthquake size is expressed as magnitude — today usually moment magnitude (Mw), which superseded the Richter scale — while shaking at a place is described by intensity scales such as Modified Mercalli. The largest instrumentally recorded earthquake was the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile (Mw 9.5); the deadliest include the 1556 Shaanxi earthquake and the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami.

Prediction and mitigation

Scientists can forecast long-term probabilities for fault segments but cannot predict individual earthquakes with useful precision. Earthquake early-warning systems exploit the speed difference between P-waves and damaging waves to give seconds of notice. Mitigation rests mainly on seismic building codes, retrofitting and public preparedness — the subject of earthquake engineering.
