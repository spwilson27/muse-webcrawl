Jonathan Swift (30 November 1667 – 19 October 1745) was an Anglo-Irish satirist, author, essayist, political pamphleteer and Anglican cleric, Dean of St Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin — regarded as the foremost prose satirist in the English language.

Early life

Born in Dublin to English parents (his father died before his birth), Swift was educated at Kilkenny College and Trinity College Dublin, and worked in England as secretary to the diplomat Sir William Temple at Moor Park, where he met Esther Johnson, the "Stella" of his later journal. After Temple's death he returned to Ireland, was ordained in the Church of Ireland, and served at Laracor.

Political writings and Dublin deanery

His early satires A Tale of a Tub and The Battle of the Books appeared in 1704. In London he first wrote for the Whigs, then defected to the Tories, editing The Examiner and writing influential pamphlets supporting the Harley ministry, including The Conduct of the Allies (1711). Hoping for an English bishopric, he instead became Dean of St Patrick's in 1713, and after the Tory fall in 1714 he lived in Ireland, which he initially considered exile. There he became an Irish patriot: the Drapier's Letters (1724) defeated the debased "Wood's halfpence" coinage scheme, and A Modest Proposal (1729) savagely ironised English indifference to Irish poverty by suggesting the poor sell their children as food.

Gulliver's Travels

His masterpiece, Travels into Several Remote Nations of the World — universally known as Gulliver's Travels (1726) — recounts Lemuel Gulliver's voyages to Lilliput, Brobdingnag, Laputa and the land of the Houyhnhnms and Yahoos, satirising politics, science, human pride and colonialism. Often abridged as a children's book, in full it is a ferocious work of misanthropic satire.

Death and legacy

Swift suffered for years from Ménière's disease-like symptoms and mental decline late in life; he died in 1745 and was buried in St Patrick's beside Stella, beneath his own famous Latin epitaph noting his "savage indignation" (saeva indignatio). He left much of his fortune to found a hospital for the mentally ill (St Patrick's Hospital, opened 1757). His works gave English the words "Lilliputian" and "Yahoo" and remain central to the satirical tradition.
