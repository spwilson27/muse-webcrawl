Heliotropism

Heliotropism is the directional growth or movement of a plant, or part of a plant, in response to sunlight. It is a form of phototropism in which the stimulus is specifically the sun. Heliotropic movements may be diaheliotropic, in which leaves orient perpendicular to the light to maximise photosynthesis, or paraheliotropic, in which leaves orient parallel to the light to avoid overheating and water loss.

Sunflower tracking

The best-known example is the young sunflower (Helianthus annuus), whose flower heads track the sun from east to west during the day and reorient during the night in anticipation of dawn. Research has shown that this behaviour is driven by an internal circadian clock acting together with light-sensitive growth responses: stems grow faster on the shaded side during the day and on the opposite side at night, causing the head to sweep across the sky. Mature sunflower heads generally face east, which warms the flowers, increases pollinator visits and seed yield, and may deter pests.

Mechanism

Heliotropic bending is mediated by photoreceptor proteins called phototropins, which detect blue light. Light perception triggers a redistribution of the plant hormone auxin to the shaded side of the stem; auxin promotes cell elongation there, bending the organ toward the light. In some species, specialised motor organs called pulvini at the base of leaves change turgor pressure to reorient leaf blades, as in many legumes such as beans, lupins and the prayer plant family.

Leaf and flower heliotropism

Many alpine and arctic plants, including mountain avens (Dryas octopetala) and arctic poppies, have cup-shaped heliotropic flowers that track the sun; the parabolic corolla focuses sunlight on the reproductive organs, raising their temperature, attracting pollinating insects and accelerating seed development. Crop plants such as soybean, cotton and sunflower also show pronounced leaf heliotropism, which can significantly affect canopy photosynthesis and water use.

History of study

The phenomenon was noted in antiquity and gave its name to the genus Heliotropium. Charles Darwin and his son Francis studied plant movements extensively in The Power of Movement in Plants (1880), demonstrating that the tip of a shoot perceives light while the bending occurs lower down — an early step toward the discovery of plant hormones.
