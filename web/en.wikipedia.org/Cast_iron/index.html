Cast iron

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cast iron
TypeIron–carbon alloy
Carbon content2–4 wt% (typically >2.1%)
Silicon content1–3 wt%
Melting range~1,150–1,200 °C (2,100–2,190 °F)
Key propertiesGood castability, compressive strength, wear resistance; brittle in most forms

Cast iron is a class of iron–carbon alloys with a carbon content of more than 2%. Its usefulness derives from its relatively low melting temperature. The alloy constituents affect its color when fractured: white cast iron has carbide impurities which allow cracks to pass straight through, while grey cast iron has graphitic flakes which deflect a passing crack and initiate countless new cracks as the material breaks. Ductile cast iron has spherical (noduled) graphite inclusions.

Carbon (C), ranging from 1.8 to 4 wt%, and silicon (Si), 1–3 wt%, are the main alloying elements of cast iron. Iron alloys with lower carbon content (~0.8%) are generally classed as steels.

History

Cast iron was first produced in China around the 5th century BC, where it was used for ploughshares, pots, and weapons. The technology reached Europe by the 14th–15th centuries. In Britain, Abraham Darby's use of coke smelting at Coalbrookdale in the early 18th century made cast iron much cheaper to produce, enabling cast iron bridges, engines, and machinery central to the Industrial Revolution. The Iron Bridge over the River Severn (1779) remains an iconic example.

Applications

Cast iron is used for engine blocks, pipes, cookware (skillets and Dutch ovens), machine-tool bases, gearbox housings, manhole covers, and architectural elements. Its low cost, castability into complex shapes, compressive strength, and vibration damping keep it in wide use despite competition from steel and aluminium.
