The Historia Regum Britanniae ("History of the Kings of Britain") is a pseudohistorical account of British history written in Latin by Geoffrey of Monmouth around 1136. Presented as a translation of an ancient British book — almost certainly a literary device — it traces the island's kings from the Trojan exile Brutus through two thousand years to the 7th-century Cadwaladr.

Contents

Geoffrey's narrative includes the founding of London as New Troy, King Leir (Shakespeare's Lear), the prophet Merlin — whose Prophecies Geoffrey also published — and, above all, the first full biography of King Arthur: his conception at Tintagel, conquests in Europe, betrayal by Mordred and departure to Avalon. Much of it elaborates hints in Gildas, Bede and Nennius, and a great deal is Geoffrey's invention.

Influence

The work was a medieval bestseller: over 200 manuscripts survive. It fixed the shape of the Arthurian legend taken up by Wace, Layamon, Chrétien de Troyes and ultimately Malory and Tennyson, and it shaped Welsh and English national mythology for centuries — Tudor monarchs cited descent from Arthur and Brutus.

Historical value

Contemporaries were already sceptical; William of Newburgh called it fabulous. Modern historians treat the Historia as literature and myth-making rather than a source for early Britain, though it may preserve fragments of Welsh tradition. It remains a landmark of medieval historiography and storytelling.
