Institutional syndrome

Institutional syndrome (also known as institutional neurosis) is a term describing deficits and abnormalities in the social and life skills of a person who has spent a long period in a mental hospital, prison, or other large institution. The concept was developed principally by the psychiatrist Russell Barton, whose 1959 book Institutional Neurosis described how the institutional environment itself — rather than only the patient's illness — produced apathy, loss of initiative, submissiveness, and difficulty functioning outside the institution.

Causes

Barton and later writers such as Erving Goffman (in his 1961 study of "total institutions", Asylums) attributed the syndrome to features of institutional life: loss of contact with the outside world, enforced idleness, regimentation and "batch living", loss of personal possessions and identity, and authoritarian staff–patient relationships. Similar effects are described in long-term prisoners (sometimes called institutionalisation or "prisonisation") and in children raised in deprived orphanages, where developmental delays may follow from lack of individual attention.

Prevention and response

Recognition of institutional syndrome contributed to the movement for deinstitutionalisation in psychiatry from the 1960s onwards, favouring community-based care, rehabilitation, normalisation, and shorter admissions. Within institutions that remain, countermeasures include personal clothing and possessions, meaningful occupation, patient autonomy, and maintaining ties with family and community.
