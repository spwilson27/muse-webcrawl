Charles Lindbergh

Charles Augustus Lindbergh (February 4, 1902 – August 26, 1974) was an American aviator and military officer who made the first solo nonstop transatlantic flight, from New York to Paris on May 20–21, 1927, in the monoplane Spirit of St. Louis. The flight made him one of the most famous people in the world and transformed public enthusiasm for aviation.

Early life and the flight

Lindbergh was born in Detroit and raised in Minnesota, the son of Congressman Charles August Lindbergh. He worked as an airmail pilot before competing for the $25,000 Orteig Prize for a nonstop New York–Paris flight. Backed by St. Louis businessmen, he flew a purpose-built Ryan monoplane; taking off from Roosevelt Field, he reached Le Bourget Field near Paris after 33½ hours alone over the Atlantic, to enormous acclaim.

Kidnapping tragedy

In 1932 his infant son, Charles Jr., was kidnapped from the family's New Jersey home and found dead, in what newspapers called the "crime of the century". Bruno Richard Hauptmann was convicted and executed for the crime. Hounded by publicity, the Lindberghs moved to Europe for several years.

Isolationism and later life

Before the Second World War, Lindbergh became a prominent spokesman for the America First Committee, opposing United States entry into the war, which damaged his reputation. After Pearl Harbor he served as a civilian adviser in the Pacific, flying combat missions. After the war he worked with airlines and on rocket pioneer Robert Goddard's behalf, and devoted his later years to conservation causes. He died in Hawaii in 1974.
