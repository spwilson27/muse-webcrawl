Singspiel

A Singspiel (German for "sing-play"; plural Singspiele) is a form of German-language music drama, now regarded as a genre of opera. It is characterised by alternating spoken dialogue and musical numbers — songs, ensembles, and choruses — typically with a comic or romantic plot, often including magical or folk elements. The spoken dialogue distinguishes it from through-sung opera, and the genre is a German counterpart to French opéra comique and English ballad opera.

History and examples

The Singspiel flourished in the later 18th century, encouraged by Emperor Joseph II's establishment of a German national Singspiel company in Vienna in 1778. Mozart composed its most enduring masterpieces: Die Entführung aus dem Serail (The Abduction from the Seraglio, 1782) and Die Zauberflöte (The Magic Flute, 1791), which mixes popular comedy with Masonic symbolism and music of transcendent seriousness. Beethoven's Fidelio and works by Hiller, Dittersdorf, and later Lortzing continue the tradition, which fed into German Romantic opera with Weber's Der Freischütz.
