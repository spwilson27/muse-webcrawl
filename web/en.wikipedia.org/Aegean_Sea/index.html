The Aegean Sea is an elongated embayment of the Mediterranean Sea between Europe and Asia, located between the Balkans and Anatolia. It covers about 214,000 square kilometres and is bounded by mainland Greece to the west and north, Turkey to the east, and the island of Crete to the south. Through the Dardanelles it connects with the Sea of Marmara and the Black Sea; its waters wash the shores of Greece and Turkey.

Geography

The Aegean is famously island-dotted: the Cyclades lie in its centre, the Dodecanese along the Turkish coast, the North Aegean islands (Lesbos, Chios, Samos, Lemnos, Samothrace) in the north, and the Sporades off Thessaly, with Euboea close to the Greek mainland. Many islands are volcanic — part of the South Aegean volcanic arc that includes Santorini (Thera), whose caldera eruption around 1600 BCE is linked with the decline of Minoan civilisation. The strongest regional wind is the summer meltemi (etesian).

History

The Aegean was the cradle of the Cycladic, Minoan and Mycenaean civilisations and of the city-states of classical Greece; the Aegean world gave rise to early Greek colonisation, the Delian League, and centuries of Venetian, Genoese and Ottoman rule. Its name was traditionally connected with King Aegeus of Athens or the Greek aiges ("waves", or "goats").

Modern issues

Sovereignty and maritime delimitation in the Aegean — territorial sea breadth, continental shelf, airspace and the demilitarisation status of eastern islands — have been a persistent source of Greek–Turkish disputes, brought near to conflict in crises such as those of 1987 and 1996 (Imia/Kardak). The sea is also a major tourism region and a route in the European migrant crisis of 2015–16.
