Unlawful combatant

An unlawful combatant, illegal combatant or unprivileged combatant/belligerent is a person who directly engages in armed conflict in violation of the laws of war or, in the terminology more commonly used after 2001, a person whom a state asserts is neither a lawful combatant entitled to prisoner-of-war status nor a civilian protected by the Fourth Geneva Convention. The term gained prominence in the context of the United States' detention of captured fighters at Guantanamo Bay following the September 11 attacks.

The Geneva Conventions of 1949 define the categories of persons protected in armed conflict.

Legal background

Under the Third Geneva Convention of 1949, lawful combatants — members of the armed forces of a party to a conflict, and members of militias or volunteer corps forming part of such forces and meeting the conditions of carrying arms openly, wearing a fixed distinctive sign, obeying the laws of war and operating under responsible command — are entitled to prisoner-of-war status upon capture and to combatant immunity for lawful acts of war. Persons who fight without meeting these conditions, such as spies, saboteurs and francs-tireurs, have historically been liable to trial and punishment for their acts, though they remain protected by fundamental guarantees of humane treatment and fair trial.

The concept has roots in the U.S. Supreme Court's decision in Ex parte Quirin (1942), which upheld the trial by military commission of German saboteurs landed in the United States, distinguishing "unlawful combatants" from lawful belligerents. International law does not, however, recognise a third status outside the categories of combatant and civilian: under the prevailing view of the International Committee of the Red Cross, a person who is not a combatant is a civilian, and civilians who directly participate in hostilities lose immunity from attack for such time but retain the protections of the Fourth Convention and of customary law.

Post-2001 usage

In January 2002 the administration of President George W. Bush determined that members of al-Qaeda and the Taliban detained in the "war on terror" were "unlawful combatants" not entitled to prisoner-of-war status, and that the Geneva Conventions did not apply to the conflict with al-Qaeda. The Military Commissions Act of 2006 adopted the statutory term "unlawful enemy combatant." The U.S. Supreme Court addressed related questions in Hamdi v. Rumsfeld (2004), Hamdan v. Rumsfeld (2006) — holding that Common Article 3 of the Geneva Conventions applied — and Boumediene v. Bush (2008), which recognised detainees' constitutional habeas corpus rights. In 2009 the Obama administration abandoned the "enemy combatant" terminology while retaining detention authority under the Authorization for Use of Military Force.

Criticism

Human rights organisations and many international lawyers criticised the category as an attempt to place detainees in a legal black hole. The ICRC and most governments maintain that the Geneva Conventions leave no gap: every person in an armed conflict is protected either as a combatant, as a civilian, or by the minimum guarantees of Common Article 3 and Additional Protocol I, article 75.
