History of Antarctica

The history of Antarctica begins with ancient speculation about a vast southern continent, Terra Australis, and proceeds through the first sightings of the continent in 1820, the exploration of its coasts and interior during the Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration, and its present-day governance under the Antarctic Treaty System.

Early speculation

Greek philosophers from Aristotle onward reasoned that the known lands of the north must be balanced by a southern landmass. Ptolemy's maps and medieval maps that followed depicted a great Terra Australis Incognita. Voyages by Magellan (1520), Drake and others gradually pushed the supposed continent southward; in 1773 Captain James Cook crossed the Antarctic Circle, and in 1775 he circumnavigated the high southern latitudes, concluding that any southern continent lay too far south to be habitable — while noting the abundance of seals and whales that would draw later commercial voyages.

First sightings and sealing era

The Antarctic mainland was first sighted in 1820, with credit claimed by three expeditions: Fabian Gottlieb von Bellingshausen (Russian), Edward Bransfield (British), and Nathaniel Palmer (American sealer). Sealers quickly followed, nearly exterminating Antarctic fur seals within a few years. In 1840–41 James Clark Ross discovered the Ross Sea, the Ross Ice Shelf and Mount Erebus, and reached a farthest south of 78°10′S. Dumont d'Urville and Charles Wilkes also charted stretches of the coast in the same period.

Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration

The period from about 1897 to 1922 is known as the Heroic Age. Carsten Borchgrevink's Southern Cross Expedition (1898–1900) made the first overwintering on the continent. Robert Falcon Scott's Discovery (1901–04) and Nimrod (1907–09, under Ernest Shackleton) expeditions pushed toward the Pole; Shackleton reached 88°23′S in 1909. On 14 December 1911 Roald Amundsen's Norwegian team became the first to reach the geographic South Pole; Scott's party arrived on 17 January 1912 and perished on the return journey. Shackleton's Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition (1914–17) failed to cross the continent but became famous for the survival of its crew after the loss of the Endurance.

Between the wars and after

Aerial exploration began in the 1920s and 1930s, notably by Richard E. Byrd, who flew over the South Pole in 1929. Several nations made territorial claims, and Operation Highjump (1946–47) was the largest expedition ever sent. During the International Geophysical Year of 1957–58, twelve nations established research stations across the continent, and the Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station was founded.

Antarctic Treaty era

The Antarctic Treaty, signed in 1959 and in force from 1961, reserved the continent for peaceful scientific use, froze territorial claims, and banned military activity and nuclear testing. The 1991 Madrid Protocol designated Antarctica a natural reserve and prohibited mineral extraction. Tourism, fishing and scientific research have grown since, while research stations investigate climate change, astronomy and glaciology.
