New Perspective on Paul

The New Perspective on Paul is a scholarly movement in New Testament studies that re-examines the writings of the Apostle Paul and his relationship to Judaism, especially in reaction to the traditional Protestant (Lutheran) reading of Paul as opposing a legalistic Judaism with a gospel of justification by faith.

Origins

The movement is usually traced to E. P. Sanders's Paul and Palestinian Judaism (1977), which argued that first-century Palestinian Judaism was characterised by "covenantal nomism" — the pattern in which belonging to the covenant was by grace, while the law regulated life within it — rather than by merit-based works-righteousness. The term "new perspective" was coined by James D. G. Dunn in a 1982 lecture.

Key claims

Scholars associated with the view, notably Dunn and N. T. Wright, argue that when Paul spoke of "works of the law," he chiefly meant Jewish boundary markers such as circumcision, food laws and Sabbath that separated Jew from Gentile, and that justification concerns who belongs to the covenant people of God rather than only how an individual is saved. Wright further emphasises the covenantal and eschatological framework of Paul's thought.

Criticism

The New Perspective has been vigorously debated, particularly by Reformed theologians who see it as undermining the doctrine of justification by faith alone, and by some scholars who question whether Sanders's account of Judaism or the reading of Paul holds uniformly.
