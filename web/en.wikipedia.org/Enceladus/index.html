Enceladus is the sixth-largest moon of Saturn and the most reflective body in the Solar System, its fresh ice surface giving it a geometric albedo above 1. It was discovered in 1789 by William Herschel and is named after the giant Enceladus of Greek mythology. Only about 500 km in diameter, it orbits Saturn within the diffuse E ring.

The Cassini discoveries

The Cassini spacecraft, which flew by Enceladus many times from 2005, revealed that the moon is geologically active: jets of water vapour and icy particles erupt from long fractures — dubbed "tiger stripes" — near the south pole. Measurements of the moon's libration and gravity showed that beneath the icy crust lies a global subsurface ocean of liquid water, in contact with a rocky core. The plume material, which supplies Saturn's E ring, contains salts, organic compounds and silica nanoparticles indicating hydrothermal activity on the ocean floor.

Habitability

Because it combines liquid water, organic chemistry and an energy source, Enceladus is considered one of the most promising places in the Solar System to search for extraterrestrial life, and dedicated orbiter/lander and plume-sampling missions have been proposed.
