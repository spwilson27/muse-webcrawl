Ixodes scapularis

Adult female Ixodes scapularis
Common name: Black-legged tick; deer tick
Family: Ixodidae
Distribution: Eastern and central North America

Ixodes scapularis, commonly known as the deer tick or black-legged tick, is a hard-bodied tick of the eastern and north-central United States and adjacent Canada. It is notorious as the principal vector of Lyme disease in North America.

Life cycle

The tick has a two-year life cycle with four stages: egg, six-legged larva, eight-legged nymph, and adult. Larvae and nymphs feed mainly on small mammals such as the white-footed mouse and on birds, while adults feed primarily on white-tailed deer, which are essential to reproduction but do not themselves transmit the Lyme spirochete Borrelia burgdorferi. Each blood meal takes several days, after which the tick drops off and molts or lays eggs.

Diseases transmitted

Besides Lyme disease (Borrelia burgdorferi and B. mayonii), I. scapularis transmits anaplasmosis, babesiosis, Powassan virus disease, ehrlichiosis (Ehrlichia muris eauclairensis) and Borrelia miyamotoi disease. The tiny nymph stage, active in late spring and summer, is responsible for most human Lyme infections because it is easily overlooked.

Range expansion

Reforestation, rebounding deer populations and a warming climate have expanded the tick's range substantially across the northeastern, mid-Atlantic and upper Midwestern states and into Canada in recent decades.
