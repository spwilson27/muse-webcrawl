Donald Olding Hebb FRS (22 July 1904 – 20 August 1985) was a Canadian psychologist who is considered the father of cognitive psychobiology and one of the most influential psychologists of the 20th century. He is best known for his theory of Hebbian learning, introduced in his 1949 book The Organization of Behavior, which provided a physiological mechanism for learning and memory in terms of connections between neurons.

Hebbian theory

Hebb's postulate is usually summarised as "cells that fire together wire together". In his own words: "When an axon of cell A is near enough to excite a cell B and repeatedly or persistently takes part in firing it, some growth process or metabolic change takes place in one or both cells such that A's efficiency, as one of the cells firing B, is increased." This synaptic plasticity mechanism became a foundation of modern neuroscience and of artificial neural network research, including long-term potentiation, whose discovery decades later broadly vindicated the proposal.

Hebb also introduced the concept of the cell assembly — a distributed network of neurons that, once strengthened by repeated co-activation, functions as the physiological basis of a thought or representation.

Career

After early work with Karl Lashley and studies of brain-injured patients alongside Wilder Penfield at the Montreal Neurological Institute, Hebb became professor of psychology at McGill University in 1947 and chairman of the department in 1948. His students included Brenda Milner and Donald Forgays. His research also covered sensory deprivation, fear, and the effects of early experience, and his Hebb–Williams maze became a standard tool for testing animal intelligence.

Hebb served as president of the American Psychological Association in 1960 and was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society. A Review of General Psychology survey ranked him among the most cited psychologists of the twentieth century.
