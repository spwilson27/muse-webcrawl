Eurasian Steppe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Eurasian Steppe, also called the Great Steppe, is the vast belt of temperate grassland stretching across Eurasia from the plains of Hungary and the Black Sea region in the west, through Ukraine, southern Russia, Kazakhstan, and Mongolia, to Manchuria in the east. It is the largest temperate grassland region in the world, covering some 8,000 kilometres.

Geography and ecology

The steppe lies in a temperate continental climate zone with hot summers, cold winters, and precipitation too low to support forests but sufficient for dense grass cover over rich black soils (chernozem). It is conventionally divided into the Pontic–Caspian steppe in the west, the Kazakh steppe (the largest single section), and the Mongolian steppe in the east. Native fauna historically included the saiga antelope, Przewalski's horse, wild ass, and large herds of grazing ungulates.

History

The steppe was the homeland of successive waves of nomadic pastoralist peoples — Scythians, Sarmatians, Huns, Turks, Khazars, Kipchaks, and Mongols — whose mobility and horse-archery repeatedly reshaped the politics of the settled agricultural civilizations on its fringes, from China to Persia and Europe. The domestication of the horse (associated with the Botai culture and later the Yamnaya culture) occurred here, and the steppe served as the principal corridor for the spread of Indo-European languages and, later, for the Silk Road trade routes.

The Mongol Empire of the 13th century unified nearly the entire steppe under one rule for the first time. From the 16th century onward, the expanding Russian and Qing empires progressively conquered and settled the steppe, a process largely complete by the 19th century; in the Soviet era, campaigns such as the Virgin Lands programme converted much of the Kazakh steppe to grain farming.
