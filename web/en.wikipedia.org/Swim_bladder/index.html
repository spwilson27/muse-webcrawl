Swim bladder

Image placeholder: the swim bladder (labelled) inside a bony fish.

The swim bladder, gas bladder, fish maw or air bladder is an internal gas-filled organ that contributes to the ability of many bony fish to control their buoyancy, allowing them to stay at their current water depth without having to expend energy in swimming. It also serves as a resonating chamber to produce or receive sound in some species.

Structure and function

The swim bladder normally comprises two gas-filled sacs located in the dorsal portion of the fish. In physostomous fish a duct (the pneumatic duct) connects the bladder to the gut, allowing the fish to gulp or release air; in the more derived physoclistous fish the connection is lost, and gas is exchanged with the blood via the rete mirabile and the oval organ. Deep-water fish fill the bladder mainly with oxygen.

Evolution

The swim bladder is evolutionarily homologous to the lungs of tetrapods; both develop as an outpocketing of the gut. Cartilaginous fish such as sharks lack a swim bladder and must swim continually or rest on the bottom.

Human use

In some Asian cultures the dried swim bladders of large fish ("fish maw") are considered a delicacy and a source of collagen; the bladder of the totoaba has driven illegal trade threatening the vaquita porpoise. Historically, swim bladders were also a source of isinglass, a clarifying agent for beer and wine.
