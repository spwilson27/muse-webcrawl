Oxygen saturation (medicine)

Oxygen saturation is the fraction of oxygen-saturated haemoglobin relative to total haemoglobin in the blood. In medicine it is a key vital sign; normal arterial oxygen saturation (SaO2) in humans is about 95–100 percent, while venous saturation (SvO2) is around 75 percent.

Measurement

Arterial saturation is measured directly from an arterial blood gas sample, whereas peripheral saturation (SpO2) is estimated non-invasively by pulse oximetry, which shines red and infrared light through a fingertip or earlobe and analyses the pulsatile absorbance of blood.

Clinical significance

Low oxygen saturation, or hypoxemia, may result from lung disease, heart failure, anaemia, or high altitude, and can cause cyanosis, confusion, and organ damage if severe. Oxygen saturation monitoring is standard in surgery, intensive care, and emergency medicine, and became widely used at home during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Limitations

Pulse oximeters can be less accurate with poor peripheral perfusion, nail polish, dark skin pigmentation, or in the presence of abnormal haemoglobins such as carboxyhaemoglobin from carbon monoxide poisoning.
