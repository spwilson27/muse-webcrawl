Extractive metallurgy

Extractive metallurgy is the practice of removing valuable metals from an ore and refining the extracted raw metals into a purer form. In order to convert a metal oxide or sulphide to a purer metal, the ore must be reduced physically, chemically, or electrolytically. Extractive metallurgists are interested in three primary streams: feed (the ore), concentrate (valuable metal oxide/sulphide), and tailings (waste).

Branches

The field divides into mineral processing — crushing, grinding, and concentrating the ore by methods such as froth flotation, gravity separation, and magnetic separation — and the chemical extraction routes themselves: pyrometallurgy (high-temperature processes, including roasting, smelting in blast and flash furnaces, and converting), hydrometallurgy (aqueous chemistry, including leaching, solvent extraction, and precipitation), and electrometallurgy (electrowinning and electrorefining, as in the Hall–Héroult process for aluminium and the electrorefining of copper).

Processes and examples

Iron is extracted by carbothermic reduction of ore in the blast furnace; copper sulphide ores are concentrated by flotation, smelted, converted to blister copper, and electrorefined to 99.99% purity; gold is recovered by cyanidation and the Merrill–Crowe or carbon-in-pulp processes; zinc is largely produced by roast–leach–electrowin flowsheets. The choice of route depends on the chemistry of the ore, economics, and increasingly on environmental constraints such as sulphur dioxide emissions and water use.

Modern developments

Declining ore grades, urban mining of electronic waste, and the demand for battery and energy-transition metals (lithium, cobalt, nickel, rare earths) have pushed research into bioleaching by microorganisms, direct lithium extraction from brines, deep-sea nodule processing, and lower-carbon smelting using hydrogen or inert-anode electrolysis.
