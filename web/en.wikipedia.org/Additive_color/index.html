Additive color

Additive color or additive mixing is a property of a color model that predicts the appearance of colors made by coincident component lights — that is, the perceived color can be predicted by summing the numeric representations of the component colors. Modern descriptions of Grassmann's laws describe the additivity in the color perception of light mixtures in terms of algebraic equations.

Principles

Additive color is the result of the way the eye detects color, and is a direct result of the kinds of color detectors in the eye. The human eye has three types of color-sensitive cone cells, roughly sensitive to red, green, and blue light. When red, green, and blue light are combined in equal intensities, the result is perceived as white; pairs of these primaries produce cyan, magenta, and yellow.

Applications

Additive mixing is used in electronic displays such as televisions, computer monitors, and phone screens, where images are formed from tiny red, green, and blue elements (the RGB color model). It is also used in stage lighting, where colored lights are combined on stage. The opposite process, used in printing and painting, is subtractive color mixing, based on cyan, magenta, and yellow pigments.

History

The principles of additive color were described by James Clerk Maxwell in the 1850s and formalized by Hermann Grassmann. Maxwell's color triangle and his experiments with spinning discs laid the groundwork for modern colorimetry.
