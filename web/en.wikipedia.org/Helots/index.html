Helots

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The helots (Ancient Greek: εἵλωτες, heílōtes) were a subjugated population of Laconia and Messenia in the ancient Greek world whose status fell between that of free citizens and chattel slaves. They were tied to the land, which they farmed for the benefit of their Spartan masters, the Spartiates, and they formed the economic foundation that allowed Spartan citizens to devote themselves entirely to military training and warfare.

Origins and status

The helots were traditionally understood to be the descendants of populations conquered as Sparta expanded: the inhabitants of Laconia and, after the Messenian Wars of the eighth and seventh centuries BCE, the Messenians, who retained a strong collective identity and repeatedly revolted. Unlike ordinary Greek slaves, helots were owned collectively by the Spartan state rather than by individuals; they could not be sold abroad, lived in their own family communities and villages, and kept a portion of their produce, handing over a fixed share (the apophora) to the Spartiate who held the land allotment (kleros) they worked.

Treatment and control

Helots greatly outnumbered the Spartiates, and fear of revolt structured Spartan life. According to later sources, the ephors annually declared war on the helots so that they could be killed without religious pollution, and the krypteia — a rite of passage for selected young Spartans — involved spying on and murdering helots, particularly the strongest. Thucydides records that during the Peloponnesian War the Spartans invited the helots to select those who had most distinguished themselves in war, promising freedom, and then made some two thousand of them disappear. Helots nonetheless served the Spartan army in large numbers as light troops, attendants, and sometimes hoplites, and could occasionally earn freedom, becoming neodamodeis.

Revolts

The greatest helot revolt followed the earthquake of about 464 BCE, when the Messenian helots fortified Mount Ithome and held out for years before being allowed to leave the Peloponnese; the Athenians settled them at Naupactus. Sparta's dependence on helot labour and terror of rebellion are often cited as reasons for its famously conservative foreign policy. In 370/369 BCE Epaminondas of Thebes liberated Messenia and founded Messene, ending helotage there; helotry in Laconia itself faded during the Hellenistic period.
