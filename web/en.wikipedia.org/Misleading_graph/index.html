Misleading graph

In statistics, a misleading graph, also known as a distorted graph, is a graph that misrepresents data, constituting a misuse of statistics and with the result that an incorrect conclusion may be derived from it. Graphs may be misleading through excessive complexity, or poorly constructed, or the data may be misinterpreted. Even when constructed to display accurately the characteristics of their data, graphs can be subject to different interpretations, or viewers might accidentally get incorrect impressions.

The classic text on the subject is Darrell Huff's How to Lie with Statistics (1954), and Edward Tufte's work on data visualisation popularised principles — such as the "lie factor" and data-ink ratio — for honest graphical design.

Common techniques

  • Truncated or broken axes — starting a bar chart's value axis above zero exaggerates small differences.
  • Non-uniform scales — irregular axis intervals or dual axes implying a false relationship.
  • Pictogram scaling — scaling an icon in two dimensions so its area grows quadratically with the value it represents.
  • 3D effects — perspective tilts that make front bars or pie slices appear larger.
  • Cherry picking — choosing start and end dates that flatter a trend.
  • Missing context — omitting absolute numbers, denominators, or uncertainty.
  • Inverted axes — reversing an axis direction to invert the apparent trend.

Detection and good practice

Readers can check whether axes start at zero (for bar charts), whether intervals are uniform, whether labels and sources are present, and whether the visual effect matches the numerical change — Tufte's "lie factor" compares the size of the visual effect to the size of the effect in the data. Good practice includes clear titles, labelled axes, consistent scales, and showing uncertainty.
