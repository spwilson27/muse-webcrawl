Colorado River

The Colorado River is one of the principal rivers (along with the Rio Grande) in the southwestern United States and northern Mexico. The 1,450-mile-long (2,330 km) river drains an expansive, arid watershed that encompasses parts of seven U.S. states and two Mexican states. It begins in the Rocky Mountains of Colorado and flows generally southwest across the Colorado Plateau and through the Grand Canyon before reaching Lake Mead on the Arizona–Nevada border, where it turns south toward the international border.

Course

From its source at La Poudre Pass in Rocky Mountain National Park, the Colorado flows through Grand Lake, down the western slope of Colorado, into Utah, and across northern Arizona, where it has carved the Grand Canyon over millions of years through uplift of the Colorado Plateau. Below Lake Mead it forms the Arizona–California border, passes Parker Dam and Imperial Dam, and historically flowed into the Gulf of California through a vast delta — although diversions mean its water now rarely reaches the sea.

History

Indigenous peoples including the Ancestral Puebloans, Hohokam, and river tribes such as the Mojave and Quechan have lived along the river for thousands of years. Spanish explorer Francisco de Ulloa reached its mouth in 1539, and Hernando de Alarcón and Melchior Díaz ascended it in 1540; García López de Cárdenas viewed the Grand Canyon the same year. John Wesley Powell led the first recorded navigation of the Grand Canyon in 1869 and 1871–72.

Water management

The river is governed by the "Law of the River", beginning with the Colorado River Compact of 1922, which apportioned 7.5 million acre-feet annually to each of the Upper and Lower Basins — based on flows measured during an anomalously wet period. Major infrastructure includes Hoover Dam (completed 1936, forming Lake Mead), Glen Canyon Dam (1966, Lake Powell), and the All-American Canal supplying California's Imperial Valley. Prolonged drought since 2000 has sharply reduced both reservoirs, prompting shortage-sharing agreements among the basin states and Mexico.

Ecology

The river supports distinctive native fish such as the humpback chub and Colorado pikeminnow, both endangered, while introduced species, dams, and water diversions have transformed its temperature and sediment regime and destroyed much of its former delta wetlands.
