Enzyme

Enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts by accelerating chemical reactions. The molecules upon which enzymes may act are called substrates, and the enzyme converts the substrates into different molecules known as products. Almost all metabolic processes in the cell need enzyme catalysis in order to occur at rates fast enough to sustain life.

Enzymes are known to catalyse more than 5,000 types of biochemical reactions. Like all catalysts, enzymes increase the reaction rate by lowering its activation energy. Some enzymes can make their conversion of substrate to product occur many millions of times faster than the uncatalysed reaction. An extreme example is orotidine 5'-phosphate decarboxylase, which allows a reaction that would otherwise take millions of years to occur in milliseconds.

Structure and mechanism

Enzymes are generally globular proteins whose three-dimensional structures contain an active site — a pocket or groove that binds the substrate with high specificity. Early models described this fit as a "lock and key"; the modern induced fit model holds that the active site reshapes slightly upon substrate binding. Many enzymes require non-protein helper molecules called cofactors, which may be inorganic ions such as Fe2+ or Mg2+, or organic coenzymes such as NAD+ and the B vitamins.

Kinetics and inhibition

Enzyme kinetics is commonly described by the Michaelis–Menten equation, in which the reaction rate rises with substrate concentration until the enzyme is saturated. Enzyme activity can be regulated by inhibitors: competitive inhibitors bind the active site, whereas non-competitive (allosteric) inhibitors bind elsewhere and change the enzyme's shape. Many drugs — including penicillin, statins, and aspirin — act as enzyme inhibitors. Enzymes are also sensitive to temperature and pH and can be denatured outside their optimal ranges.

Nomenclature and classes

Enzymes are classified by the Enzyme Commission (EC) into seven main classes according to the reaction catalysed: oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, ligases, and translocases. Their names typically end in "-ase" (e.g., lactase, DNA polymerase). A few catalytic RNA molecules, called ribozymes, show that not all biological catalysts are proteins.

Applications

Enzymes are widely used in industry — in detergents, food processing, brewing, biofuel production, and molecular biology (for example, polymerases in PCR) — and in medicine, both as drug targets and as therapeutic agents.
