Meiji era

The Meiji era (明治時代, Meiji jidai) is the 45-year period of Japanese history running from 23 October 1868 to 30 July 1912, corresponding to the reign of Emperor Meiji. It began with the Meiji Restoration, which ended the Tokugawa shogunate and restored practical imperial rule, and it saw Japan transform from a feudal, isolationist society into an industrialised world power.

Restoration and consolidation

The era opened with the Boshin War (1868–1869), in which imperial forces defeated the shogunate. The new government abolished the feudal domains (han) in 1871, replaced them with prefectures, dismantled the hereditary class system, and introduced conscription in 1873, ending the samurai monopoly on military service. The Iwakura Mission of 1871–1873 toured the United States and Europe to study Western institutions.

Modernisation

Under the slogan fukoku kyōhei ("enrich the country, strengthen the military"), the government built railways, telegraph lines, lighthouses and modern industries, hired thousands of foreign advisers (oyatoi gaikokujin), and sent students abroad. A centralised education system was established, and the Bank of Japan was founded in 1882. The Meiji Constitution, promulgated in 1889, created a constitutional monarchy with an elected lower house in the Imperial Diet.

Wars and expansion

Japan's victory in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895) gained Taiwan and ended Chinese influence in Korea; victory in the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905) made Japan the first Asian power to defeat a European great power in modern warfare. Korea was annexed in 1910.

Legacy

The Meiji era laid the foundations of the modern Japanese state: a centralised bureaucracy, a national education system, modern armed forces and an industrial economy. Its rapid social change also produced tensions, including samurai revolts such as the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877 and later debates over the direction of Japanese democracy.
