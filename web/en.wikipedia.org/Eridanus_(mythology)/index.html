Eridanus (mythology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Eridanus (Ancient Greek: Ἠριδανός, Ēridanós) is a river of Greek mythology, celebrated by the ancient poets as a great stream of the far north (or far west) of Europe, associated with amber and with the myth of Phaethon. Hesiod mentions it among the rivers born of Oceanus and Tethys, and Herodotus reported — though he doubted the story — that the Eridanus flowed into the northern sea and was the source of amber.

The Phaethon myth

The Eridanus is best known as the river into which Phaethon fell after losing control of the chariot of his father Helios, the sun god. Struck down by Zeus's thunderbolt to save the earth from burning, Phaethon plunged into the Eridanus, where his sisters, the Heliades, mourned him on the banks and were transformed into poplar trees; their tears became drops of amber. The story is told most fully in Ovid's Metamorphoses.

Identification with real rivers

Because the Eridanus was linked with amber, which reached the Mediterranean from the Baltic coasts, ancient and modern writers alike proposed identifications with real rivers. The most common is the Po in northern Italy, near whose mouth amber was traded and into which later geographers placed Phaethon's fall; other candidates include the Rhône and the Rhine. No single identification commanded universal assent in antiquity, and the river remained partly a stream of myth and the distant north.

Astronomy

The name survives in the sky as Eridanus, one of the 48 constellations listed by Ptolemy and still among the 88 modern constellations: a long, winding line of stars representing the mythical river, extending from near Orion far into the southern sky.
