Thomas Cranmer (2 July 1489 – 21 March 1556) was an English Reformer who served as Archbishop of Canterbury during the reigns of Henry VIII, Edward VI and, for a short time, Mary I. He helped build the case for the annulment of Henry's marriage to Catherine of Aragon, which was one of the causes of the separation of the English Church from union with the Holy See.

Early life and rise

Cranmer was born at Aslockton in Nottinghamshire and educated at Jesus College, Cambridge. He came to royal attention in 1529 when he suggested that Henry's case be argued before the universities of Europe rather than in the papal court. In 1532 he was sent as ambassador to the Holy Roman Empire, where he married Margaret, the niece of the Lutheran reformer Andreas Osiander. On his return he was consecrated Archbishop of Canterbury in March 1533, and shortly afterwards declared Henry's marriage to Catherine null and his marriage to Anne Boleyn valid.

Reformation work

Under Edward VI, Cranmer was able to promote major reforms. He wrote and compiled the first two editions of the Book of Common Prayer (1549 and 1552), which established the liturgical structure of the Church of England and remain a lasting influence on English language and literature. He also authored the Book of Homilies and contributed to the Forty-two Articles, the doctrinal statement later revised into the Thirty-nine Articles.

Downfall and death

After Edward's death in 1553, Cranmer supported the succession of Lady Jane Grey, but Mary I succeeded and had him imprisoned for treason and heresy. Under pressure he signed recantations of his Protestant faith, but on the day of his execution, 21 March 1556, he publicly repudiated them before being burned at the stake in Oxford. According to tradition, he held the hand that had signed the recantations in the flame first. He is commemorated as one of the Oxford Martyrs.
