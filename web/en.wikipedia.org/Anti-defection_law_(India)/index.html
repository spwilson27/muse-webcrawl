Anti-defection law (India)

The anti-defection law in India, enshrined in the Tenth Schedule of the Constitution, seeks to prevent political defections by members of legislatures — the practice popularly associated with the phrase "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" — by providing for the disqualification of members who voluntarily give up membership of their political party or vote contrary to the party's direction (whip) without permission.

History

The Tenth Schedule was inserted by the Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985, during the premiership of Rajiv Gandhi, after decades in which floor-crossing repeatedly toppled state governments. The Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003 deleted the provision that had protected a "split" involving one-third of a legislature party, leaving only a merger supported by at least two-thirds of the party's members as a defence.

Provisions

A member may be disqualified if they voluntarily give up party membership, or vote or abstain contrary to a whip without prior permission and the act is not condoned within fifteen days. Independent members who join a party, and nominated members who join a party after six months, are also disqualified. Questions of disqualification are decided by the Speaker or Chairman of the House, whose decision is subject to judicial review, as the Supreme Court held in Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992).

Criticism

Critics argue the law curbs legislators' freedom of speech and conscience, strengthens party leaderships, and that presiding officers — usually aligned with the ruling party — often delay disqualification decisions, undermining the law's purpose.
