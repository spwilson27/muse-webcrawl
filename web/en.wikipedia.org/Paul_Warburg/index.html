Paul Moritz Warburg (1868–1932) was a German-born American banker and a leading advocate of central banking in the United States. He is regarded as one of the principal architects of the Federal Reserve System and served on the first Federal Reserve Board from 1914 to 1918.

Career

Warburg was born into the Hamburg banking family behind M. M. Warburg & Co. He emigrated to New York in 1902, became a partner at Kuhn, Loeb & Co., and married Nina Loeb. Alarmed by the Panic of 1907, he wrote influential essays arguing that the United States needed a modern central banking system modeled partly on European practice, and in 1910 he attended the secret meeting on Jekyll Island, Georgia, where bankers and Senator Nelson Aldrich drafted a plan for banking reform.

Federal Reserve

Many of Warburg's ideas — an elastic currency, discounting of commercial paper, and a network of regional reserve banks under a central board — were incorporated into the Federal Reserve Act of 1913. Appointed by President Woodrow Wilson to the Federal Reserve Board, he served as its vice governor during the First World War. After the war he founded the International Acceptance Bank and helped organize financing for German reparations.
