Civil–military relations

Civilian oversight of the armed forces is the central concern of the field.

Civil–military relations is the field of study — spanning political science, sociology and history — concerned with the relationship between a society's armed forces and its civilian political leadership and society at large. Its central question is how democratic societies maintain civilian control of the military: ensuring that the institution with a monopoly on organised violence remains subordinate to elected authority while remaining effective enough to defend the state.

Classical theory

Samuel P. Huntington's The Soldier and the State (1957) framed the problem in terms of "objective control": maximising military professionalism, granting officers autonomy in their technical sphere in exchange for political neutrality and obedience. Morris Janowitz, in The Professional Soldier (1960), offered a contrasting view, arguing that convergence between military and civilian values and the political awareness of officers were inevitable and could be managed. Later scholars, including Peter Feaver, developed principal–agent models of civilian control, examining how civilians monitor and punish military shirking or disobedience.

Key issues

Topics in the field include coups d'état and their prevention ("coup-proofing"), the political influence of defence establishments, conscription versus all-volunteer forces, military involvement in internal security and disaster relief, the role of private military companies, veterans in politics, and the norms governing the use of force. The "civil–military gap" — cultural and experiential distance between service members and the wider public — is a recurring concern in countries with small professional militaries.

Practice across states

Most democracies institutionalise civilian control through a civilian defence ministry, legislative control of defence budgets, and constitutional provisions making the head of government or state commander-in-chief. Where such arrangements break down, militaries may intervene directly in politics, as in the many military regimes of the 20th century. See also military and coup d'état.
