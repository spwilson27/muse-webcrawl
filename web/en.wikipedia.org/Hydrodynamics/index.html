Hydrodynamics

Flow patterns around a body moving through water

In physics, hydrodynamics is a sub-discipline of fluid dynamics concerned with the motion of fluids, especially liquids such as water, and the forces acting on solid bodies immersed in them. In practice the term is often used more broadly for the dynamics of liquids in motion, in contrast to hydrostatics, the study of liquids at rest. Its governing equations are the Navier–Stokes equations, together with the continuity equation expressing conservation of mass.

Principles

For incompressible flow of an ideal (inviscid) fluid, motion is described by Euler's equations, from which Bernoulli's principle follows: along a streamline, an increase in speed is accompanied by a decrease in pressure or potential energy. Real liquids have viscosity, which produces shear stresses, boundary layers, turbulence, and drag. Key dimensionless quantities include the Reynolds number, which predicts the transition between laminar and turbulent flow, and the Froude number, which compares inertial forces with gravity and governs free-surface phenomena such as waves and ship wakes.

Applications

Hydrodynamics underlies naval architecture and ship design (hull resistance, propulsion, and stability), hydraulic engineering, oceanography and the study of currents and tides, and the design of pipelines, pumps, and turbines. Specialised branches include magnetohydrodynamics, which treats electrically conducting fluids such as plasmas, and quantum hydrodynamics. Model testing in towing tanks and water tunnels, and computational fluid dynamics simulations, are standard tools for predicting hydrodynamic behaviour.

History

The subject began with Archimedes' work on hydrostatics and was placed on a mathematical footing by Daniel Bernoulli (Hydrodynamica, 1738), Leonhard Euler, and Claude-Louis Navier and George Gabriel Stokes in the 19th century. Practical puzzles such as d'Alembert's paradox — that an ideal fluid exerts no drag on a body moving steadily through it — were resolved by Ludwig Prandtl's boundary layer theory in the early 20th century.
