Oyneg Shabes (Yiddish: עונג שבת, "Joy of the Sabbath"; also rendered Oneg Shabbat) was the code name of a clandestine organisation led by the historian Emanuel Ringelblum in the Warsaw Ghetto during the Second World War. The group collected documents, diaries, essays, photographs, drawings, official decrees, and ephemera in order to preserve a record of Jewish life under Nazi occupation and of the persecution and mass murder of Polish Jewry.

Activity

Ringelblum, a professional historian associated before the war with YIVO, began gathering material soon after the German invasion of Poland in 1939. The group, meeting on Saturdays (hence the name), eventually involved dozens of writers, teachers, economists, rabbis, and activists across the political spectrum. Contributors documented hunger, disease, smuggling, education, religious life, the work of the Judenrat, and the deportations to Treblinka that began in the summer of 1942. Unlike official German records, the archive deliberately preserved the Jewish perspective.

Burial and recovery

As the ghetto faced destruction, the archive was buried in batches in metal boxes and milk cans beneath buildings. The first cache was recovered in September 1946 and a second in December 1950; a third cache is believed never to have been found. Only three members of the group — among them Hersz Wasser, whose knowledge located the caches, and the writer Rachela Auerbach — are known to have survived the war. Ringelblum himself escaped briefly but was discovered and murdered with his family in 1944.

Legacy

The Ringelblum Archive, comprising tens of thousands of pages, is held by the Jewish Historical Institute in Warsaw and was inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 1999. It is regarded as one of the most important bodies of testimony from the Holocaust, foundational for historians of the Warsaw Ghetto. A major English-language scholarly account is Samuel Kassow's Who Will Write Our History? (2007), later adapted into a documentary film.
