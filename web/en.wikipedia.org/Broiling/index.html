Broiling

MethodDry-heat cooking
Heat sourceDirect radiant heat from above
UK termGrilling

Broiling is a cooking method that involves exposing food to direct radiant heat, either on a grill over live coals or below a gas burner or electric coil in an oven. In North American usage, broiling specifically means cooking with the heat source above the food, whereas in British and Commonwealth English the same technique is called grilling, and "broiling" is uncommon.

Technique

Broiling uses very high temperatures, typically 290–315 °C (550–600 °F) in domestic ovens, and cooks food quickly. The food is placed on a broiler pan, often with a slotted tray that lets fat drip away. Because of the intense heat, broiling suits relatively thin cuts of meat, fish fillets, and vegetables, and produces browning and caramelisation through the Maillard reaction. Food is usually turned once during cooking.

Comparison with grilling

Broiling differs from (American) grilling mainly in the direction of the heat: broilers apply heat from above, while grills apply it from below. Both are dry-heat methods grouped with roasting and baking. In a salamander broiler, used in professional kitchens, an overhead element finishes dishes such as gratins and crème brûlée toppings.

Safety

Because dripping fat can ignite under intense heat, food should be monitored closely during broiling, and ovens recommend leaving the door slightly ajar or closed depending on the appliance design. Broiling also generates smoke and should be done with ventilation.
