Hélio Gracie (October 1, 1913 – January 29, 2009) was a Brazilian martial artist who, together with his brother Carlos Gracie, founded Gracie Jiu-Jitsu, the self-defence system now known worldwide as Brazilian jiu-jitsu (BJJ). A slight and physically frail man, he adapted the judo/jujutsu taught to his family by Mitsuyo Maeda so that leverage and technique could allow a smaller person to defeat a larger, stronger opponent.

Early life and adaptation of the art

Hélio was the youngest son of Gastão Gracie. His brother Carlos learned jujutsu from the Japanese judoka Mitsuyo Maeda in Belém. Because of his small build — he stood about 5 ft 7 in and weighed roughly 140 lb — Hélio was initially forbidden to train, and instead learned by watching classes. When he was given the chance to teach in Carlos's place, he began modifying techniques to rely on timing and leverage rather than strength, refining the ground-fighting system that became Gracie Jiu-Jitsu.

Fighting career

Hélio defended the family name in challenge matches (vale tudo) for decades. His most famous bout was against the judoka Masahiko Kimura in 1951: Kimura finally won with a shoulder lock that broke Hélio's arm — Hélio refused to submit, and the technique was afterwards renamed the "Kimura" lock in BJJ. In 1955 he fought former student Waldemar Santana in a match lasting 3 hours and 45 minutes, one of the longest in history. He continued teaching and even rolling on the mat into his nineties.

Legacy

His sons — among them Rorion, Relson, Rickson, and Royce Gracie — carried the art to the United States; Royce's victories in the early Ultimate Fighting Championship events in the 1990s demonstrated BJJ's effectiveness and transformed modern mixed martial arts. Hélio died in 2009 at age 95 and is recognised as a grandmaster and one of the founders of Brazilian jiu-jitsu.
