Overcriminalization

Overcriminalization is the concept that criminalization has become excessive or unnecessary in a society, with too many acts and behaviours defined as crimes and too many sanctions prescribed for them. Critics argue that the proliferation of criminal statutes — particularly in regulatory and federal law — exposes ordinary people to prosecution for conduct that is not intuitively wrongful.

Background

The term gained prominence in American legal scholarship as the number of federal criminal laws grew into the thousands, alongside tens of thousands of regulations carrying criminal penalties. Scholars such as William J. Stuntz argued that broad, overlapping statutes give prosecutors enormous discretion.

Criticism and debate

Proponents of reform argue that overcriminalization contributes to mass incarceration, punishes conduct lacking criminal intent (mens rea), and burdens marginalised communities. Defenders of expansive criminal codes respond that complex modern societies require extensive regulation and that prosecutorial discretion is usually exercised responsibly.

Reform efforts

Reform proposals include default mens rea requirements, sunsetting obsolete offences, codifying regulatory crimes more clearly, and expanding the rule of lenity in statutory interpretation.
