Cloud forest

A cloud forest, also called a water forest, primas forest, or tropical montane cloud forest, is a generally tropical or subtropical, evergreen, montane, moist forest characterised by a persistent, frequent, or seasonal low-level cloud cover, usually at the canopy level. Cloud forests often exhibit an abundance of ground and tree mosses, giving them the alternative name mossy forests, and they harbour exceptional biodiversity for their size, covering only a small fraction of the world's tropical forest area.

Characteristics

Cloud forests typically occur at elevations between 500 and 4,000 metres, where moisture-laden air is forced upward by mountain slopes and condenses at the lifting condensation level. Persistent mist reduces direct sunlight and evapotranspiration, keeping the forest cool and saturated. Trees are generally shorter and more gnarled than in lowland rainforest, with dense crowns heavily laden with epiphytes — orchids, bromeliads, ferns, mosses, and lichens. Windswept ridge-top cloud forests of stunted trees are known as elfin forests.

Hydrological importance

Vegetation in cloud forests strips liquid water directly from passing clouds, a process called horizontal precipitation or fog drip, which can add substantially to the water available to streams and aquifers downslope. Cloud forests therefore act as critical water towers for agriculture, hydroelectric generation, and urban supplies in regions such as Central America and the Andes.

Distribution and biodiversity

Notable cloud forests include the Monteverde reserve in Costa Rica, the Yungas of Bolivia and Argentina, the forests of Mount Kinabalu in Borneo, and remnants in the Western Ghats and East African mountains. These forests are refuges for many endemic species, including the resplendent quetzal, mountain gorillas, and numerous amphibians found nowhere else.

Threats

Cloud forests are threatened by clearing for agriculture and grazing, logging, road building, and climate change: a warming climate raises the average cloud base, drying the forest, a mechanism implicated in the extinction of the golden toad of Monteverde. Because they occupy narrow altitudinal bands, upslope migration may leave species with nowhere to go.
