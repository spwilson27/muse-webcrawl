Duck as food

Roast duck, a classic preparation

In many parts of the world, duck is eaten as meat. Most commercial duck production uses the domesticated Pekin duck, a descendant of the mallard, and the Muscovy duck. Duck meat is a rich, dark red meat with a strong flavour, covered by a thick layer of fat beneath the skin.

Production

China is by far the world's largest producer and consumer of duck meat, followed by France and other countries in Europe and Asia. Ducks are typically slaughtered at seven to eight weeks of age for the Pekin breed. Duck farming is also important in Vietnam, Thailand, and Indonesia.

Culinary uses

Duck is prepared in many ways: roasted whole, as in the famous Peking duck of Beijing, prized for its crisp lacquered skin; as confit, in which the legs are salted and slowly cooked in their own fat (southwest France); as magret, the breast of a duck raised for foie gras, served like a steak; and in curries in Southeast Asia. Duck eggs are eaten in China (including salted duck eggs and century eggs) and in the Philippines, where the fertilised egg balut is a popular street food. Duck fat is valued for roasting potatoes, and the liver is made into foie gras.

Nutrition

Duck meat is a good source of protein, iron, selenium and B vitamins. Because of the thick fat layer under the skin, skinless duck breast is much leaner than meat eaten with the skin, and much of the fat can be rendered off during cooking.

Notable dishes

  • Duck à l'orange (France)
  • Confit de canard (France)
  • Bebek betutu (Bali, Indonesia)
