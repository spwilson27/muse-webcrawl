Medicare Part D, also called the Medicare prescription drug benefit, is an optional United States federal-government program to help Medicare beneficiaries pay for self-administered prescription drugs. Part D was enacted as part of the Medicare Prescription Drug, Improvement, and Modernization Act of 2003 (MMA) and went into effect on January 1, 2006. Under the program, drug benefits are provided by private insurance plans that receive payments from Medicare, rather than being administered directly by the federal government.

Benefit design

Part D plans are offered by private insurers approved by Medicare. Beneficiaries may enroll in a stand-alone Prescription Drug Plan (PDP) to supplement Traditional Medicare, or obtain drug coverage through a Medicare Advantage plan that includes drug benefits. Plans charge a monthly premium, an annual deductible, and cost-sharing that varies by the plan's formulary — the list of covered drugs, typically organized into tiers.

Historically, the standard benefit included a coverage gap known as the "donut hole," in which enrollees paid a larger share of drug costs after initial coverage ended and before catastrophic coverage began. The Affordable Care Act gradually closed the gap, and the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 restructured the benefit further, introducing an annual out-of-pocket cap of $2,000 beginning in 2025 and eliminating the coverage gap phase.

Eligibility and enrollment

Anyone entitled to Medicare Part A or enrolled in Part B is eligible for Part D. Most beneficiaries enroll during an initial enrollment period around their 65th birthday or during the annual open enrollment period each fall. Those who delay enrollment without other creditable drug coverage may pay a late-enrollment penalty added to their premium for as long as they have Part D. Low-income beneficiaries may qualify for the Extra Help program (the Part D Low-Income Subsidy), which pays part or all of premiums and cost-sharing.

Costs and financing

Part D is financed by general revenues, beneficiary premiums, and state payments for dual-eligible beneficiaries. Premiums cover roughly a quarter of the cost of the standard benefit, with the remainder subsidized by Medicare. Higher-income beneficiaries pay income-related monthly adjustment amounts (IRMAA) in addition to their plan premium. The program's spending has grown steadily with enrollment, which surpassed 50 million beneficiaries in the mid-2020s.

Negotiation debate

As originally enacted, the MMA contained a "noninterference clause" prohibiting the Secretary of Health and Human Services from interfering with price negotiations between drug manufacturers and plan sponsors. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 authorized Medicare, for the first time, to negotiate prices for a limited number of high-cost drugs covered under Parts D and B, with the first negotiated prices taking effect in 2026.
