Anabaptism

Anabaptism (from Greek ana-, "again," and baptisma, "baptism" — the "rebaptizers") is a Christian movement that arose in the Radical Reformation of the 16th century, teaching that baptism is valid only when the candidate professes faith in Christ — that is, believer's baptism — and that infant baptism is therefore unscriptural. Beginning with the Swiss Brethren around Conrad Grebel and Felix Manz in Zürich in January 1525, Anabaptists advocated a voluntary, disciplined church separated from state control, and most groups also taught nonresistance (pacifism), refusal of oaths, and communal discipline. Persecuted by Catholic and Protestant authorities alike, Anabaptist survivors gave rise to today's Mennonites, Amish, and Hutterites.

Origins

The movement began when former associates of Ulrich Zwingli, frustrated that Zürich's council would not abolish the Mass and infant baptism, baptized one another as adults on 21 January 1525 at the house of Felix Manz's mother. The movement spread rapidly through Switzerland, south Germany, Austria, and the Netherlands, drawing support from peasants, artisans, and some clergy. The Schleitheim Confession of 1527, drafted under Michael Sattler, articulated the mainstream's positions: believer's baptism, the ban (church discipline), separation from "the world," refusal of oaths and the sword.

Persecution and Münster

Both Catholic and Protestant states made rebaptism a capital crime (the imperial recess of Speyer, 1529, mandated death). Thousands were executed by drowning — mockingly called the "third baptism" — burning, or the sword; Felix Manz was drowned in the Limmat in 1527, and Michael Sattler was burned the same year. Their stories were later gathered in the Dutch Martyrs Mirror (1660). The movement's reputation suffered badly from the violent apocalyptic regime of Anabaptist prophets at Münster (1534–35), which seized the city, practised polygamy, and was crushed by siege — an episode peaceful Anabaptists repudiated.

Survival and modern Anabaptists

Under the former priest Menno Simons, Dutch and north German Anabaptists reorganized into the peaceable congregations that took his name — Mennonites. Jakob Hutter's followers in Moravia formed communal colonies (Hutterites); in 1693 Jakob Ammann's stricter Swiss followers split off as the Amish. Migrations carried these groups to Russia, and then to North and South America, where today Mennonites number over two million worldwide (with large churches in Ethiopia, India, and Congo), alongside some 360,000 Amish and about 50,000 Hutterites. Anabaptist ideas — especially believer's baptism, congregational polity, and separation of church and state — influenced English Baptists and broader free-church traditions.
