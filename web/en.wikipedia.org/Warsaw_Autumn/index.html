The Warsaw Autumn is Poland's leading festival of contemporary music.

The Warsaw Autumn (Polish: Warszawska Jesień) is an annual international festival of contemporary music held in Warsaw, Poland. Founded in 1956 by composers Tadeusz Baird and Kazimierz Serocki under the auspices of the Polish Composers' Union, it became one of the most important contemporary music festivals in the world and, during the Cold War, a rare point of contact between Eastern and Western musical avant-gardes.

History

The first festival took place in October 1956, in the climate of political thaw following Stalin's death and the Polish October. Its stated aim was to present new Polish music alongside international developments that had been largely inaccessible in Poland. From the start, works by Western modernists such as Pierre Boulez, Karlheinz Stockhausen, Luigi Nono and John Cage were performed in Warsaw — repertoire rarely heard elsewhere in the Eastern Bloc — while Polish composers including Witold Lutosławski, Krzysztof Penderecki and Henryk Górecki reached international audiences.

The festival helped define the so-called Polish School of composition and the technique of sonorism, exemplified by Penderecki's Threnody to the Victims of Hiroshima (1960), premiered in that period of the festival's activity. Except for two years in the early 1980s under martial law, the festival has been held annually, typically over about a week in the second half of September.

Character

Programming combines orchestral, chamber, electronic and experimental music, with dozens of premieres each year. Performances take place at venues such as the Warsaw Philharmonic and the Witold Lutosławski Concert Studio of Polish Radio. The festival remains a central institution of Polish musical life and a significant venue for premieres of new works from around the world.
