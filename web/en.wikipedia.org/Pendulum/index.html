A pendulum is a weight suspended from a pivot so that it can swing freely under gravity. When displaced from its rest position it oscillates back and forth with a remarkably regular period, a property that made it the basis of the world's most accurate timekeepers for three centuries and a key instrument in the development of classical mechanics.

Anatomy of a simple pendulum

Period of oscillation

For small amplitudes, the motion is approximately simple harmonic, with period T ≈ 2π√(L/g), where L is the length from pivot to the centre of mass and g is the acceleration due to gravity. Remarkably, the period is (to first approximation) independent of the mass of the bob and of the swing amplitude — the property of isochronism studied by Galileo around 1602, according to legend after watching a lamp swing in Pisa Cathedral. A "seconds pendulum", with a half-period of one second, is about 0.994 m long at sea level.

History and uses

In 1656 Christiaan Huygens built the first pendulum clock and derived the theory of the cycloidal pendulum; pendulum regulators remained the standard for precision timekeeping until the quartz clock of the 1930s. Because the period depends on local gravity, pendulums were used to measure variations in g and hence the shape of the Earth. In 1851 Léon Foucault suspended a 28 kg bob on a 67 m wire in the Panthéon in Paris, and the slow rotation of its swing plane gave the first direct, tabletop demonstration that the Earth rotates. Related devices include the torsional pendulum, the ballistic pendulum, and the inverted pendulums used in seismology.
