Louis Braille

Louis Braille (4 January 1809 – 6 January 1852) was a French educator and inventor who created the braille system of reading and writing for blind and visually impaired people, still used worldwide today. Blinded in childhood by an accident in his father's workshop, he developed his raised-dot code as a teenager, adapting Charles Barbier's military "night writing".

Life

Born in Coupvray near Paris, Braille injured one eye with an awl at age three; infection spread to the other eye, leaving him blind. A scholarship took him to the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in Paris, where he excelled as a student and later taught. In 1824, at fifteen, he created the first version of his system: characters formed from six raised dots in a 3×2 cell, capable of representing letters, numbers, punctuation, and later musical notation. He published the system in 1829 and a revised version in 1837.

Legacy

Braille's system was resisted by sighted administrators during his lifetime and adopted at the institute only after his death from tuberculosis in 1852. It spread across the world and was adapted to many languages. In 1952 his remains were transferred to the Panthéon in Paris in recognition of his achievement. His birthplace in Coupvray is preserved as a museum.
