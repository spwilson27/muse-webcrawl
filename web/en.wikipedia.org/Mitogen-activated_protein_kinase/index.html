Mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) are protein kinases that transfer phosphate groups to serine and threonine residues on target proteins, and that are activated when an extracellular stimulus — a mitogen, growth factor, cytokine, or stress — triggers a signalling cascade. MAPK pathways are among the most ancient and most widely used signal transduction mechanisms in eukaryotes, regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, motility, stress responses, and programmed cell death.

The three-tier cascade

MAPKs sit at the bottom of a three-tier phosphorylation relay. A MAPK kinase kinase (MAPKKK, e.g. Raf) activates a MAPK kinase (MAPKK, e.g. MEK), which in turn activates the MAPK by phosphorylating threonine and tyrosine residues within a conserved Thr-X-Tyr motif in its activation loop. Only in this dual-phosphorylated state is the MAPK fully active. Scaffold proteins organise the tiers and insulate one pathway from another.

Major families

In mammals the classical MAPKs include the extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERK1/2), activated chiefly by growth factors via the Ras–Raf–MEK pathway and promoting proliferation; the c-Jun N-terminal kinases (JNKs), activated by stress and cytokines; and the p38 kinases, involved in inflammatory and stress responses. Atypical MAPKs include ERK3/4, ERK7/8, and NLK.

Clinical significance

Dysregulation of MAPK signalling is a hallmark of many cancers: activating mutations in BRAF, for example, drive a large fraction of melanomas and constitutively switch on the ERK pathway. Drugs targeting the pathway — including BRAF inhibitors such as vemurafenib and MEK inhibitors such as trametinib — are established cancer therapies, though resistance commonly develops.
