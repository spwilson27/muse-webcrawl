Paleolithic Europe

Paleolithic Europe covers the Old Stone Age occupation of the European continent, from the first hominin presence over one million years ago (and possibly more than 1.4 million years ago at sites in Spain) through the end of the last Ice Age around 10,000 years ago. The period encompasses the lives of several hominin species, including Homo antecessor, Homo heidelbergensis, Neanderthals, Denisovans (at the eastern fringes), and finally anatomically modern humans from around 45,000 years ago.

Lower Paleolithic

The earliest evidence of occupation comes from sites such as Atapuerca (Sima del Elefante and Gran Dolina) in Spain, Pirro Nord in Italy, and Happisburgh in England. Early populations made simple pebble tools and later Acheulean handaxes. Occupation was intermittent, concentrated in southern refugia during glacial maxima, and hominins gradually adapted to colder climates with fire, clothing, and shelter.

Middle Paleolithic

From around 300,000 years ago, Neanderthals dominated Europe, producing Mousterian stone tool industries, hunting large game, burying at least some of their dead, and using pigments and ornaments. Their populations remained small and dispersed across a mosaic of steppe, forest, and coastline environments.

Upper Paleolithic

Modern humans spread across Europe from about 45,000 years ago, bringing Aurignacian and later Gravettian, Solutrean, and Magdalenian cultures. This period produced the celebrated cave art of Chauvet, Lascaux, and Altamira, carved figurines such as the Venus of Willendorf, bone flutes, tailored clothing, and elaborate burials. Neanderthals disappeared by about 40,000 years ago, though they left a genetic legacy of roughly 1–4% ancestry in non-African populations. After the Last Glacial Maximum (~20,000 years ago), humans recolonised northern Europe, and the Mesolithic transition followed the warming climate into the Holocene.
