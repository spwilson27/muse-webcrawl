Stereopsis

Stereopsis (from Ancient Greek στερεός stereos "solid", and ὄψις opsis "appearance, sight") is the perceived visual perception of depth and three-dimensional structure obtained on the basis of visual information deriving from two eyes by individuals with normally developed binocular vision. Because the eyes are horizontally separated by about 50–75 mm (interpupillary distance), each retina receives a slightly different image; this binocular disparity is processed in the visual cortex to yield depth.

Mechanism

Points falling on corresponding retinal locations define the horopter; points in front of or behind it produce crossed or uncrossed disparities. Neurons in area V1 tuned to specific disparities were demonstrated by Béla Julesz's random-dot stereograms, which prove disparity alone — without monocular cues — suffices for depth perception.

Clinical aspects

Stereoacuity is measured in arcseconds; fine stereopsis reaches thresholds near 20 arcsec. Conditions such as strabismus and amblyopia can cause stereoblindness; roughly 5–10% of people lack usable stereopsis but navigate using monocular cues like motion parallax and occlusion.

Applications

Stereopsis underlies stereoscopy, 3D film, virtual reality headsets, and stereo photogrammetry. In machine vision, stereo cameras estimate depth from disparity maps, mimicking the biological system.
