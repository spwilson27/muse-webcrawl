Minced oath

TypeEuphemistic expletive
ExamplesGosh, darn, heck, shoot, jeepers

A minced oath is a euphemistic expression formed by altering or clipping a profane or blasphemous word so that speakers can convey the force of swearing without uttering the taboo term itself. Examples in English include gosh and golly for "God," darn and dang for "damn," heck for "hell," jeepers and gee for "Jesus," and fudge, shoot, and sugar for stronger obscenities.

The term "minced oath" was coined in the early twentieth century, though the practice is centuries older: by the 1590s "sblood" (God's blood) and "'swounds" (God's wounds) were common in Elizabethan speech and drama. Minced oaths flourished under religious and social prohibitions on blasphemy, and later under broadcasting codes that restricted profanity on radio and television.

Formation

Minced oaths are typically created by changing the first or last sounds of the taboo word, shortening it, or substituting a phonetically similar harmless word. Some become so domesticated that their profane origins are forgotten: "bloody," "blimey" (from "God blind me"), and "crikey" began as minced or oath-derived expressions. Similar devices exist in many languages, such as French mince! and Spanish euphemistic interjections.
