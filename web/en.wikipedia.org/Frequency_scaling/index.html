Frequency Scaling

In computer architecture, frequency scaling (also called CPU throttling when reduced) is the technique of adjusting the clock frequency of a processor in order to change its performance and power consumption. Raising the frequency allows a processor to complete more operations per second, while lowering it conserves energy and reduces heat output.

Dynamic voltage and frequency scaling

Frequency scaling is usually combined with voltage scaling in a technique known as dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS). Because dynamic power consumption rises roughly with the square of the supply voltage, reducing frequency together with voltage yields substantial energy savings. A related technique, dynamic frequency scaling, adjusts frequency in response to workload: operating systems use governors that raise frequency under load and lower it when the system is idle.

Uses and effects

Frequency scaling is central to power management in laptops, mobile phones, and data centres, where it extends battery life and limits cooling requirements. It is also used in overclocking, where enthusiasts run processors above their rated frequency, and in thermal throttling, where firmware reduces frequency to prevent overheating. Historically, Dennard scaling allowed frequency to rise with each process generation, but since the mid-2000s power limits have capped clock rates, shifting performance gains toward multi-core designs.
