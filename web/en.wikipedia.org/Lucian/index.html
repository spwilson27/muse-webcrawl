Lucian

Lucian of Samosata
Bornc. 125 AD, Samosata, Roman Syria (now Samsat, Turkey)
DiedAfter 180 AD
OccupationSatirist, rhetorician
LanguageAttic Greek

Lucian of Samosata (c. 125 – after 180) was a Hellenized Syrian satirist, rhetorician and pamphleteer who is best known for his characteristic tongue-in-cheek style, with which he frequently ridiculed superstition, religious practices, and paranormal beliefs. Although his native language was probably Syriac, he wrote exclusively in Ancient Greek.

Life

Lucian was born in Samosata in the province of Syria. According to his own account in The Dream, he was apprenticed to a sculptor uncle before turning to literature and rhetoric. He travelled widely in the Roman world, practising as a travelling lecturer (sophist) in Greece, Italy and Gaul, before settling for a time in Athens. In old age he accepted a post in the imperial administration in Egypt.

Works

Some 80 works attributed to Lucian survive. His writings include satirical dialogues mocking the gods, philosophers and historians; the Dialogues of the Dead; Dialogues of the Gods; and prose romances such as A True Story (Vera Historia), a fantastic voyage to the Moon and beyond that is often considered a precursor of science fiction. In How to Write History he laid down principles of truthful historiography, and in Alexander the False Prophet he exposed a fraudulent oracle.

Legacy

Lucian's wit influenced writers from Erasmus and Rabelais to Jonathan Swift and Voltaire. His dialogues of the dead inspired a long literary tradition, and A True Story has been called the earliest surviving work of science fiction.
