Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States regarding campaign finance and corporate political speech. The Court held that the First Amendment prohibits the government from restricting independent political expenditures by corporations, labour unions and other non-profit organisations.

The case arose when Citizens United, a conservative non-profit organisation, sought to air and advertise Hillary: The Movie, a documentary critical of Senator Hillary Clinton, during the 2008 Democratic primaries. Provisions of the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act (McCain–Feingold) barred corporate-funded "electioneering communications" within a period before elections.

Holding and reasoning

In a 5–4 decision authored by Justice Anthony Kennedy, the Court held that political speech is "indispensable to decisionmaking in a democracy" and does not lose First Amendment protection because its speaker is a corporation. The decision overruled Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce (1990) and part of McConnell v. FEC (2003). The Court upheld disclaimer and disclosure requirements. Justice John Paul Stevens's dissent warned of the threat of corporate domination of elections.

Impact

Together with SpeechNow.org v. FEC, the ruling enabled the creation of "super PACs" — committees that may raise and spend unlimited sums independently of candidates — and sharply increased outside spending in United States elections. The decision has remained politically controversial, drawing sustained criticism and proposals for a constitutional amendment to overturn it.
