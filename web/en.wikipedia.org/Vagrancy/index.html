Vagrancy

Vagrancy is the condition of homelessness without regular employment or income, in which a person wanders from place to place. Historically, vagrancy was treated as a criminal offence in many legal systems, and vagrancy laws punished people for being poor, idle or without a fixed abode.

History

In England, statutes from the 14th century onward — beginning with the Ordinance of Labourers of 1349, enacted after the Black Death created labour shortages — criminalised wandering and begging. Tudor poor laws distinguished the "deserving" poor from "sturdy beggars", who could be whipped, branded or enslaved. The Vagrancy Act 1824 consolidated these offences and remained partly in force in England and Wales until its repeal was completed in 2022.

United States

American colonies copied English vagrancy laws, and after the Civil War Southern states used them in the Black Codes to coerce freedpeople into labour contracts. In Papachristou v. City of Jacksonville (1972) the Supreme Court struck down a typical vagrancy ordinance as void for vagueness, and later decisions limited laws punishing sleeping in public where no shelter exists.

Modern debates

Contemporary policy debates surround anti-camping ordinances, sit-lie bans and encampment clearances, balancing the rights of homeless people against public-space concerns. Many jurisdictions have shifted from criminalisation toward housing-first and outreach approaches, though punitive measures remain widespread.
