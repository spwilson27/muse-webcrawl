The Joint Strike Fighter program (JSF) was a United States Department of Defense development and acquisition program to replace a wide range of aging fighter, strike and ground-attack aircraft for the United States and its allies with a single family of stealth multirole fighters. It culminated in the selection of the Lockheed Martin F-35 Lightning II in 2001 and became the largest military procurement program in history.

Origins

The program emerged from the merger in 1993–94 of the DARPA/Navy Common Affordable Lightweight Fighter project (which explored a lift-fan STOVL design) and the Joint Advanced Strike Technology (JAST) effort, set up amid post-Cold War budget cuts to replace the F-16, A-10, F/A-18 and AV-8B Harrier with common variants: a conventional take-off and landing version (CTOL) for the Air Force, a short take-off and vertical landing version (STOVL) for the Marine Corps and Royal Navy, and a carrier version (CV) for the Navy. The United Kingdom became the first and only Tier 1 partner, with Italy, the Netherlands, Australia, Canada, Denmark, Norway and Turkey as partners.

Competition

In 1996 Boeing and Lockheed Martin were chosen to build competing concept demonstrators, the X-32 and X-35. Boeing's X-32 used a direct-lift system similar to the Harrier; Lockheed's X-35 used a shaft-driven lift fan developed from the CALF work. After flight testing in 2000–01 — including the X-35B's celebrated transition to supersonic flight and vertical landing in a single sortie — the Pentagon announced on 26 October 2001 that the X-35 had won, becoming the F-35, with Lockheed Martin as prime contractor, Northrop Grumman and BAE Systems as principal partners, and Pratt & Whitney's F135 engine as the powerplant.

Development and legacy

Development (System Development and Demonstration) began in 2001 with first flight of the F-35A in December 2006. The program suffered substantial cost growth, delays and a 2010 Nunn–McCurdy breach and restructuring, drawing sustained criticism over concurrency, software complexity and unit cost, even as production numbers grew to thousands of aircraft for the U.S. and export customers. The F-35 entered U.S. service from 2015 (Marines) onward and has been widely exported, making the JSF competition's outcome one of the most consequential in aviation history.
