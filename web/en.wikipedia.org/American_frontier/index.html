American frontier

The American frontier, also known as the Old West or the Wild West, is the geography, history, folklore, and culture of the westward expansion of European-American settlement in North America, conventionally dated from the early seventeenth century to the early twentieth. The U.S. Census Bureau's declaration in 1890 that a continuous frontier line could no longer be drawn is often taken as marking its symbolic close.

Expansion

The frontier moved west in stages: across the Appalachians after independence, through the Louisiana Purchase of 1803, into Texas and the Oregon Country, and to California, annexed after the Mexican–American War of 1846–48. The Homestead Act of 1862 offered 160 acres to settlers who improved the land, while the transcontinental railroad (1869) and later rail lines bound the West to eastern markets. Gold rushes, cattle drives, wagon trails such as the Oregon Trail, and army campaigns against Native American nations punctuated the process; the era's wars, treaties, and forced removals displaced Indigenous peoples from most of their ancestral lands by the 1890s.

The frontier thesis

In 1893 the historian Frederick Jackson Turner delivered "The Significance of the Frontier in American History," arguing that the experience of the frontier — the availability of free land and the repeated rebirth of society at the edge of settlement — had shaped American democracy, individualism, and national character. Turner's thesis dominated American historiography for decades and has since been extensively revised and criticised by "New Western" historians, who emphasise conquest, environment, gender, race, and the West as a distinctive region rather than a process.

In popular culture

The frontier generated an enduring mythology: cowboys, outlaws, lawmen, and gunfighters populated dime novels, Buffalo Bill's Wild West show, and, in the twentieth century, the Western film and television genres, from Stagecoach and the films of John Wayne to revisionist works such as Unforgiven.
