Fred Hoyle

Sir Fred Hoyle FRS (24 June 1915 – 20 August 2001) was an English astronomer who formulated the theory of stellar nucleosynthesis and was one of the authors of the famous B²FH paper. He is also remembered as the foremost defender of the steady-state model of the universe and as the person who coined the term "Big Bang" — which he used, he said, descriptively rather than derisively — during a 1949 BBC radio broadcast.

Scientific work

Hoyle studied at Emmanuel College, Cambridge, and worked on radar during the Second World War. In 1946 he published the first theoretical account of how elements heavier than helium are forged in stars. His most celebrated prediction came in 1953, when he argued that the abundance of carbon in the universe required a previously unknown excited state (resonance) of the carbon-12 nucleus at about 7.7 MeV; experimentalists soon confirmed the "Hoyle state." The 1957 B²FH paper (with Margaret and Geoffrey Burbidge and William Fowler) laid out stellar nucleosynthesis comprehensively; Fowler received the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physics for related work, and Hoyle's omission is often cited as a notable Nobel oversight.

Steady state and later views

With Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold, Hoyle developed the steady-state theory in 1948, which he continued to defend in modified form (with Jayant Narlikar and Geoffrey Burbidge) long after most cosmologists accepted the Big Bang. He was Plumian Professor at Cambridge and founder of the Institute of Theoretical Astronomy. Hoyle also wrote popular science books and science fiction, including the novel The Black Cloud (1957), and held controversial later views on panspermia and evolution.
