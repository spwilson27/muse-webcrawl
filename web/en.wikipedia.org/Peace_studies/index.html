Peace studies


Peace studies examines the causes of violence and the conditions for peace.

Peace studies is an interdisciplinary academic field that studies the causes of conflict and violence and the conditions, processes, and institutions that make sustainable peace possible. Drawing on political science, sociology, psychology, economics, history, and international relations, it considers both the absence of war (negative peace) and the presence of just, equitable social arrangements (positive peace).

History

The field took institutional shape after the Second World War, amid concern about nuclear weapons and the failure of earlier peace movements. Journals such as the Journal of Peace Research, founded in 1964 under the editorship of Johan Galtung, and institutes such as the Peace Research Institute Oslo helped define its research agenda. University programmes expanded from the 1960s onward, with Bradford University's Department of Peace Studies (founded 1973) among the best known.

Key concepts

Galtung's distinction between direct, structural, and cultural violence is foundational: structural violence refers to harm built into social and economic arrangements, while cultural violence covers the beliefs and symbols that legitimise the other two. Other core topics include conflict resolution and transformation, nonviolence, disarmament, peacebuilding after civil wars, and the role of international institutions.

Criticism

Critics have argued that peace studies can be normative rather than analytical, and that its central concept — peace — is contested. Defenders reply that the field combines empirical research on conflict dynamics with an explicit, transparent commitment to reducing violence.
