Conquistador

Conquistadors (Spanish: "conquerors") were the explorer-soldiers of the Spanish and Portuguese Empires of the 15th through 17th centuries who colonised much of the world for Spain and Portugal. During the Age of Discovery they sailed beyond Europe to the Americas, Oceania, Africa and Asia, conquering territory and opening trade routes.

Background

The conquistadors emerged from a Spain shaped by the centuries-long Reconquista, completed in 1492 with the fall of Granada — the same year Columbus reached the Americas. Expeditions were usually privately financed ventures licensed by the Crown, which claimed a royal fifth (the quinto real) of treasure. Generous rewards, land and encomienda grants attracted younger sons and minor nobility (hidalgos) with limited prospects at home.

Major conquests

Hernán Cortés conquered the Aztec Empire (1519–1521) with a few hundred Spaniards aided by large numbers of Indigenous allies, notably the Tlaxcalans, and by European diseases that devastated native populations. Francisco Pizarro conquered the Inca Empire (1532–1533), capturing the emperor Atahualpa at Cajamarca. Other expeditions included Pedro de Alvarado in Guatemala, Hernando de Soto in the southeastern North American interior, Francisco Vásquez de Coronado in the southwest, and Pedro de Valdivia in Chile.

Technology and tactics

Steel weapons and armour, horses, war dogs, firearms and ships gave the Spanish tactical advantages, but historians stress the decisive roles of Indigenous alliances, diplomacy, interpreters such as La Malinche, and above all epidemic disease — smallpox and other Old World infections killed a large share of the Americas' population.

Controversy and legacy

The conquests brought catastrophic demographic collapse, forced labour under the encomienda system, and cultural destruction, criticised even at the time by figures such as Bartolomé de las Casas. Their legacy — linguistic, religious, demographic and political — continues to shape Latin America and remains intensely debated.
