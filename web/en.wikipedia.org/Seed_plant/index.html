Seed plant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Flowering plants, the angiosperms, are the largest group of seed plants.

A seed plant or spermatophyte is a plant that produces seeds, hence the alternative name seed-bearing plant. Seed plants form a clade within the vascular plants and comprise the living gymnosperms and angiosperms (flowering plants), together with extinct groups such as the seed ferns. The seed — an embryo enclosed in a protective coat with a food supply — frees reproduction from dependence on external water for fertilization and allows the plant to disperse and survive dormancy.

Evolution

The earliest seed plants appeared in the Late Devonian period, around 360–380 million years ago, with fossil seed-like structures such as Elkinsia. Seed plants diversified in the Carboniferous, when seed ferns, cordaites, and early conifers became prominent, and they came to dominate terrestrial flora in the Mesozoic. Angiosperms radiated explosively in the Cretaceous and now number roughly 300,000 species, dwarfing the roughly 1,000 living gymnosperms.

Groups

  • Angiosperms — flowering plants whose seeds are enclosed in an ovary (fruit); includes monocots and dicots.
  • Gymnosperms — plants with "naked" seeds, typically on cones: conifers, cycads, ginkgo, and gnetophytes.
  • Extinct groups — seed ferns (pteridosperms), Bennettitales, and others known only from fossils.

Life cycle

Seed plants are heterosporous, producing microspores (pollen) and megaspores. Pollination brings pollen to the ovule without the need for free water, and fertilization produces the embryo within the seed. This independence from surface water, combined with efficient vascular tissue and often woody growth, underlies the ecological dominance of seed plants on land.
