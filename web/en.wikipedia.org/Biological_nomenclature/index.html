Biological nomenclature

Biological nomenclature is the system of scientific naming of organisms. Governed by international codes, it gives each taxon a unique, stable Latin or latinized name so scientists worldwide can communicate unambiguously, regardless of common names.

Binomial nomenclature

Formalized by Carl Linnaeus in the 18th century, species names consist of two parts: a genus name (capitalized) and a specific epithet (lowercase), both italicized — e.g. Homo sapiens. Higher taxa have single names, with standardized endings indicating rank in many groups.

Codes

Separate codes govern animals (ICZN), algae, fungi and plants (ICNafp), bacteria (ICNP), viruses (ICTV) and cultivated plants. Common principles include priority of first valid publication, typification (names anchored to type specimens), and rules for synonyms and homonyms.

Formation and changes

Names may commemorate people, places or traits, and are treated as Latin. Names change when taxonomic revision moves a species to another genus or splits/lumps taxa; the codes regulate author citation and parentheses signalling changed combinations.
