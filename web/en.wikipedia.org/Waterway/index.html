A waterway is any navigable body of water. Broadly, a body of water that is not navigable is not a waterway in this sense; the term includes rivers, lakes, seas, oceans, and canals that vessels can traverse. The distinction between a sea and a waterway subject to inland navigation regulations can be blurred in estuaries and harbours.

Types

Natural waterways include navigable rivers (such as the Rhine, Mississippi, and Yangtze) and straits connecting larger bodies of water. Artificial waterways are canals, built for navigation, irrigation, or drainage; famous examples include China's Grand Canal, the Suez Canal, the Panama Canal, and the canal networks of Britain and the Netherlands. "Improved" waterways combine both: rivers deepened by dredging and regulated by locks and dams, like the St. Lawrence Seaway and the U.S. Intracoastal Waterway, a sheltered channel along the Atlantic and Gulf coasts.

Importance in transport

Waterborne transport is the most energy-efficient way to move bulk goods: a single barge can carry the load of dozens of trucks or rail wagons. Inland waterways remain vital for coal, grain, petroleum, aggregates, and containers; the Rhine–Main–Danube system links the North Sea to the Black Sea, and China's waterways carry enormous volumes of domestic freight. Waterways also serve passenger ferries, tour boats, and recreational boating.

Management and law

Navigability has legal significance: in the United States, "navigable waters" fall under federal jurisdiction and public-trust doctrines. International waterways such as the Suez and Panama canals and certain straits are governed by treaties guaranteeing passage. Waterway authorities maintain channels through dredging, locks, weirs, and navigation aids, balancing transport against flood control, water supply, and ecological protection.
