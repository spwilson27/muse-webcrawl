Kalinga (historical region)

Kalinga (Odia: କଳିଙ୍ଗ) is a historical region of India, a coastal territory on the eastern seaboard roughly corresponding to modern-day Odisha, together with parts of northern Andhra Pradesh and Chhattisgarh. Bounded by the Ganges delta in the north and the Godavari in the south, Kalinga was famed in antiquity for its maritime trade, its elephants and its formidable armies.

History

Kalinga appears in early Indian literature, including the Mahābhārata, as a powerful kingdom whose sailors traded across the Bay of Bengal with Southeast Asia; the annual Bali Jatra festival in Odisha still commemorates these voyages. In the 3rd century BCE the region was ruled by the Nanda Empire and then became independent. Around 261 BCE the Mauryan emperor Ashoka invaded Kalinga in the Kalinga War, one of the bloodiest campaigns of ancient India. According to Ashoka's own edicts, 100,000 people were slain and 150,000 deported. The devastation is traditionally said to have filled Ashoka with remorse and turned him towards Buddhism and a policy of dhamma.

After the Mauryan decline, Kalinga regained prominence under Kharavela of the Mahameghavahana dynasty in the 1st century BCE, and in later centuries its ports sustained trade with Sri Lanka, Burma and Southeast Asia. The Eastern Ganga dynasty, which ruled from the 11th to the 15th century, built the great temples of Puri and Konark.

Legacy

The name survives in the Philippines' Kalinga Province and in Indonesian and Malay references to South Asian traders (Keling). The historical territory's core forms the modern Indian state of Odisha, whose people commemorate the maritime heritage of ancient Kalinga.
