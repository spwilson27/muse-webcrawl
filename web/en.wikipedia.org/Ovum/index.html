An ovum (Latin for "egg"; plural ova) is the female reproductive cell, or gamete, in most anisogamous organisms — organisms that reproduce sexually with a larger, non-motile female gamete and a smaller, motile male gamete (sperm). The ovum contains half the genetic material of a typical cell of the organism, together with the cytoplasm and organelles needed to support early development of the embryo after fertilisation.

Structure

The human ovum is one of the largest cells in the body, about 0.1–0.12 mm in diameter and just visible to the naked eye. It is surrounded by a protective glycoprotein layer, the zona pellucida, and by a cluster of follicular (cumulus) cells. The cytoplasm, or ooplasm, contains yolk granules, mitochondria — which are inherited exclusively from the mother — and maternal messenger RNAs and proteins that direct the first divisions of the embryo.

Development

Ova develop from primordial germ cells by the process of oogenesis in the ovaries. In humans, oogonia proliferate before birth and enter meiosis as primary oocytes, which arrest in prophase I until ovulation, in some cases decades later. At ovulation a secondary oocyte is released; meiosis is completed only if fertilisation occurs. Unlike males, who produce sperm continuously, females are born with a finite number of oocytes that declines throughout life.

In other organisms

In flowering plants, the ovum is contained within the ovule inside the ovary of the flower. In many animals the ovum is provisioned with large quantities of yolk and enclosed in shells or membranes, forming the familiar egg of birds and reptiles. Karl Ernst von Baer is credited with identifying the mammalian ovum in 1827.
