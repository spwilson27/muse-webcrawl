The HfG Ulm (Hochschule für Gestaltung Ulm, "Ulm School of Design") was a school of design founded in Ulm, West Germany, in 1953 by Inge Aicher-Scholl, Otl Aicher, and Max Bill. Though it closed in 1968, it is regarded as one of the most influential design schools of the twentieth century — the successor to the Bauhaus and a birthplace of the systematic, science-based approach to design known as the "Ulm Model".

History

The school was founded in the memory of Hans and Sophie Scholl of the White Rose resistance, with support including American funding. Its building, designed by Max Bill — the school's first rector — was inaugurated in 1955. After Bill resigned in 1957 amid disputes over the school's direction, Tomás Maldonado and others pushed the curriculum toward semiotics, ergonomics, operations research, and design methodology. Chronic funding battles with the state government of Baden-Württemberg, combined with internal political conflict in the late 1960s, led to closure in 1968.

Legacy

The school's collaboration with the manufacturer Braun — whose products, designed by HfG graduates and teachers such as Hans Gugelot together with Dieter Rams, defined postwar German industrial design — and its graphic identity work for Lufthansa under Otl Aicher became canonical. Ulm's methodology reshaped design education worldwide.
