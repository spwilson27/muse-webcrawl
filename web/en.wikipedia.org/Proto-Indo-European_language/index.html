Proto-Indo-European (PIE) is the reconstructed common ancestor of the Indo-European language family. No direct records of Proto-Indo-European exist; its proposed features have been derived through linguistic reconstruction from documented Indo-European languages. PIE is estimated to have been spoken as a single language from approximately 4500 BCE to 2500 BCE, during the Neolithic to Early Bronze Age, though estimates vary by more than a thousand years.

According to the widely held Kurgan hypothesis, the original speakers of PIE, the Proto-Indo-Europeans, were a population of the Pontic–Caspian steppe in what is now Ukraine and southern Russia who were associated with the Yamnaya culture and other related cultures during the 4th millennium BCE.

Descendant languages

The Indo-European languages include some 445 living and extinct languages, spoken by about 46% of the world's population. Major branches include Indo-Iranian, Greek, Italic (including Latin and the Romance languages), Germanic, Balto-Slavic, Celtic, Armenian, and Albanian, as well as the extinct Anatolian and Tocharian branches. Well-known reconstructed roots include *ph₂tḗr "father", *méh₂tēr "mother", and *ḱm̥tóm "hundred".

Reconstruction

There is no direct evidence of PIE, since it was never written. All PIE sounds and words are reconstructed by linguists using the comparative method from similarities among modern and ancient Indo-European languages. An asterisk (*) is used to mark reconstructed forms. Features of the reconstruction include a system of ablaut (vowel gradation), a complex verbal morphology with several aspects and moods, and a nominal system with up to eight or nine cases and three genders (masculine, feminine, and neuter).

Phonology

Reconstructed PIE phonology includes the controversial laryngeal theory, which posits a set of consonantal sounds (conventionally written h₁, h₂, h₃) that have mostly disappeared in descendant languages but left traces in vowel quality and length, and are preserved in some form in Hittite. The traditional reconstruction of the stop system — with voiceless, voiced, and voiced aspirated series — has been challenged by the glottalic theory.

Historical development of studies

The existence of a common ancestor was first proposed in the late 18th century, notably by Sir William Jones in 1786, who observed striking similarities between Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin. The 19th century saw systematic work by scholars such as Franz Bopp, Jacob Grimm, and August Schleicher, whose Stammbaum (family tree) model of language divergence became standard.
