Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus (c. 163 – 133 BC) was a Roman politician best known for his agrarian reform law entailing the transfer of land from the Roman state and wealthy landowners to poorer citizens. Against a background of mounting inequality and the decline of the smallholder class that supplied Rome's legions, his reforms met fierce opposition from the Senate. He was murdered, along with many of his supporters, by a mob led by his cousin Publius Cornelius Scipio Nasica Serapio during riots sparked by his tribunate — an episode often seen as the opening violence of the Roman Republic's final century of civil strife.

Early life and career

Tiberius was the son of the twice-consul and censor Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus and of Cornelia, daughter of Scipio Africanus, and elder brother of Gaius Gracchus. He served with distinction in the Third Punic War at Carthage and in Spain, where as quaestor in 137 BC he negotiated a treaty that saved a Roman army, only for the Senate to repudiate it — a humiliation thought to have alienated him from the senatorial aristocracy.

Tribunate and the agrarian law

Elected tribune of the plebs for 133 BC, Tiberius proposed the lex Sempronia agraria, which revived limits on how much public land (ager publicus) any one person could hold (500 iugera, with allowances for sons) and redistributed the surplus in small plots to the poor, administered by a three-man commission on which he, his brother, and his father-in-law sat. When a fellow tribune, Marcus Octavius, vetoed the bill, Tiberius had him deposed by the plebeian assembly — an unprecedented act. The law passed, and Tiberius further proposed using the bequest of King Attalus III of Pergamum to fund the new farms.

Death and legacy

When Tiberius sought re-election — contrary to custom — his opponents accused him of aiming at kingship. On election day in 133 BC, senators led by Scipio Nasica armed themselves with chair legs and clubs and stormed the Forum; Tiberius and some three hundred supporters were beaten to death, and their bodies thrown into the Tiber. His brother Gaius revived and extended the reforms a decade later. The Gracchi became enduring symbols of popular reform and of the breakdown of republican norms.
