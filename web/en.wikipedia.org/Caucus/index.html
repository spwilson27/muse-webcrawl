Caucus

A caucus is a meeting of supporters or members of a political party or movement. The term is used both for local gatherings at which voters express preferences among candidates — most famously the Iowa caucuses in United States presidential nominations — and for the organized group of legislators belonging to a party within a legislature, such as a congressional caucus.

Etymology

The origin of the word is uncertain. It appears in Boston in the 1760s, possibly derived from an Algonquian word for a counsellor or adviser, or from the Greek kaukos (drinking cup), jokingly applied to political clubs that met socially.

Presidential caucuses in the United States

In states that use caucuses rather than primary elections, party members gather at precinct meetings, publicly group themselves by candidate preference, and elect delegates to county and state conventions. The Iowa Democratic caucuses of 1972 and the victories of Jimmy Carter (1976) and Barack Obama (2008) gave the Iowa caucuses an outsized role as the first contest of the nomination season. Critics argue caucuses depress turnout and exclude shift workers and people with disabilities; after 2020 the Democratic Party moved many states toward primaries and changed its calendar.

Legislative caucuses

Within legislatures, a caucus is the body of members of one party, meeting to set strategy and elect leaders. In the United States Congress there are also hundreds of issue and identity caucuses, such as the Congressional Black Caucus and the Freedom Caucus. In Westminster-style parliaments, the term is used for a party's parliamentary membership as a whole.
