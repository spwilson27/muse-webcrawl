New town

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Milton Keynes, designated in 1967

A new town is a town that has been planned and constructed from scratch, generally on previously undeveloped land, often as part of a government policy to relieve housing shortages, decongest large cities, or stimulate regional development. The term is especially associated with the British new towns created after the Second World War under the New Towns Act 1946.

Background

The modern new-town idea grew out of Ebenezer Howard's garden city movement, embodied in Letchworth (1903) and Welwyn Garden City (1920). After 1945, Britain faced a housing crisis and the reconstruction of bombed cities; the New Towns Act empowered the government to designate areas and create development corporations with powers to acquire land and build complete communities.

British new towns

Some 32 new towns were designated in the United Kingdom in three waves, including Stevenage (the first, designated in 1946), Crawley, Basildon, Milton Keynes, and, in Scotland, East Kilbride, Cumbernauld and Livingston. Together they housed millions of people. Critics questioned their social mix, architecture, and dependence on the car, while defenders credited them with providing good housing and green space at scale.

Around the world

New towns have been built worldwide — from Brasília and Canberra as planned capitals, to new communities in post-colonial Africa, satellite cities around Asian megacities, and "new cities" in China and the Gulf states such as NEOM's The Line.
