Noether's theorem

Noether's theorem states that every differentiable symmetry of the action of a physical system with conservative forces has a corresponding conservation law. The theorem was proved by mathematician Emmy Noether in 1915 and published in 1918 for the purpose of clarifying the role of symmetries in the general theory of relativity and in classical field theory.

Statement

Roughly, if a system's Lagrangian does not change under a continuous transformation — for example, a shift in time, a translation in space, or a rotation — then there is a quantity associated with the system that does not change over time. Time-translation symmetry gives conservation of energy; spatial translation gives conservation of linear momentum; rotational symmetry gives conservation of angular momentum.

Significance

The theorem is one of the foundational results of theoretical physics, linking the symmetries of nature to its conservation laws. It underlies modern formulations of classical mechanics, quantum field theory and gauge theory, where gauge symmetries lead to conserved charges such as electric charge.

History

Noether developed the theorem in Göttingen at the invitation of David Hilbert and Felix Klein, who sought to understand energy conservation in general relativity. She in fact proved two theorems; the second addresses infinite-dimensional (gauge) symmetries.
