Linoleum

Linoleum is a floor covering made from renewable natural materials including solidified linseed oil (linoxyn), pine resin, ground cork dust, sawdust, and mineral fillers such as calcium carbonate, most commonly on a burlap or canvas backing. Pigments are often added to the materials. The word is derived from the Latin linum (flax) and oleum (oil). Linoleum, invented in the 1860s, is often confused with vinyl flooring, but is a distinct, biodegradable product.

History

Linoleum was invented by Englishman Frederick Walton, who patented it in 1863 and refined the manufacturing process in subsequent years. Production centred on Kirkcaldy in Scotland, which became the world's leading linoleum manufacturing town. The material was ubiquitous in homes, offices, ships and public buildings in the first half of the twentieth century, valued as durable, warm and easy to clean. It lost ground to vinyl after the Second World War but has enjoyed a revival due to its natural composition and sustainability.

Marmoleum and art

The leading modern brand, Forbo's Marmoleum, is supplied in sheet and click formats in marbled and plain patterns. Linoleum is also used in printmaking: "linocut" blocks are carved and printed in relief, a technique used by artists including Picasso and Matisse.
