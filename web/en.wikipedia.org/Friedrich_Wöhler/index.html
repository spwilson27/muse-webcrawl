Friedrich Wöhler (1800–1882) was a German chemist best known for his 1828 synthesis of urea from inorganic ammonium cyanate — the first synthesis of an organic compound from inorganic materials, a landmark often said to have dealt a blow to the doctrine of vitalism.

Career

Wöhler took a medical degree at Heidelberg in 1823, then worked with Berzelius in Stockholm. Teaching in Berlin and Kassel, he isolated aluminium (1827) and beryllium (1828) in metallic form. In 1836 he became professor at Göttingen, where he remained for the rest of his life, teaching generations of chemists and publishing influential textbooks.

Urea and isomerism

Attempting to make ammonium cyanate, Wöhler obtained crystals identical to urea, writing to Berzelius that he could "make urea without needing a kidney, whether of man or dog". His observation that silver cyanate and fulminic acid had the same composition yet different properties led, with Berzelius, to the concept of isomerism. With Justus von Liebig he studied the benzoyl radical (1832), foundational work for organic structural theory, as well as uric acid, alkaloids and, with students, compounds such as calcium carbide chemistry precursors.
