The Analects (Chinese: 論語, Lúnyǔ, "Selected Sayings") is a collection of sayings, dialogues, and anecdotes attributed to the Chinese philosopher Confucius (551–479 BC) and his contemporaries, compiled by his disciples and their followers. It is the most reliable surviving record of Confucius's teaching and became one of the Four Books of Confucianism.

Contents

The text consists of twenty short chapters. Its themes include ren (benevolence or humaneness), li (ritual propriety), filial piety, the ideal of the junzi (gentleman or exemplary person), the rectification of names, and government by virtue rather than by coercion: Confucius teaches that a ruler who governs by moral example will be followed like the pole star. Many passages are brief exchanges with named disciples such as Zigong, Yan Hui, and Zilu.

Influence

For over two thousand years the Analects was memorised by schoolchildren across East Asia and was a core text of the imperial civil-service examinations in China. It shaped the ethical and political culture of China, Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, and remains one of the most translated works of world literature.
