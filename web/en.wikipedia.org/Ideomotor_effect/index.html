Ideomotor effect

The ideomotor effect is a psychological phenomenon wherein a subject makes motions unconsciously, triggered by thoughts or expectations rather than deliberate volition. The term was coined by William Benjamin Carpenter in 1852, in reference to the muscular movements that can be attributed to the power of ideas. It is the accepted scientific explanation for a range of purportedly paranormal phenomena in which participants sincerely believe an external force is moving an object, when in fact their own minute, involuntary movements are responsible.

A Ouija planchette: its movement is attributed to the ideomotor effect.

Phenomena explained

The ideomotor effect underlies dowsing rods and pendulums, Ouija boards, table-turning séances, the Chevreul pendulum, and facilitated communication. It is also invoked in the operation of "Ouija-like" devices and some automatic writing. In 1853 Michael Faraday experimentally demonstrated that table-turning was caused by sitters' unconscious muscular action, using stacked indicator boards to show the hands moved before the table. Carpenter's related "ideomotor principle" — that merely imagining a movement tends to produce it in miniature — informed later psychology of suggestion and hypnosis.

Evidence and experiments

Controlled studies show that when participants are blindfolded or given false expectations, Ouija responses become garbled or reflect suggestion rather than any external knowledge. Research (e.g. Gauchou, Rensink and Fels, 2012) found that ideomotor responses during Ouija use can nonetheless tap non-conscious knowledge, yielding better-than-chance answers to questions participants could not consciously answer. The facilitated communication scandal of the 1990s — in which messages attributed to non-speaking people with severe disabilities were shown to originate with facilitators — became a cautionary example: double-blind tests showed answers matched what facilitators, not clients, had seen.

Related concepts

The ideomotor effect is distinct from reflexes and from conversion symptoms, though Carpenter linked it to the same "unconscious cerebration" he used to discuss trance states. It is related to the ideomotor theory of action in modern cognitive psychology, in which actions are represented and selected by their anticipated sensory effects — a framework quite different in tone from the idealism of Berkeley, though both centre on the causal power of ideas.
