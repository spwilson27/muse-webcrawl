A Mersenne prime is a prime number that is one less than a power of two; that is, it is a prime number of the form Mn = 2n − 1 for some integer n. The exponents n which give Mersenne primes are 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, ... They are named after Marin Mersenne, a French Minim friar who studied them in the early 17th century. If n is composite, then 2n − 1 is also composite, so n must itself be prime — but not every prime exponent yields a Mersenne prime (211 − 1 = 2047 = 23 × 89).

Perfect numbers

Mersenne primes are intimately connected with perfect numbers. Euclid proved that if 2n − 1 is prime, then 2n−1(2n − 1) is a perfect number; Euler proved that every even perfect number has this form. Thus each Mersenne prime generates an even perfect number, and it is unknown whether there are infinitely many of either — or any odd perfect number at all.

Searching for Mersenne primes

Mersenne numbers are easier to test for primality than most numbers of comparable size, thanks to the Lucas–Lehmer primality test. The search for ever-larger Mersenne primes has driven computing since the dawn of the electronic era: Édouard Lucas proved M127 prime in 1876, the largest prime known for 75 years. Since 1996 the Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search (GIMPS) has coordinated volunteers' computers worldwide; most of the largest known primes are Mersenne primes. The largest known prime overall, 2136279841 − 1, with 41,024,320 decimal digits, was found by GIMPS volunteer Luke Durant in October 2024.
