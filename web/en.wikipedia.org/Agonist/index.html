Agonist

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An agonist is a chemical that activates a receptor to produce a biological response. In pharmacology, agonists bind to receptors and mimic the action of endogenous signalling molecules such as neurotransmitters or hormones. This contrasts with antagonists, which bind to receptors but block their activation.

Types of agonists

  • Full agonists bind and activate a receptor with maximal efficacy, producing the full response of which the tissue is capable (e.g. morphine at μ-opioid receptors).
  • Partial agonists activate receptors but produce a submaximal response even at full occupancy (e.g. varenicline at nicotinic receptors, aripiprazole at dopamine receptors).
  • Inverse agonists reduce the constitutive (baseline) activity of a receptor, producing an effect opposite to an agonist.
  • Co-agonists must bind simultaneously with another molecule to activate a receptor (e.g. glutamate and glycine at NMDA receptors).
  • Irreversible agonists bind permanently, often covalently, to a receptor.
  • Superagonists produce a response greater than that of the endogenous agonist.

Mechanism

Agonist potency depends on binding affinity (how strongly it attaches to the receptor) and intrinsic efficacy (how well binding activates the receptor). These are commonly described with dose–response curves and parameters such as EC50. Drugs in many classes work as agonists, including opioid analgesics, beta-2 agonists used in asthma (e.g. salbutamol), and dopamine agonists used in Parkinson's disease.

Other uses

In anatomy, an agonist muscle (prime mover) is the muscle chiefly responsible for generating a given movement. In literature, "agonist" relates to the protagonist of a story.
