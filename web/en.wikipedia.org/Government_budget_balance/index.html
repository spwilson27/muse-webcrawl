Government budget balance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The government budget balance, also informally known as the government budget surplus or deficit, is the difference between government revenues and government spending for a given period, usually a fiscal year. A positive balance is a budget surplus; a negative balance is a budget deficit. The balance is commonly expressed as a percentage of gross domestic product (GDP).

Definitions

The primary balance excludes interest payments on government debt from spending, and so measures the current fiscal stance apart from the cost of past borrowing. The overall balance includes interest and therefore reflects the change in the government's net debt. Economists also distinguish the cyclically adjusted (structural) balance, which estimates what the balance would be if the economy were at potential output, from the actual balance, which fluctuates automatically with the business cycle because tax receipts fall and welfare spending rises in recessions — the "automatic stabilisers".

Financing and consequences

A deficit must be financed by issuing government bonds or by other borrowing, adding to the public debt; persistent large deficits can raise debt ratios and interest costs, though the sustainability of a given path depends on interest rates, growth and inflation. Surpluses may be used to repay debt or accumulate assets in sovereign wealth funds. Budget rules in many jurisdictions — such as the European Union's fiscal limits — cap the size of permitted deficits.
