Thomas Hardy (2 June 1840 – 11 January 1928) was an English novelist and poet. A Victorian realist in the tradition of George Eliot, he was influenced both in his novels and in his poetry by Romanticism, especially William Wordsworth. He was highly critical of much in Victorian society, especially on the declining status of rural people in Britain, such as those from his native South West England.

While Hardy wrote poetry throughout his life and regarded himself primarily as a poet, his first collection was not published until 1898. Initially, he gained fame as the author of novels such as Far from the Madding Crowd (1874), The Mayor of Casterbridge (1886), Tess of the d'Urbervilles (1891) and Jude the Obscure (1895). During his final years he enjoyed a vogue as a leading poet, and his poems influenced writers such as Philip Larkin and W. H. Auden.

Wessex

Most of Hardy's fiction is set in "Wessex," a partly real, partly dream-country based on the counties of south-western and south-central England. Hardy renamed actual places and counties—Dorchester becomes Casterbridge, Dorset becomes South Wessex—creating a unified fictional landscape across his novels and poems. His works frequently explore the struggle of characters against their passions and social circumstances, and his bleak view of an indifferent universe is often summarised by his phrase "the President of the Immortals" at play with human lives.

Life

Hardy was born in Higher Bockhampton, Dorset, the son of a stonemason and builder. Trained as an architect in Dorchester and London, he worked in church restoration before turning to literature. He married Emma Gifford in 1874; after her death in 1912 he wrote the celebrated sequence of elegies known as the "Poems of 1912–13." In 1914 he married Florence Dugdale. He died at Max Gate, the Dorchester house he designed for himself, in 1928; his ashes are in Westminster Abbey's Poets' Corner, while his heart is buried in Stinsford churchyard in Dorset.
