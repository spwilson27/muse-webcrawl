Gaius Gracchus

Gaius Sempronius Gracchus (154–121 BC)

Gaius Sempronius Gracchus (154–121 BC) was a Roman politician and reformer, and the younger brother of Tiberius Gracchus. As tribune of the plebs in 123 and 122 BC, he advanced a broad programme of legislation — land reform, subsidised grain, judicial reform, and citizenship extension — that deepened the political crisis of the Roman Republic. Together the Gracchi brothers are traditionally taken to mark the beginning of the Republic's century of revolution.

Background

Gaius belonged to one of Rome's most distinguished families: his father, Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus, was twice consul and censor, and his mother Cornelia was the daughter of Scipio Africanus. He served in the Numantine War in Spain and as quaestor in Sardinia. His brother Tiberius, as tribune in 133 BC, had pushed through an agrarian law redistributing public land and had been killed by a senatorial mob — the first political murder in Rome in centuries — an event that shaped Gaius's career and enmities.

Tribunate and reforms

Elected tribune for 123 BC (and re-elected for 122, contrary to custom), Gaius enacted an extensive legislative package. He revived and extended his brother's land commission, founded colonies including one at Carthage, and passed a grain law (lex frumentaria) providing subsidised grain to Rome's citizens. His judiciary law transferred membership of the extortion court from senators to equestrians, splitting the senatorial order's power, and other laws reformed tax farming in Asia, military service, and provincial administration. His most controversial proposal, to extend Roman citizenship to Rome's Latin and Italian allies, lost him popular support.

Downfall and death

After failing to win a third tribunate, and with the Senate working to repeal his measures, violence erupted in 121 BC. The Senate passed the first senatus consultum ultimum ("ultimate decree"), authorising the consul Lucius Opimius to defend the state. Gaius fled to the Aventine Hill and, as his supporters were defeated, ordered his slave to kill him. Some 3,000 of his followers were executed; Opimius built a Temple of Concord in the Forum to mark the victory. Gaius's reforms, however, largely endured, and the Gracchi became lasting symbols — praised by later reformers and blamed by conservatives — of popular politics at Rome.
