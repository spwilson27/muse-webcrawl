Saul (Hebrew: שָׁאוּל, Šāʾūl, "asked for, prayed for") was, according to the Hebrew Bible, the first king of the united Kingdom of Israel. The Books of Samuel describe how he was anointed by the prophet Samuel, led Israel in war against the Philistines and other neighbours, and fell from divine favour, to be succeeded by David.

Biblical account

Saul, a tall and handsome Benjaminite, son of Kish, was searching for his father's lost donkeys when he met Samuel, who anointed him and later presented him to the assembled tribes at Mizpah, where he was chosen by lot. After defeating the Ammonites at Jabesh-Gilead, Saul's kingship was confirmed at Gilgal.

His reign is marked by wars against the Philistines, Moabites, Ammonites, Edomites and Amalekites. His son Jonathan features prominently as a heroic warrior and David's loyal friend. According to the narrative, Saul twice forfeited divine approval: first by offering a sacrifice himself at Gilgal instead of waiting for Samuel, and then by sparing Agag, king of Amalek, and the best of the livestock he had been ordered to destroy. Samuel announced that God had rejected Saul and secretly anointed David.

Conflict with David and death

Tormented by an "evil spirit", Saul took the young David into his service as a musician and armour-bearer, but grew jealous of David's fame after the slaying of Goliath, and repeatedly tried to kill him. David fled and became an outlaw, twice sparing Saul's life. On the eve of his final battle, Saul consulted the medium of Endor, who summoned the spirit of Samuel to pronounce his doom. The next day the Philistines crushed Israel at Mount Gilboa; Saul, wounded, fell on his own sword, and Jonathan died with him. David's lament over Saul and Jonathan ("the beauty of Israel is slain upon thy high places") is among the most famous poems of the Bible.

Historical assessment

Scholars debate the historicity and dating of Saul's kingdom (traditionally placed around the late 11th century BCE). There is no direct extra-biblical attestation of Saul, and the biblical sources — written later and favouring David — are read critically. Nevertheless, many historians accept a core tradition of a Benjaminite chief or king who first united the Israelite tribes.
