Gyroscope

A device for measuring or maintaining orientation, based on the conservation of angular momentum.

A gyroscope (from Ancient Greek γῦρος gŷros, "circle" and σκοπέω skopéō, "to look") is a device used for measuring or maintaining orientation and angular velocity. It is a spinning wheel or disc in which the axis of rotation (spin axis) is free to assume any orientation by itself. When rotating, the orientation of this axis is unaffected by tilting or rotation of the mounting, according to the conservation of angular momentum.

The classic gyroscope is a rotor mounted in gimbals that allow it to keep its orientation while the housing turns around it. Gyroscopes based on other principles also exist, including optical gyroscopes (ring laser and fibre-optic gyroscopes exploiting the Sagnac effect), hemispherical resonator gyroscopes, and the microscopic MEMS gyroscopes found in smartphones, game controllers and drones.

History

Léon Foucault built and named the gyroscope in 1852 in connection with experiments on the rotation of the Earth, following earlier instruments by Johann Bohnenberger and others. Practical marine gyrocompasses were developed by Elmer Sperry and Hermann Anschütz-Kaempfe in the first decade of the 20th century, freeing navigation from dependence on the magnetic compass.

Applications

Gyroscopes are fundamental to inertial navigation systems in aircraft, spacecraft, submarines and missiles; to gyrocompasses and artificial horizons; to stabilisers for ships, cameras, satellites and the Hubble Space Telescope's pointing system; and to the control moment gyroscopes of the International Space Station. The precession of a gyroscope also explains phenomena from the stability of a bicycle under way to the slow precession of the Earth's axis.

Physics

The essential behaviour follows from the equation τ = dL/dt: an applied torque τ changes the angular momentum vector L. When a spinning wheel is subjected to a torque about an axis perpendicular to its spin axis, the spin axis does not fall in the direction of the torque but precesses, moving at right angles to both. This counterintuitive response—gyroscopic precession—together with nutation, governs the device's operation.
