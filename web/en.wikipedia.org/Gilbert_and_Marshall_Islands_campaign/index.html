The Gilbert and Marshall Islands campaign was a series of battles fought from November 1943 to February 1944 in the central Pacific theatre of World War II. It was the first major offensive of the United States' "island hopping" drive across the Central Pacific toward Japan, conducted under Admiral Chester W. Nimitz. American forces seized Japanese-held atolls in the Gilbert and Marshall Islands to gain airfields and anchorages for the subsequent advance on the Marianas.

Background

Japan had occupied the Gilberts and Marshalls since early in the war, building airfields and seaplane bases on atolls such as Tarawa, Makin, Kwajalein and Eniwetok. The U.S. plan, Operation Galvanic and its successors, aimed to crack Japan's outer defensive perimeter, bypassing strongly held bases such as Truk rather than assaulting every island.

The Gilberts: Tarawa and Makin

On 20 November 1943 the 2nd Marine Division landed on Betio islet in the Tarawa atoll while the Army's 27th Infantry Division attacked Makin (Butaritari). At Tarawa, unexpectedly low tides left landing craft stranded on the reef, forcing Marines to wade ashore under fire; in 76 hours of fighting against some 4,700 entrenched defenders, over 1,000 Marines were killed and the Japanese garrison was virtually annihilated. The bloody photographs from Tarawa shocked the American public but taught hard lessons in amphibious warfare — longer preliminary bombardments, better tidal reconnaissance and more tracked landing vehicles — applied in later operations.

The Marshalls: Kwajalein and Eniwetok

Applying those lessons, U.S. forces struck Kwajalein Atoll on 31 January 1944, securing it within days at comparatively low cost, then took Eniwetok in mid-to-late February 1944 (Operation Catchpole). Simultaneously, fast carrier task forces devastated the great Japanese base at Truk in Operation Hailstone (17–18 February 1944), neutralizing it without invasion.

Significance

The campaign breached Japan's outer Pacific perimeter, provided the U.S. Navy with forward bases, and validated the strategy of bypassing strongpoints, which were left to "wither on the vine." Losses prompted reforms in American amphibious doctrine. The captured atolls supported the next phase of the Central Pacific drive: the invasion of the Mariana Islands in June 1944.
