Veliky Novgorod

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Veliky Novgorod (Russian: Великий Новгород, "Great Newtown"), also known simply as Novgorod, is one of the oldest cities in Russia, located in the north-west of the country on the Volkhov River, just below its outflow from Lake Ilmen. First mentioned by the chronicles in 859, it was a principal city of Kievan Rus' and, from the 12th to the 15th century, the capital of the Novgorod Republic — a vast medieval state that traded furs, wax, and honey across northern Europe and maintained a famously democratic form of government centred on the veche (popular assembly). The city is often called "the birthplace of Russian democracy."

History

According to tradition, the Varangian prince Rurik was invited to rule Novgorod in 862, an event often taken as the founding moment of the Russian state; the 900th anniversary was marked in 1862 by the Millennium of Russia monument, which still stands in the Novgorod Kremlin. In 1136 the Novgorodians expelled their prince and asserted control over their own affairs, evolving into a republic in which princes were hired as military commanders while power lay with the veche and the archbishop.

Novgorod escaped the Mongol destruction that devastated other Rus' cities, and under leaders such as Alexander Nevsky it defended Russia's north-western frontier, defeating the Swedes at the Neva in 1240 and the Teutonic Knights at the Battle on the Ice in 1242. Its merchants traded with the Hanseatic League, whose kontor, the Peterhof, stood in the city.

The republic's independence ended in 1478, when Ivan III of Moscow annexed Novgorod after the Battle of Shelon (1471). In 1570 Ivan the Terrible's oprichniki sacked the city and massacred thousands of its inhabitants. The city suffered further under Swedish occupation during the Time of Troubles, and its commercial importance declined as St Petersburg grew.

Landmarks and culture

Novgorod is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Its Kremlin (Detinets) contains Saint Sophia Cathedral (1045–1050), the oldest church in Russia still in use, with bronze Magdeburg doors and frescoes. Dozens of medieval churches survive across the city, many decorated with frescoes by Theophanes the Greek and his school. Excavations have unearthed more than a thousand birch bark letters — everyday documents scratched on bark — which give a unique picture of medieval literacy and daily life. The nearby Vitoslavlitsy museum preserves wooden architecture from across the region.

Modern city

Severely damaged during the Second World War, when it was occupied from 1941 to 1944, Novgorod was painstakingly restored. In 1999 the city officially adopted the name Veliky Novgorod ("Novgorod the Great") to distinguish it from Nizhny Novgorod. Today it is the administrative centre of Novgorod Oblast, with a population of around 220,000, and an economy based on chemicals, manufacturing, and tourism.
