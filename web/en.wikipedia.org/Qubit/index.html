Qubit

In quantum computing, a qubit (pronounced /ˈkjuːbɪt/) or quantum bit is a basic unit of quantum information — the quantum version of the classic binary bit physically realized with a two-state device. A qubit is a two-state (or two-level) quantum-mechanical system, one of the simplest quantum systems displaying the peculiarity of quantum mechanics.

Superposition and measurement

Unlike a classical bit, which is either 0 or 1, a qubit can be in a coherent superposition of both states, described by a state vector |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ with complex amplitudes satisfying |α|² + |β|² = 1. Measurement in the computational basis yields 0 with probability |α|² and 1 with probability |β|², collapsing the state. Two or more qubits can also be entangled, so that their joint state cannot be factored into individual states — a resource central to quantum algorithms.

Physical implementations

Qubits have been realized with superconducting circuits (transmons used by IBM and Google), trapped ions, photons, quantum dots, neutral atoms, and defects such as nitrogen-vacancy centres in diamond. Key engineering challenges are decoherence — the loss of quantum information to the environment — and gate errors, addressed by quantum error correction, in which many physical qubits encode one logical qubit.

History

The term "qubit" was coined by Benjamin Schumacher in a 1995 paper, building on earlier work on quantum information theory. Since then, qubit counts and quality have driven progress in quantum computing, from early few-qubit demonstrations to processors with hundreds or thousands of physical qubits.
