Aliasing

In signal processing and related disciplines, aliasing is the effect that causes different signals to become indistinguishable (or aliases of one another) when sampled. It also often refers to the distortion or artifact that results when a signal reconstructed from samples is different from the original continuous signal.

Aliasing can occur in signals sampled in time, for instance digital audio or the temporal aliasing in videos, and in spatially sampled signals, such as images. A familiar example of spatial aliasing is the moiré pattern observed when fine stripes are rendered at insufficient resolution. In video and film, the wagon-wheel effect—in which spoked wheels appear to rotate backwards—is a form of temporal aliasing.

Description

When a continuous signal is sampled at discrete intervals, frequencies above half the sampling rate (the Nyquist frequency) cannot be distinguished from lower frequencies. The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem states that a signal can be perfectly reconstructed from its samples if the sampling rate exceeds twice the highest frequency present in the signal.

Anti-aliasing

Aliasing is generally avoided by applying low-pass filters, called anti-aliasing filters, to the input signal before sampling, removing frequency components above the Nyquist limit. In computer graphics, techniques such as supersampling and multisample anti-aliasing reduce jagged edges, or “jaggies,” on rendered images.
