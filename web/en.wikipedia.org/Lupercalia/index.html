Lupercalia was an ancient Roman pastoral festival of purification and fertility, observed annually on 15 February. Among the most archaic festivals in the Roman calendar, it centred on the Lupercal, the cave at the foot of the Palatine Hill where, according to legend, the she-wolf had suckled Romulus and Remus. The rite combined the sacrifice of goats and a dog, the ritual smearing of young priests' foreheads with blood and milk, and a near-naked run around the Palatine, in which the runners struck women with goatskin thongs to promote fertility and easy childbirth.

Origins and name

The festival's origins were obscure already to Roman antiquarians. The name is usually connected with lupus ("wolf") and the god Lupercus, identified with Faunus (the Roman counterpart of Pan); Ovid and Plutarch connected the rites with the Arcadian Lykaia of Pan Lykaeus, brought to Italy, in legend, by Evander. February itself took its name from the februa, instruments of purification — including the goatskin thongs used in the festival — that marked the old Roman year's end.

Rites

The ceremony began at the Lupercal, where the Luperci — priests organised in colleges, the Quinctiales and Fabiani, joined in 45 BC by the Juliani in honour of Julius Caesar — sacrificed goats and a dog. Two youths of noble birth had their foreheads touched with the bloody sacrificial knife and then wiped with wool dipped in milk, at which they were required to laugh. After a feast, the priests cut the goatskins into strips (februa) and, wearing only goatskin loincloths, ran a circuit around the Palatine — later through the Forum and city streets — striking those they met. Women deliberately placed themselves in the way, believing the blows promoted conception and eased delivery. Shakespeare dramatised the custom in Julius Caesar, where Caesar bids Mark Antony touch Calpurnia during the run "in this holy chase".

Later history

Lupercalia remained popular into the Christian era; in 44 BC Mark Antony, as a Lupercus, famously offered Caesar a crown during the run. The festival survived the empire's Christianisation by centuries — evidence of its tenacity — until Pope Gelasius I suppressed it around AD 494, denouncing its continuing celebration by nominally Christian Romans. A persistent modern claim that Saint Valentine's Day (14 February) was instituted to replace Lupercalia is not supported by the sources; the feast of St Valentine predates Gelasius's action and has no documented link to the festival.
