Pali Canon

The Pāli Canon is the standard collection of scriptures in the Theravada Buddhist tradition, preserved in the Pāli language. It is the most complete surviving early Buddhist canon and is also known as the Tipiṭaka (Pāli for "three baskets"), referring to its three main divisions.

Contents

The three baskets are the Vinaya Piṭaka, rules of monastic discipline; the Sutta Piṭaka, discourses attributed to the Buddha and his close disciples, arranged in five Nikāyas including the Dīgha, Majjhima, Saṃyutta, Aṅguttara and Khuddaka collections; and the Abhidhamma Piṭaka, systematic philosophical and psychological treatises.

History

The texts were transmitted orally for centuries after the death of the Buddha. According to tradition they were first committed to writing in Sri Lanka in the first century BCE, during the reign of King Vaṭṭagāmaṇi Abhaya, at Aluvihāra. A notable later recension was sponsored by King Mindon of Burma, who had the canon inscribed on 729 marble slabs at Kuthodaw Pagoda in Mandalay in the 19th century.

Significance

The Pali Canon is regarded by Theravada Buddhists in Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos and Cambodia as the authoritative record of the Buddha's teaching. Modern scholars use it, alongside parallel texts preserved in Chinese and Tibetan translation, to reconstruct early Buddhism. See also Buddhism and Tripitaka.
