Basaglia Law

The Basaglia Law (Italian: Legge Basaglia), formally Law 180 (Legge 180) of 13 May 1978, is the Italian mental health law that reformed the psychiatric system in Italy. It is named after its chief promoter, the psychiatrist Franco Basaglia, and is credited with beginning the closure of psychiatric hospitals (asylums) in Italy and inspiring deinstitutionalisation movements worldwide.

Franco Basaglia (1924–1980), whose work led to Law 180.

Background

Basaglia developed his ideas while directing the asylum at Gorizia from 1961 and later at Trieste, where he and his team dismantled the institutional regime from within: abolishing restraints, opening the wards, and building community-based alternatives, in a movement known as Psichiatria Democratica (Democratic Psychiatry). His 1968 book L'istituzione negata ("The Negated Institution") became a classic critique of total institutions.

Provisions

Law 180 prohibited new admissions to psychiatric hospitals, ordered the gradual phasing out of existing asylums, and restricted compulsory treatment (trattamento sanitario obbligatorio) to small psychiatric units in general hospitals, subject to strict safeguards and review by a judge. Responsibility for mental health care passed to community mental health services. The law was incorporated later in 1978 into Law 833, which established Italy's national health service. The last Italian psychiatric hospitals closed by the late 1990s, making Italy the first country to abolish the asylum system by law.
