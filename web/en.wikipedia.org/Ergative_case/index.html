Ergative case

In ergative languages the transitive subject is marked differently from the intransitive subject

In linguistics, the ergative case is the grammatical case that identifies a nominal phrase as the subject of a transitive verb in ergative–absolutive languages. The ergative case is typically marked, while the absolutive case — used for the object of a transitive verb and the subject of an intransitive verb — is usually the unmarked or citation form.

Distribution

Ergative case marking is found in languages such as Basque, Georgian, many Mayan languages, Inuit languages, numerous Australian Aboriginal languages (such as Dyirbal), and various languages of the Caucasus and the Himalayas. In Basque, for example, the ergative is marked with the suffix -k, while the absolutive is unmarked.

Split ergativity

Many languages show ergative case marking only in parts of their grammar — a phenomenon called split ergativity. In Hindi-Urdu, for instance, ergative marking of the subject appears with perfective aspect transitive verbs but not elsewhere. In other languages the split depends on the type of noun phrase involved: in Dyirbal, pronouns pattern nominative–accusatively while full nouns pattern ergatively.

Optional ergativity

In some languages the ergative case marker is optional and its use conveys pragmatic nuances such as emphasis on the agent's volition or effort. Such optional ergative marking has been documented in a number of Australian and New Guinea languages.
