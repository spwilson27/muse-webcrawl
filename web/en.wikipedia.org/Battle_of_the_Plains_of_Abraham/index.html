The Battle of the Plains of Abraham, also known as the Battle of Quebec, was fought on 13 September 1759 during the French and Indian War (the North American theatre of the Seven Years' War). British troops under Major-General James Wolfe defeated the French army of the Marquis de Montcalm on a plateau just outside the walls of Quebec City. Both commanders died of wounds, and the city surrendered five days later—a decisive step in the British conquest of Canada.

Background

A British expedition had sailed up the St. Lawrence in the summer of 1759 but spent months frustrated by Montcalm's strong positions at Beauport and Montmorency. In the night of 12–13 September, Wolfe gambled on landing at Anse-au-Foulon and scaling the cliffs by a narrow path, putting some 4,400 men on the heights above the city by morning—the Plains of Abraham, named for a seventeenth-century landowner.

The battle

Montcalm, fearing the British would entrench and believing his supply line threatened, attacked at once rather than wait for Bougainville's detachment upstream. The French advanced in columns with Canadian militia skirmishers; the British, who had held their fire and double-loaded their muskets, delivered a single devastating close-range volley and advanced with the bayonet, Highlanders charging with broadswords. The French line broke within about fifteen minutes of musketry. Wolfe was mortally wounded on the field—tradition has him expire on hearing the French were fleeing—and Montcalm died in the city the next morning.

Aftermath

Quebec capitulated on 18 September. The French nearly retook it after winning the Battle of Sainte-Foy in April 1760, but British ships arrived first up the St. Lawrence, and Montreal surrendered in September 1760. The Treaty of Paris (1763) ceded Canada to Britain. The battlefield is now preserved as Battlefields Park in Quebec City, and the battle remains a foundational—and debated—memory in both English- and French-Canadian history.
