Antoine Watteau

Watteau pioneered the fête galante genre of French Rococo painting

Jean-Antoine Watteau (10 October 1684 – 18 July 1721) was a French painter whose brief career spurred the revival of interest in colour and movement, anticipating the Rococo style. He is best known for inventing the fête galante—scenes of courtship and leisure set in idealized parkland landscapes—exemplified by Pilgrimage to Cythera (1717).

Life and career

Born in Valenciennes, Watteau moved to Paris around 1702, where he worked copying paintings and studied theatre scenes and the work of Rubens. He won the second prize in the Prix de Rome in 1709 and was admitted to the Académie Royale in 1717 on the strength of Pilgrimage to Cythera, submitted as a reception piece in a genre the Academy created specially for him. Plagued by tuberculosis, he died in 1721 at the age of 36.

Works

Major works include Gilles (the Pierrot figure), The Shop Sign of Gersaint (1720), Mezzetino, and numerous drawings in the trois crayons technique. His paintings depict elegant figures in shimmering fabrics against dreamy landscapes, combining observation of the Parisian theatre—especially the commedia dell'arte—with poetic melancholy.

Influence

Watteau's followers, including Nicolas Lancret and Jean-Baptiste Pater, popularized the fête galante, and his art profoundly influenced the development of Rococo painting in France and across Europe.
