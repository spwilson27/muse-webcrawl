Necker cube

The Necker cube is an optical illusion that was first published as a rhomboid in 1832 by Swiss crystallographer Louis Albert Necker. It is a simple wire-frame drawing of a cube with no depth cues, whose ambiguous line drawing produces a multistable perceptual effect: the cube intermittently appears to flip between two orientations, with either the lower-left or the upper-right square appearing to be the front face.

Perceptual ambiguity

Because the drawing contains no cues as to which edges occlude which, the visual system cannot settle on a unique three-dimensional interpretation, and perception alternates spontaneously between the two consistent readings. The Necker cube has been used extensively in the study of visual perception, bistable perception, and models of consciousness, illustrating that perception is an active construction rather than a passive recording of sensory input.

Related figures

The Necker cube belongs to a family of ambiguous figures alongside the Rubin vase, the duck–rabbit illusion, and the impossible cube, which adds contradictory depth cues to the same wireframe. Variations in reported flip rates have also been studied in connection with attention, age, and certain neurological and psychiatric conditions.
