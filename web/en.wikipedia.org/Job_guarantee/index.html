Job guarantee

A job guarantee (also called a job guarantee program or employer of last resort) is a proposed economic policy under which a government commits to providing a job at a living wage to every working-age citizen who wants one and cannot find employment in the private sector. The policy is associated especially with Modern Monetary Theory and with earlier Keynesian and post-Keynesian proposals for full employment.

Design and arguments

Proponents argue that a job guarantee would eliminate involuntary unemployment, act as an automatic stabiliser (spending rises in slumps and falls in booms), provide useful public services and care work, and anchor wages by setting an effective floor without competing with private employers. Critics raise concerns about administrative feasibility, the productivity and value of make-work jobs, fiscal exposure, and possible inflationary pressure.

Examples and antecedents

Large-scale public employment schemes often compared to job guarantees include the United States' Works Progress Administration during the New Deal, India's Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA), which guarantees rural households up to 100 days of wage employment per year, and Argentina's Jefes de Hogar programme. In the United States, a federal job guarantee has been proposed in several legislative bills and became a prominent topic in debates over a Green New Deal.
