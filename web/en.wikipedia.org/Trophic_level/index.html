Trophic level

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The trophic level of an organism is the position it occupies in a food web — the sequence of organisms that eat and are eaten within an ecosystem. The concept was developed from the work of Raymond Lindeman (1942), building on the food-chain ideas of Charles Elton and August Thienemann.

Levels

  • Level 1 — primary producers: photosynthetic plants, algae and some bacteria that make food from sunlight and inorganic matter.
  • Level 2 — primary consumers: herbivores that eat producers.
  • Level 3 — secondary consumers: carnivores that eat herbivores.
  • Level 4 and above: tertiary consumers and apex predators with no natural enemies.
  • Decomposers and detritivores (fungi, bacteria, worms) break down dead material from every level and are sometimes treated as a separate level.

Energy transfer

Only about 10 per cent of the energy at one level is typically incorporated into the next — the "ten per cent law" associated with Lindeman — the rest being lost as heat, waste and unconsumed material. This inefficiency limits most food chains to four or five levels and produces the familiar ecological pyramid of biomass and numbers. Many organisms feed at more than one level (omnivores), so species are often assigned fractional trophic levels; humans average about 2.2.

Applications

Trophic-level analysis underpins fisheries science, biomagnification studies (contaminants such as mercury concentrate at higher levels), and measures such as the mean trophic level of fish catches, used as an indicator of ecosystem health.
