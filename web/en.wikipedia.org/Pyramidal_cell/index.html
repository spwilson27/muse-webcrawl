Pyramidal cell

Pyramidal cells, or pyramidal neurons, are a type of multipolar neuron found in areas of the brain including the cerebral cortex, the hippocampus, and the amygdala. They are the principal excitatory neurons of the mammalian forebrain, using glutamate as their main neurotransmitter, and are named for their roughly triangular, pyramid-shaped cell bodies (somas).

Pyramidal cell
Type: Excitatory neuron
Neurotransmitter: Glutamate
Locations: Cortex, hippocampus, amygdala
First described by: Santiago Ramón y Cajal

Structure

A pyramidal cell has a conical soma, a single long apical dendrite that extends toward the cortical surface, multiple basal dendrites spreading laterally, and a single axon that projects to other cortical areas or subcortical targets. Its dendrites are covered with small protrusions called dendritic spines, which receive most of the cell's excitatory synaptic inputs and are implicated in learning and memory.

Types

Pyramidal neurons vary by region and layer. Corticospinal neurons of layer 5, including the giant Betz cells of the primary motor cortex, send axons down the pyramidal tracts to the spinal cord. Hippocampal pyramidal cells in fields CA1 and CA3 form the backbone of the hippocampal circuit essential to memory formation.
