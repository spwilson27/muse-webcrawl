Extermination camp

Nazi Germany used six extermination camps (German: Vernichtungslager) during World War II in Central and Eastern Europe to systematically murder millions of people during the Holocaust. Unlike concentration camps, which were primarily places of detention and forced labour, the extermination camps were purpose-built killing facilities whose victims were murdered almost immediately upon arrival, chiefly in gas chambers.

The camps

The six extermination camps were Chełmno, Bełżec, Sobibór, Treblinka, Auschwitz II–Birkenau, and Majdanek, all located in German-occupied Poland. The first gassings of Jews took place at Chełmno in December 1941, using gas vans. Bełżec, Sobibór, and Treblinka were established in 1942 under Operation Reinhard and used stationary gas chambers fed by carbon monoxide from engine exhaust; together they murdered roughly 1.7 million Jews. Auschwitz-Birkenau, the largest camp, used Zyklon B and murdered about 1.1 million people, most of them Jews, alongside Poles, Roma, and Soviet prisoners of war.

Victims

In total, approximately 2.7 million Jews were murdered in the extermination camps, forming a central part of the Nazis' "Final Solution to the Jewish Question". The victims also included Roma and other groups targeted by the regime.

Aftermath

Most of the camps were dismantled by the SS in 1943–44 in an effort to conceal the crimes. The camps are now memorial and museum sites, and their history was documented at the Nuremberg trials and subsequent proceedings. Survivor testimony and the work of historians such as Raul Hilberg have established the evidentiary record.
