Goldschmidt classification

The Goldschmidt classification, developed by Victor Goldschmidt (1888–1947), is a geochemical classification which groups the chemical elements within the Earth according to their preferred host phases into lithophile (rock-loving), siderophile (iron-loving), chalcophile (sulfide ore-loving), and atmophile (gas-loving or volatile).

Goldschmidt, regarded as the founder of modern geochemistry, developed the scheme while working on the differentiation of the Earth and on meteorite compositions in the 1920s and 1930s, publishing it most fully in his posthumous Geochemistry (1954).

The four phases

Lithophile elements

Lithophile elements readily combine with oxygen and are concentrated in the Earth's crust and mantle as oxides and silicates. They include silicon, aluminium, calcium, magnesium, sodium, potassium, and the rare earth elements.

Siderophile elements

Siderophile elements dissolve readily in iron and, during planetary differentiation, sank with metallic iron into the core. They include iron itself, nickel, cobalt, platinum, gold, iridium, and osmium, and are correspondingly depleted in the crust.

Chalcophile elements

Chalcophile elements have an affinity for sulfur and concentrate in sulfide minerals and ores. They include copper, zinc, lead, silver, cadmium, mercury, and sulfur itself.

Atmophile elements

Atmophile elements are the volatiles that occur mainly as gases in the atmosphere and hydrosphere: hydrogen, nitrogen, carbon (variable), and the noble gases.

Explanation

The classification follows from the competition between an element's affinity for oxygen, iron, and sulfur, quantifiable through the free energies of formation of its oxide, its alloying with iron, and its sulfide. Some elements (such as iron, carbon, and phosphorus) show mixed affinities and are partitioned among phases depending on oxidation state and conditions. The scheme explains crustal abundances, ore formation, and the compositions of meteorites, which preserve separate metal, sulfide, and silicate fractions.
