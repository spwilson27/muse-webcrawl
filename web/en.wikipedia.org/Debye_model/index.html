Debye model

The Debye model is a method developed by Peter Debye in 1912 for estimating the phonon contribution to the specific heat (heat capacity) of a solid. It treats the vibrations of the atomic lattice as phonons in a box, in analogy to photons in electromagnetic radiation.

Assumptions

Unlike the earlier Einstein model, which assumed all atoms vibrate at a single frequency, the Debye model assumes a continuous spectrum of vibrational modes with linear dispersion up to a maximum cutoff frequency, the Debye frequency. The corresponding Debye temperature is a material-specific parameter; above it, all modes are excited, and below it modes progressively freeze out.

Results

The model reproduces Dulong–Petit behaviour at high temperatures and, crucially, predicts that at low temperatures the heat capacity varies as T³, matching experiments where the Einstein model failed. It remains a standard approximation in solid-state physics, though it neglects details such as phonon dispersion and optical branches.

Applications

Beyond heat capacity, Debye-model ideas underlie the Debye–Waller factor in diffraction and estimates of thermal conductivity and melting behaviour.
