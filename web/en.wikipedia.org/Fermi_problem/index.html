Fermi problem

A Fermi problem, also called a Fermi estimate or order-of-magnitude problem, is an estimation problem designed to teach dimensional analysis and approximation: a seemingly impossible-to-calculate quantity is estimated by breaking it into simpler sub-problems whose values can be reasonably guessed. The technique is named after physicist Enrico Fermi, renowned for making good approximate calculations with little or no actual data.

Examples

The classic example is "How many piano tuners are there in Chicago?", solved by estimating the population, the fraction of households with pianos, tuning frequency, and the number of pianos a tuner can service per year. Fermi famously estimated the yield of the first atomic bomb test by observing how far scraps of paper were blown by the blast wave. Other famous examples include the Drake equation, which estimates the number of communicative civilizations in the galaxy by multiplying a chain of rough factors.

Method and rationale

Fermi estimates work because errors in individual guesses tend partially to cancel, and because quantities constrained within an order of magnitude multiply to give an answer correct within a factor of a few. Such estimates are widely used in physics, engineering, and business interviews to check whether detailed calculations are plausible.
