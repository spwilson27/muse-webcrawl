Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis


Diagram of the HPA axis and its negative feedback regulation.

The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis (HPA axis or HTPA axis) is a complex set of direct influences and feedback interactions among three components: the hypothalamus, the pituitary gland (a pea-shaped structure located below the hypothalamus), and the adrenal glands (small, conical organs on top of the kidneys).

These organs and their interactions constitute the HPA axis, a major neuroendocrine system that controls reactions to stress and regulates many body processes, including digestion, the immune system, mood and emotions, sexuality, and energy storage and expenditure. It is the common mechanism for interactions among glands, hormones, and parts of the midbrain that mediate the general adaptation syndrome (GAS).

Anatomy

The key elements of the HPA axis are:

  • The paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus, which contains neurosecretory neurons that synthesize and secrete vasopressin and corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH). These two peptides regulate:
    • The anterior lobe of the pituitary gland, which secretes adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) in response to CRH.
  • The adrenal cortex, which produces glucocorticoid hormones (mainly cortisol) in response to stimulation by ACTH. Glucocorticoids in turn act back on the hypothalamus and pituitary gland in a negative feedback cycle.

Function

Release of CRH from the hypothalamus is influenced by stress, physical activity, illness, blood levels of cortisol and by the sleep/wake cycle (circadian rhythm). In healthy individuals, cortisol rises rapidly after wakening, reaching a peak within 30–45 minutes (the cortisol awakening response). It then gradually falls over the day, rising again in late afternoon. Cortisol levels then fall in late evening, reaching a trough during the middle of the night. Abnormally flattened circadian cortisol regulation has been associated with chronic fatigue syndrome, insomnia and burnout.

The HPA axis is responsible for modulating many processes, including:

  • Stress response — the "fight or flight" preparation of the body.
  • Digestion — temporarily suppressing digestive activity during acute stress.
  • Immune system — cortisol has anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects.
  • Mood and emotions — interactions with serotonergic and other neurotransmitter systems.

Clinical significance

Dysregulation of the HPA axis is implicated in numerous conditions, including Cushing's syndrome (hypercortisolism), Addison's disease (hypocortisolism), major depressive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, and chronic fatigue syndrome. Studies of people who have experienced severe early life stress, such as childhood abuse, suggest lasting alterations in HPA axis reactivity.
