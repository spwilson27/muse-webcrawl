Robber baron (industrialist)

A contemporary cartoon depiction of the "robber barons"

Robber baron is a term of social criticism originally applied to certain late 19th-century American businessmen who allegedly used questionable practices to amass their wealth. The term combines the sense of criminal ("robber") and illegitimate aristocracy ("baron") in the United States.

Origin and usage

The phrase appeared in print from the 1870s, applied first to railroad magnates. It was popularised by Matthew Josephson's 1934 book The Robber Barons. Figures commonly labelled robber barons include Cornelius Vanderbilt, John D. Rockefeller, Andrew Carnegie, J. P. Morgan, Jay Gould and Leland Stanford.

Debate

Critics charged these men with exploiting workers, corrupting politics and crushing competition through trusts and rebates. Revisionist historians and economists countered with the alternative label "captains of industry", arguing they lowered prices, built essential infrastructure and made large philanthropic gifts. The controversy helped drive antitrust law, beginning with the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890.

Modern usage

The term is still used in journalism for wealthy businesspeople accused of using market power or political influence for private gain, including in debates about technology companies in the 21st century.
