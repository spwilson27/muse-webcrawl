Jetty

A jetty is a human-made structure that projects from land out into water. A jetty may serve as a breakwater, as a walkway, or in pairs as a means of constricting a channel. The term derives from the French word jetée, "thrown", signifying something thrown out.

Types

Jetties for rivers and entrances

At the entrances of rivers that flow into the sea, or at harbour mouths, jetties are often built in pairs to direct and confine the flow of the river or tidal current, scouring a deeper channel and preventing the silting up of the entrance. Notable examples flank the mouth of the Mississippi River, where James Buchanan Eads installed a jetty system in the 1870s that deepened the South Pass channel.

Jetties for docking

In smaller harbours, a jetty may be a pier-like structure at which boats can moor, load, and unload. Such structures are commonly built of wood, earth, stone, or concrete, and extend from the shore into the water.

Effects

Jetties interrupt the natural longshore drift of sand: sediment accumulates on the updrift side of a jetty while the downdrift shore is starved of sand and erodes. For this reason jetties are often compared with groynes, which are shorter structures built perpendicular to the shore specifically for managing beach sediment.
