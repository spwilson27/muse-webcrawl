American bison

KingdomAnimalia
OrderArtiodactyla
FamilyBovidae
GenusBison
SpeciesB. bison

The American bison (Bison bison), commonly known as the American buffalo, is a species of bison native to North America. It is one of two extant species of bison, the other being the European bison. Its historical range by 9000 BCE is described as the Great Bison Belt, a tract of grassland stretching from Alaska to the Gulf of Mexico, though it in fact ranged across most of North America.

Once roaming in vast herds numbering in the tens of millions, the species nearly became extinct by a combination of commercial hunting and slaughter in the 19th century and introduction of bovine diseases from domestic cattle. A population of roughly 541,000 currently exists, mostly in commercial herds, although only about 31,000 are considered truly wild and free of cattle-gene introgression. Yellowstone National Park maintains the largest population of genuinely wild, free-ranging bison in the United States.

Description

The American bison is a large mammal, with bulls reaching up to 2 m (6.6 ft) tall and weighing 360–998 kg (794–2,200 lb). Both sexes have short, curved horns that can grow up to 60 cm (2 ft) long. Bison have a thick, shaggy coat of dark brown hair that is especially heavy on the head, neck, and forequarters, giving the front of the body a massive appearance. They shed their winter coat in the spring, and the species is known for rolling in dust or mud — a behavior called wallowing.

History

For millennia, bison were central to the cultures of the Indigenous peoples of the Great Plains, who used virtually every part of the animal. European settlement brought market hunting on an industrial scale; by 1884 the wild population had collapsed to an estimated 325 animals in the United States. Conservation efforts by figures such as William Temple Hornaday and Theodore Roosevelt helped rescue the species, and in 2016 the American bison was designated the national mammal of the United States.
