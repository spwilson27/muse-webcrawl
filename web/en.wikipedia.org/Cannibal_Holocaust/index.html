Cannibal Holocaust is a 1980 Italian cannibal horror film directed by Ruggero Deodato and written by Gianfranco Clerici. It stars Robert Kerman as Harold Monroe, an anthropologist from New York University who leads a rescue team into the Amazon rainforest to locate a crew of filmmakers that has gone missing while filming a documentary on local cannibal tribes.

The film is notable as one of the earliest and most influential examples of the "found footage" format: the second half presents the missing crew's recovered reels, whose escalating brutality frames the film's critique of exploitative Western media and sensationalist journalism.

Controversy

Cannibal Holocaust became one of the most controversial films ever made. Its graphic violence and the real on-screen killing of several animals — including a muskrat, a turtle, a snake, a monkey, and a pig — led to bans or heavy censorship in numerous countries; it appeared on the British "video nasties" list in the 1980s.

The actors' staged deaths were so convincing that Deodato was arrested in Italy on suspicion of murder. The cast, who had signed contracts to stay out of the media for a year to fuel the illusion, had to appear in court and on television to prove they were alive, and Deodato demonstrated the special effects to have the charges dropped.

Legacy

Despite — or because of — its notoriety, the film is regarded as a landmark of horror cinema. Its found-footage structure anticipated The Blair Witch Project (1999) and the genre that followed, while Riz Ortolani's elegiac score is widely praised for its unsettling contrast with the images. Modern critical readings often treat the film as a satire of the mondo documentary genre and of media violence, though its animal cruelty remains a standing ethical objection.
