Language immersion

Language immersion, or simply immersion, is a technique in bilingual language education in which two languages are used for instruction in a variety of topics, including math, science, or social studies. The languages used for instruction are referred to as the L1 and the L2 for the student's native language and second language, respectively. Unlike some other forms of bilingual education, in immersion the academic curriculum is taught through the medium of the second language for a substantial portion of the school day.

History

The modern immersion movement began in 1965 in St. Lambert, a suburb of Montreal, Canada, where a group of English-speaking parents persuaded their school board to establish a French kindergarten in which their children would learn through French. The experiment, evaluated by McGill University psychologists Wallace Lambert and G. Richard Tucker, was a success and was widely imitated across Canada and later internationally, including large programs in the United States, Finland, Australia, and elsewhere.

Types of immersion

Programs are commonly classified along two dimensions. By starting age: early immersion begins in kindergarten or grade 1, middle immersion in grades 4–5, and late immersion in secondary school. By intensity: total immersion conducts nearly 100% of instruction in the L2 in the early years, while partial immersion uses the L2 for about half the day. Two-way (dual language) immersion mixes native speakers of each language in one classroom so both groups serve as peer models. A related model, submersion, places minority-language students in mainstream classes without support and is generally not considered true immersion.

Outcomes

Decades of research indicate that immersion students typically achieve high levels of second-language comprehension and functional proficiency — usually well beyond conventional foreign-language classes — while keeping pace with non-immersion peers in mathematics, science, and eventually in first-language literacy, after a temporary lag in early English (or other L1) test scores. Speaking and writing in the L2 often remain less nativelike than comprehension, particularly in grammatical accuracy, leading researchers to advocate more focus on form within immersion classrooms.
