Atargatis

Atargatis (Ancient Greek: Ἀταργάτις) was the chief goddess of northern Syria in classical antiquity, a fertility and earth-and-water goddess who was worshipped as the "Dea Syria" — "the Syrian Goddess" — in Lucian's treatise De Dea Syria. Her principal sanctuary was at Hierapolis Bambyce (modern Manbij), one of the great pilgrimage centres of the ancient Near East.

Origins and name

The name is thought to combine the goddesses Anat and Astarte (or the Aramaic form ʿAtarʿatta). A goddess likely corresponding to her appears on coins of Hierapolis from the 4th century BCE, and her worship may reach back to Bronze Age antecedents among the fertility deities of the Levant, including Asherah and Ishtar.

Cult at Hierapolis

Lucian describes a vast temple precinct with a lake of sacred fish, which devotees might not eat; a distinctive body of temple servants and self-castrated priests called galli; phallic monuments; and twice-yearly processions in which water was carried from the sea and poured into a chasm in the temple, commemorating the flood of Deucalion. Atargatis was paired with the storm god Hadad, enthroned beside her.

Iconography

Atargatis is often depicted enthroned between lions, wearing a mural crown, holding a mirror, tympanum or ears of grain, sometimes with a fish-tailed lower body — a form that made her the earliest known "mermaid" goddess. In Carthage she influenced the iconography of Tanit.

Spread of the cult

Syrian merchants and slaves carried her worship across the Mediterranean: shrines are attested at Delos, in Rome, and as far as Britain's Hadrian's Wall region. The cult flourished into the Roman imperial period before fading with Christianisation.
