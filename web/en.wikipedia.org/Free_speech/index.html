Free speech

Speakers' Corner, a traditional site of public oration

Free speech is a principle that supports the freedom of an individual or a community to articulate opinions and ideas without fear of retaliation, censorship, or legal sanction. The related right of freedom of speech is recognized in international human rights law, including Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.

History and philosophy

Arguments for free expression run from ancient Athens and Rome through John Milton's Areopagitica (1644) to John Stuart Mill's On Liberty (1859), which defended speech on the grounds that suppressed opinions may be true, that even false opinions sharpen the truth through contest, and that individuality is itself a good. The First Amendment to the United States Constitution (1791) prohibits Congress from abridging the freedom of speech, and the U.S. Supreme Court has built an extensive jurisprudence around it.

Limits

No legal system treats speech as absolutely protected. Common exceptions include incitement to imminent violence, defamation, obscenity, fraud, and true threats; many democracies also restrict hate speech, while others — notably the United States — protect it. Restrictions are usually tested against standards such as proportionality in Europe or strict scrutiny for content-based laws in the U.S.

Modern debates

Contemporary controversies involve social media moderation and deplatforming, campus speech, misinformation and foreign influence operations, compelled speech, and the tension between private platforms' editorial rights and their role as public forums.
