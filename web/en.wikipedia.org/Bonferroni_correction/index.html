Bonferroni correction

Field: Statistics

Named after: Carlo Emilio Bonferroni

Purpose: Controlling the family-wise error rate

The Bonferroni correction is a method used in statistics to counteract the problem of multiple comparisons. When several hypothesis tests are performed at once, the chance that at least one of them produces a false positive — the family-wise error rate — grows with the number of tests. The Bonferroni correction controls this rate by testing each individual hypothesis at a reduced significance level: for m tests and an overall level α, each test is required to reach a p-value below α/m.

Rationale

The method rests on Boole's inequality, which states that the probability of a union of events is at most the sum of their individual probabilities. If each of m tests is performed at level α/m, the probability of any false rejection is at most α, regardless of the dependence structure among the tests. The approach is named for the Italian mathematician Carlo Emilio Bonferroni, whose inequalities the argument generalises; the correction itself was described by Olive Jean Dunn.

Criticism and alternatives

The Bonferroni correction is simple and widely applicable, but it is conservative: it can greatly reduce statistical power, especially when tests are numerous or correlated, increasing the risk of false negatives. Less conservative procedures that still control the family-wise error rate include the Holm–Bonferroni method, which applies a step-down sequence of thresholds. In fields such as genomics, where millions of tests are routine, researchers often instead control the false discovery rate, for example with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Applications

The correction is used across medicine, psychology, and the social sciences whenever a study reports many simultaneous comparisons, and in genome-wide association studies a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold near 5 × 10−8 is the conventional standard for significance.
