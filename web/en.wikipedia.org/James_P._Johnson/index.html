James Price Johnson (February 1, 1894 – November 17, 1955) was an American pianist and composer. A pioneer of stride piano — the virtuosic Harlem style that grew out of ragtime — he was a mentor to Fats Waller and a formative influence on pianists such as Duke Ellington, Art Tatum and Thelonious Monk. His composition "The Charleston" (1923) became the anthem of the Jazz Age.

James P. Johnson, "the father of stride piano"

Early life

Johnson was born in New Brunswick, New Jersey, and raised in Jersey City and New York City. He learned piano from his mother, studied classical technique and harmony, and absorbed ragtime and the playing of East Coast pianists in the clubs and rent parties of New York. By his teens he was making piano rolls and performing in Harlem venues, developing a powerful style built on a striding left hand — alternating bass notes and chords in tenths — under a syncopated, improvising right hand.

Career

Johnson's piano roll and recording of "Carolina Shout" (1921) became a test piece for aspiring stride pianists. He composed for Broadway revues, including Runnin' Wild (1923), whose "Charleston" launched the dance craze of the decade; recorded with singers such as Bessie Smith; and led the famous Harlem "cutting contests" in which pianists competed for supremacy. His pupils and protégés included Fats Waller, whom he taught and championed. Beyond jazz, Johnson harboured ambitions as a composer of concert music, writing the orchestral suite Yamekraw (performed at Carnegie Hall in 1928) and other extended works, most of which were lost or neglected in his lifetime.

Later years and legacy

A series of strokes in the 1940s curtailed Johnson's performing career, and he died in 1955. His reputation has grown steadily since: his recordings are regarded as foundational documents of jazz piano, and his concert works have been reconstructed and performed. He was posthumously inducted into the Big Band and Jazz Hall of Fame and honoured in his birthplace of New Brunswick.
