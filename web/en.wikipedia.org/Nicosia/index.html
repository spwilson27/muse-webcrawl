Nicosia (Greek: Λευκωσία, Lefkosia; Turkish: Lefkoşa) is the capital and largest city of Cyprus. It is the world's last divided capital: since 1974 the city has been split between the internationally recognized Republic of Cyprus in the south and the Turkish Cypriot north, separated by the United Nations Buffer Zone known as the Green Line. Located on the Pedieos River in the central Mesaoria plain, Nicosia is the island's financial, administrative, and cultural center.

History

The site of Nicosia has been inhabited since the Bronze Age; the ancient city-kingdom of Ledra stood here. The city became the island's capital under Byzantine rule around the 10th century, owing to its defensible inland position away from coastal raids. Under the Lusignan kings of Cyprus it flourished as a Gothic city, and under Venetian rule in the 16th century it was ringed with the distinctive star-shaped walls that survive today. The city passed to the Ottoman Empire in 1570 and to Britain in 1878.

Division

Intercommunal violence in 1963–64 led to the drawing of the Green Line across the city. Following the Turkish invasion of Cyprus in 1974, Nicosia became a divided capital, with the buffer zone cutting through its historic center. The Ledra Street crossing reopened to pedestrians in 2008, symbolizing limited rapprochement, though Cyprus remains divided.

Geography and climate

Nicosia lies in the Mesaoria plain between the Kyrenia mountains to the north and the Troodos massif to the southwest. It has a hot semi-arid to Mediterranean climate, with very hot, dry summers — temperatures regularly exceed 40 °C — and mild, wetter winters.

Economy and culture

The city hosts the headquarters of Cyprus's banks, the Cyprus Stock Exchange, and most international businesses on the island. Landmarks include the Selimiye Mosque (the former Gothic cathedral of St. Sophia), the Büyük Han caravanserai, the Cyprus Museum, and the Famagusta Gate. The University of Cyprus and several other institutions are based in the city.
