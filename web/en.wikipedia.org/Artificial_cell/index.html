Artificial cell

An artificial cell or artificial cell is an engineered particle that mimics one or more of the biological functions or properties of a real, living cell. Often, artificial cells are biological or polymeric membranes which enclose biologically active materials. As such, nanoparticles, liposomes, polymersomes, microcapsules and a number of other particles have variously been referred to as artificial cells.

History

The concept of the artificial cell was introduced by the Canadian researcher Thomas Ming Swi Chang, who in 1957 reported the first artificial cells made of ultrathin polymer membranes enclosing haemoglobin and enzymes. Chang coined the term "artificial cells" in a 1964 paper, and the approach was later extended to cells containing enzymes, hormones, proteins, and even living microorganisms.

Types

Artificial cells may be broadly divided into those that act as semipermeable microcapsules for drug delivery or enzyme replacement, and those designed as synthetic minimal cells intended to reproduce the self-organising and self-replicating behaviour of living cells. The latter field overlaps with the study of protocells and the origin of life.

Applications

Applications investigated include treatment of enzyme deficiency diseases, haemoperfusion in poisoning and liver failure, artificial blood substitutes based on encapsulated haemoglobin, and the delivery of therapeutic proteins. Artificial cells are also used as research models in systems chemistry and synthetic biology.
