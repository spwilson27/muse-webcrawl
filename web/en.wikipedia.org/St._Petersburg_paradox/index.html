St. Petersburg paradox

The St. Petersburg paradox is a paradox related to probability and decision theory in economics. It is based on a theoretical lottery game that leads to a random variable with infinite expected value but nevertheless seems, to most people, to be worth only a very small amount. The paradox was first published by Daniel Bernoulli in 1738, building on a problem posed by his cousin Nicolas Bernoulli in 1713.

The game

A fair coin is tossed repeatedly until it comes up heads. If heads first appears on the n-th toss, the player wins 2n−1 ducats. The probability that the game lasts exactly n tosses is 1/2n, so each outcome contributes the same amount to the expected value, and the sum diverges: the mathematical expectation of the winnings is infinite. Yet few people would pay more than a modest sum to play, which is the paradox.

Resolutions

Daniel Bernoulli resolved the paradox by proposing that people value money according to its utility rather than its face value, with diminishing marginal utility — the foundational idea of expected utility theory. Other resolutions invoke finite bankrolls, the impossibility of indefinitely many tosses, risk aversion, and reference-dependent preferences.
