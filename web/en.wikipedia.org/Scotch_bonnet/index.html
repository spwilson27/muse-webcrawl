The Scotch bonnet (also known as Bonney pepper, or Caribbean red pepper) is a variety of chili pepper named for its supposed resemblance to a Tam o' Shanter hat. It is ubiquitous in West Africa as well as in the Caribbean. Like the closely related habanero, Scotch bonnets have a heat rating of 100,000–350,000 Scoville Units. For comparison, most jalapeño peppers have a heat rating of 2,500 to 8,000 on the Scoville scale. However, completely sweet varieties of Scotch bonnet called cachucha are grown on some of the Caribbean islands.

These peppers are used in many different dishes and cuisines worldwide but generally come in two colors, yellow and red. Scotch bonnet is the key ingredient in several hot sauces and condiments. They are used especially to flavor jerk pork and chicken dishes in Jamaica and various Caribbean curries. Fresh, ripe Scotch bonnets can change from green to yellow to scarlet red; some varieties of this pepper can ripen to orange, yellow, peach, or even a chocolate brown.

Name

The name is said to derive from the pepper's resemblance to a Scottish tam o' shanter bonnet. The name is occasionally rendered "Scotch bonnets", "Scotty Bons", "Bonney peppers", or "Caribbean red peppers".

Culinary uses

Scotch bonnets are mostly used in Maldivian, West African, Kittitian/Nevisian, Antiguan, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Anguillian, Dominican, Saint Lucian, Vincentian, Grenadian, Trinidadian, Jamaican, Barbadian, Guyanese, Surinamese, Haitian and Caymanian dishes, as well as in pepper sauces.

They are the main flavour in jerk chicken and jerk pork. They are usually blended with other ingredients to produce the sauce known as jerk sauce. They are commonly used in many Caribbean curries as well as pepper sauces, especially in Jamaica where the brightly coloured Scotch bonnet is a staple in the national dish of ackee and saltfish, though usually in small amounts.

The Scotch bonnet is also used in Costa Rican dishes such as rondon, rice and beans and patí (a spicy meat-filled turnover). It is frequently an ingredient in West African jollof rice and in Sierra Leonean dishes such as groundnut stew. In the United States and the United Kingdom, many hot sauces marketed as Caribbean-style feature Scotch bonnet as a primary ingredient, lending a fruity heat distinct from the simpler burn of the habanero.

Characteristics

The plant is a short-lived perennial, growing 0.5 to 1.5 metres (roughly 1.5–5 feet) tall. The fruit is round to squashed, usually 2.5–5 cm in diameter, and deeply lobed, giving it the wrinkled, bonnet-like shape that inspired its name. While similar in heat to the habanero, the Scotch bonnet is generally described as fruitier, with notes of apple and apricot beneath the fire. Its active compound is capsaicin, which is also partially responsible for its use in topical analgesics.
