Frederick the Great

Frederick II (German: Friedrich II.; 24 January 1712 – 17 August 1786) was King in (later of) Prussia from 1740 until his death in 1786. Known as Frederick the Great, he was a military commander of genius whose seizure of Silesia and survival in the Seven Years' War made Prussia a European great power. An exponent of enlightened absolutism, he modernized the Prussian administration and judiciary, patronized the arts and philosophy, corresponded with Voltaire, and was himself an accomplished flautist and composer.

Early life

Frederick's brutal upbringing under his father, Frederick William I, culminated in his attempted flight to England in 1730, for which his friend Hans Hermann von Katte was executed before his eyes. Reconciled with his father, he developed his literary and musical interests at Rheinsberg.

Wars

Immediately upon succeeding, Frederick invaded Austrian Silesia, fighting the War of the Austrian Succession (1740–48) to secure it. In the Seven Years' War (1756–63) Prussia, allied with Britain, faced Austria, France, Russia and Sweden; Frederick's victories at Rossbach, Leuthen and Zorndorf alternated with disasters such as Kunersdorf, and Prussia survived partly through the "Miracle of the House of Brandenburg" — Russia's withdrawal in 1762. In 1772 he took part in the First Partition of Poland.

Domestic rule and culture

Frederick rebuilt Berlin and Potsdam — his beloved palace of Sanssouci — founded the Berlin Opera, reformed the courts, abolished torture, promoted religious toleration ("everyone must be saved in his own way"), encouraged immigration and agriculture (notably the potato), and drained marshlands for settlement.

Legacy

Frederick transformed Prussia into a leading European state and became a central figure of German national memory, admired and contested ever since.
