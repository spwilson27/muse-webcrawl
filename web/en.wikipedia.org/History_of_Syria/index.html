History of Syria

The history of Syria spans some of the earliest urban civilisation on earth. The territory of modern Syria, at the crossroads of Mesopotamia, Anatolia, Egypt and the Mediterranean, has been ruled by Eblaite, Akkadian, Aramaean, Assyrian, Persian, Greek, Roman, Byzantine, Arab, Crusader, Mamluk and Ottoman powers before becoming a French mandate and, in 1946, an independent state.

Ancient Syria

The kingdom of Ebla (c. 3000–2300 BCE) produced one of the earliest written archives, in Eblaite cuneiform. Cities such as Mari, Ugarit and Aleppo flourished in the Bronze Age; Ugarit gave the world an early alphabet. After the Bronze Age collapse, Aramaean city-states such as Damascus arose, and Aramaic became the lingua franca of the Near East. Syria was then incorporated into the Neo-Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid Persian empires.

Greco-Roman and Byzantine Syria

Alexander the Great conquered Syria after Issus (333 BCE); under the Seleucids, Antioch became one of the greatest cities of the Greek world. Rome annexed Syria in 64 BCE, and the province grew rich on trade: Palmyra, under Queen Zenobia, briefly ruled an empire of its own in the third century CE. Syria was an early centre of Christianity — Paul's conversion occurred on the road to Damascus — and of monasticism. In the Byzantine period it suffered Persian invasions and plague before the Muslim conquest of the 630s.

Islamic Syria

Damascus became the capital of the Umayyad Caliphate (661–750), the first great Islamic empire, and its Great (Umayyad) Mosque remains a landmark. Under the Abbasids the political centre moved to Baghdad. From the eleventh century Syria was contested by Crusader states (notably the Principality of Antioch), Zengids, and Ayyubids under Saladin; the Mamluks of Egypt, victors over the Mongols at Ain Jalut (1260), ruled Syria until 1516.

Ottoman Syria

Selim I conquered Syria in 1516, and it remained Ottoman for four centuries, administered from Damascus, Aleppo and Tripoli. Prosperity fluctuated; the nineteenth century brought Egyptian occupation under Muhammad Ali's son Ibrahim Pasha (1831–40), Tanzimat reforms, sectarian violence in 1860, and growing European influence.

Mandate and independence

The Arab Revolt and the Sykes–Picot Agreement shaped Syria's fate after the First World War: Faisal's Arab kingdom in Damascus was overthrown by France in 1920, and Syria became a French mandate, divided into statelets. The Great Syrian Revolt of 1925–27 shook French rule. Full independence came in 1946 with the evacuation of French troops.

Republic, Ba'ath and civil war

Independent Syria suffered repeated coups and a brief union with Egypt as the United Arab Republic (1958–61). The Ba'ath Party seized power in 1963; Hafez al-Assad took control in 1970 and ruled until his death in 2000, when his son Bashar al-Assad succeeded him. Syria fought Israel in 1967 (losing the Golan Heights) and 1973. In 2011 protests against the Assad government escalated into a devastating civil war involving regional and global powers and the Islamic State, displacing over half the population. In December 2024 a rebel offensive led by Hayat Tahrir al-Sham captured Damascus and ended the Assad regime; a transitional government under Ahmed al-Sharaa took power.
