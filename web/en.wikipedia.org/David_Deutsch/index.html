David Deutsch

David Elieser Deutsch (born 18 May 1953) is a British physicist at the University of Oxford. He is regarded as a founding figure of quantum computing: his 1985 paper described a universal quantum computer and articulated the Church–Turing–Deutsch principle, and an algorithm designed by Deutsch was the first to show a potential advantage of quantum over classical computation. He is a leading advocate of the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics and of Karl Popper's philosophy of science.

Quantum computation

Deutsch's 1985 paper "Quantum theory, the Church–Turing principle and the universal quantum computer" recast computation in physical terms, arguing that every finitely realizable physical system can be simulated by a universal quantum computer. The Deutsch algorithm, and its generalization by Richard Jozsa, demonstrated that a quantum device could solve certain problems using fewer queries than any classical machine. His work laid conceptual foundations for the field that Shor's factoring algorithm later made famous.

Constructor theory

More recently Deutsch has developed constructor theory, a proposed reformulation of physics in terms of which transformations are possible and which are impossible, rather than in terms of trajectories and initial conditions. With Chiara Marletto he has applied the framework to information, thermodynamics, and quantum theory.

Books

Deutsch's The Fabric of Reality (1997) weaves together quantum physics, computation, evolution, and epistemology around the many-worlds interpretation. The Beginning of Infinity (2011) argues that good explanations are the engine of progress and that human beings, as universal explainers and constructors, can in principle reach any goal not forbidden by the laws of physics. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 2008 and received the Dirac Prize and the Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics (2023, shared for quantum information).
