Hippocratic Oath

The Hippocratic Oath is an oath of ethics historically taken by physicians. It is one of the most widely known of Greek medical texts and forms part of the Hippocratic Corpus, the collection of works associated with Hippocrates and his school, probably dating to the late 5th century BCE. In its original form it required a new physician to swear, by a number of healing gods, to uphold specific ethical standards — among them teaching medicine without fee, benefiting the sick, abstaining from harm and from giving deadly drugs, keeping confidences, and refusing certain surgical procedures.

The original text

The oath opens with an invocation of Apollo the Physician, Asclepius, Hygieia and Panacea. Its clauses bind the swearer to regard his teacher as a parent, to share his knowledge with his teacher's sons and with sworn disciples but no one else, to apply dietetic measures for the benefit of the sick and to keep patients "from harm and injustice", to give no lethal drug nor an abortive pessary, to avoid surgery for the stone (leaving it to specialists), to enter houses only for the benefit of the sick, to abstain from sexual relations with household members, and to keep secret what is seen or heard in practice.

Modern use

Few modern physicians swear the original text verbatim; its references to pagan gods, specific prohibitions and guild-like provisions have led most medical schools to use revised versions. A widely used modern text is the version written in 1964 by Louis Lasagna, and the World Medical Association's Declaration of Geneva (1948, repeatedly revised) serves as a contemporary counterpart. In many countries medical graduates take some form of oath or declaration, though its legal force varies, and surveys regularly debate its relevance to modern practice.

Popular misconceptions

The famous phrase "first, do no harm" (primum non nocere) does not appear in the oath; the closest passage is in another Hippocratic text, Epidemics ("to help, or at least to do no harm"). Nor is taking the oath a universal legal requirement for practising medicine, despite frequent depiction in popular culture.
