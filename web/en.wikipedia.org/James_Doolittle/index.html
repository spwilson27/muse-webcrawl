James Harold Doolittle (December 14, 1896 – September 27, 1993) was an American military general and aviation pioneer who received the Medal of Honor for his daring raid on Japan during World War II. He also made early contributions to the development of instrument flying.

Early life and aviation career

Doolittle was born in Alameda, California, and raised partly in Alaska. He enlisted in the Army Signal Corps in 1917 and became a flying instructor during World War I. In the 1920s he became famous as an air racer and test pilot: he flew the first coast-to-coast flight across the United States in less than 24 hours (1922), won the Schneider Cup (1925) and the Bendix Trophy (1931) and Thompson Trophy (1932) air races. He earned a doctorate in aeronautics from MIT — among the first awarded in the United States — and in 1929 performed the first successful "blind" flight, taking off, flying and landing entirely on instruments.

The Doolittle Raid

On 18 April 1942, Lieutenant Colonel Doolittle led sixteen B-25B Mitchell medium bombers from the aircraft carrier USS Hornet in the first American air raid on the Japanese home islands, striking Tokyo and other cities barely four months after Pearl Harbor. The raid did little physical damage but was a major morale boost for the United States and a shock to Japanese military leadership, contributing to the Japanese decision to extend their defensive perimeter, leading to the Battle of Midway. Most raiders crash-landed or bailed out over China; Doolittle was promoted to brigadier general and awarded the Medal of Honor by President Franklin D. Roosevelt.

Later war service and postwar

Doolittle went on to command the Twelfth Air Force in North Africa, the Fifteenth Air Force in the Mediterranean, and the Eighth Air Force in Europe and the Pacific, finishing the war as a lieutenant general. After the war he returned to the oil industry with Shell, and served on government commissions, including chairing the board that produced the 1954 Doolittle Report on covert operations. In 1985 he was promoted to full general on the retired list by act of Congress. He died in 1993 and is buried at Arlington National Cemetery.
