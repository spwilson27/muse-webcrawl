Formes fixes

A formes fixes song in a fourteenth-century chansonnier.

The formes fixes (French for "fixed forms") are the three principal poetic and musical forms of French secular song in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries: the ballade, the rondeau and the virelai. Derived from dance songs, each form is defined by a fixed pattern of refrains and stanza structure with corresponding musical repetition schemes. Together they dominate the output of the ars nova and the Burgundian School.

The three forms

  • Ballade — typically three stanzas (musical form aab), each ending with the same refrain line, sometimes followed by a short envoi addressed to a prince or patron. Guillaume de Machaut composed over two hundred ballades.
  • Rondeau — based on a two-line refrain whose text and music return in full and in part through the poem, giving the musical form ABaAabAB in the common eight-line version. The rondeau became the most popular of the forms in the fifteenth century in the hands of Guillaume Dufay and Gilles Binchois.
  • Virelai — a refrain frames stanzas whose opening section repeats the refrain music (AbbaA), and whose verse lines often use internal rhymes.

History

The forms crystallized in the thirteenth century with poet-composers such as Adam de la Halle and Jehan de Lescurel, and reached their classical state with Machaut (c. 1300–1377), the first composer whose complete works survive. In the fifteenth century the formes fixes became the vehicle of the Burgundian chanson, and poets including Christine de Pizan and Charles d'Orléans continued to write the texts after musical settings gradually gave way to freer forms around 1500.

Later usage

The poetic forms survived in French literature long after their musical currency faded; the ballade was revived by poets such as François Villon, and fixed-form lyric remained a touchstone of French verse technique.
