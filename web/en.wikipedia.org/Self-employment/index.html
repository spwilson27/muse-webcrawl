Self-employment

Self-employment is the state of working for oneself rather than for an employer. A self-employed person earns income directly from a business, trade or profession they operate, rather than receiving a wage or salary. The category includes sole traders, independent contractors, freelancers, and partners in unincorporated businesses, and spans occupations from farmers and shopkeepers to gig-economy drivers and highly paid consultants.

Forms and prevalence

Rates of self-employment vary greatly by country and era: it dominates employment in much of the developing world, especially in agriculture and informal trade, while comprising roughly a tenth to a sixth of the workforce in most developed economies. It declined through much of the twentieth century with the growth of large firms, and has attracted renewed attention with the rise of platform-mediated "gig work."

Taxation and regulation

Self-employed workers typically bear responsibilities that employers otherwise handle: they must remit their own income tax (for example, through self-assessment in the UK or quarterly estimated payments in the US), pay both employer and employee shares of social contributions (the US self-employment tax funds Social Security and Medicare), and arrange their own insurance, pensions and record-keeping. They usually lack employment protections such as minimum wage, paid leave and unfair-dismissal rights. The boundary with employment is contested: tax and labour authorities apply control and dependency tests, and the misclassification of employees as independent contractors is a persistent regulatory dispute, sharpened by platform companies.

Motivations and outcomes

Research distinguishes "opportunity" self-employment, chosen for autonomy and potential earnings, from "necessity" self-employment taken up for lack of jobs. The self-employed report higher job satisfaction on average but more variable income, longer hours for many, and greater exposure to economic downturns.
