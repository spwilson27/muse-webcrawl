Social realism

Social realism is an artistic movement, principally in painting, photography, literature, and film, that depicts working-class life and social conditions realistically, often with an implicit critique of the structures that produce poverty and inequality. Its practitioners turned to factories, tenements, farms, and city streets as subjects worthy of serious art. Social realism is distinct from socialist realism, the officially sanctioned style of the Soviet Union, though the two are sometimes confused.

Origins and the American scene

The movement's roots lie in nineteenth-century realist painting (Courbet, Millet, Daumier) and in the Ashcan School of early twentieth-century New York, whose artists painted the city's immigrant neighborhoods. Social realism flourished in the United States during the Great Depression: painters such as Reginald Marsh, Isabel Bishop, Raphael Soyer, and Ben Shahn recorded breadlines, burlesque houses, and rallies, while government programs — the Works Progress Administration's Federal Art Project and the Farm Security Administration's photography unit, which employed Dorothea Lange and Walker Evans — gave the style unprecedented public reach. Mexican muralism, above all Diego Rivera, José Clemente Orozco, and David Alfaro Siqueiros, strongly influenced its public, monumental forms.

Themes and legacy

Social realist works characteristically emphasize labor, unemployment, racial injustice, and collective resistance, in a representational idiom meant to be legible to broad audiences. In literature and cinema the sensibility continued through proletarian novels of the 1930s, Italian neorealism, and the British "kitchen sink" films of the 1950s–60s. Although abstract expressionism displaced it in the postwar art world, social realism remains a reference point for socially engaged art.
