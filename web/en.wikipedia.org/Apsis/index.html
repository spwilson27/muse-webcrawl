Apsis

An apsis (plural apsides) is the farthest or nearest point in the orbit of a planetary body about its primary body. The line of apsides is the line connecting the two extreme values. Apsis can also refer to the distance of these points from the primary body.

General description

There are two apsides in any elliptical orbit. The name for each apsis is created from the prefixes ap-, apo- (from ἀπ(ό), "away from") for the farthest, or peri- (from περί, "near") for the closest point to the primary body, followed by a suffix that describes the primary body. The suffix for Earth is -gee, so the apsides' names are apogee and perigee. For the Sun, the suffix is -helion, so the names are aphelion and perihelion. For a generic primary body, the terms apoapsis and periapsis (or apocentre and pericentre) are used.

Formulae

For an orbit with semi-major axis a and eccentricity e, the periapsis distance is a(1 − e) and the apoapsis distance is a(1 + e), measured from the centre of the primary body. For Earth, perihelion occurs in early January, at about 147.1 million km, and aphelion in early July, at about 152.1 million km.

The gradual rotation of the line of apsides is known as apsidal precession.
