Export-oriented industrialization

Export-oriented industrialization (EOI), sometimes called export-led industrialization or export-led growth, is a trade and industrial policy that aims to speed up the industrialization process of a country by exporting goods for which the nation has a comparative advantage. Export-led growth implies opening domestic markets to foreign competition in exchange for market access in other countries.

History

The strategy is most associated with the postwar development of Japan and the "Four Asian Tigers" — South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, and Hong Kong — whose governments combined export promotion with selective intervention: subsidized credit for exporters, undervalued currencies, export targets for firms, and investment in education and infrastructure. It contrasted with import substitution industrialization, pursued in much of Latin America and South Asia, which sought to replace imports with domestic production behind tariff walls.

Assessment

Supporters credit export orientation with disciplining firms through world-market competition and earning the foreign exchange needed to import capital goods. Critics, including dependency theorists and some development economists, argue the model depends on suppressed wages, receptive foreign markets, and state capacities that are hard to replicate. China's reforms after 1978 adapted many elements of the strategy on a continental scale.
