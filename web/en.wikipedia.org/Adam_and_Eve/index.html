Adam and Eve

Adam and Eve, according to the creation myth of the Abrahamic religions, were the first man and woman. They are central to the belief that humanity is in essence a single family, with everyone descended from a single pair of original ancestors. They also provide the basis for the doctrines of the fall of man and original sin, which are important beliefs in Christianity, although not held in Judaism or Islam.

In the Book of Genesis of the Hebrew Bible, chapters one through five, there are two creation narratives with two distinct perspectives. In the first, Adam and Eve are not named. Instead, God created humankind in God's image and instructed them to multiply and to be stewards over everything God had made. In the second narrative, God fashions Adam from dust and places him in the Garden of Eden. Adam is told that he can eat freely of all the trees in the garden, except for a tree of the knowledge of good and evil. Subsequently, Eve is created from one of Adam's ribs to be his companion.

The Fall

A serpent persuades Eve to eat fruit from the forbidden tree, and she gives some to Adam. These acts give them additional knowledge, but also the ability to create evil and negative inclinations. God curses the serpent, Adam, and Eve, and expels Adam and Eve from the Garden of Eden, condemning them to lives of toil and suffering. The couple have three named sons: Cain, Abel, and Seth. Cain kills Abel, and Seth becomes the ancestor of Noah and thus of all later humanity.

Interpretation

The story of Adam and Eve has been interpreted in many ways across religious traditions and scholarship. In Christianity it is closely tied to the doctrine of original sin and the need for redemption. In Judaism, the narrative is read as part of the Torah's account of Israel's origins, without the doctrine of original sin. In Islam, Adam is regarded as the first prophet, and his error is forgiven by God. Modern scholarship treats the Genesis narratives as theological literature drawing on older Near Eastern motifs, and evolutionary biology holds that modern humans descend from a population rather than a single couple, though research on "Mitochondrial Eve" and "Y-chromosomal Adam" — the most recent common ancestors along purely matrilineal and patrilineal lines — is sometimes popularly compared with the biblical pair.

In art and culture

Adam and Eve are among the most frequently depicted figures in Western art, appearing in works by Masaccio, Michelangelo (on the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel), Albrecht Dürer, and Lucas Cranach the Elder. Their story has influenced literature from Milton's Paradise Lost to Mark Twain's humorous The Diary of Adam and Eve.
