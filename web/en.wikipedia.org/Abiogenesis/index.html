Abiogenesis

Abiogenesis is the natural process by which life arises from non-living matter, such as simple organic compounds. The prevailing scientific hypothesis is that the transition from non-living to living entities on Earth was not a single event but a process of increasing complexity involving the formation of a habitable planet, the prebiotic synthesis of organic molecules, molecular self-replication, self-assembly, autocatalysis and the emergence of cell membranes.

Historical concepts

The term originally referred to the now-discredited doctrine of spontaneous generation — the idea that complex organisms arise directly from inanimate matter, as maggots were once thought to arise from meat. Experiments by Francesco Redi, Lazzaro Spallanzani and Louis Pasteur (whose swan-neck flask experiments of 1859–1861 are considered decisive) refuted spontaneous generation for familiar organisms, establishing that modern life arises only from pre-existing life. Modern abiogenesis research instead asks how the first life arose under the very different conditions of the early Earth.

Scientific hypotheses

In the 1920s Alexander Oparin and J. B. S. Haldane independently proposed that a chemically reducing early atmosphere, energised by sunlight and lightning, could produce organic building blocks that accumulated in the oceans — the "primordial soup". The Miller–Urey experiment (1953) demonstrated that amino acids could indeed be formed under simulated early-Earth conditions. Later work has explored settings such as alkaline hydrothermal vents, which provide natural proton gradients and mineral catalysts, and has developed competing frameworks for the earliest self-replicating chemistry: the "RNA world", in which RNA served as both genetic material and catalyst, and "metabolism-first" models based on autocatalytic reaction networks.

Evidence and timeline

Geological evidence suggests liquid water existed on Earth by about 4.4 billion years ago, and contested microfossil and isotopic evidence places life by 3.7–4.1 billion years ago. The last universal common ancestor (LUCA) was already a sophisticated cellular organism, so abiogenesis research focuses on the chemical steps preceding it. Relevant organic molecules — amino acids, nucleobases and sugars — have been found in meteorites and interstellar clouds, showing that prebiotic chemistry is widespread.
