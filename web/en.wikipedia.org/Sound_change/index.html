Sound change


The Great Vowel Shift in English vowels

Sound change is the process by which the pronunciation of a language changes over time. Sound changes operate on the sounds of a language, in particular consonants and vowels, and are a central object of study in historical linguistics. When a sound change operates across an entire language community without exception in a given phonetic environment, it is called a sound law.

Principles

The Neogrammarian school of the nineteenth century proposed that sound laws operate without exceptions — the regularity hypothesis. Changes may be conditioned (occurring only in particular environments, such as a consonant changing between vowels) or unconditioned (affecting a sound wherever it occurs). A change may cause phonemes to merge, split, or create chains of movement in the sound system, as in chain shifts.

Types of change

Common processes include assimilation (a sound becoming more like a neighbouring sound), lenition (weakening, for example of stops to fricatives), elision (loss of sounds), epenthesis (insertion of sounds), metathesis (reordering), dissimilation, and changes in vowel quality such as raising, lowering, and diphthongization.

Famous examples

Grimm's law describes the systematic shift of consonants in the Germanic languages. The Great Vowel Shift radically altered the long vowels of English between about 1400 and 1700 and is largely responsible for the irregular correspondence between English spelling and pronunciation. The High German consonant shift distinguishes High German from other West Germanic languages.

Study

Historical linguists reconstruct earlier pronunciations by comparing cognates across related languages using the comparative method, and by examining variation in present-day speech, where changes can be observed in progress.
