Bibliography

Bibliography (from Ancient Greek for "book writing") is traditionally the academic study of books as physical, cultural objects; in this sense, it is frequently called bibliology. More commonly, the term denotes an enumerated list of written works — books, articles, and other sources — used or consulted in the preparation of a work, or a list of works on a particular subject by a particular author.

Types

Enumerative bibliographies list publications according to a system, such as by author, subject, or date. Analytical or critical bibliography studies the physical production of books, including printing, paper, and binding, to establish the history of a text. Descriptive bibliography records the physical characteristics of a specific edition in detail.

Bibliographic citation

Bibliographies are built from citations formatted according to style guides such as the Chicago Manual of Style, MLA style, APA style, or the Vancouver system. A citation typically includes the author, title, publisher, place and date of publication, and, for articles, the journal name, volume, and page range.

History

Systematic bibliography emerged in the early modern period; Conrad Gessner's Bibliotheca Universalis (1545) attempted to list all known books. The discipline developed alongside library cataloguing, and in the 20th century was transformed by computing, citation indexes, and digital identifiers such as ISBN and DOI.

Digital bibliography

Online databases, reference managers, and union catalogues such as WorldCat have made bibliographic data widely searchable. Citation analysis, or bibliometrics, uses bibliographic data to study patterns of scholarly communication.
