Ediacara biota

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ediacara (formerly Vendian) biota is a taxonomic period classification that consists of all life forms that were present on Earth during the Ediacaran Period (c. 635–538.8 million years ago). These were composed of enigmatic tubular and frond-shaped fossils, mostly sessile organisms. Trace fossils of these organisms have been found worldwide, and represent the earliest known complex multicellular organisms.

Dickinsonia, an Ediacaran fossil

The Ediacara biota may have undergone an evolutionary radiation in a proposed event known as the Avalon explosion, 575 million years ago. Famous fossil assemblages occur in the Ediacara Hills of South Australia (after which the period is named), Mistaken Point in Newfoundland (the Avalon assemblage), the White Sea coast of Russia, and Namibia (the Nama assemblage). Organisms such as Dickinsonia, Charnia, Spriggina, Tribrachidium, and the possible early mollusc-like Kimberella typify these communities.

Nature and affinities

The biological affinities of Ediacaran organisms are debated. They were once interpreted as early animals (jellyfish, sea pens, worms); Martin Glaessner championed this view after Reg Sprigg's 1946 discoveries in South Australia. Adolf Seilacher instead proposed that many were a failed experiment in multicellular life — the "Vendobionta" — with a quilted body plan unlike that of any later animals. Discoveries of cholesterol biomarkers in Dickinsonia fossils in 2018 confirmed that at least some Ediacaran organisms were animals, among the earliest known. Most Ediacaran forms disappear from the fossil record near the end of the period, shortly before the rapid diversification of animals in the Cambrian explosion.

Significance

The Ediacara biota forms the basis of the Ediacaran Period, the first geological period of the Neoproterozoic Era and the first to be defined on the basis of fossil content. Its appearance after the severe Cryogenian glaciations marks a turning point in the history of life: the first large, complex ecosystems, with evidence of predation, burrowing, and animal mobility emerging by the period's end.
