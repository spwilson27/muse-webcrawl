Thru-hiking

Thru-hiking, or through-hiking, is the act of hiking an established end-to-end trail or long-distance trail with continuous footsteps in one direction. It is distinguished from section hiking, in which a trail is completed in segments over multiple trips or seasons.

Overview

Thru-hikers typically carry all their food, shelter, and equipment in a backpack, resupplying at towns near the trail at intervals of several days. A long thru-hike can take four to six months, during which hikers may cover 15 to 25 miles (24 to 40 km) per day.

Notable trails

In the United States, the best-known thru-hiking routes are the three trails of the “Triple Crown”: the Appalachian Trail (about 2,200 miles), the Pacific Crest Trail (about 2,650 miles), and the Continental Divide Trail (about 3,100 miles). Completing all three earns a hiker the Triple Crown of Hiking award. Other popular routes include the John Muir Trail, the Colorado Trail, the Long Trail in Vermont — generally regarded as the oldest long-distance hiking trail in the United States — and, internationally, trails such as the Te Araroa in New Zealand and the GR routes of Europe.

History

The term came into use with the growth of long-distance trails in the twentieth century. Earl Shaffer is widely credited with the first reported thru-hike of the Appalachian Trail in 1948, and Emma Gatewood, known as “Grandma Gatewood”, became famous for completing it in 1955 at the age of 67. Popular books and films, including Bill Bryson's A Walk in the Woods (1998) and Cheryl Strayed's Wild (2012), greatly increased public interest in thru-hiking.

Culture

Thru-hiking has its own customs and vocabulary. Many hikers adopt a “trail name”, receive help from “trail angels” who provide food, lodging, or rides (collectively called “trail magic”), and aim for “zero days” of rest in town. Ultralight backpacking, minimizing carried weight, is widely practised among thru-hikers.
