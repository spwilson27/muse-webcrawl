Hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsy

Specialty: Neurology
Inheritance: Autosomal dominant
Gene: PMP22 (chromosome 17p11.2)
Also called: HNPP, tomaculous neuropathy

Hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsy (HNPP) is an autosomal dominant peripheral neuropathy characterised by recurrent episodes of painless focal nerve palsy, typically triggered by minor compression, stretch, or trauma to a peripheral nerve. It was first described in the 1940s in Dutch families. HNPP is caused in most cases by a 1.5-megabase deletion of chromosome 17p11.2 that includes the PMP22 gene — the reciprocal of the duplication that causes Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 1A.

Signs and symptoms

Episodes commonly begin in adolescence or early adulthood. Common presentations are foot drop from peroneal nerve palsy at the fibular head, wrist drop from radial nerve compression, and numbness in the ulnar distribution after leaning on the elbows. Recovery over days to weeks is usual, though residual deficits can accumulate. Many carriers also show a mild diffuse demyelinating polyneuropathy on nerve conduction studies.

Pathology and diagnosis

Nerve biopsy historically showed focal thickenings of the myelin sheath called tomacula ("sausage-like" swellings), hence the older name tomaculous neuropathy. Diagnosis is now made by genetic testing for the PMP22 deletion; electrophysiology typically shows prolonged distal latencies and conduction slowing at entrapment sites even in asymptomatic carriers.

Management

There is no specific treatment. Management is preventive: avoiding prolonged pressure on vulnerable nerves, not crossing the legs, using padded supports, and caution during anaesthesia and surgery. Most episodes resolve spontaneously.
