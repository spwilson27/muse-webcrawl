Kinetoscope

The Kinetoscope is an early motion picture exhibition device, developed between 1889 and 1892 in Thomas Edison's laboratory, principally by William K. L. Dickson. A single viewer at a time watched a short film by looking through a peephole in a cabinet as a loop of perforated 35 mm film ran continuously over an electric lamp and shutter.

Development

Edison filed a patent application in 1891 (granted 1893 and 1897) and publicly demonstrated a prototype in May 1891. Commercial parlours opened in New York in April 1894, spreading quickly to other American cities and to London and Paris. Films were produced at Edison's Black Maria studio in New Jersey and typically ran under a minute, showing dancers, boxers, strongmen and vaudeville acts.

Related inventions

The companion camera was the Kinetograph; the Kinetophone attempted to synchronise the films with phonograph sound. Because the kinetoscope served only one customer at a time, Edison initially resisted projection, but competition from projected film led to the Vitascope projector in 1896, which effectively ended the peephole era and launched cinema as a mass medium.
