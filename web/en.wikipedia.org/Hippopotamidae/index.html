Hippopotamidae is a family of large, semiaquatic, even-toed ungulates, the hippopotamuses. Only two species survive today, both in Africa: the common hippopotamus (Hippopotamus amphibius) of rivers and lakes across sub-Saharan Africa, and the much smaller pygmy hippopotamus (Choeropsis liberiensis) of West African forests. Molecular studies show that hippos are the closest living relatives of cetaceans (whales and dolphins), with which they form the clade Whippomorpha.

Evolution

Hippopotamids evolved from anthracothere ancestors in Africa during the Miocene, around 15–20 million years ago. The family was formerly far more diverse and widespread, with species in Europe and Asia and dwarf species on Mediterranean islands such as Crete, Cyprus and Sicily, as well as several species in Madagascar that survived until within the last few thousand years.

Characteristics

Hippos are massive, barrel-bodied animals with short legs, huge heads and nearly hairless skin that secretes a reddish natural sunscreen ("blood sweat"). They are adapted to life in the water, with eyes, ears and nostrils set high on the head. Both living species are herbivorous grazers despite their formidable canine tusks, which are used mainly in fighting. The common hippo is highly social and territorial in water, while the pygmy hippo is solitary and more terrestrial.
