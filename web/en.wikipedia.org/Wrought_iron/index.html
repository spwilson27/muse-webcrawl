Wrought iron is an iron alloy with a very low carbon content (less than 0.08%) in contrast to cast iron (2.1% to 4.5%). It is a semi-fused mass of iron with fibrous slag inclusions (up to about 2% by weight), which give it a "grain" resembling wood when bent or etched. Wrought iron is tough, malleable, ductile, corrosion-resistant and easily welded.

Manufacture

Historically, wrought iron was produced in bloomery furnaces and later by puddling — developed by Henry Cort in 1784 — in which pig iron was stirred in a reverberatory furnace to burn off carbon. The resulting bloom was hammered and rolled to expel slag and consolidate the metal. The name derives from the past participle of "work": iron that is "wrought" (worked) by hammering.

Uses and decline

Before the development of cheap steel, wrought iron was the most common malleable iron, used for rivets, chains, railway couplings, water and steam pipes, and ornamental ironwork such as gates and balconies. The Eiffel Tower was built of puddled iron. Commercial production effectively ended in the 1970s (the last British works closed in 1973, its plant moved to a museum), and the term is now often loosely applied to mild-steel decorative work. Genuine wrought iron remains valued in restoration and heritage blacksmithing.
