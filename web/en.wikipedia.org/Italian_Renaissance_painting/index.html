Italian Renaissance painting

Italian Renaissance painting is the painting of the period beginning in the late 13th century and flourishing from the early 15th to the late 16th century, centered on Florence, Rome, and Venice. It is distinguished by the development of linear perspective, naturalistic anatomy and light, and a revival of classical themes, and it produced many of the most famous works in Western art.

Origins: the Proto-Renaissance

Giotto di Bondone (c. 1267–1337), in frescoes at Assisi, Padua (the Scrovegni Chapel), and Florence, broke with the flat Byzantine manner, giving figures weight, emotion, and spatial presence. Sienese masters such as Duccio and Simone Martini pursued a more lyrical, courtly style.

Early Renaissance (15th century)

Florentine artists systematized the new art: Brunelleschi demonstrated linear perspective; Masaccio applied it in the Brancacci Chapel frescoes and the Holy Trinity; Fra Angelico, Piero della Francesca, Mantegna, and Botticelli developed distinct syntheses of perspective, antiquity, and observation. Tempera gave way gradually to oil painting, adopted from Netherlandish practice.

High Renaissance and Venice

Around 1500 the art reached its classical peak in Leonardo da Vinci (The Last Supper, Mona Lisa), Michelangelo (the Sistine Chapel ceiling), and Raphael (the Vatican Stanze). In Venice, Giovanni Bellini, Giorgione, and Titian privileged colour (colorito) over Florentine line (disegno), developing rich, atmospheric oil painting.

Mannerism

From the 1520s younger artists such as Pontormo, Parmigianino, and Bronzino elongated forms and complicated compositions in the self-conscious style later called Mannerism, which spread across European courts.
