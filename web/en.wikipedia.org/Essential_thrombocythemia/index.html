Essential thrombocythemia

Peripheral blood in essential thrombocythemia, showing thrombocytosis

Essential thrombocythemia (ET), also known as essential thrombocytosis, is a rare chronic blood cancer (myeloproliferative neoplasm) characterised by the overproduction of platelets by megakaryocytes in the bone marrow. It may develop slowly for years and is often discovered incidentally on a routine blood count.

Signs and symptoms

Many people with essential thrombocythemia have no symptoms. When symptoms occur they reflect abnormal clotting or bleeding: headache, dizziness, visual disturbances, erythromelalgia (burning pain and redness of the hands and feet), and an increased risk of thrombosis, including stroke, heart attack, and deep vein thrombosis. Paradoxically, extremely high platelet counts can cause acquired von Willebrand syndrome and bleeding.

Cause and diagnosis

About half of patients carry a mutation in the JAK2 gene (V617F); others have mutations in CALR or MPL, which similarly drive uncontrolled megakaryocyte growth. Diagnosis requires a sustained platelet count of at least 450 × 109/L, characteristic bone marrow findings, exclusion of other myeloid neoplasms and of reactive causes of thrombocytosis, and usually demonstration of one of the driver mutations.

Treatment

Management aims to prevent thrombosis. Low-risk patients may receive low-dose aspirin alone; higher-risk patients (older age or prior thrombosis) are treated with cytoreductive therapy such as hydroxycarbamide, or alternatives including interferon alfa and anagrelide. Prognosis is often measured in decades, though the disease can transform to myelofibrosis or, rarely, acute myeloid leukaemia.
