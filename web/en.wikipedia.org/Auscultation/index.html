Auscultation

Auscultation (from Latin auscultare, "to listen") is the clinical practice of listening to the internal sounds of the body, usually with a stethoscope, as part of a physical examination. It is most often applied to the heart, lungs, blood vessels and gastrointestinal tract.

History

Physicians had listened directly with an ear pressed to the chest since antiquity, but modern auscultation began in 1816 when René Laennec invented the stethoscope, reportedly after finding it awkward to place his ear on a young female patient's chest; he rolled a notebook into a tube and found the heart sounds clearer. His 1819 treatise on mediate auscultation systematised the vocabulary of breath sounds and murmurs still used today.

Practice

Cardiac auscultation assesses the first and second heart sounds, added sounds such as gallops, and murmurs caused by turbulent flow. Pulmonary auscultation distinguishes normal vesicular breath sounds from adventitious sounds such as crackles (rales), wheezes and rhonchi. Abdominal auscultation evaluates bowel sounds, and a stethoscope is also used with a sphygmomanometer to hear Korotkoff sounds when measuring blood pressure. Doppler devices and electronic stethoscopes extend the technique beyond unaided hearing.
