Zombie taxon

In paleontology, a zombie taxon (plural zombie taxa), also known as a zombie lineage, is a taxon that appears out of place in the fossil record because the remains of specimens attributed to it were reworked from older strata into younger sediments. In effect, the organism lived at an earlier time, but its fossils were eroded from their original deposits and redeposited, creating the illusion that the taxon survived longer than it actually did.

Description

The term contrasts with a Lazarus taxon, a group that disappears from the fossil record and later reappears, apparently resurrected. A zombie taxon never actually persisted into the younger beds in which its reworked remains are found. Recognising reworking is important for biostratigraphy, since uncritical use of zombie fossils can distort estimates of extinction times and faunal turnover, including around mass extinctions such as the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event.

Causes

Reworking can occur through erosion of fossiliferous rock, transport by rivers or currents, bioturbation, and redeposition in conglomerates or lag deposits. Durable remains such as teeth, shells and microfossils are especially prone to survive reworking. Geologists use sedimentological context, abrasion, mineralisation differences and associated assemblages to identify suspect specimens.
