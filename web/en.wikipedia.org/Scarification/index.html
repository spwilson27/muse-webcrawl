Scarification

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Scarification, sometimes called cicatrization, is the practice of producing permanent marks on the skin by cutting, scratching, etching, or branding, so that the healed wounds form raised scars (keloids) or designs. It is done for aesthetic, cultural, religious, and social reasons, and in modern times also as a form of body modification.

Cultural and historical practice

Scarification has been practised for millennia in many parts of the world, notably in sub-Saharan Africa, among Aboriginal Australians, and in Papua New Guinea and the Amazon. Among peoples with darker skin, raised scars are more visible than tattoos and do not fade, making cicatrization a favoured form of permanent marking. Patterns could signify ethnic identity, lineage, social status, bravery, or the passage through rites of initiation into adulthood. Among the Yoruba of Nigeria, facial marks (ila) historically indicated family and town of origin, though the practice has declined sharply since the late twentieth century.

Methods

Traditional methods involve cutting the skin with a blade, thorn, or hook and irritating the wound—with ash, plant juices, or other substances—to promote keloid formation. In contemporary body-modification studios, scarification is performed with scalpels (cutting or skin removal), or by branding with heated metal or cautery pens. Because the result depends on individual healing, outcomes are less predictable than tattooing.

Health considerations

Scarification carries risks of infection, excessive scarring, and transmission of bloodborne disease if instruments are not sterile. It is regulated or restricted in some jurisdictions, and some regions have legislated against traditional facial marking of children.
