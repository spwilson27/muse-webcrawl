Meat-packing industry

The meat-packing industry handles the slaughtering, processing, packaging and distribution of meat from animals such as cattle, pigs, sheep and other livestock. The industry emerged in its modern form in the 19th century with the growth of railroads, refrigeration and urban markets.

History

Cincinnati and then Chicago became early centres of pork and beef packing in the United States. Firms such as Armour and Swift used refrigerated rail cars and disassembly lines to ship dressed meat nationwide; Chicago's Union Stock Yards opened in 1865. Upton Sinclair's 1906 novel The Jungle exposed conditions in the industry and helped prompt the US Meat Inspection Act and Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906.

Modern industry

In the 20th century the industry shifted toward rural plants near livestock supplies, pioneered by Iowa Beef Processors (later Tyson). The sector is now highly concentrated, with a few large firms — Tyson Foods, JBS, Cargill and Smithfield — dominating production. Issues in the industry include worker safety, animal welfare, environmental impact and food safety.
