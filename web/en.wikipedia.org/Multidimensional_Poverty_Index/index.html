Multidimensional Poverty Index


Launched: 2010
Developed by: OPHI (University of Oxford) and UNDP
Dimensions: Health, education, standard of living

The Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) is an international measure of acute multidimensional poverty covering over 100 developing countries. It was developed in 2010 by the Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative (OPHI) and the United Nations Development Programme, and is published annually in the Human Development Report.

Method

The global MPI uses the Alkire–Foster method. It assesses poverty at the household level across three equally weighted dimensions — health, education, and standard of living — using ten indicators, including nutrition, child mortality, years of schooling, school attendance, cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, and assets. A household is identified as multidimensionally poor if it is deprived in at least one-third of the weighted indicators. The index itself multiplies the headcount ratio of poor people by the average intensity of their deprivation.

Findings and use

Recent global reports estimate roughly 1.1 billion people live in acute multidimensional poverty, about half of them children, with the largest populations in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Because the MPI can be decomposed by region and indicator, governments use it to target policy; several countries, including Mexico, Colombia, and Bhutan, publish official national MPIs.

Relation to other measures

The MPI complements monetary poverty lines (such as the World Bank's international poverty line) by capturing overlapping non-income deprivations, though it does not measure income or inequality among the poor.
