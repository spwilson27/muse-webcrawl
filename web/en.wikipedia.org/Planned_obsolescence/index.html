Planned obsolescence

The Centennial Light in Livermore, California — an enduring counterpoint to deliberate short product life

Planned obsolescence, or built-in obsolescence, in industrial design and economics is a policy of planning or designing a product with an artificially limited useful life or a purposely frail design, so that it becomes obsolete after a certain pre-determined period of time upon which it decrementally functions or suddenly ceases to function, or might be perceived as unfashionable. The rationale behind this strategy is to generate long-term sales volume by reducing the time between repeat purchases (referred to as "shortening the replacement cycle").

History

A famous early case was the Phoebus cartel (1924–1939), in which major lightbulb manufacturers including Osram, Philips and General Electric agreed to limit bulb lifetimes to 1,000 hours — an episode brought to wide attention by the documentary The Light Bulb Conspiracy. The phrase was popularised in the United States by industrial designer Brooks Stevens in the 1950s, who defended instilling in the buyer "the desire to own something a little newer, a little better, a little sooner than is necessary". General Motors' annual model changes under Alfred P. Sloan exemplified stylistic obsolescence in the car industry.

Forms and responses

Obsolescence may be contrived through fragile components, non-replaceable batteries, discontinued spare parts or software support, incompatible accessories, or purely psychological fashion cycles. Critics link the practice to waste, resource depletion and consumer cost. Responses include durability standards, repairability scoring, extended producer responsibility and right-to-repair legislation; France made planned obsolescence a criminal offence in 2015, and the European Union has adopted ecodesign rules requiring spare-part availability for appliances.
