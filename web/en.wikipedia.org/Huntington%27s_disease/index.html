Huntington's disease

Medical summary

Type: Inherited neurodegenerative disorder

Gene: HTT (huntingtin), chromosome 4

Inheritance: Autosomal dominant

Typical onset: Age 30–50

Hallmark symptom: Chorea (involuntary movements)

Huntington's disease (HD) is an inherited neurodegenerative disorder that causes the progressive breakdown of nerve cells in the brain, particularly in the striatum. It is named after George Huntington, the American physician who described it in 1872. The disease affects movement, cognition, and mental health, and it is ultimately fatal.

Symptoms

Symptoms usually appear between the ages of 30 and 50. Early signs may include subtle changes in mood, personality, and coordination. As the disease progresses, uncontrolled movements known as chorea become prominent, along with declining cognitive abilities, dementia, depression, and difficulties with speech and swallowing. Juvenile Huntington's disease, with onset before age 20, tends to feature rigidity rather than chorea and progresses more rapidly.

Cause and genetics

HD is caused by an expanded CAG trinucleotide repeat in the HTT gene, which produces an abnormally long huntingtin protein. It is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern: a child of an affected parent has a 50% chance of inheriting the mutation. People with 36 or more CAG repeats generally develop the disease, and longer repeats are associated with earlier onset, a phenomenon called anticipation. Predictive genetic testing is available for at-risk adults, usually with genetic counselling.

Treatment

There is no cure and no treatment proven to stop progression. Medications such as tetrabenazine can reduce chorea, and psychiatric symptoms are treated with standard drugs; physical, occupational, and speech therapy help maintain function. Death typically occurs 15 to 20 years after onset, often from complications such as pneumonia or heart failure.
