The Global Positioning System (GPS), originally Navstar GPS, is a satellite-based radio navigation system owned by the United States government and operated by the United States Space Force. It provides geolocation and time information to a receiver anywhere on Earth where there is an unobstructed view of four or more GPS satellites.

How it works

Each satellite continuously broadcasts its precise position and the time from its onboard atomic clocks. A receiver measures the travel time of signals from several satellites and computes its own position by trilateration; because the signals travel at the speed of light, timing must be accurate to nanoseconds, and the satellites' clocks are corrected for relativistic effects. Civilian accuracy is typically within a few metres, with augmentation systems such as WAAS improving it further.

History

GPS grew out of earlier military navigation projects including Transit and Timation, informed by scientists' tracking of Sputnik in 1957. The system was approved in 1973, the first satellite launched in 1978, and full operational capability was declared in 1995. Civilian use expanded after Korean Air Lines Flight 007 was shot down in 1983, and in 2000 the United States switched off "Selective Availability", the deliberate degradation of the civilian signal, improving accuracy tenfold overnight.

Applications

GPS is now integral to aviation, shipping, surveying, agriculture, emergency services, financial timestamping and consumer navigation in phones and vehicles. It is one of several global systems, alongside Russia's GLONASS, China's BeiDou and the European Union's Galileo.
