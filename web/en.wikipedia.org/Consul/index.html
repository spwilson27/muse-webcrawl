Consul

Modern consuls serve in consulates abroad, assisting their country's nationals.

A consul is an official appointed by a state to live in a foreign city and protect the interests of the appointing state and its nationals there. The term has a long history and has denoted quite different offices in different eras.

Roman consul

In the Roman Republic, the consuls were the two highest elected magistrates, serving jointly for one-year terms. They commanded armies, presided over the Senate and assemblies, and gave their names to the year. The office survived in diminished form into the Roman Empire, and consular dating persisted into late antiquity. The prestige of the title later influenced Napoleon Bonaparte's assumption of the office of First Consul in 1799, and the short-lived French Consulate.

Modern consuls

The modern consular system grew out of medieval Mediterranean trading cities, whose merchants elected "consuls" to judge commercial disputes abroad. Today consular relations are governed by the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations of 1963. Consuls, headed by consuls-general in major cities, issue visas and passports, assist citizens in distress, notarise documents, register births and deaths, and promote trade. Unlike ambassadors, consuls do not primarily represent their state politically, and their immunity is functional rather than absolute.

Honorary consuls

Many states also appoint honorary consuls, often local residents of the host city, who perform limited consular functions without being career diplomats.
