Anti-vaccination movement

The anti-vaccination movement comprises opposition to vaccination that has existed since Edward Jenner's introduction of the smallpox vaccine in 1796. Opposition has rested on religious, philosophical, safety, and civil-liberties grounds, and has been amplified in the modern era by discredited claims linking vaccines to other conditions.

History

Compulsory smallpox vaccination in nineteenth-century Britain provoked organised resistance, including the Anti-Vaccination League (founded 1866) and mass demonstrations such as the Leicester demonstration of 1885; conscientious-objector provisions followed. In the 1970s, claims about brain damage from the DPT vaccine in Britain and the United States sharply reduced coverage and led to pertussis outbreaks, though later studies did not support the claims.

The MMR controversy

In 1998 Andrew Wakefield and colleagues published a paper in The Lancet suggesting a link between the MMR vaccine and autism. The study was later shown to be fraudulent, the paper was retracted in 2010, and Wakefield was struck off the UK medical register. Extensive research has found no link between vaccines and autism, but the claim continues to circulate.

Public health impact

Vaccine hesitancy — named by the World Health Organization in 2019 as one of the top ten threats to global health — contributes to outbreaks of measles and other preventable diseases when immunisation coverage falls below herd-immunity thresholds.
