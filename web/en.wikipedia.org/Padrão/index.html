Padrão

A padrão (Portuguese pronunciation: [pɐˈðɾɐ̃w]; plural padrões) was a large stone pillar erected by Portuguese explorers during the Age of Discovery, topped with a cross and bearing the coat of arms of Portugal, to mark land claimed for the Portuguese Crown. Padrões were placed at significant points along newly charted coastlines, particularly in Africa and Asia, during the 15th and 16th centuries.

Description

The typical padrão was a pillar of limestone, usually about 2 metres (roughly 7 feet) tall, carved in Lisbon and carried aboard ship. It bore an inscription in Portuguese — sometimes accompanied by Latin and Arabic translations — stating the king's name, the date, and the name of the captain who erected it. A lead seal and a carved cross surmounted the pillar, asserting both sovereignty and the Christian faith.

Notable padrões

Diogo Cão erected padrões along the west coast of Africa in the 1480s, including at the mouth of the Congo River and at Cape Cross in present-day Namibia; the Cape Cross pillar, removed to Germany in the 19th century, was returned to Namibia in 2019. Bartolomeu Dias set up padrões along the southern African coast on his 1488 voyage around the Cape of Good Hope, including at Kwaaihoek near the Bushman's River mouth. Vasco da Gama erected padrões on his 1497–99 voyage to India, including at Malindi and Calicut.

Legacy

Several original padrões survive in museums and in situ, and modern replicas mark sites such as Dias Cross at Kwaaihoek in South Africa. The padrão remains a symbol of Portugal's maritime expansion and of the first sustained European contact with sub-Saharan Africa and Asia.
