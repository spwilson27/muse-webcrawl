Dither

Dither

Type: Signal processing technique

Purpose: Reduce quantization error; randomize rounding

Applications: Digital audio, image processing, printing

Common algorithm: Floyd–Steinberg error diffusion

Dither is an intentionally applied form of noise used to randomize quantization error, preventing large-scale patterns such as color banding in images. The term derives from an old English word meaning a state of trembling, and its use in engineering dates to early work on analogue systems in the Second World War era.

Digital audio

In digital audio, dither is added to a signal before reducing its bit depth — for example, when mastering a 24-bit recording down to the 16-bit CD format. Without dither, the quantization error is correlated with the signal and is heard as harsh distortion, especially in quiet passages. Adding a small amount of noise (typically around the level of the least significant bit) decorrelates the error, trading it for a low, steady hiss. Noise shaping can push this noise into less audible frequency ranges.

Images

In image processing, dithering creates the illusion of color depth in images with a limited color palette. By distributing pixels of the available colors in fine patterns, the eye perceives intermediate tones. The Floyd–Steinberg algorithm (1976) diffuses the quantization error of each pixel to its neighbours, and ordered dithering uses a fixed threshold matrix such as a Bayer matrix. Dithering was essential in early computer graphics and remains fundamental to halftone printing.

Other uses

Dither is used in analogue-to-digital conversion to linearize the converter, in control systems to overcome static friction and backlash, and in astronomy and microscopy to smooth images. In each case, a small amount of added randomness converts systematic error into benign noise.
