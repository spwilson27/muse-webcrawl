The Khmer Empire (Khmer: ចក្រភពខ្មែរ) was a Hindu-Buddhist empire in Southeast Asia, centred on hydraulic cities in what is now Cambodia. The empire, which grew out of the former kingdoms of Funan and Chenla, at times ruled over and/or vassalised most of mainland Southeast Asia, including parts of modern Laos, Thailand, Vietnam, Myanmar, and Malaysia.

History

The empire conventionally begins in 802, when Jayavarman II proclaimed himself universal monarch on Phnom Kulen and founded the devaraja cult. His successors built a succession of capitals around Angkor, supported by immense reservoirs (baray) and irrigation that underpinned rice surpluses. Indravarman I built Preah Ko and Bakong; Yasovarman I founded Yasodharapura.

Suryavarman II (r. 1113–c. 1150) built Angkor Wat, the largest religious monument in the world, as his temple dedicated to Vishnu. After Cham invasions sacked Angkor in 1177, Jayavarman VII (r. 1181–c. 1218), a Mahayana Buddhist, rebuilt the capital at Angkor Thom, crowned by the Bayon with its giant stone faces, and constructed hospitals and rest houses across the empire.

Decline

From the 13th century, Theravada Buddhism spread, the hydraulic infrastructure strained, and pressure grew from Ayutthaya and Dai Viet; droughts and floods revealed by tree-ring studies also stressed the water system. Ayutthaya sacked Angkor in 1431, after which the court moved south towards Phnom Penh, ending the Angkorian era.

Society and legacy

The empire left hundreds of stone temples, inscriptions in Sanskrit and Old Khmer, and sophisticated road and water networks. Its greatest legacy, Angkor, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and a national symbol of Cambodia, appearing on the national flag.
