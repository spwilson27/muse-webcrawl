Red giant

A red giant is a luminous giant star of low or intermediate mass (roughly 0.3 to 8 solar masses) in a late phase of stellar evolution. Its outer atmosphere is inflated and tenuous, giving it a huge radius — tens to hundreds of times that of the Sun — and a relatively cool surface temperature of about 2,200–5,200 K, which makes it appear orange-red. Well-known red giants include Aldebaran, Arcturus and Betelgeuse (a red supergiant).

Formation

A star becomes a red giant after exhausting the hydrogen in its core. With fusion in the core halted, the core contracts and heats while hydrogen continues to burn in a surrounding shell. The energy from the shell causes the outer layers to expand and cool: the star climbs the red-giant branch of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram. In stars of roughly solar mass, the degenerate helium core eventually ignites in a helium flash, after which core helium fusion places the star on the horizontal branch.

Characteristics

Despite surface temperatures far lower than the Sun's, red giants are highly luminous — up to thousands of times the solar luminosity — because of their enormous surface area. Their spectra show strong molecular bands (spectral classes K and M), and their extended, weakly bound envelopes lose mass through stellar winds. Many red giants are variable stars.

Later evolution

After core helium is exhausted, the star ascends the asymptotic giant branch (AGB), with helium- and hydrogen-burning shells around a carbon–oxygen core. Thermal pulses and intense mass loss eventually expel the envelope as a planetary nebula, leaving a white dwarf. More massive stars (above about 8 solar masses) instead become red supergiants and end as supernovae.

The Sun's future

In about five billion years the Sun will become a red giant, expanding to roughly the orbit of Venus or Earth and rendering the Earth uninhabitable long before through rising luminosity during its later main-sequence life.
