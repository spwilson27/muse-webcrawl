Rational choice theory

Rational choice models actors as weighing costs and benefits

Rational choice theory refers to a set of guidelines that help understand economic and social behaviour. The theory originated in economics and has since become an umbrella term for approaches in the social sciences that model individuals as rational agents who make decisions by weighing costs and benefits, acting consistently in pursuit of their preferences and goals. It is the dominant theoretical paradigm in microeconomics and an influential approach in political science and sociology.

Assumptions

In its standard form the theory assumes that individuals have preferences that are complete and transitive, that they act to maximise their own utility or self-interest subject to constraints, and that they make decisions on the basis of the information available to them. Methodological individualism underlies the approach: social outcomes are to be explained as the aggregate result of individual choices, including their unintended consequences, as in the analysis of collective action problems by Mancur Olson.

Applications

Rational choice models underpin public choice theory in political science, the economic analysis of crime associated with Gary Becker, game-theoretic analyses of strategic interaction, and models of voting, bargaining and international relations. The rational choice programme in sociology, associated with James S. Coleman, applies the framework to norms, trust and social organisation.

Criticism

Critics argue that the assumptions of perfect information, stable self-interested preferences and unlimited cognitive capacity are unrealistic. Behavioural economics has documented systematic deviations such as loss aversion and framing effects, leading to models of bounded rationality. Defenders respond that the theory is an instrument for prediction rather than a literal description of mental processes.
