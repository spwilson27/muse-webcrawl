Cryogenics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physics, cryogenics is the production and behaviour of materials at very low temperatures. It is conventionally taken to begin below about −150 °C (123 K), the region in which the permanent gases such as nitrogen, oxygen, hydrogen, and helium can be liquefied. The word derives from the Greek κρύος (kryos), "cold", and γενής (genes), "born" or "produced".

History

The field developed with the liquefaction of gases in the late nineteenth century: Louis Cailletet and Raoul Pictet liquefied oxygen in 1877, James Dewar liquefied hydrogen in 1898 and invented the vacuum flask that bears his name, and Heike Kamerlingh Onnes liquefied helium in 1908, work that soon led to the discovery of superconductivity.

Applications

Cryogenic fluids such as liquid nitrogen and liquid helium are used for cooling superconducting magnets in MRI scanners and particle accelerators, in cryopreservation of biological samples, in food freezing, in rocket propellants such as liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, and in cryosurgery. Specialized equipment includes cryostats, cryocoolers, and vacuum-insulated dewars for storage and transfer.

Cryogenics versus cryonics

Cryogenics should not be confused with cryonics, the speculative practice of preserving human bodies at low temperature in the hope of future revival; cryonics is not an established application of cryogenic science.
