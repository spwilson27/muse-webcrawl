Graupel

Graupel pellets are soft, opaque, and typically 2–5 mm across

Graupel (German: [ˈɡʁaʊpl̩]), also called soft hail or snow pellets, is precipitation that forms when supercooled water droplets in a cloud accumulate and freeze on falling snowflakes, a process called riming. The result is small, opaque, whitish pellets of rime, typically 2 to 5 millimetres in diameter.

Formation and characteristics

Graupel forms in convective clouds and strong updrafts, where snow crystals collide with many supercooled droplets that freeze on contact, eventually obscuring the original crystal shape. The pellets are soft and fragile — they compress and crumble easily when handled — and are roughly conical or spherical. Because of its low density and airy structure, graupel differs from hail, which consists of hard, layered ice, and from ice pellets (sleet), which are translucent frozen raindrops. In METAR weather reports, graupel is coded GS.

Occurrence and effects

Graupel falls most often in cold-season showers and thunderstorms near coasts and mountains, and can briefly whiten the ground. On steep mountain slopes, fresh graupel acts like ball bearings beneath subsequent snowfall and is a recognized contributor to slab avalanche release, making it a significant concern for backcountry travelers and avalanche forecasters.
