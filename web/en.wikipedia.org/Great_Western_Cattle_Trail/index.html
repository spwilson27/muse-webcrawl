Great Western Cattle Trail

Also known as: Western Trail, Dodge City Trail

Active: 1874–1897

Route: South Texas to Nebraska and beyond

The Great Western Cattle Trail (also called the Western Trail or the Dodge City Trail) was a 19th-century cattle trail used for moving longhorn cattle from Texas to railheads and ranges in Kansas, Nebraska and farther north, including Dakota Territory, Wyoming and Montana. It was the longest and last of the great cattle trails, in use from about 1874 until the mid-1890s.

Route and history

The trail ran from the vicinity of Bandera and Kerrville in Texas, through Indian Territory (Oklahoma) to Dodge City, Kansas — its best-known destination — and on to Ogallala, Nebraska. It developed after quarantine laws and settlement closed the more easterly Chisholm Trail to Texas cattle, which could carry Texas fever ticks. Millions of cattle — estimates run to six or seven million head, plus horses — were driven north over the trail during its lifetime to stock northern ranches and supply eastern markets by rail.

Trail drives faced river crossings, drought, storms, stampedes and conflicts over land and quarantine lines. Kansas quarantine legislation in 1885 effectively closed the state to through drives, but the trail continued to more northerly destinations until barbed wire, railroads into Texas and changing markets ended the era of the long drive.

Legacy

Markers placed by the Great Western Cattle Trail Association and local groups commemorate the route in Texas, Oklahoma and Kansas, including a well-known marker programme using concrete posts. Dodge City celebrates its cattle-trail heritage as one of the archetypal "cow towns" of the American West.
