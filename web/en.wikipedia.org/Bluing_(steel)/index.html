Bluing (steel)

Bluing is a passivation process in which steel is partially protected against rust by a black oxide coating. It is named for the blue-black appearance of the resulting finish when the process is properly applied. Despite the name, bluing provides only limited corrosion resistance on its own and is usually supplemented with oil; it is valued mainly for appearance and for minimising light reflection on firearms and tools.

Processes

Hot bluing immerses parts in a boiling alkaline oxidising solution, typically based on potassium or sodium nitrates and hydroxides at around 135–155 °C, converting the surface to magnetite (Fe3O4). Cold bluing uses room-temperature compounds, often based on selenium dioxide, and is mainly used for touch-ups. Older techniques include rust bluing, in which a controlled rust layer is converted by boiling, and fume bluing.

Applications

Bluing is most familiar on gun barrels and receivers, but it is also applied to hand tools, springs, gears, surgical instruments and clock parts. The fine black finish on blued watch screws is produced by heat alone.
