Nihilism

Nihilism (from Latin nihil, "nothing") is a family of views denying fundamental aspects of existence — that life has intrinsic meaning (existential nihilism), that knowledge is possible (epistemological nihilism), that moral values are binding (moral nihilism), or that anything really exists (metaphysical nihilism).

History

The term was popularized by Ivan Turgenev's 1862 novel Fathers and Sons, whose character Bazarov embodied the Russian nihilist movement of young radicals rejecting all authority. Friedrich Nietzsche made nihilism a central diagnosis of modern European culture, warning that the "death of God" would dissolve inherited values — a crisis he sought to overcome through revaluation rather than despair. Fyodor Dostoevsky explored nihilism's moral consequences in Demons and Crime and Punishment.

Forms and responses

Philosophers distinguish passive nihilism (resignation) from active nihilism (destruction of old values to create new ones). Existentialists such as Sartre and Camus treated meaninglessness as a starting point for freely self-created meaning.
