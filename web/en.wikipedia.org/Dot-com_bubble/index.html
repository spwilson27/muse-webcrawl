Dot-com bubble

The Nasdaq Composite rose fivefold and then lost most of its value.

The dot-com bubble (also known as the dot-com boom, the tech bubble, and the Internet bubble) was a stock market bubble in the late 1990s. The period coincided with massive growth in Internet adoption, a proliferation of venture capital funding for Internet start-ups, and the rapid growth of valuations in Internet companies. The Nasdaq Composite index rose from under 1,000 in 1995 to a peak of 5,048.62 on 10 March 2000, then lost nearly 80% of its value by October 2002.

Background

The release of the Mosaic web browser in 1993 and the opening of the Internet to commercial use fuelled excitement about a "new economy". Netscape's 1995 initial public offering, which doubled on its first day, is often seen as the start of the boom. Low interest rates, abundant venture capital, and metrics such as "eyeballs" and growth over profits encouraged speculative investment in companies with ".com" in their names, many without viable business models.

The burst

After the March 2000 peak, confidence collapsed as companies such as Pets.com, Webvan, and eToys ran out of cash, accounting scandals emerged, and the Federal Reserve raised interest rates. Trillions of dollars in market value were erased and many firms went bankrupt, though survivors such as Amazon and eBay went on to prosper, and the fibre-optic networks laid during the boom later underpinned cheap broadband.

Aftermath

The bust contributed to the early-2000s recession, triggered regulatory reforms including the Sarbanes–Oxley Act, and left a lasting cautionary tale about speculative manias — though economists note the underlying technology was real and transformative, only the valuations were premature.
