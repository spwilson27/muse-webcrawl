Human relations movement

Human relations movement
Field: Management theory, industrial psychology
Key figures: Elton Mayo, Fritz Roethlisberger, Mary Parker Follett
Origin: Hawthorne studies, 1924–1932
Successor influence: Organisational behaviour, HRM

The human relations movement is a school of management thought that arose in the 1920s and 1930s, emphasising that workers' social needs, group relationships and sense of being valued strongly influence productivity. It reacted against the purely mechanistic model of Frederick Winslow Taylor's scientific management, which treated workers as interchangeable parts motivated mainly by pay.

The Hawthorne studies

The movement grew out of experiments at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works near Chicago (1924–1932), initially designed to test the effect of lighting on output. Researchers led by Australian psychologist Elton Mayo found that output rose whether lighting was increased or decreased — a result attributed to the attention paid to the workers, later dubbed the "Hawthorne effect". Subsequent studies of relay assembly workers and a large interviewing programme emphasised informal work groups, norms and supervisors' interpersonal skills as determinants of performance.

Ideas and legacy

Writers such as Mary Parker Follett and Chester Barnard, and later Abraham Maslow and Douglas McGregor (Theory X and Theory Y), developed the movement's ideas into modern human resource management and organisational behaviour. Critics have charged that the original studies were methodologically flawed, that effects were exaggerated, and that human relations could serve as a subtle tool of managerial control rather than genuine worker empowerment; nevertheless, its core claim — that organisations are social systems — remains foundational in management education.
