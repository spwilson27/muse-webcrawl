Impedance

Electrical impedance is the measure of the opposition that a circuit presents to a current when a voltage is applied. The term complex impedance may be used interchangeably. Quantitatively, it is the ratio of the voltage to the current in an alternating current (AC) circuit, and it generalizes the concept of resistance to AC circuits.

Impedance is a complex number, with the same units as resistance, for which the SI unit is the ohm (Ω). Its symbol is usually Z and it may be represented in polar form by its magnitude and phase. In Cartesian form, impedance is defined as Z = R + jX, where the real part R is the resistance and the imaginary part X is the reactance.

History

The concept of impedance was introduced by Oliver Heaviside in the 1880s, and the term was coined in 1886. Arthur E. Kennelly was the first to represent impedance with complex numbers in 1893. Early application of impedance diagrams to AC power systems and to telephone and radio engineering made impedance a central concept in electrical engineering.

Applications

Impedance is fundamental to the analysis of AC circuits, filters, transmission lines, antennas, and audio and radio-frequency systems. Impedance matching — choosing load impedance to maximize power transfer or minimize reflections — is a key design principle in electronics and telecommunications. Analogous concepts of impedance exist in acoustics and mechanics.
