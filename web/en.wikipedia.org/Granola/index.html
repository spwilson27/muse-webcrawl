Granola

Granola is a food consisting of rolled oats, nuts, seeds, honey or other sweeteners such as brown sugar, and sometimes puffed rice, that is usually baked until crisp, toasted and golden brown. During the baking process, the mixture is stirred to maintain a loose breakfast cereal consistency. Dried fruit, such as raisins and dates, and confections such as chocolate are sometimes added.

History

Granola was invented in Dansville, New York, by Dr. James Caleb Jackson at the Jackson Sanitarium in 1863. Jackson's "granula" was made of graham flour dough rolled into sheets, baked until crisp and crumbled into pieces. A similar cereal was developed by John Harvey Kellogg, who also called it granula before renaming it "granola" to avoid a trademark dispute.

The food was revived in the 1960s, when it became associated with the health food movement and hippie culture; fruit and nuts were added and it was marketed as a natural breakfast and snack food. Layton Gentry launched one of the first commercial granolas in 1964, and commercial brands multiplied in the 1970s.

Granola bars

Granola pressed into a flat or shaped bar, often coated or bound with honey, syrup or chocolate, is sold as a snack. Granola bars were patented in the United States in the 1970s and became a staple convenience food, though many varieties are high in sugar.

Nutrition

Oats and nuts give granola fibre, protein and unsaturated fats, but commercial granolas are often energy-dense and sweetened substantially; a typical serving may contain 200–300 kilocalories with significant added sugar and oil.
