Ozone

The ozone molecule, O3

Ozone (O3), or trioxygen, is an inorganic molecule with the chemical formula O3. It is a pale blue gas with a distinctively pungent smell. It is an allotrope of oxygen that is much less stable than the diatomic allotrope O2, breaking down in the lower atmosphere to O2 (dioxygen). Ozone is formed from dioxygen by the action of ultraviolet (UV) light and electrical discharges within the Earth's atmosphere.

Ozone is present in low concentrations throughout the Earth's atmosphere. Most ozone (about 90%) resides in the stratosphere, in the ozone layer, 20 to 30 kilometres above the surface, where it absorbs most of the Sun's ultraviolet radiation. In the troposphere, by contrast, ozone is an air pollutant harmful to the respiratory systems of animals and to plant tissue.

Properties

Ozone is a powerful oxidant, much more so than O2, and has many industrial and consumer applications related to oxidation. The molecule is bent, with a bond angle of 116.8°, and has a dipole moment of 0.53 D. It was identified as a distinct chemical substance by Christian Friedrich Schönbein in 1840, who named it after the Greek verb ozein ("to smell") because of its characteristic odor, noticeable after thunderstorms.

The ozone layer and its depletion

The ozone layer absorbs ultraviolet radiation with wavelengths between roughly 200 and 315 nanometres, shielding life on Earth from DNA-damaging sunlight. In 1974, Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland showed that chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) could catalytically destroy stratospheric ozone, and in 1985 scientists of the British Antarctic Survey reported a severe seasonal thinning—the "ozone hole"—over Antarctica. These findings led to the Montreal Protocol of 1987, which phased out CFCs and related substances; stratospheric ozone is slowly recovering, with the Antarctic hole expected to close around the 2060s.

Applications and hazards

Ozone is used industrially for disinfecting water and air, bleaching, and chemical synthesis. However, the same oxidizing power makes ground-level ozone—formed by photochemical reactions between nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds—harmful to health, contributing to smog and aggravating asthma and other respiratory diseases. Occupational exposure limits are typically set around 0.1 parts per million.
