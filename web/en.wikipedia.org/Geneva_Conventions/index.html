Geneva Conventions

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Geneva Conventions are four treaties, and three additional protocols, that establish international legal standards for humanitarian treatment in war. The singular term Geneva Convention usually denotes the agreements of 1949, negotiated in the aftermath of the Second World War (1939–1945) and updating the terms of the two 1929 treaties and the 1864 and 1906 conventions.

The four conventions

  1. First Geneva Convention: for the amelioration of the condition of the wounded and sick in armed forces in the field.
  2. Second Geneva Convention: for the amelioration of the condition of wounded, sick and shipwrecked members of armed forces at sea.
  3. Third Geneva Convention: relative to the treatment of prisoners of war.
  4. Fourth Geneva Convention: relative to the protection of civilian persons in time of war.

Protocols

Two Additional Protocols of 1977 extend protections to victims of international (Protocol I) and non-international (Protocol II) armed conflicts; a Third Protocol of 2005 adopted the red crystal as an additional protective emblem alongside the red cross and red crescent. Common Article 3 of the 1949 conventions provides minimum protections in conflicts not of an international character.

Application and enforcement

The conventions have been ratified by 196 states — essentially universal acceptance. Violations defined as "grave breaches" must be prosecuted by states parties. The International Committee of the Red Cross, founded in Geneva in 1863 and instrumental in the original 1864 convention inspired by Henry Dunant's account of the Battle of Solferino, serves as a neutral intermediary and guardian of the conventions.

The Geneva Conventions form the core of international humanitarian law, the branch of international law governing the conduct of armed conflict.
