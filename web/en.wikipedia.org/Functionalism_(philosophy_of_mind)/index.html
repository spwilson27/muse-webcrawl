Functionalism (philosophy of mind)

Theory that mental states are defined by their functional roles

Functionalism is a theory in the philosophy of mind holding that mental states — beliefs, desires, pains and so on — are constituted not by their internal physical makeup but solely by their functional role: their causal relations to sensory inputs, to other mental states, and to behavioural outputs. Just as a mousetrap is whatever performs the function of catching mice, a pain is whatever state is typically caused by tissue damage, causes distress and avoidance behaviour, whatever its physical realisation.

Multiple realizability

Developed in the 1960s by Hilary Putnam and others, partly by analogy with Turing machines and digital computers, functionalism supports multiple realizability: the same kind of mental state could be realised in a human brain, an octopus's very different nervous system, a Martian's hydraulic brain, or conceivably a computer. This freed psychology from strict identification of mental states with particular brain states (the identity theory) while remaining a physicalist, scientific outlook, and it became the working assumption of much cognitive science and artificial intelligence research.

Criticisms

Critics argue functionalism cannot account for qualitative experience (qualia): Ned Block's "absent qualia" and "inverted qualia" thought experiments imagine functional duplicates lacking inner experience, and John Searle's Chinese room argument purports to show that symbol manipulation by functional rules does not yield understanding. Functionalists reply by refining the relevant functions — down to finer causal grains or out to the environment (externalist and teleological versions, the latter defining functions by evolutionary purpose).
