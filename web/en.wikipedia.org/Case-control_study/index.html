Case-control study

The case-control design: sampling by outcome and looking back at exposures

A case-control study (also known as a case-referent study) is a type of observational study in epidemiology in which two groups differing in outcome are identified and compared on the basis of some supposed causal attribute. Researchers select people with a condition (the cases) and people without it (the controls), then look back in time to compare how frequently an exposure or risk factor appears in each group.

Design

Cases are defined by the outcome of interest (for example, a disease) and controls are sampled from the same source population that gave rise to the cases; controls are often matched to cases on variables such as age and sex. Exposure histories come from interviews, questionnaires or records. Because sampling is based on outcome, incidence cannot be calculated directly; instead, association is measured with the odds ratio, which approximates the relative risk when the outcome is rare — the "rare disease assumption".

Advantages and limitations

Case-control studies are relatively quick and inexpensive and are the design of choice for rare diseases and diseases with long latency, since researchers begin with cases already diagnosed rather than waiting for outcomes to develop. They are efficient for studying multiple exposures for a single disease. Their limitations include susceptibility to recall bias (cases may remember exposures differently) and selection bias in choosing controls, difficulty establishing temporal sequence, and unsuitability for studying rare exposures.

History and examples

The design's power was demonstrated by landmark studies of the 1950s: Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill's case-control study (1950) of smoking and carcinoma of the lung helped establish the smoking–cancer link, and case-control methods were later used to link aspirin use in children to Reye's syndrome and toxic shock syndrome to tampon use. Variants include the nested case-control study, conducted within a defined cohort, and the case-cohort design.
