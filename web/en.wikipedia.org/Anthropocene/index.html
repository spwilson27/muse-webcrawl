Anthropocene

Human activity now leaves planet-wide geological signatures

The Anthropocene is a proposed geological epoch dating from the commencement of significant human impact on Earth's geology, ecosystems and climate. The term was popularised in 2000 by atmospheric chemist Paul Crutzen and biologist Eugene Stoermer. Proposed start dates range from the beginning of agriculture, through the Industrial Revolution, to the mid-20th-century "Great Acceleration" marked by radionuclides from nuclear weapons testing.

Evidence

Signatures of human influence include sharply rising atmospheric carbon dioxide and methane, global nitrogen and phosphorus cycle disruption, ocean acidification, mass extinction rates well above background, and novel materials — plastics, concrete, fly ash and fallout radionuclides such as plutonium-239 — preserved in sediments worldwide.

Formalisation debate

The Anthropocene Working Group (established 2009) recommended formalising the epoch with a start around 1950 and proposed Crawford Lake in Ontario, Canada, whose annually layered sediments record plutonium fallout, as the "golden spike" (GSSP) site. In March 2024, however, the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy voted against the proposal, so the Anthropocene remains an informal term — though it is used extremely widely across the sciences, humanities and public discourse.
