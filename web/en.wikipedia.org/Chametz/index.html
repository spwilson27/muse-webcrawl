Chametz

Chametz (Hebrew: חָמֵץ, also romanized hametz, chometz, ḥameṣ) is a term in Jewish religious law for leavened foods made from one of the five species of grain — wheat, barley, oat, spelt, and rye — that have come into contact with water and been allowed to rise. Chametz is forbidden to Jews during the festival of Passover.

Definition and prohibition

According to the Torah, leavened bread is associated with the Exodus from Egypt: the Israelites left in such haste that their dough had no time to rise. In commemoration, Jews are commanded not to eat, own, or derive benefit from chametz from the morning before Passover (Erev Pesach) until the end of the festival. The prohibition is considered so strict that even a minute mixture of chametz can render food forbidden.

Search and removal

Before Passover, Jewish homes are thoroughly cleaned. On the night before the festival, a ritual search called bedikat chametz is performed by candlelight to find any remaining leavened food, which is then burned the following morning (biur chametz). Because completely removing all chametz can be impractical, many Jews formally sell their chametz to a non-Jew for the duration of the holiday (mechirat chametz), and a declaration nullifying any unknown remaining chametz (bitul chametz) is recited.

Kitniyot

Ashkenazi Jews traditionally also avoid kitniyot — legumes, rice, and similar foods — during Passover, although these are not chametz in the technical sense and are permitted by Sephardi custom.
