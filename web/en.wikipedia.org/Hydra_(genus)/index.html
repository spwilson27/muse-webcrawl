Hydra is a genus of small freshwater hydrozoans of the phylum Cnidaria. They are native to the temperate and tropical regions of the world and are found in unpolluted ponds, lakes, and slow-moving streams, where they attach to submerged vegetation or stones. Hydras are typically a few millimetres to about 2 cm long when extended. The genus contains a few dozen species, including the widely studied Hydra vulgaris and the green hydra, Hydra viridissima, which hosts symbiotic algae.

Morphology and feeding

The body is a hollow tube (the gastric cavity) topped by a mouth surrounded by a ring of tentacles armed with stinging cells called cnidocytes, containing nematocysts used to capture small prey such as water fleas and insect larvae. The base of the tube forms a basal disc that secretes a sticky substance for attachment. Despite lacking a brain, hydras possess a nerve net and can contract, extend, bend, and even move by looping or somersaulting.

Reproduction and regeneration

Hydras usually reproduce asexually by budding: a new individual grows out of the body wall and detaches when mature. Under stress or seasonal change they can reproduce sexually. Hydras are famous for their regenerative ability — an animal cut into pieces can regenerate each piece into a complete individual — which depends on populations of multipotent interstitial stem cells. Abraham Trembley's experiments on hydra regeneration in the 1740s were landmarks in experimental biology.

Apparent biological immortality

Studies of long-term cultures suggest that hydras show negligible senescence: their mortality rate and fertility do not appear to change with age, leading researchers to describe them as potentially biologically immortal under laboratory conditions, because their stem cells continually renew all cell lineages.
