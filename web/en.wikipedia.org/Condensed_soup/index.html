Condensed soup

Condensed soup is a type of canned soup prepared with a reduced proportion of water. The consumer then adds water (or sometimes milk) to reconstitute it before heating and serving. Condensed soups are a staple ingredient in many casseroles and other home-cooked dishes, as well as being eaten on their own.

History

Condensed soup was invented in 1897 by John T. Dorrance, a chemist working for the Campbell Soup Company in Camden, New Jersey. By removing half the water — the heaviest and most expensive part of the product to ship — Dorrance halved the cost of distribution, and Campbell's condensed soups were sold for ten cents a can. The red-and-white label design, introduced in 1898, became an American cultural icon, later immortalised in Andy Warhol's Campbell's Soup Cans silkscreens of 1962.

Use

Classic varieties include tomato, cream of mushroom, and cream of chicken. Condensed cream soups form the base of many mid-20th-century American recipes, such as the green bean casserole (created by Campbell's test kitchen in 1955). Because they are shelf-stable and concentrated, condensed soups are also common in emergency food supplies.
