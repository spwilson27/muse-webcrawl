Re-election

Incumbents face voters again when seeking re-election

Re-election is the process by which an incumbent holder of an elective office is returned to that office in a subsequent election for a further term. The officeholder who seeks it is said to "run for re-election" or "stand for re-election".

Incumbency advantage

Incumbents typically enjoy advantages over challengers, including name recognition, established fundraising networks, official resources and a record of constituency service; in many legislatures incumbents are re-elected at rates above 90 per cent. This "incumbency advantage" is a central subject of electoral studies, and its strength varies with institutions, districting and campaign finance rules.

Term limits

Many constitutions restrict re-election through term limits. The Twenty-second Amendment to the United States Constitution limits presidents to two elected terms; Mexico prohibits re-election of its president entirely. Historically, some systems forbade immediate re-election while permitting a return after an interval (a rule long applied in Chile and other Latin American countries). Reform debates often weigh the accountability provided by the possibility of re-election against the risks of entrenched power and the personalisation of office.
