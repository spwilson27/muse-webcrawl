Saalian glaciation

The Saalian glaciation (also known as the Saale glaciation, or in North America broadly correlated with the Illinoian Stage) was a glacial period of the Pleistocene Epoch in northern Europe, lasting approximately from 238,000 to 126,000 years ago. It takes its name from the river Saale in Germany, and corresponds broadly to Marine Isotope Stages 8 through 6. During its maximum advance, the Scandinavian ice sheet covered much of northern Europe, reaching into the Netherlands, northern Germany and Poland, and in places further south than any later glaciation.

The Saalian ice sheet at its greatest extent, around 150,000 years ago.

Chronology and extent

The Saalian is a complex stage containing several advances and interstadials. Its Drenthe substage saw the greatest Pleistocene ice advance in the Netherlands, where push moraines such as the Utrecht Hill Ridge were formed by the pressure of the ice lobes. In Britain, deposits of the correlative Wolstonian glaciation record ice reaching the Midlands. The Saalian ended with the warm Eemian interglacial (MIS 5e), the last interglacial before the present Holocene, during which sea levels stood several metres higher than today.

Legacy

Saalian deposits underlie much of the north European plain; its older, subdued moraine landscapes contrast with the fresher terrain left by the younger Weichselian glaciation, and its outwash plains and buried valleys are important aquifers. Neanderthals lived in Europe throughout the Saalian, retreating south during glacial maxima and recolonising the north in warm intervals such as the Eemian.
