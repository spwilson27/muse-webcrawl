Regent

A regent (from Latin regens, "ruling") is a person appointed to govern a state pro tempore (Latin for "for the time being") because the actual monarch is a minor, absent, incapacitated, or unable to discharge the powers and duties of the monarchy. A regent may also be the head of state of a republic or other polity in certain circumstances. The period of a regent's rule is called a regency.

History

Regencies have often been powerful positions. In feudal Europe, regents for child kings frequently consolidated lasting power: Blanche of Castile governed France for the young Louis IX, and Catherine de' Medici dominated French politics as regent and queen mother. In England, the Regency Acts provided for regency during minority or incapacity; the most famous British regency is that of the future George IV, Prince Regent from 1811 to 1820 during the illness of George III, which gave its name to the Regency era.

In imperial China and Japan, regents (such as the Fujiwara regents, the sesshō and kampaku) could overshadow reigning emperors for generations. The Ottoman Empire saw the "Sultanate of Women," in which valide sultans acted as regents for young sultans.

Modern usage

Modern constitutions commonly provide for a regent or council of regency when a monarch is underage or incapacitated. Belgium's constitution allows the chambers to appoint a regent during a minority, while in Liechtenstein and Monaco the term applies to a designated deputy exercising the prince's functions. In republics, the title has been used by figures such as Miklós Horthy, Regent of Hungary from 1920 to 1944.
