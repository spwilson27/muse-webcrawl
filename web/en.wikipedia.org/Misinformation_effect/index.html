Misinformation effect

The misinformation effect occurs when a person's recall of an episodic event becomes less accurate because of misleading information presented after the event. It is one of the most studied phenomena in the psychology of memory, associated above all with the work of Elizabeth Loftus, and has major implications for the reliability of eyewitness testimony.

The paradigm

In the classic experimental design, participants watch a film or slides of an event such as a car accident. Some are then exposed to misleading details in a questionnaire or narrative — for example, a question that presupposes a yield sign where a stop sign appeared — and finally everyone's memory is tested. Participants given misleading post-event information reliably "remember" the suggested details, and can incorporate entirely fictitious objects and actions into their accounts.

In the well-known Loftus and Palmer studies, the verb used in a question about a filmed collision ("smashed" versus "hit") changed both speed estimates and later reports of broken glass that was never shown. Later research showed that even rich false memories of whole events, such as being lost in a shopping mall as a child, could be planted in a substantial minority of participants.

Explanations and implications

Explanations include interference and overwriting of the original memory trace, source-monitoring errors (remembering the suggestion but attributing it to the event), and simple response biases. The effect, together with the related literature on false memories, has influenced police interviewing practice, leading-question rules in court, and expert testimony on eyewitness reliability.
