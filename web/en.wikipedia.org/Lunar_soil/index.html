Lunar soil

Lunar soil grains under magnification

Also called: lunar regolith
Mean grain size: 0.13–0.30 mm
Bulk density: ~1.5 g/cm³

Lunar soil is the fine fraction of the lunar regolith, the layer of loose, heterogeneous superficial deposits covering solid rock on the surface of the Moon. Unlike terrestrial soil, it contains no organic matter and has not been shaped by wind or water; instead it is produced by the mechanical comminution of lunar rocks through meteoroid impacts, thermal fatigue, and the charging effects of the solar wind.

Formation

Over billions of years, impacts of micrometeorites and larger bodies have ground the lunar surface into a blanket of fragmental material, typically several metres thick over the maria and up to ten metres or more over the highlands. Impact events also melt and weld soil particles into glassy aggregates called agglutinates.

Composition

Lunar soil consists mainly of oxygen (about 45% by weight), silicon, iron, calcium, aluminium, and magnesium, in the form of minerals such as plagioclase, pyroxene, and olivine, together with impact glass. Exposure to the solar wind implants hydrogen and helium — including the rare isotope helium-3, sometimes proposed as a future fusion fuel.

Properties and hazards

Lunar grains are sharp and abrasive because they have never been worn smooth. Apollo astronauts found the dust clung to suits and equipment, irritated lungs and eyes, and smelled faintly of spent gunpowder. Because the Moon has no atmosphere or liquid water, lunar soil also lacks the chemistry of terrestrial soils and contains no nitrogen in plant-available form, complicating its use for agriculture without treatment.

Returned samples

The Apollo missions returned 382 kg of lunar material, and the Soviet Luna probes and China's Chang'e 5 mission returned smaller amounts, allowing detailed laboratory study of soil maturity, grain size, and exposure history.
