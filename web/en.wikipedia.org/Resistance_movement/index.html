Resistance movement

A resistance movement is an organised collective effort by civilians, political groups, military personnel, or others to oppose an occupying power, authoritarian government, or dominant authority. Resistance may be violent or nonviolent and can include clandestine organisation, propaganda, intelligence gathering, sabotage, strikes, civil disobedience, sheltering persecuted people, and armed combat.

Forms

Nonviolent resistance seeks to withdraw cooperation, raise the costs of rule, persuade members of the opposing system, or mobilise outside support. Armed resistance ranges from assassinations and raids to guerrilla warfare. Many movements combine methods and include political parties, labour networks, religious communities, student groups, and former soldiers.

Organisation

Clandestine movements often use compartmentalised cells, couriers, safe houses, false documents, and coded communications to limit damage if one member is captured. External governments may provide money, training, broadcasting, weapons, or diplomatic recognition. Such support can increase effectiveness while also shaping a movement's goals.

Second World War

The term became closely associated with movements resisting Axis occupation in Europe. The French Resistance, Polish underground state, Yugoslav Partisans, Danish resistance, Norwegian resistance, and many other groups performed intelligence, escape, rescue, sabotage, and combat operations. Their scale and success varied greatly, as did relations with governments-in-exile and Allied forces.

Terminology and legitimacy

Whether a group is called a resistance movement, insurgency, liberation movement, terrorist organisation, or government-in-waiting depends in part on political perspective and on the group's methods and targets. International humanitarian law distinguishes civilians from combatants and prohibits attacks directed at civilians even when a cause is considered legitimate.
