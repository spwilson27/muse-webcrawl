Fibration

In algebraic topology, a fibration is a map between topological spaces that satisfies a certain homotopy lifting property. Fibrations play a fundamental role in algebraic topology and differential geometry. The concept was introduced by Hassler Whitney in 1940 and developed by Witold Hurewicz and Jean-Pierre Serre.

Definition

A continuous map p : E → B is a (Hurewicz) fibration if, for every space X, every homotopy of maps into B can be lifted to a homotopy of maps into E once an initial lift is given. The space E is called the total space, B the base space, and for a point b in B the preimage p⁻¹(b) is the fibre over b.

Examples

The projection of a product space X × F onto X is a fibration with fibre F, and more generally every fibre bundle is a fibration. A celebrated example is the Hopf fibration, which maps the 3-sphere S³ onto the 2-sphere S² with circles S¹ as fibres. Covering spaces are fibrations with discrete fibres.

Long exact sequence

A fibration F → E → B gives rise to a long exact sequence of homotopy groups, a powerful tool for computing the homotopy groups of spheres and other spaces. The associated notion of a fibre sequence is also central in homotopy theory and plays a role alongside the theory of fields of fractions as a standard construction in modern algebra and geometry textbooks.
