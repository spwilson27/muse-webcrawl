The lumpfish (Cyclopterus lumpus), also called the lumpsucker, is a species of marine fish in the family Cyclopteridae, found in the cold waters of the North Atlantic and adjacent Arctic oceans. It is named for its stout, lumpy body covered in bony tubercles, and it is notable for the suction disk on its belly, formed from modified pelvic fins, with which it clings to rocks.

Description

Lumpfish are deep-bodied, almost spherical fish with thick, scaleless skin bearing several longitudinal rows of large bony knobs. They are poor swimmers and spend much time attached to hard surfaces. Males, normally greyish-blue, turn orange-red in the breeding season; females are larger and greenish. Individuals commonly reach 30–50 cm, with exceptional fish over 60 cm and 9 kg.

Distribution and behaviour

The species ranges from Greenland, Iceland and the Barents Sea south to the coasts of northwestern Europe and down the North American coast to about Chesapeake Bay. Adults live offshore in mid-water for much of the year but migrate inshore in spring to spawn over rocky ground. After the female lays a spongy mass of eggs (which may number over 100,000), the male guards and fans them for weeks, not feeding, until they hatch.

Uses

Lumpfish roe, dyed red or black, has long been sold as an inexpensive caviar substitute; Iceland, Norway, Denmark and Canada have supported roe fisheries, with the flesh also eaten in Iceland (especially the hard, dried male, grásleppa is the female, rauðmagi the male). Since the 2010s, farmed lumpfish have been widely deployed in salmon aquaculture as "cleaner fish" that eat salmon lice — a role that has made the species economically important in Norway and Scotland, while raising welfare and sustainability questions. Assessments have found some populations declining, and the species has been listed as Near Threatened by the IUCN.
