The Invention of Tradition
Editors: Eric Hobsbawm, Terence Ranger
Language: English
Subject: Historiography
Publisher: Cambridge University Press
Publication date: 1983

The Invention of Tradition is a 1983 book edited by historians Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger, based on a conference held at King's College, Cambridge. The contributors argue that many traditions that appear or claim to be old are in fact quite recent in origin and sometimes deliberately constructed. The concept of an invented tradition introduced by the book has become a widely used analytical tool in history, anthropology, and cultural studies.

Concept

Hobsbawm defined an invented tradition as "a set of practices, normally governed by overtly or tacitly accepted rules and of a ritual or symbolic nature, which seek to inculcate certain values and norms of behaviour by repetition, which automatically implies continuity with the past." He distinguished such traditions from both slowly evolving custom and mere convention, and argued that invented traditions proliferated during periods of rapid social change, particularly between 1870 and 1914, when mass politics required new forms of loyalty and identity.

Contents

Case studies in the volume include Trevor-Roper's famous essay on the Highland tradition of Scotland, arguing that the kilt and clan tartans were largely 18th- and 19th-century creations; studies of British royal ritual, which acquired its modern pomp only in the late Victorian era; Welsh cultural nationalism; and colonial ceremonies in Africa and India.

Influence and criticism

The book reshaped the study of nationalism and ritual, influencing Benedict Anderson's Imagined Communities (published the same year) and countless subsequent studies. Critics have questioned the implied contrast between "invented" and "genuine" traditions, noting that all traditions are to some degree constructed and reinterpreted over time. Ranger himself later revisited and softened the concept, preferring "imagined tradition."
