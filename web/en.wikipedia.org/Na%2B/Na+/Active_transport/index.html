Active transport

Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a cell membrane against their concentration gradient — from a region of lower concentration to one of higher concentration. Because it moves substances "uphill," it requires an input of energy, which distinguishes it from passive transport and facilitated diffusion.

Primary and secondary active transport

In primary active transport, energy is derived directly from the breakdown of adenosine triphosphate (ATP). The best-known example is the sodium–potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase), which expels three sodium ions from the cell and imports two potassium ions per ATP hydrolysed, maintaining the electrochemical gradients essential to nerve impulses, nutrient uptake and cell volume control. Other primary pumps include the calcium ATPases and the proton pumps of lysosomes and of gastric parietal cells.

In secondary active transport, the energy stored in an electrochemical gradient created by primary transport drives the movement of another substance. Symporters, such as the sodium–glucose cotransporter of the intestine and kidney, carry both substances in the same direction; antiporters, such as the sodium–calcium exchanger, move them in opposite directions. Active transport is fundamental to absorption of nutrients, excretion of waste, and signalling throughout biology.
