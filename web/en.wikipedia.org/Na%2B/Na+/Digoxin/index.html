Digoxin

Digoxin is a medication used to treat heart failure and certain irregular heartbeats, particularly atrial fibrillation. It is a cardiac glycoside obtained from the leaves of the foxglove plant Digitalis lanata. William Withering first described the medical use of foxglove preparations in 1785, and digoxin itself was isolated in 1930.

Mechanism and uses

Digoxin works by inhibiting the sodium–potassium ATPase pump in heart muscle cells. The resulting rise in intracellular sodium reduces the activity of the sodium–calcium exchanger, raising intracellular calcium and thereby increasing the force of cardiac contraction (a positive inotropic effect). Digoxin also increases vagal activity, slowing conduction through the atrioventricular node and helping to control ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Safety

Digoxin has a narrow therapeutic index: the dose needed for effect is close to the dose that causes toxicity. Signs of digoxin toxicity include nausea, visual disturbances (such as yellow-green halos), confusion and potentially fatal arrhythmias. Toxicity is worsened by low potassium or magnesium, and severe cases are treated with digoxin-specific antibody fragments. Blood levels are commonly monitored during therapy.
