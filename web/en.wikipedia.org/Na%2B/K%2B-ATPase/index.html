Na+/K+-ATPase

Sodium–potassium pump
TypeP-type ATPase; transmembrane ion pump
ReactionExports 3 Na+, imports 2 K+ per ATP hydrolysed
Discovered byJens Christian Skou (1957)
InhibitorsOuabain, cardiac glycosides (e.g. digoxin)

Na+/K+-ATPase, commonly called the sodium–potassium pump, is an enzyme found in the plasma membrane of all animal cells. Using energy from ATP hydrolysis, it pumps three sodium ions out of the cell and two potassium ions in, maintaining the electrochemical gradients that underlie membrane potential, nutrient transport, and cell volume regulation.

Discovery

The pump was identified in 1957 by the Danish scientist Jens Christian Skou, who isolated an ATPase activity from crab nerve membranes that required both sodium and potassium ions. Skou shared the 1997 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery, the first of an ion-transporting enzyme.

Function

Because the exchange is unequal (3 Na+ out for 2 K+ in), each cycle moves a net positive charge outward, making the pump electrogenic and contributing to the negative resting potential. The sodium gradient it maintains drives secondary active transport of glucose, amino acids, and other solutes, and the pump consumes a large fraction of a cell's ATP — a substantial share of total resting energy expenditure, especially in neurons and kidneys.

Pharmacology

Cardiac glycosides such as digoxin inhibit the pump; the resulting rise in intracellular sodium indirectly increases calcium in heart muscle cells, strengthening contraction — the basis of digoxin's use in heart failure. The pump is also a signalling scaffold interacting with Src-family kinases.
