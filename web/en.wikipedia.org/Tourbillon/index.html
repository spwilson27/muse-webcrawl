Tourbillon

A tourbillon cage rotating once per minute

A tourbillon (French: [tuʁbijɔ̃] "whirlwind") is an addition to the mechanics of a watch escapement to increase accuracy, developed by the Swiss-French watchmaker Abraham-Louis Breguet and patented in 1801. In a tourbillon, the escapement and balance wheel are mounted in a rotating cage, the goal being to average out the effects of gravity on the balance by continuously rotating the entire assembly, usually at one revolution per minute.

History

Breguet received a French patent for the tourbillon on 26 Messidor, year IX (26 June 1801). The device was designed for pocket watches, which spend long periods in a single vertical position in a waistcoat pocket or lying flat, so that gravity-induced errors in one position could be averaged away. Breguet's early tourbillons were made in very small numbers and were among the most expensive mechanisms of their day.

Operation

The rotating cage carries the balance wheel, balance spring, and escapement; the cage itself is driven by the movement's gear train and typically carries or acts as the seconds indicator. Because all positions are visited equally over each revolution, positional rate errors tend to cancel. In modern wristwatches — which move constantly on the wrist — the practical accuracy benefit is debated, but the tourbillon remains a prestigious complication prized for its visual drama and craftsmanship, often displayed through an aperture in the dial.

Variants

Developments include the multi-axis tourbillon, which rotates on two or three axes; the flying tourbillon, supported from below only so it appears to float; and the karussel, a related rotating carriage devised by Bahne Bonniksen in 1892.
