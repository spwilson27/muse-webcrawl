Foie gras

Foie gras terrine, a classic preparation of French cuisine.

Foie gras (French for "fatty liver") is a specialty food product made of the liver of a duck or goose. According to French law, foie gras is defined as the liver of a duck or goose fattened by gavage (force-feeding). In Spain and other countries, foie gras produced by natural feeding is sometimes marketed as an alternative, exploiting the natural tendency of migratory waterfowl to store fat in the liver before migration.

Foie gras is a popular and well-known delicacy in French cuisine. Its flavour is described as rich, buttery and delicate, unlike that of the liver of an ordinary duck or goose. Foie gras is sold whole, or is prepared as a mousse, parfait or pâté, and may also be served as an accompaniment to another food item, such as steak. France is the largest producer and consumer of foie gras, though it is produced and consumed worldwide, particularly in other European nations, the United States and China.

History

The practice of fattening birds for their livers is ancient. Tomb paintings in Egypt, dating from around 2500 BC, depict the force-feeding of cranes and geese, and the technique spread through the Mediterranean world. The Romans fattened geese with figs — the Latin iecur ficatum ("fig-fattened liver") gave French its word foie. Jewish communities in medieval Europe preserved and spread goose-fat cookery, and southwestern France — Gascony, Périgord and Alsace — became the heartland of the modern product after maize from the Americas provided cheap feed.

Production

Conventional production involves gavage: during the final weeks of life, ducks (usually mule ducks) or geese are fed a corn-based mash through a tube several times daily, causing the liver to grow to many times its normal size as it stores fat (hepatic steatosis). The birds are then slaughtered and the livers graded, sold fresh, or cooked into terrines and preserves.

Controversy

Foie gras production is one of the most contested practices in modern food ethics. Animal welfare organisations consider gavage cruel; producers respond that waterfowl naturally gorge and store liver fat before migration and lack a gag reflex comparable to mammals. Production by force-feeding is banned in a number of countries and regions, and sales bans have been enacted or debated in places including California and New York City, while France protects foie gras as part of its cultural and gastronomic heritage by law.
