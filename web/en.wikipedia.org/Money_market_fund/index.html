Money market fund

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A money market fund (also called a money market mutual fund) is an open-ended mutual fund that invests in short-term debt securities such as Treasury bills, commercial paper, and certificates of deposit. Money market funds are widely used as a cash-management vehicle by both retail and institutional investors, offering a rate of return modestly above bank deposit accounts together with same-day or next-day liquidity.

Stable value and "breaking the buck"

In the United States, money market funds historically sought to maintain a stable net asset value of $1.00 per share, returning yields in the form of dividends. When a fund's market-based value falls below $1, it is said to have "broken the buck" — a rare but consequential event. The most famous case occurred in September 2008, when the Reserve Primary Fund, holding debt of the failed Lehman Brothers, fell to $0.97 per share, triggering a run on prime money funds that required a temporary US Treasury guarantee programme.

Regulation

US money market funds are regulated under Rule 2a-7 of the Investment Company Act of 1940, which restricts the credit quality, maturity, and diversification of their holdings. Reforms adopted after 2008 — and implemented in 2016 — required institutional prime funds to adopt a floating net asset value and allowed funds to impose liquidity fees and redemption gates during stress. Further amendments followed the market turmoil of March 2020.

Money market funds are investment products, not bank deposits, and (outside exceptional guarantees) are not insured by the FDIC — a distinction regulators require to be disclosed to investors.
