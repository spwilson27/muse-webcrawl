A Roman province (Latin: provincia, pl. provinciae) was the largest territorial and administrative unit of the Roman Empire's foreign possessions, ruled by a governor appointed from Rome. Originally denoting a magistrate's assigned sphere of responsibility, the term came to mean a conquered territory administered as a unit. The system began with Sicily in 241 BC and expanded with Roman conquest until, at its greatest extent under Trajan in AD 117, the empire comprised some 46 provinces; under Diocletian they were subdivided into about a hundred smaller provinces grouped into dioceses.

Republican system

After the First Punic War, Rome annexed Sicily (241 BC) and Sardinia-Corsica (238 BC) and, rather than incorporating them as allies, administered them through annually appointed magistrates — the first provincial governors. As Rome expanded into Spain (Hispania Citerior and Ulterior, 197 BC), Macedon, Africa, Asia, Gaul, and the East, the Senate assigned provinces by lot to ex-consuls and ex-praetors (proconsuls and propraetors). Republican governors combined military command, jurisdiction, and tax supervision, and their virtually unchecked power abroad led to notorious extortion — prosecuted, sometimes, in the standing court de repetundis, as in Cicero's prosecution of Verres, governor of Sicily.

Imperial provinces

Augustus reorganised the system in 27 BC. Provinces were divided into imperial provinces — frontier and militarised territories governed by the emperor's legates (legati Augusti pro praetore), who commanded legions — and senatorial (public) provinces, peaceful interior territories governed by proconsuls chosen by the Senate, such as Asia and Africa Proconsularis. A third class, small equestrian provinces like Judaea and Raetia, were run by procurators or prefects. Egypt occupied a unique position as the emperor's personal possession, administered by an equestrian prefect and closed to senators without permission. Governors were salaried, served longer terms, and were more tightly supervised than their Republican predecessors, though corruption persisted.

Administration and society

Provincial government was thin: a governor with a small staff of quaestors, legates, and clerks depended on local city councils (the poleis of the Greek East and municipia of the West) to collect taxes, maintain roads, and run daily affairs. Provincial councils (concilia) organised the imperial cult and could petition Rome. Taxation took the form of land taxes (tributum soli) and poll taxes (tributum capitis), collected first by publicani under the Republic and later by local elites and officials. Over time, provincials entered the Senate and even the throne — emperors came from Spain, Gaul, Africa, Syria, and Illyria — and in AD 212 citizenship became nearly universal.

Late antiquity

Diocletian (r. 284–305) doubled the number of provinces by subdivision, separated civil governors from military commanders (duces), and grouped provinces into dioceses under vicars, who answered to praetorian prefects. This more bureaucratic structure persisted in the Byzantine East for centuries in modified form.
