Downwelling

Downwelling is the downward movement of fluid, in oceanography the process by which surface water sinks to greater depth. It occurs where surface currents converge or where winds push surface water toward a coast, and in the formation regions of deep and bottom water where cooling and brine rejection make surface water dense enough to sink. Downwelling is the descending limb of the ocean's overturning circulation and the counterpart of upwelling.

Mechanisms

Wind-driven downwelling arises from Ekman transport: winds blowing along a coast (or patterns of wind stress curl over the open ocean) drive surface water together, depressing the thermocline and pushing water downward — "Ekman pumping" in reverse. Downwelling also occurs at convergent fronts, at the centres of subtropical gyres, and seasonally where autumn storms and cooling densify surface water. At high latitudes, sea-ice formation rejects salt, producing cold, salty, dense water that sinks dramatically in the Nordic Seas and around Antarctica, forming North Atlantic Deep Water and Antarctic Bottom Water.

Effects

Downwelling carries oxygen, dissolved carbon dioxide, and heat from the surface into the deep ocean, ventilating the interior and sequestering carbon for centuries to millennia — a key part of the oceanic carbon store. Because it pushes nutrient-rich surface water away from the sunlit zone and replaces it with no input from below, downwelling zones are generally biologically unproductive, in contrast to the rich fisheries of upwelling regions. Changes in downwelling and deep-water formation, such as a weakening of the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, are a major concern in climate science because they affect heat transport, sea level, and carbon storage.
