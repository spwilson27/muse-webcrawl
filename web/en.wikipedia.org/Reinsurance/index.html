Reinsurance

Reinsurance is insurance that an insurance company purchases from another insurance company to insulate itself (at least in part) from the risk of a major claims event. With reinsurance, the insurer passes on ("cedes") some part of its own insurance liabilities to the reinsurer. The company acquiring reinsurance is called the ceding company or cedent, and the company issuing it is the reinsurer.

Functions

Reinsurance allows insurers to increase their underwriting capacity, stabilise results by smoothing large losses, obtain catastrophe protection, and gain access to the reinsurer's expertise and capital. It is central to the solvency management of insurers under regimes such as Solvency II in Europe.

Types

Facultative reinsurance is negotiated for a single specific risk or policy, while treaty reinsurance covers a whole portfolio of policies automatically. Under proportional (pro rata) treaties, the reinsurer takes a fixed share of premiums and losses, often paying a ceding commission. Under non-proportional treaties, such as excess-of-loss cover, the reinsurer pays only when losses exceed an agreed retention (attachment point). A reinsurer may itself buy reinsurance, known as retrocession.

History

The earliest reinsurance contracts date to the 14th century in marine insurance. The Reinsurance Company of Cologne (Kölnische Rückversicherungsgesellschaft), founded in the 1840s, is regarded as the first professional independent reinsurer; Swiss Re followed in 1863 after a major fire in Glarus, and Munich Re in 1880. The market is concentrated in hubs including London (Lloyd's), Bermuda, Zurich and Munich. Modern alternatives include catastrophe bonds and other insurance-linked securities.

Contracts and law

Reinsurance contracts rest on principles of utmost good faith and, historically, "follow the fortunes" of the cedent. Disputes commonly concern aggregation of losses, exclusions, and late notice; reinsurance arbitration panels are common in London and Bermuda practice.
