Substance monism

Substance monism is the philosophical view that there exists only one kind of substance, or—in its strongest form—only one substance altogether. It contrasts with substance dualism, which holds that mind and matter are two distinct substances, and with pluralism, which admits many.

Substance monism
Field: Metaphysics
Key figure: Baruch Spinoza
Contrasts: Dualism, pluralism

Spinoza

The most famous substance monist is Baruch Spinoza, who argued in his Ethics (1677) that there is exactly one substance—God or Nature (Deus sive Natura)—which is infinite and self-caused. Mind and body are not separate substances but two attributes under which the same substance is conceived.

Varieties

Materialism holds that the one substance is physical; idealism holds that it is mental; neutral monism holds that it is neither, with mind and matter both constructed from a more basic neutral stuff. Priority monism, defended in contemporary metaphysics, claims that the whole cosmos is the one fundamental object and its parts are derivative.

Criticism

Opponents argue that monism struggles to explain the apparent plurality and diversity of things and the distinctness of conscious experience.
