Nuremberg principles

The Nuremberg principles are a set of guidelines for determining what constitutes a war crime. The document was created by the International Law Commission of the United Nations to codify legal principles underlying the Nuremberg trials of Nazi party members following World War II. The principles affirm that individuals — not only states — bear responsibility under international law for crimes against peace, war crimes and crimes against humanity.

The principles

Principle I states that any person who commits an act which constitutes a crime under international law is responsible therefor and liable to punishment. Principle II holds that the fact that internal law does not impose a penalty does not relieve a person of responsibility. Principle III denies immunity to heads of state and government officials. Principle IV provides that acting pursuant to superior orders does not relieve responsibility, provided a moral choice was in fact possible. Principle V guarantees a fair trial. Principle VI defines crimes against peace, war crimes and crimes against humanity. Principle VII makes complicity in such crimes itself a crime.

Influence

The principles became foundational for modern international criminal law. They informed the Genocide Convention (1948), later ad hoc tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda, and the Rome Statute establishing the International Criminal Court in 1998. The rejection of the superior-orders defence and of official immunity remains central to international prosecutions.

Criticism

Critics have raised concerns about retroactivity — that the defendants at Nuremberg were judged by standards not codified when the acts occurred ("victors' justice"). Defenders reply that the underlying acts were already criminal under treaties and custom, and that the principles were subsequently accepted by the UN General Assembly.
