Sèvres syndrome

Sèvres syndrome (Turkish: Sevr sendromu) is a term used in Turkish politics and public discourse for the persistent belief that hostile foreign powers — and internal minorities acting as their proxies — are conspiring to weaken, divide, and ultimately partition Turkey. It is named after the Treaty of Sèvres of 1920, the abortive peace settlement that would have dismembered the Ottoman Empire at the end of the First World War.

Background

The Treaty of Sèvres, signed at Sèvres, France, in August 1920, awarded large parts of Anatolia to Greece, Armenia, and Kurdish autonomy schemes, placed the Straits under international control, and reduced Turkish sovereignty to a rump around Ankara. Mustafa Kemal Atatürk's nationalist movement repudiated the treaty, won the Turkish War of Independence, and replaced it with the Treaty of Lausanne (1923), which secured the borders of the modern republic. For many Turks the episode remains the defining proof that the West seeks Turkey's partition whenever it appears weak.

Use of the term

Scholars such as Fatma Müge Göçek have described the “syndrome” as a lens through which events — Kurdish separatism, Armenian genocide recognition campaigns, European Union accession conditions, Cyprus disputes, or foreign criticism of domestic politics — are interpreted as steps toward a new Sèvres. Analysts debate how far the concept explains Turkish foreign and domestic policy, and critics argue that politicians invoke it to discredit legitimate dissent as treason. The term is frequently contrasted with the official memory of Lausanne as national redemption.
