Neuroanatomy

Neuroanatomy is the study of the structure and organization of the nervous system. In contrast to animals with radial symmetry, whose nervous system consists of a distributed network of cells, animals with bilateral symmetry have segregated, defined nervous systems. Their neuroanatomy is therefore better understood.

Organization

In vertebrates, the nervous system is divided into the central nervous system, consisting of the brain and spinal cord, and the peripheral nervous system, consisting of cranial and spinal nerves and their ganglia. Neuroanatomy studies structures at scales from the whole brain and its major regions — cerebral cortex, cerebellum, brainstem, diencephalon and limbic structures — down to individual neurons, synapses and neural circuits.

Methods

Classical methods include dissection and staining techniques such as the Golgi stain, which revealed individual neurons and led Santiago Ramón y Cajal to formulate the neuron doctrine. Modern neuroanatomy uses microscopy, tract tracing, immunohistochemistry, genetic labelling, and in vivo imaging such as MRI and diffusion tractography to map connections — the connectome.

Terminology

Neuroanatomical directions use terms such as dorsal/ventral, rostral/caudal and medial/lateral, and standard planes (sagittal, coronal, axial). Latin and Greek-derived names dominate the nomenclature, codified in the Terminologia Anatomica.
