The Ethics is a philosophical treatise by Baruch Spinoza, published posthumously in Amsterdam in 1677 as part of his Opera Posthuma. Written in Latin and cast in the form of Euclidean geometry, the book develops Spinoza's doctrine that God and Nature are one substance, that mind and body are two attributes of that substance, and that human freedom consists in understanding the necessity of things.

Publication and reception

Spinoza withheld the work during his lifetime for fear of persecution; after his death in February 1677 his friends published it together with the unfinished Tractatus Politicus and other writings. The book was banned by the Dutch authorities in 1678 and widely denounced as atheist for more than a century, though its influence quietly persisted and it was later championed by Lessing, Goethe, and the German idealists.

Structure

The treatise comprises five parts treating, in turn, God, the mind, the affects, human bondage to the passions, and human freedom through reason. Its geometrical apparatus—definitions, axioms, propositions, corollaries, and scholia—makes it famously demanding; the scholia contain much of its most vivid and accessible prose.
