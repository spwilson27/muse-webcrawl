Satire

Satire is a genre of literature, and sometimes graphic and performing arts, in which vices, follies, abuses and shortcomings are held up to ridicule, often with the intent of exposing and shaming individuals, corporations, governments or society itself into improvement. Its principal devices are irony, exaggeration, parody, burlesque and juxtaposition. Although satire is usually meant to be humorous, its greater purpose is often constructive social criticism, using wit to draw attention to issues in contemporary society.

History

Satire is one of the oldest literary forms. Its roots lie in ancient Greece — the plays of Aristophanes and the figure of the satyr play — and it was codified in Rome by Horace, whose gentle, urbane mode became known as Horatian satire, and by Juvenal, whose savage indignation defined the harsher Juvenalian mode. The Roman term satura meant a medley or mixture, referring to the verse miscellanies of Ennius and Lucilius.

The tradition runs through medieval estates satire and Chaucer, Erasmus's In Praise of Folly, Cervantes's Don Quixote, and the great age of English Augustan satire: Dryden, Pope's The Dunciad, and Jonathan Swift, whose A Modest Proposal (1729) remains the canonical example of satirical irony. Voltaire's Candide, the novels of Dickens, and Mark Twain continued the form into modernity.

Modern satire

In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries satire flourished in new media: Orwell's Animal Farm, Heller's Catch-22, magazines such as Punch, Private Eye and the fake-news outlet The Onion, and television programmes from That Was the Week That Was to Saturday Night Live, The Daily Show and Charlie Hebdo. Satire directed at political and religious authority has repeatedly provoked censorship and controversy, and satirists have both been celebrated as defenders of free speech and prosecuted as offenders against public order.

Techniques

Satirists deploy exaggeration, incongruity, parody, understatement and the naive or unreliable persona; a frequent risk is that an audience mistakes the satire for sincerity — a phenomenon sometimes summarised by Poe's law for extremist parody online.
