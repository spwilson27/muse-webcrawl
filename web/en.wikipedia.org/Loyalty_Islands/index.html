Loyalty Islands

The Loyalty Islands (French: Îles Loyauté) are an archipelago in the Pacific Ocean, part of the French collectivity of New Caledonia, lying about 100 kilometres east of the New Caledonian mainland (Grande Terre). The province of the same name comprises the islands of Maré, Lifou (Drehu), Ouvéa and Tiga, together with smaller islets; its capital alternates between Wé on Lifou and Tadine on Maré.

Geography and people

The islands are raised coral atolls (makatea) with dramatic cliffs, caves and lagoons rather than rivers; fresh water collects in underground lenses. The indigenous Kanak inhabitants speak Melanesian languages including Drehu (on Lifou), Nengone (on Maré) and Iaai (on Ouvéa, which also has a Polynesian-speaking community descended from Wallisian settlers). The population is overwhelmingly Kanak, and the province is governed largely through customary chiefdoms.

History

British traders and missionaries arrived in the early nineteenth century — the islands' name is often attributed to the loyalty of the islanders to the British, or to the ship Loyalty. France annexed them with New Caledonia in the nineteenth century. The island of Ouvéa was the scene of the 1988 hostage crisis and the ensuing French military assault, a pivotal episode on the road to the Matignon and Nouméa Accords.
