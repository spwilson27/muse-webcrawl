Shivaji I (Shivaji Shahaji Bhonsale, c. 19 February 1630 – 3 April 1680) was an Indian ruler and a member of the Bhonsale clan. He carved out the Maratha Empire from the declining Adilshahi Sultanate of Bijapur, resisting the expansion of the Mughal Empire under Aurangzeb, and is revered in Maharashtra as a heroic folk figure and a symbol of Hindu resistance and regional pride.

Early life

Shivaji was born at the hill fort of Shivneri, near Junnar in present-day Maharashtra, to Shahaji, a Maratha general in the service of the Deccan sultanates, and Jijabai, whose religious devotion and stories from the epics deeply shaped the young Shivaji. Raised largely in Pune under the guardianship of Dadoji Konddeo, he learned administration and gathered a band of loyal companions from the Maval region.

Rise to power

Beginning in the 1640s, Shivaji seized a series of hill forts around Pune, including Torna, Rajgad and Kondana, exploiting the Sahyadri terrain with fast-moving light cavalry and guerrilla tactics (ganimi kava). His killing of the Bijapuri general Afzal Khan at Pratapgad in 1659 became legendary. He raided the Mughal port of Surat in 1664 and 1670, and in 1665 signed the Treaty of Purandar with the Mughal commander Jai Singh I, only to be placed under house arrest in Agra in 1666 — from which he famously escaped.

Coronation and administration

On 6 June 1674 Shivaji was crowned Chhatrapati at Raigad Fort, formally founding the Maratha kingdom. His administration featured the Ashtapradhan council of eight ministers, revenue reforms, a disciplined military, and a network of sea forts and a navy along the Konkan coast. He is noted for his relatively progressive policies, religious tolerance in governance, and respect for women.

Death and legacy

Shivaji died at Raigad in April 1680, likely of illness, and was succeeded by his son Sambhaji. The Maratha Empire he founded grew to dominate much of the Indian subcontinent in the 18th century. Shivaji remains one of the most celebrated figures in Indian history, commemorated in ballads, literature, and public monuments across Maharashtra.
