Monism

Monism is a philosophical view that attributes oneness or singleness to existence. Monism proposes that all existing things go back to a source that is distinct from them; for example, in some versions of monism, the whole of reality is held to consist of a single substance, a single principle, or a single kind of thing. It is contrasted with dualism, which holds that reality is fundamentally composed of two substances (classically, mind and matter), and with pluralism, which posits many fundamental kinds or entities.

Types of monism

Substance monism holds that there is only one substance. Baruch Spinoza's philosophy is the classic example: for Spinoza, God and Nature are one infinite substance of which minds and bodies are attributes or modes. Idealistic monism (or mental monism) holds that reality is fundamentally mental; Berkeley's idealism and some readings of Advaita Vedanta take this form. Material (physical) monism holds that everything is ultimately physical. Neutral monism, defended in different ways by Bertrand Russell and William James, holds that mind and matter are both composed of a more basic neutral stuff. Existence monism, a contemporary thesis associated with Jonathan Schaffer's priority monism, holds that exactly one concrete object — the cosmos as a whole — is fundamental, with everything else derivative from it.

History

Elements of monism appear in the pre-Socratic philosophers, who sought a single principle (archē) underlying the world — water for Thales, the apeiron for Anaximander, air for Anaximenes — and in Parmenides, who argued that Being is one. Monistic ideas are prominent in Hindu philosophy (especially Advaita Vedanta's doctrine that Brahman alone is real) and influenced German idealism. In modern analytic philosophy, monism remains a live position in debates about the mind–body problem, where monist theories compete with dualism.
