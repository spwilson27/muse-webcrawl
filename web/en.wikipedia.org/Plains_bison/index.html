The plains bison (Bison bison bison) is one of two subspecies of the American bison, the other being the wood bison (B. b. athabascae). Once numbering in the tens of millions across the Great Plains of North America, plains bison were hunted to near extinction in the 19th century, with only a few hundred animals surviving by the 1880s. Conservation efforts beginning in the early 20th century have rebuilt the population to roughly half a million animals, though most live in commercial herds and only a minority roam in conservation herds.

Description

Plains bison are slightly smaller than wood bison. Bulls stand up to 1.8 metres at the shoulder, supported by a massive hump of muscle over the forequarters, and weigh between 450 and 900 kilograms. Both sexes grow curved horns up to about 60 centimetres long. The shaggy, dark brown coat is much thicker over the head, neck and shoulders, forming a distinctive cape, while the hindquarters are comparatively lightly furred. Calves are born a reddish-orange colour that darkens within a few months.

Behaviour and ecology

Bison are grazers, feeding mainly on grasses and sedges of the open plains. Cows and calves live in maternal herds, while mature bulls are mostly solitary or in small bachelor groups outside the summer rut. During the rut, bulls bellow, wallow in dust, and clash head-on for dominance. Historically, plains bison migrated seasonally over hundreds of kilometres, shaping the ecology of the prairie through their grazing, wallowing and trampling. Their principal historical predators were wolves and grizzly bears; Indigenous peoples of the Plains hunted them sustainably for millennia, using nearly every part of the animal.

Near extinction and recovery

Market hunting, facilitated by railroads and encouraged as a strategy to subdue Plains peoples, reduced an estimated 30–60 million bison to under a thousand by 1884. A handful of ranchers and conservationists, including Charles Goodnight and James "Scotty" Philip, preserved remnant herds. The American Bison Society, founded in 1905, and reserves such as Yellowstone National Park — home to the only continuously wild, free-ranging herd in the United States — anchored the recovery. Today plains bison are raised widely for meat, and reintroduction projects have restored herds to tribal lands and public grasslands.
