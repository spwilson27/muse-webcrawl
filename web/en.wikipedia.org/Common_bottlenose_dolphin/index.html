The common bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops truncatus) is one of the best-known species of dolphin, familiar from marine parks, television, and coastal wildlife watching. It is a member of the family Delphinidae and is distinguished by its short, stocky beak or "bottlenose", curved dorsal fin, and robust grey body.

Description and behaviour

Adults typically measure 2.5 to 3.5 metres and have a fusiform body, a pronounced melon, and a falcate dorsal fin set midway along the back. Bottlenose dolphins are highly social, living in fission–fusion groups whose composition changes over hours or days; some coastal populations show strong site fidelity while offshore animals range widely. They are accomplished echolocators, using clicks generated in the nasal passages to find fish and squid, and communicate with whistles — each individual developing a distinctive "signature whistle".

Intelligence and human interaction

The species is renowned for its large brain, problem-solving, tool use (such as sponge-carrying by some Shark Bay dolphins in Australia), and capacity for learning trained behaviours. Dolphins have been kept in captivity since the early twentieth century, starred in the television series Flipper, and been trained by navies for mine detection. Wild populations face threats from bycatch in fisheries, pollution, noise, habitat degradation, and hunting in a few regions such as the Faroe Islands and Taiji, Japan.

Taxonomy

The genus Tursiops was long treated as a single species; the Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphin (T. aduncus) and the Burrunan dolphin (T. australis) have since been recognised by many authorities, and offshore and coastal ecotypes of T. truncatus may themselves represent distinct lineages.
