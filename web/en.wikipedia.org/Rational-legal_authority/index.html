Rational-legal authority

Rational-legal authority (also known as rational authority, legal authority, rational domination, legal domination, or bureaucratic authority) is a form of leadership in which the authority of an organisation or a ruling regime is largely tied to legal rationality, legal legitimacy and bureaucracy. It is one of the three types of legitimate domination in Max Weber's tripartite classification of authority, alongside traditional authority and charismatic authority.

Weber's account

Weber argued that most modern states rest on rational-legal authority: obedience is owed not to a person but to a set of impersonal, formally enacted rules and to the offices those rules define. Officials are appointed on the basis of qualifications, act within fixed jurisdictions, keep written records, and are separated from ownership of the means of administration. The purest administrative form of this authority is bureaucracy, which Weber regarded as technically superior — precise, quick, unambiguous and continuous — though he feared its "iron cage" effect on human freedom.

Characteristics

Key features include codified law, hierarchy of offices, professional training, impersonal application of rules, and legitimacy derived from belief in the legality of enacted rules. When a charismatic leader's movement survives, Weber predicted its authority would become "routinised" into traditional or rational-legal forms.
