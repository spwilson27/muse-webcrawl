Arithmetic progression

[Image placeholder]

An arithmetic progression or arithmetic sequence (AP) is a sequence of numbers such that the difference between any two consecutive terms is constant. This difference is called the common difference. For example, the sequence 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, ... is an arithmetic progression with a common difference of 2.

Formulas

If the first term is a1 and the common difference is d, then the n-th term is:

an = a1 + (n − 1)d

The sum of the first n terms (an arithmetic series) is:

Sn = n/2 · (a1 + an) = n/2 · (2a1 + (n − 1)d)

A famous anecdote tells of the young Carl Friedrich Gauss summing the integers 1 to 100 instantly by pairing them (1+100, 2+99, ...) into 50 pairs each equal to 101, giving 5050. The product of the terms of an arithmetic progression is given by a formula involving the gamma function; the arithmetic mean of any two terms equidistant from the ends equals the mean of the whole progression.

Properties and generalisations

Arithmetic progressions are a central object in number theory. The Green–Tao theorem (2004) states that the prime numbers contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. Van der Waerden's theorem guarantees monochromatic arithmetic progressions in any finite colouring of the integers. Generalisations include arithmetic progressions in several dimensions and polynomial ("generalised") progressions studied in additive combinatorics.
