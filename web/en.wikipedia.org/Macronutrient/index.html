Macronutrients are the categories of nutrients that organisms need in the largest quantities. In human nutrition, the principal macronutrients are carbohydrates, proteins, and fats, which together supply the body's energy (measured in kilocalories) and the raw materials for growth and repair. Water is also sometimes classed as a macronutrient because of the quantities required. They contrast with micronutrients — vitamins and minerals — needed only in small amounts.

A meal illustrating the three energy-yielding macronutrients: carbohydrate, protein and fat.

The three main macronutrients

Carbohydrates — sugars, starches and fibre — are the body's most readily used energy source, providing about 4 kcal per gram. Proteins, built from amino acids (nine of which are essential in the human diet), also provide about 4 kcal per gram and supply the structural and enzymatic machinery of cells. Fats are the most energy-dense macronutrient at about 9 kcal per gram, serving as energy stores, insulation, and carriers of fat-soluble vitamins, and providing essential fatty acids such as linoleic and alpha-linolenic acid.

Dietary guidance

Health authorities commonly express recommendations as Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Ranges: for adults, roughly 45–65% of energy from carbohydrate, 10–35% from protein, and 20–35% from fat. Fibre, technically a carbohydrate, is recommended at around 14 grams per 1,000 kcal consumed.

In other contexts

In plant nutrition and soil science, "macronutrients" refers to elements required in large amounts — nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and sulphur — as distinct from trace micronutrients.
