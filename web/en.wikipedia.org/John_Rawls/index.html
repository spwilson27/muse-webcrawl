John Rawls

Born: 21 February 1921, Baltimore, Maryland, U.S.
Died: 24 November 2002 (aged 81), Lexington, Massachusetts
Fields: Political philosophy, ethics
Institutions: Harvard University
Notable work: A Theory of Justice (1971); Political Liberalism (1993)

John Bordley Rawls (February 21, 1921 – November 24, 2002) was an American moral, legal and political philosopher in the modern liberal tradition. He is widely considered the most important political philosopher of the twentieth century, best known for his theory of "justice as fairness".

A Theory of Justice

Rawls's magnum opus A Theory of Justice (1971) revived normative political philosophy. It asks readers to imagine choosing the principles governing society from an "original position" behind a "veil of ignorance", unaware of their own place, talents or conception of the good. Rawls argued such deliberators would choose two principles: first, equal basic liberties for all; second, that social and economic inequalities are permissible only if attached to positions open to all and arranged to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged — the difference principle.

Later work

In Political Liberalism (1993) Rawls recast his theory to account for reasonable pluralism, introducing the ideas of an "overlapping consensus" and public reason. The Law of Peoples (1999) extended the framework to international justice. His Harvard lectures, published as Lectures on the History of Moral Philosophy, influenced generations of students.

Legacy

Rawls's work generated an enormous literature, including libertarian, communitarian and feminist critiques, and reshaped debates about distributive justice worldwide. He received the National Humanities Medal in 1999.
