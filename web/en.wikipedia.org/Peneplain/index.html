Peneplain

A peneplain is a low-relief plain formed by prolonged erosion. The concept was developed by the American geographer William Morris Davis as the final stage of his cycle of erosion, in which a landscape is worn down over immense spans of time to a nearly featureless surface close to base level (usually sea level).

Concept and history

In Davis's model, uplifted land passes through youth, maturity, and old age; in old age the rivers have graded their valleys and interfluves are lowered until only isolated resistant remnants — monadnocks — rise above the broad plain. The term combines the Latin paene ("almost") with "plain", signalling that a true peneplain is an ideal end-state rarely perfectly attained.

Criticism and modern views

Later geomorphologists, including Walther Penck and Lester King (who proposed the related pediplain), questioned whether landscapes remain stable long enough for peneplanation to occur, given ongoing tectonics and climate change. Extensive low-relief erosion surfaces — for example in Africa, Australia, and the Canadian Shield — are nonetheless widely interpreted as ancient peneplains, often later uplifted and dissected.
