Rivet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A rivet is a permanent mechanical fastener. Before installation it consists of a smooth cylindrical shaft with a head on one end; during installation the plain end is deformed ("upset") to form a second head, so that the rivet clamps the joined parts together. Riveted joints are strong in shear and were the standard method of joining structural steel and boiler plate before the widespread adoption of welding and high-strength bolting.

Types

Solid rivets are the oldest and most reliable form, still used in aircraft structures where their shear strength and fatigue performance are valued; they are set with a rivet gun and bucking bar, or by squeezing. Blind rivets ("pop rivets", patented in the 1930s) can be installed from one side only: a mandrel is drawn through the hollow rivet body until it breaks at a preset load. Other forms include semi-tubular rivets, drive rivets, split rivets, and flush (countersunk) rivets used where a smooth surface is required.

History

Rivets are known from Bronze Age metalwork and were used in Egyptian and Roman armour and construction. Hot-driven iron rivets held together the great structures of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — the Eiffel Tower contains some 2.5 million rivets, and the hull of RMS Titanic about three million. In shipbuilding and bridge work, riveting gangs heated rivets in portable forges and drove them with pneumatic hammers.

Applications

Although welding has replaced riveting in most heavy construction, rivets remain essential in aircraft assembly, where welded heat would distort thin aluminium skins, and in sheet metal work, electronics, leather goods, and the decorative studs of denim jeans.
