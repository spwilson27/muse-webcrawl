Prince William Augustus, Duke of Cumberland (15 April 1721 – 31 October 1765) was the third and youngest son of King George II of Great Britain and Caroline of Ansbach. A career soldier, he is best remembered for commanding the government army that defeated the Jacobite rising of 1745 at the Battle of Culloden in 1746 — and for the harsh reprisals that followed, which earned him the nickname "Butcher" Cumberland in Scotland.

Early military career

Cumberland served in the Royal Navy briefly before transferring to the army, where he fought under his father's command at the Battle of Dettingen in 1743, the last occasion on which a British monarch led troops in battle. As Captain-General of the British Army he commanded allied forces in Flanders during the War of the Austrian Succession, losing the Battle of Fontenoy in 1745 despite a celebrated infantry advance.

The Forty-Five

Recalled to Britain to face the Jacobite rising led by Charles Edward Stuart, Cumberland took command in Scotland in early 1746. On 16 April 1746 his army destroyed the Jacobite force at Culloden Moor near Inverness in under an hour. In the aftermath, government troops carried out punitive measures against the Highland clans — killings of wounded Jacobites, executions, and imprisonment — and Cumberland's name became reviled in parts of Scotland, while in England he was fêted as a hero and the flower Sweet William was popularly associated with him (in Scotland the same name is given to a weed).

Later life

Cumberland's attempt to reform the army after Culloden was curtailed by his defeat at the Battle of Hastenbeck in 1757 during the Seven Years' War and the subsequent Convention of Klosterzeven, which his father repudiated. He resigned his military offices and spent his remaining years in politics and horse racing; he founded the bloodline of the racehorse Herod. He died unmarried in 1765.
