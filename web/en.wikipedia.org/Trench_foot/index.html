Trench foot, also known as immersion foot, is a medical condition caused by prolonged exposure of the feet to damp, unsanitary, and cold conditions. It is a non-freezing cold injury: unlike frostbite, it occurs at temperatures above freezing (roughly 0–15 °C) when wet feet lose heat many times faster than dry feet, blood vessels constrict, and tissues are starved of oxygen and nutrients. The condition is named for its prevalence among soldiers in the flooded trenches of the First World War.

Signs and symptoms

Trench foot typically develops over days of standing in cold water or mud. The foot first feels numb and becomes pale or mottled; victims often describe it as feeling "wooden". On removal from the cold and rewarming, the hyperaemic phase follows, with intense pain, redness, swelling and sensitivity to touch and heat. In severe cases, blisters form, tissue dies, and gangrene can set in, historically leading to amputation. Survivors may suffer long-term nerve damage, with chronic pain, cold sensitivity and excessive sweating of the feet.

History

Comparable conditions were described during Napoleon's retreat from Russia in 1812 and in later campaigns, but the condition became notorious in the trenches of the Western Front in the winter of 1914–1915; by some estimates the British Army recorded tens of thousands of cases during the war — figures of around 75,000 British and 2,000 American casualties are commonly cited. Other armies, fighting in the waterlogged trenches of Flanders and in flooded shell holes, suffered similarly. Trench foot recurred in the Second World War, in the Korean War, and among Argentine and British troops in the wet cold of the Falklands War (1982). The term "immersion foot" was applied to shipwreck survivors whose feet had been immersed in cold sea water in lifeboats.

Prevention and treatment

Military authorities in the First World War eventually controlled the condition by enforcing foot discipline: daily changes of dry socks, greasing feet with whale oil or other fats, keeping feet raised, rotating troops out of flooded trenches, improving drainage and duckboards, and — in the British Army — making officers responsible for inspections, with trench foot treated in some units as a self-inflicted, punishable injury. Treatment involves gentle, gradual rewarming (avoiding direct heat and massage), elevation, pain relief and meticulous wound care; necrotic tissue may need surgical removal. Modern cases occur among homeless people, festival-goers and hikers in prolonged wet conditions, and prevention remains simply keeping feet dry and warm with regular sock changes.
