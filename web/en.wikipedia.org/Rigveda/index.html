The Rigveda (Sanskrit: ऋग्वेद, "praise knowledge") is an ancient Indian collection of Vedic Sanskrit hymns. It is one of the four sacred canonical Hindu texts known as the Vedas, and the oldest of them. Its early layers represent one of the oldest extant texts in any Indo-European language, composed in the northwestern region of the Indian subcontinent, roughly the area of modern-day Punjab.

Composition and dating

The Rigveda was composed orally and transmitted with extraordinary fidelity through mnemonic techniques for more than two millennia before being written down. Philological and archaeological evidence dates most of its hymns to roughly 1500–1200 BCE, during the early Vedic period. The text is organised into ten books, called mandalas. Books 2–7, the "family books," are the oldest core, attributed to particular families of poet-priests (rishis); books 1 and 10 are later additions, with book 10 containing famous speculative hymns such as the Nasadiya Sukta (hymn of creation) and the Purusha Sukta.

Content

The hymns praise deities such as Indra, the most frequently addressed god, a slayer of the serpent Vritra; Agni, the fire god and mediator between humans and gods; Soma, both a plant-based ritual drink and a god; Varuna, guardian of cosmic order (rita); and Ushas, goddess of dawn. The religion reflected in the text centred on sacrifice (yajna), in which offerings were made into the ritual fire accompanied by recitation.

Recensions and transmission

The Rigveda survives primarily in the Shakala recension (shakha). Its preservation relied on elaborate recitation patterns — including pada (word-by-word), krama (step-by-step) and more complex permutations — designed to prevent any alteration of the text. In 2007 UNESCO inscribed Rigveda manuscripts in its Memory of the World register. The standard scholarly edition remains that associated with Theodor Aufrecht and the commentary of Sayana, the 14th-century Vijayanagara scholar.

Significance

Together with the Samaveda, Yajurveda and Atharvaveda, the Rigveda forms the foundation of the later Hindu tradition, and its language — Vedic Sanskrit — is the ancestor register from which Classical Sanskrit, codified by Panini, developed. It is also a key source for the comparative study of Indo-European mythology and religion.
