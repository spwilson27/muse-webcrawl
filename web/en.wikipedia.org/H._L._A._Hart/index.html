Herbert Lionel Adolphus Hart (18 July 1907 – 19 December 1992) was an English legal philosopher and a major figure in political and legal philosophy. He was Professor of Jurisprudence at the University of Oxford and the author of The Concept of Law (1961), one of the most influential works of legal theory of the twentieth century.

Life

Hart read classics and philosophy at New College, Oxford, practised as a barrister in London, and worked for MI5 during the Second World War. Returning to Oxford, he taught philosophy before succeeding Arthur Goodhart in the chair of jurisprudence in 1952. He later served as Principal of Brasenose College.

The Concept of Law

Hart revitalised legal positivism, arguing that a legal system rests on the union of primary rules (imposing duties) and secondary rules (conferring powers), above all a "rule of recognition" by which valid law is identified, as accepted from the "internal point of view" of officials. The book criticised John Austin's command theory and Hans Kelsen's pure theory, while separating the question of what law is from what it ought to be.

Debates and other work

Hart's 1958 exchange with Lon Fuller on law and morality and his debate with Lord Devlin over the legal enforcement of morals — following the Wolfenden report — shaped liberal legal thought; his Law, Liberty, and Morality (1963) defended the Millian position. His student Ronald Dworkin became his most famous critic, while others, including Joseph Raz and John Finnis (in different directions), carried his project forward. A posthumous "Postscript" to The Concept of Law (1994) replied to Dworkin.
