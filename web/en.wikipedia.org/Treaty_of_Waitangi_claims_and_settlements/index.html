Treaty of Waitangi claims and settlements

Treaty of Waitangi claims and settlements are the claims brought by Māori against the New Zealand Crown alleging breaches of the principles of the Treaty of Waitangi, signed in 1840, and the negotiated settlements by which the Crown has apologised for and compensated those breaches. Since the 1990s such settlements have become a central feature of New Zealand public life.

The Waitangi Tribunal

The Waitangi Tribunal was established by the Treaty of Waitangi Act 1975 as a standing commission of inquiry. Originally limited to claims about contemporary Crown actions, it was given retrospective jurisdiction in 1985 to investigate claims dating back to 1840. The tribunal inquires into claims and makes findings and recommendations, which are generally not binding on the Crown except in certain cases involving state enterprise and Crown forest assets.

Negotiated settlements

The Office of Treaty Settlements (now Te Arawhiti) negotiates settlements on behalf of the Crown. Major settlements include the Waikato-Tainui raupatu settlement of 1995, the first major historical settlement, which included a formal apology and NZ$170 million in redress; the Ngāi Tahu settlement of 1998 covering most of the South Island; and the Tūhoe and Waikato River settlements. Settlements typically combine a Crown apology, cultural redress such as the return or renaming of significant sites, and financial and commercial redress. The 1992 Sealord deal settled Māori commercial fishing claims separately.

Criticism and debate

The process has been debated over the adequacy of redress relative to what was lost, the "full and final" character of settlements, and their mandating and ratification requirements. Successive governments aimed to complete historical settlements, and dozens of deeds of settlement have been enacted into law.
