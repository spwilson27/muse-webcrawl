Vision

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia · This article is about the sense. For other uses, see Vision (disambiguation).

Vision, or visual perception, is the ability to interpret the surrounding environment through photopic vision (daytime vision), color vision, scotopic vision (night vision), and mesopic vision (twilight vision), using light in the visible spectrum reflected by objects in the environment. This is different from visual acuity, which refers to how clearly a person sees (for example "20/20 vision"). A related problem is that of color blindness.

The major components of the visual system can be broken up into the eye, the retina, the optic nerve, the optic chiasma, the optic tract, the lateral geniculate nucleus, the optic radiation, the visual cortex and the visual association cortex. The resulting perception is also known as sight, eyesight, or viewing.

The eye and retina

Light enters the eye through the cornea and pupil and is focused by the lens onto the retina at the back of the eye. In the retina, photoreceptor cells — rods, which are sensitive to low light, and cones, which mediate color vision and fine detail — convert light into neural signals. The fovea, at the centre of the macula, contains only cones and provides the sharpest vision. Ganglion cells in the retina send their axons through the optic nerve to the brain.

Visual processing

Signals pass through the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus to the primary visual cortex (V1) in the occipital lobe. Processing is often described in terms of two streams: the ventral stream ("what" pathway), involved in object recognition, and the dorsal stream ("where/how" pathway), involved in spatial awareness and visually guided action.

History of study

Ancient theories included the emission theory of Euclid and Ptolemy, which held that the eye sends out rays, and the intromission theory developed by Alhazen (Ibn al-Haytham) in his Book of Optics (c. 1021), which correctly proposed that vision occurs when light reflected from objects enters the eye. Kepler later described the retinal image, and in the 20th century Hubel and Wiesel's recordings from visual cortex revealed feature-detecting neurons.
