Reification (Marxism)

In Marxism, reification (German: Verdinglichung, "making into a thing") is the process by which social relations between people come to appear as relations between things, and abstract concepts are treated as if they were concrete, independent entities. The concept develops Marx's analysis of commodity fetishism in Capital, where the social character of labour appears as an objective property of the products of labour themselves.

Lukács

The concept was made central to Western Marxism by György Lukács in History and Class Consciousness (1923). Lukács argued that under capitalism the commodity form becomes universal: human activity is fragmented, quantified and subjected to calculable, thing-like laws, producing a "contemplative" stance in which people confront social processes as a second nature beyond their control. Overcoming reification required, for Lukács, the class consciousness of the proletariat, which could grasp society as a historical totality.

Later usage

Reification became a key theme of the Frankfurt School, informing Adorno and Horkheimer's critique of instrumental reason, and has been taken up more recently by theorists such as Axel Honneth, who recast it as a forgetting of recognition. The term is also used in philosophy of language and logic for treating an abstraction as a material thing.
