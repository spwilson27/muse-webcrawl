Pre-Socratic philosophy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The pre-Socratics initiated the Western tradition of rational inquiry into nature.

Pre-Socratic philosophy, also known as early Greek philosophy, is the ancient Greek philosophy that flourished before and during the lifetime of Socrates (469–399 BC). Its practitioners, working mainly in the Greek cities of Ionia (on the coast of modern Turkey), southern Italy, and Sicily, were the first in the Western tradition to seek explanations of the natural world in terms of impersonal principles rather than myth and divine agency. Their works survive only in fragments and in reports by later writers (the standard collection is Diels and Kranz's Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker, 1903).

Pre-Socratic philosophy

Period: 6th–5th centuries BC

Regions: Ionia, Magna Graecia, Sicily, Thrace

Central question: The archē — the origin and principle of all things

Main schools and figures

The Milesian school — Thales, Anaximander, and Anaximenes of Miletus — proposed a single material archē (originating principle): water for Thales, the indefinite apeiron for Anaximander, and air for Anaximenes. Heraclitus of Ephesus emphasised universal change, symbolised by fire and governed by the logos. Parmenides of Elea argued by pure reason that being is one, eternal, and unchanging, and that change and plurality are illusions — a conclusion his follower Zeno defended with famous paradoxes, and which later pluralists (Empedocles, with his four elements; Anaxagoras, with his cosmic Mind; and Leucippus and Democritus, with atoms and void) tried to answer while saving the phenomena. The Pythagoreans placed number at the heart of reality, and the Sophists of the later 5th century turned philosophy toward rhetoric, ethics, and the contrast between nature (physis) and convention (nomos).

Legacy

The pre-Socratics founded natural philosophy and, with it, the intellectual lineage leading to modern science: their search for unifying principles, their mathematical models of the cosmos (including early atomism and heliocentric speculation by Aristarchus's predecessors), and their practice of giving reasons and arguments shaped Plato and Aristotle, who treated them as foundational predecessors even while criticising them.
