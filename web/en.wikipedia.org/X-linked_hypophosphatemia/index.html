X-linked hypophosphatemia

X-linked hypophosphatemia (XLH), also known as X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets or vitamin D-resistant rickets, is an X-linked dominant form of rickets (osteomalacia in adults) that differs from nutritional vitamin D deficiency. It is caused by loss-of-function mutations in the PHEX gene on the X chromosome, leading to excess fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23), renal phosphate wasting, and impaired bone mineralisation.

Specialty
Endocrinology, medical genetics
Cause
PHEX gene mutation; excess FGF23
Inheritance
X-linked dominant
Symptoms
Bowed legs, short stature, bone pain, dental abscesses
Treatment
Burosumab; phosphate and calcitriol

Signs and symptoms

Children with XLH typically present in the second year of life with progressive bowing of the legs, short stature, delayed walking, and bone pain; craniosynostosis and spontaneous dental abscesses are also characteristic. Adults may develop osteomalacia, pseudofractures, enthesopathy (calcification of tendons and ligaments), osteoarthritis, and hearing loss.

Pathophysiology

The PHEX gene encodes a cell-surface endopeptidase expressed in osteocytes and teeth. Its inactivation raises circulating FGF23, which acts on the kidney to decrease phosphate reabsorption and suppress production of active vitamin D (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D), producing chronic hypophosphatemia and defective mineralisation of bone.

Treatment

Conventional therapy combines oral phosphate supplements with active vitamin D analogues (such as calcitriol), requiring careful monitoring for secondary hyperparathyroidism and kidney calcification. Burosumab, a monoclonal antibody against FGF23, was approved for XLH in 2018 and has become a standard targeted treatment for children and adults.

Epidemiology

XLH is a rare disease, with an estimated prevalence of roughly 1 in 20,000 to 1 in 60,000 people; it is the most common form of inherited hypophosphatemic rickets.
