Purgatorio (Italian: [purɡaˈtɔːrjo]; Italian for "Purgatory") is the second part of Dante Alighieri's 14th-century epic poem the Divine Comedy, following the Inferno and preceding the Paradiso. The poem relates Dante's journey up the Mount of Purgatory, guided first by the Roman poet Virgil and, at its summit, by Beatrice.

Structure

Dante's Purgatory is depicted as a seven-storey mountain island in the Southern Hemisphere, created by the displacement of rock when Satan fell. After an ante-Purgatory for the excommunicated and late-repentant, seven terraces purge the seven deadly sins — pride, envy, wrath, sloth, avarice (and prodigality), gluttony and lust — in reverse order to their gravity in Hell. Unlike the damned, the souls here suffer willingly and hope for Heaven; at the top lies the Earthly Paradise, the Garden of Eden.

Synopsis

Dante and Virgil emerge from Hell on Easter morning and are greeted by Cato of Utica, guardian of the shore. Ascending the terraces, Dante meets figures including the musician Casella, the ruler Manfred, the sculptor Oderisi, the poet Statius — whose conversion and release from the mountain cause it to tremble — and the troubadours Guido Guinizzelli and Arnaut Daniel. On the summit Beatrice rebukes Dante for his failings; he is immersed in the rivers Lethe and Eunoe, and Virgil departs, having led Dante as far as reason can go.

Themes

The cantica's central discourse, delivered by Marco Lombardo in Canto XVI, concerns free will and the soul's responsibility for evil. The Purgatorio is frequently considered the most human and lyrical of the three parts, meditating on art, love, politics and the nature of moral transformation.
