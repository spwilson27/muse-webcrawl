Electric generator

An electric generator is a device that converts motion-based power (mechanical energy) into electrical power for use in an external circuit. Sources of mechanical energy include steam turbines, gas turbines, water turbines, internal combustion engines, wind turbines and even hand cranks. The first electromagnetic generator, the Faraday disk, was invented in 1831 by British scientist Michael Faraday. Generators provide nearly all of the power for electrical grids.

Principle of operation

Generators work on Faraday's law of induction: moving a conductor through a magnetic field induces an electromotive force. In a typical machine, a rotating part (rotor) carrying field magnets spins inside a stationary part (stator) wound with coils, producing alternating current whose frequency is set by the rotation speed and pole count. Direct current machines use a commutator to rectify the output.

History

After Faraday's disk of 1831, Hippolyte Pixii built an early alternator in 1832. The self-excited dynamo of Werner von Siemens and others (1866–67) made generation practical, and the Gramme machine enabled commercial DC power. The development of AC systems by Westinghouse and Tesla in the 1880s–90s, using alternators driven by steam and water power, created the modern grid.

Types and applications

Generators range from tiny bicycle dynamos to the turbogenerators of power stations, rated at over 1,000 MVA and driven by steam from coal, gas or nuclear plants, or by falling water in hydroelectric dams. Standalone engine-generator sets provide backup power for hospitals and data centres, and portable generators serve construction sites and homes during outages. An alternator in a car charges the battery and powers the electrical system.
