The Palmer Method of business writing was a simplified style of handwriting instruction developed by Austin Norman Palmer in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. It became the dominant system of penmanship taught in American schools for much of the 20th century, displacing the more ornate Spencerian script.

Development

Palmer, a penmanship teacher from Iowa, founded the Western Penman magazine (1882) and the A. N. Palmer Company to market his instruction. His manual Palmer's Guide to Business Writing appeared in 1894, and The Palmer Method of Business Writing followed in 1901. Palmer criticised Spencerian penmanship, which relied on finger movement, as too slow and elaborate for commercial work, and advocated a freer "muscular movement" of the arm.

Technique

The method teaches students to write from the shoulder and forearm rather than the fingers, keeping the hand's muscles moving in smooth, rhythmic motions. Instruction proceeded through repetitive drills — ovals, push-pull strokes and connected letter forms — practised to a count or rhythm, before advancing to a plain, unshaded cursive alphabet designed for speed and legibility in business correspondence.

Spread and decline

By the 1920s the Palmer Method was used in schools across the United States, and the A. N. Palmer Company supplied copybooks, pens and teacher training nationwide. From the mid-20th century, rival systems such as the Zaner-Bloser method and later the D'Nealian script (1978) eroded its position, and cursive instruction itself declined with the spread of typewriters and computers.
