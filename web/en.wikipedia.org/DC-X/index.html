McDonnell Douglas DC-X

DC-X (Delta Clipper)
Manufacturer: McDonnell Douglas
Country of origin: United States
First flight: 18 August 1993
Status: Retired
Type: SSTO technology demonstrator

The McDonnell Douglas DC-X, short for Delta Clipper Experimental, was an uncrewed prototype of a reusable single-stage-to-orbit launch vehicle built by McDonnell Douglas in conjunction with the United States Department of Defense's Strategic Defense Initiative Organization (SDIO) from 1991 to 1993. Starting in 1994 the program was transferred to NASA.

Design

The DC-X was a one-third-scale prototype, roughly 12 metres tall, powered by four RL-10A-5 rocket engines burning liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen. It took off and landed vertically, and could pitch over to fly nose-first and glide, demonstrating both vertical and horizontal landing attitudes.

Flight tests

Between 1993 and 1995 the DC-X made a series of low-altitude test flights at White Sands Missile Range, New Mexico, demonstrating rapid turnaround — one turnaround took less than 24 hours. After transfer to NASA it was upgraded as the DC-XA ("Clipper Graham"), which in 1996 set turnaround records but was damaged when a landing strut failed to deploy and the vehicle tipped over and exploded.

Legacy

The DC-X demonstrated that vertical takeoff and vertical landing rocket operations were practical, pioneering techniques later used by Blue Origin's New Shepard and SpaceX's Falcon 9 first-stage recovery program.
