Social determinants of health

The social determinants of health (SDOH) are the non-medical conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age that shape health outcomes and health inequities. The World Health Organization defines them as the conditions of daily life — "the causes of the causes" of illness — driven by the distribution of money, power, and resources at global, national, and local levels.

Domains

Frameworks such as Healthy People 2030 group the determinants into five domains: economic stability (employment, income, poverty, food and housing security); education access and quality (early childhood education, literacy, higher education); health care access and quality (insurance coverage, primary care, health literacy); neighborhood and built environment (housing quality, transportation, safety, environmental exposures); and social and community context (social cohesion, discrimination, incarceration, civic participation).

Evidence

Research since the British Whitehall studies of civil servants has documented steep social gradients in health: at each step down the occupational hierarchy, morbidity and mortality rise, even among people with full access to medical care. Michael Marmot's WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health (2005–2008) reported that social inequalities in health arise from the unequal distribution of power, income, goods, and services, and called for action on the conditions of daily life, structural inequity, and measurement. Studies commonly estimate that clinical care accounts for only about 10–20 percent of modifiable health outcomes, with social and environmental factors accounting for much of the rest.

Policy responses

Approaches include "Health in All Policies" frameworks, community-level investment, screening patients for unmet social needs (food insecurity, housing instability), and partnerships between health systems and community organizations. Critics note measurement difficulties, the risk of medicalizing social problems, and debate over whether individual-level screening can substitute for structural change.
