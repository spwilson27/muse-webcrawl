Methods of detecting exoplanets

Any planet is an extremely faint light source compared to its parent star, so exoplanets are rarely observed directly; instead they are detected by their effects on their host stars. By the mid-2020s more than 5,500 exoplanets had been confirmed, the great majority by the transit and radial-velocity methods.

Transit photometry

When a planet crosses in front of its star, the star's brightness dips periodically by a small amount (about 1% for a Jupiter-sized planet around a Sun-like star, 0.01% for an Earth-sized one). This method, used by the Kepler and TESS missions, yields the planet's radius and orbital period, and accounts for the majority of known exoplanets.

Radial velocity

A planet's gravity makes its star wobble; the wobble is detected as periodic Doppler shifts in the star's spectrum. This method, which produced the first exoplanet found around a Sun-like star (51 Pegasi b, 1995), measures the planet's minimum mass.

Other methods

Direct imaging photographs planets by blocking starlight with a coronagraph, favouring young, distant, self-luminous planets. Gravitational microlensing detects planets through brief brightening of background stars. Astrometry measures a star's changing position, and pulsar timing detects planets around pulsars — the very first exoplanets found (1992) were of this type. Combining transit and radial-velocity data gives a planet's density and hence its composition.
