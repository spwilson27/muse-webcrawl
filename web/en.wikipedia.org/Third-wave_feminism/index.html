Third-wave feminism

Third-wave feminism is a feminist movement that began in the early 1990s, arising as a response to perceived failures of second-wave feminism and to the backlash against feminist initiatives of the 1980s. Scholars distinguish the third wave from earlier waves by its embrace of diversity, ambiguity, and individualism, and by its focus on the intersections of gender with race, class, and sexuality. Some sources date the movement from Rebecca Walker's 1992 article "Becoming the Third Wave" in Ms. magazine, written in response to the Anita Hill hearings.

History

The third wave emerged among women who had grown up with feminism as an established part of the culture — sometimes called Generation X feminists. Key early texts include Walker's anthology To Be Real (1995), while the punk subculture of riot grrrl in the Pacific Northwest spread feminist zines, music, and do-it-yourself activism.

Key ideas

Third-wave feminists sought to challenge definitions of femininity that they saw as rooted in the experiences of upper-middle-class white women. Drawing on poststructuralist thought, they treated gender identity as fluid and rejected essentialist accounts of womanhood. The concept of intersectionality, coined by legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw in 1989, became central: systems of oppression based on gender, race, and class were understood as interlocking rather than separable.

The movement also worked to reclaim derogatory terms, celebrate expressions of female sexuality previously criticized as objectifying, and expand feminism's agenda to include men and gender-nonconforming people. Use of irony, pop culture, and new media — later including blogs and social media — distinguished its style from earlier waves.

Criticism and legacy

Critics have questioned whether the "wave" metaphor accurately describes feminist history, and some charged the third wave with privileging individual empowerment over collective political change. From the 2010s, many commentators described a fourth wave characterized by online activism and campaigns against sexual harassment and assault, such as the Me Too movement.
