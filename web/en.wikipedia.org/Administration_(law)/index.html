Administration (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, administration refers to a procedure under insolvency law in several common-law jurisdictions, most prominently the United Kingdom, in which an insolvent company is placed under the control of an insolvency practitioner (the administrator) so that its affairs can be managed to achieve a better outcome for creditors than immediate liquidation—ideally by rescuing the company as a going concern.

United Kingdom

Administration in England and Wales is governed by the Insolvency Act 1986, as substantially reformed by the Enterprise Act 2002. An administrator may be appointed by the court, by the holder of a qualifying floating charge, or by the company or its directors out of court. Once appointed, a statutory moratorium prevents creditors from enforcing security or commencing legal proceedings without the administrator's consent or the court's permission.

The administrator must pursue a hierarchy of objectives: first, rescuing the company as a going concern; second, achieving a better result for creditors as a whole than liquidation; and third, realising property to distribute to secured or preferential creditors. In practice, many administrations involve a "pre-pack" sale of the business, agreed before appointment and executed immediately afterwards, a practice that has attracted both use and criticism for its lack of transparency.

Other senses

"Administration" is also used in other legal contexts: the administration of estates, in which an administrator (appointed where a person dies intestate) collects and distributes a deceased person's assets, in contrast to an executor appointed by will; and historically, letters of administration granted by probate courts. In Scotland, the equivalent insolvency concept developed along similar lines under UK-wide legislation, while other countries—such as Australia, which uses the term "voluntary administration"—operate comparable rescue procedures.
