Church-Turing thesis

In computability theory, the Church-Turing thesis (also known as the Church-Turing conjecture or Turing's thesis) states that a function on the natural numbers can be calculated by an effective method if and only if it is computable by a Turing machine. The thesis is named after American mathematician Alonzo Church and British mathematician Alan Turing, who independently proposed equivalent formalisms of computation in the 1930s.

History

The thesis arose from attempts to give a precise definition of "effectively calculable" in connection with Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem. Church proposed in 1936 that the effectively calculable functions are exactly the lambda-definable (equivalently, general recursive) functions, while Turing introduced his automatic machines and showed his model equivalent to Church's. Stephen Kleene later coined the name "Church's thesis" and the combined label "Church-Turing thesis."

Status

Because "effective calculability" is an intuitive notion, the thesis cannot be proved mathematically; it is supported by the fact that every independently proposed model of computation — lambda calculus, recursive functions, register machines, and many others — has turned out to define exactly the same class of functions. No genuinely computable function has been found that a Turing machine cannot compute.

Variations

The physical Church-Turing thesis extends the claim to physical processes, asserting that every physically realizable process can be simulated by a Turing machine. The Church-Turing-Deutsch principle, proposed by David Deutsch, underlies the theory of quantum computing. So-called hypercomputation proposals would, if physically realizable, refute stronger forms of the thesis.
