Confidence interval

In frequentist statistics, a confidence interval (CI) is a range of estimates for an unknown parameter, constructed so that, in repeated sampling, a specified proportion of such intervals — the confidence level, commonly 95% — would contain the true parameter value. The interval is computed from a sample of data and quantifies the precision of an estimate.

Interpretation

A 95% confidence interval does not mean there is a 95% probability that the parameter lies within the particular interval obtained; rather, the procedure that generated it succeeds 95% of the time in the long run. Noting this subtlety, intervals are widely used in scientific reporting as a more informative alternative to a bare point estimate or p-value, since they show both the estimate and its uncertainty on the scale of the quantity measured.

Construction

For a sample mean with known variance, an interval takes the form of the estimate plus or minus a multiple of the standard error, the multiplier coming from the normal or t distribution. More generally, confidence intervals can be obtained by inverting hypothesis tests, by likelihood methods, or by resampling techniques such as the bootstrap.
