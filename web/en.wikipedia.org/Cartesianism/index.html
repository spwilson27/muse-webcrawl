Cartesianism

René Descartes (1596–1650), founder of Cartesianism

Cartesianism is the philosophical and scientific system founded by the French philosopher René Descartes (1596–1650), developed principally in his Discourse on the Method (1637), Meditations on First Philosophy (1641) and Principles of Philosophy (1644). It dominated much of European thought in the later 17th century before being displaced by Newtonian physics and empiricist criticism.

Core doctrines

Cartesianism rests on several distinctive theses. Methodologically, Descartes proposed radical doubt and the search for indubitable foundations, beginning with the certainty expressed in cogito ergo sum ("I think, therefore I am"). Metaphysically, he defended substance dualism: reality consists of thinking substance (res cogitans) and extended substance (res extensa), whose essences are thought and spatial extension respectively. His physics treated matter as pure extension in motion, explained natural phenomena mechanistically through the contact of particles and great vortices of subtle matter, and denied the existence of a vacuum. Cartesian epistemology held that true knowledge comes through clear and distinct ideas perceived by reason, and that the existence of a non-deceiving God guarantees the reliability of reason.

Followers and development

Descartes's ideas were taken up — and modified — by followers such as Henricus Regius, Johannes Clauberg and the Oratorian Nicolas Malebranche, whose doctrine of occasionalism tried to solve the mind-body interaction problem: on this view, God is the true cause of all events, and apparent causal interactions between mind and body are occasions of divine action. Baruch Spinoza and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz both developed their systems partly in reaction to Cartesian dualism.

Criticism and decline

Cartesian physics was undermined by Newton's theory of gravitation, which replaced vortices with action-at-a-distance forces, and by the empiricism of Locke and Hume. The Church placed Descartes's works on the Index of Prohibited Books in 1663, and Cartesian teaching was periodically banned in Dutch and French universities amid theological controversy. Nevertheless, Cartesian dualism, the foundationalist project in epistemology, and Descartes's analytic geometry (the Cartesian coordinate system) profoundly shaped modern philosophy and mathematics; he is often called the father of modern philosophy.
