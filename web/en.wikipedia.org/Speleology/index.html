Speleology

Speleology is the scientific study of caves and other karst features, as well as their make-up, structure, physical properties, history, life forms, and the processes by which they form (speleogenesis) and change over time (speleomorphology). The term speleology is also sometimes applied to the recreational pursuit of exploring caves, which is more properly known as caving or potholing. The term is derived from the Greek spḗlaion ("cave") and logos ("study").

History

Édouard-Alfred Martel (1859–1938) is regarded as the father of speleology; his explorations of caves in France and elsewhere, including the Padirac Chasm, established cave exploration as a scientific pursuit. The International Union of Speleology, founded in 1965, coordinates research worldwide.

Branches

Speleology overlaps with geology, hydrology, and biology. Cave biology studies troglobites — organisms adapted to permanent darkness. Speleothems such as stalactites and stalagmites provide palaeoclimate records.

Famous cave systems

Mammoth Cave in Kentucky is the world's longest known cave system, with more than 670 km of surveyed passage. Krubera Cave in Georgia was the deepest known cave, exceeding 2,000 m in depth.
