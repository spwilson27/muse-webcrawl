Aztec codices

Form: Screenfold books on amatl (bark paper) or deerskin

Content: Calendars, history, tribute, ritual, cartography

Notable examples: Codex Borbonicus, Codex Mendoza, Florentine Codex

Aztec codices are Mesoamerican manuscripts produced by the Nahua peoples of central Mexico before and during the early Spanish colonial period. Written in the Aztec pictorial and glyphic writing system on bark paper (amatl) or deer skin, they recorded calendars, rituals, dynastic histories, landholdings, and the tribute owed to the Aztec Empire.

Pre-conquest codices

Only a handful of pre-conquest books survive, because most were destroyed during and after the Spanish conquest. The Codex Borbonicus, a ritual-calendrical manuscript, is the best known; the related Borgia Group of codices comes from the wider Puebla–Tlaxcala–Mixteca region.

Colonial codices

After the conquest, Nahua scribes (tlacuiloque) continued the tradition under Spanish patronage, often adding alphabetic Nahuatl or Spanish glosses. The Codex Mendoza (c. 1541) documents Aztec rulers, conquered towns, and tribute. Bernardino de Sahagún's bilingual Florentine Codex, compiled with Nahua informants, is an encyclopaedic account of Aztec culture. Census and land documents such as the Codex Santa María Asunción give detailed household-level records.

Content and script

The writing combines logographic and phonetic (rebus) glyphs with highly conventionalised images; place names, personal names, dates in the 52-year calendar, and quantities are precisely encoded, while narrative content relied partly on oral performance accompanying the images.
