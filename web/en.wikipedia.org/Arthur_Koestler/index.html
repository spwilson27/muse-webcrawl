Arthur Koestler (5 September 1905 – 1 March 1983) was a Hungarian-born British author and journalist. A former member of the Communist Party of Germany, he became one of the most prominent ex-communist intellectuals after publishing Darkness at Noon (1940), a novel about the Moscow show trials that became a classic of twentieth-century political literature.

Early life

Koestler was born in Budapest to a Jewish family and educated in Vienna, where he studied engineering without completing his degree. He worked in Palestine in the 1920s, then as a journalist in Berlin, joining the Communist Party in 1931 and travelling in the Soviet Union.

Spanish Civil War and Darkness at Noon

Covering the Spanish Civil War, Koestler was captured by Franco's forces and sentenced to death before being freed in a prisoner exchange. Disillusioned with Stalinism, he resigned from the Communist Party in 1938. His novel Darkness at Noon, following the interrogation of an old Bolshevik named Rubashov, was published in English in 1940 and became hugely influential during the Cold War.

Later work

After settling in Britain, Koestler wrote widely on politics, science and creativity, including The Sleepwalkers (1959), a history of cosmology, and The Act of Creation (1964). He contributed the lead essay to the anti-communist anthology The God That Failed (1949). Suffering from Parkinson's disease and leukaemia, he died by suicide with his wife Cynthia in 1983.
