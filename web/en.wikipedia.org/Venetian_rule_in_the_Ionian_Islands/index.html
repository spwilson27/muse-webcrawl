Venetian rule in the Ionian Islands

The Ionian Islands were ruled by the Republic of Venice from the late 14th century until 1797, a period of roughly four centuries that left a deep imprint on the islands' culture, architecture, language, and institutions. The islands — Corfu, Paxos, Lefkada, Ithaca, Cephalonia, Zakynthos, and Kythira — formed a strategic chain guarding the entrance to the Adriatic Sea.

Acquisition

Venice acquired the islands piecemeal: Corfu voluntarily submitted in 1386, and the others followed over the next century, with Lefkada conquered from the Ottomans in 1684. Unlike mainland Greece, the Ionian Islands never fell under sustained Ottoman rule, though they endured repeated Ottoman sieges, most famously the sieges of Corfu in 1537, 1571, and 1716 — the last repelled with the aid of Johann Matthias von der Schulenburg.

Administration and society

Each island was governed by Venetian officials (a provveditore) alongside local councils dominated by a Greco-Venetian aristocracy recorded in the Libro d'Oro. Society was stratified among nobles, burghers, and peasants. Venice encouraged olive cultivation and currant production for export. The population remained largely Greek Orthodox, tolerated by the Catholic Venetian authorities, while Italian became the language of administration and high culture, blending with Greek in everyday life.

Culture

Venetian rule fostered the flowering of the Heptanesian School of painting and music, fortresses in the Venetian style, and carnival traditions. Corfu town's fortresses and old quarter are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

End of Venetian rule

The Republic of Venice was dissolved by Napoleon in 1797, and under the Treaty of Campo-Formio the islands passed to France, beginning a turbulent period that led to the Septinsular Republic and eventual union with Greece in 1864.
