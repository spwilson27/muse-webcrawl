The Patriarch of Moscow and all Rus' (Russian: Патриарх Московский и всея Руси) is the primate and highest office of the Russian Orthodox Church, the largest autocephalous Eastern Orthodox church. The patriarchate was established in 1589, when the Metropolitan of Moscow was raised to patriarchal rank, making the Russian Church formally independent of the Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople, who recognised it as the fifth patriarchate in the Orthodox world.

History

The first Patriarch, Job, was enthroned in 1589 with the consent of Patriarch Jeremias II of Constantinople, who was visiting Moscow. The office grew in power through the 17th century, culminating in Patriarch Nikon, whose claims for the priesthood's superiority over the tsardom led to his deposition in 1666. In 1721 Peter the Great abolished the patriarchate and replaced it with the Most Holy Synod, a state-supervised council; for nearly two centuries the Russian Church was governed as, in effect, a department of state.

The patriarchate was restored by the All-Russian Church Council of 1917, which elected Metropolitan Tikhon as Patriarch amid the collapse of the monarchy. Under Soviet persecution the office was suppressed again after Tikhon's death in 1925 until Stalin permitted the election of Sergius in 1943. Since 1990 the office has been held by Alexy II (1990–2008) and, from 2009, Kirill. The Patriarch is elected for life by a Local Council of bishops, clergy and laity.

Role

The Patriarch presides over the Holy Synod and the Bishops' Council, represents the Russian Orthodox Church externally, and is diocesan bishop of Moscow. In the diptychs of the Orthodox churches the Moscow Patriarchate ranks fifth, after Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem.
