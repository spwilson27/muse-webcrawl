Capsizing

Capsizing or keeling over occurs when a boat or ship is turned on its side or upside down. The act of intentionally capsizing a small vessel is sometimes called turtling when the boat ends up fully inverted. Capsizing may result from broaching, strong winds, shifting cargo, flooding, or loss of stability when the vessel's center of gravity rises too high relative to its center of buoyancy.

Stability

A vessel's resistance to capsizing depends on its metacentric height and righting arm: as the boat heels, the center of buoyancy shifts and generates a righting moment. Beyond the angle of vanishing stability, the moment reverses and the vessel capsizes. Large ships can be lost when cargo liquefies or shifts, or when free surface effects in partly filled tanks reduce stability.

Recovery

Small sailing dinghies are designed to be righted by their crews standing on the centerboard. Self-righting lifeboats and some yachts can recover from a complete inversion. Kayakers use the Eskimo roll to recover without leaving the boat.
