Clausius–Clapeyron relation

The Clausius–Clapeyron relation, named after Rudolf Clausius and Émile Clapeyron, specifies the temperature dependence of pressure, most importantly in relation to phase transitions between two phases of matter. It relates the slope of the coexistence curve on a pressure–temperature diagram to the latent heat and volume change of the transition:

dP/dT = L / (T ΔV)

where dP/dT is the slope of the tangent to the coexistence curve, L is the specific latent heat, T is the absolute temperature, and ΔV is the specific volume change of the phase transition.

Derivation

Along a coexistence curve the Gibbs free energies of the two phases are equal. Differentiating this equality and using the thermodynamic identities for the differentials of the Gibbs free energy yields the relation, with the latent heat connected to the entropy change of the transition, L = T ΔS. Clapeyron originally proposed the equation in 1834; Clausius gave it a rigorous foundation in the kinetic theory and thermodynamics two decades later.

Applications

The relation is used in meteorology and climatology to describe how the saturation vapour pressure of water increases with temperature — roughly 7% per degree Celsius near Earth's surface temperatures — a key feedback in climate change. It also underlies the analysis of phase diagrams in chemistry and materials science, and, in integrated approximate form, the Antoine equation for vapour pressures.
