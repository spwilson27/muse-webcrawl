Interest group

Interest group
Also calledAdvocacy group, pressure group, special interest
MethodsLobbying, litigation, campaigning, public education
ExamplesTrade unions, business associations, environmental NGOs

An interest group, also called an advocacy group or pressure group, is an organisation that seeks to influence public policy in favour of a particular interest or cause. Interest groups differ from political parties in that they do not usually seek to win elections and govern themselves, but instead attempt to shape the decisions of government from outside. Examples range from trade associations and professional bodies to trade unions, environmental organisations and civil-rights movements.

Types

Political scientists distinguish sectional groups, which represent a specific segment of society (such as farmers, doctors or an industry), from promotional or cause groups, which advance an idea open to anyone (such as environmental protection or gun rights). Insider groups are regularly consulted by government, while outsider groups rely on public campaigns and protest.

Methods

Interest groups lobby legislators and officials, provide technical information and draft legislation, fund litigation, organise grassroots campaigns, and contribute to election campaigns — in the United States through political action committees (PACs) and Super PACs. In corporatist systems, major groups are formally integrated into policymaking; in pluralist systems, groups compete openly for influence.

Theory and criticism

Pluralists such as Robert Dahl argued that dispersed group competition prevents any single interest from dominating, while Mancur Olson's The Logic of Collective Action (1965) showed why small, concentrated interests organise more effectively than large, diffuse ones. Critics contend that wealthy business interests enjoy disproportionate influence, fuelling regulatory capture and unequal representation.
