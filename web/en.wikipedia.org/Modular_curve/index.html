Modular curve

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In number theory and algebraic geometry, a modular curve is a Riemann surface, or algebraic curve, obtained by quotienting the complex upper half-plane by the action of a congruence subgroup of the modular group SL(2, ℤ), and adding finitely many points at infinity (cusps). The resulting curve parametrises elliptic curves equipped with extra "level structure", which makes modular curves central objects of modern number theory.

Examples

The curve X(1), for the full modular group, is the familiar j-line: it is a sphere whose points classify elliptic curves up to isomorphism via the j-invariant. The curves X0(N) classify pairs of elliptic curves linked by a cyclic isogeny of degree N, while X(N) classifies elliptic curves with a full level-N structure. Each is defined over the rational numbers, so its rational points, genus and Jacobian can be studied arithmetically.

Role in number theory

The genus of X0(N) is zero for only finitely many N — a fact connected to the "monstrous moonshine" observations about the j-function. Mazur's celebrated torsion theorem was proved by studying rational points on modular curves. Most famously, the modularity theorem (formerly the Taniyama–Shimura conjecture) asserts that every elliptic curve over ℚ is parametrised by some modular curve X0(N); its proof for semistable curves by Andrew Wiles, completed with Richard Taylor, yielded the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. Heegner points on modular curves and the associated Gross–Zagier formula have been equally important in progress on the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture.
