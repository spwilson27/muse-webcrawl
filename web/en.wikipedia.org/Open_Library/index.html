Open Library is an online project of the nonprofit Internet Archive. Its stated goal is to create "one web page for every book ever published." The site functions both as a vast editable catalogue of books—anyone with an account can add or edit records—and as a lending library that lets registered users borrow digital copies of books for a limited period.

Catalogue

The catalogue aggregates bibliographic records from libraries and publishers worldwide and organizes them into a distinction between works (the abstract creation) and editions (particular published versions). It contains tens of millions of edition records and millions of author pages. Records use identifiers including ISBNs and the project's own OLIDs. The software is open source, and the catalogue data is released for reuse, in keeping with programmer and activist Aaron Swartz's vision of an open, wiki-like database of books.

Controlled digital lending

Open Library lends scanned copies of physical books owned by the Internet Archive and partner libraries under a model called controlled digital lending: one reader may borrow a given copy at a time, loans expire after a set period, and digital rights management restricts copying. During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, the Internet Archive temporarily suspended waitlists under a "National Emergency Library," a move that drew sharp criticism from authors and publishers.

Lawsuit

Four major publishers—Hachette, HarperCollins, Penguin Random House, and Wiley—sued the Internet Archive in 2020, alleging copyright infringement. In March 2023, a U.S. federal court ruled against the Internet Archive, holding that its scanning and lending program was not fair use; an appeals court affirmed the decision in 2024. The Archive subsequently removed large numbers of books from lending.

Accessibility

The platform includes a collection of books formatted for readers with print disabilities and DAISY-compatible files, and it links editions to their availability in libraries through services such as WorldCat.
