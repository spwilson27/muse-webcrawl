Collective unconscious

The collective unconscious is a concept in analytical psychology proposed by the Swiss psychiatrist Carl Gustav Jung. It refers to the part of the unconscious mind that Jung believed is shared by all human beings, containing universal structures called archetypes, in contrast to the personal unconscious, which contains an individual's own forgotten or repressed experiences.

Jung's theory

Jung developed the idea in essays such as "The Concept of the Collective Unconscious" (1936). He argued that myths, religions, fairy tales and dreams across widely separated cultures contain recurring motifs — the Hero, the Great Mother, the Wise Old Man, the Shadow, the Self — that cannot be explained by personal experience alone and must derive from inherited psychic structures. Archetypes are not inherited images but inherited dispositions or "forms without content" that shape how experiences are organised and imagined.

Criticism

The concept has been influential in literature, art, religious studies and popular culture, but it is controversial within psychology: critics regard it as unfalsifiable, note the lack of a plausible biological mechanism for inheriting acquired mental content, and point out that cross-cultural similarities may instead reflect common human environments and experiences, diffusion, or the universality of the human brain.
