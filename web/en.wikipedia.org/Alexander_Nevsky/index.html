Alexander Yaroslavich Nevsky (c. 1221 – 14 November 1263) was Prince of Novgorod, Grand Prince of Kiev and Grand Prince of Vladimir during the period of Mongol domination of the Rus' lands. He is celebrated as a national hero of Russia for his military victories over Swedish and German invaders — at the Battle of the Neva (1240) and the Battle on the Ice (1242) — and was canonised by the Russian Orthodox Church in 1547.

Saint Alexander Nevsky, depicted in a later Russian icon.

Early life and Novgorod

Alexander was the second son of Yaroslav II, Grand Prince of Vladimir. In 1236 the citizens of Novgorod invited the fifteen-year-old Alexander to become their prince and military leader. As the Mongols devastated the southern Rus' principalities in 1237–1240, Novgorod escaped invasion, but faced threats from the west.

Battle of the Neva

In July 1240 a Swedish expedition landed at the confluence of the Izhora and Neva rivers, intending to seize the trade route to Novgorod. Alexander attacked with his small retinue before the Swedes could entrench, winning a victory that tradition celebrated as divinely aided and that earned him the epithet "Nevsky" ("of the Neva"). Despite the triumph, Novgorod's boyars, fearing his growing power, drove him out the following winter.

Battle on the Ice

Recalled when the Livonian Knights — the German crusading order — captured Pskov and advanced toward Novgorod, Alexander retook Pskov and met the knights on the frozen Lake Peipus on 5 April 1242. In the "Battle on the Ice", his forces defeated the crusaders, checking German expansion eastward and securing Novgorod's western frontier. The battle became a cornerstone of Russian patriotic memory, immortalised in Sergei Eisenstein's 1938 film Alexander Nevsky, with music by Prokofiev.

Relations with the Mongols

Alexander judged resistance to the Golden Horde futile and pursued a policy of submission and diplomacy: he travelled repeatedly to the Horde, was confirmed as Grand Prince of Vladimir in 1252, suppressed anti-Mongol revolts in Novgorod, and enforced the Mongol census and tribute. Admirers credit this pragmatism with preserving the Rus' lands; critics note the cost of enforcing Mongol rule. Returning from his last journey to the Horde, he fell ill and died at Gorodets on 14 November 1263, having taken monastic vows.

Veneration and legacy

Peter the Great transferred Alexander's relics to the Alexander Nevsky Lavra in St Petersburg in 1724. Imperial Russia, the Soviet Union (which instituted the Order of Alexander Nevsky in 1942) and the Russian Federation have all honoured him; in a 2008 nationwide poll he was voted the greatest Russian in history.
