Climate justice

Climate justice is a concept that addresses the just division, fair sharing, and equitable distribution of the burdens of climate change and the responsibilities to deal with it. It holds that those who have contributed least to greenhouse gas emissions — including low-income countries, Indigenous peoples, and marginalised communities within wealthy states — often suffer the worst consequences, while those most responsible have the greatest capacity to act. The idea emerged from the environmental justice movement and now shapes international climate negotiations, activism, and litigation.

Principles

Climate justice frameworks emphasise historical responsibility for cumulative emissions, the polluter-pays principle, capability to pay, and equal per-capita rights to the atmosphere. In international law these ideas underlie the principle of "common but differentiated responsibilities", first codified in the 1992 UN Framework Convention on Climate Change, which assigns developed countries the lead in cutting emissions and providing finance.

Loss and damage

A central demand of climate justice advocates is support for "loss and damage" — harms from climate impacts that cannot be adapted to, such as sea-level rise swallowing island states. After decades of negotiation, countries agreed at the 2022 COP27 conference in Egypt to establish a dedicated loss and damage fund, operationalised the following year at COP28.

Activism and litigation

Climate justice is the rallying concept of much of the global climate movement, including youth-led school strikes inspired by Fridays for Future. It also informs a growing body of climate litigation, in which communities and young people sue governments and corporations, arguing that failure to act on climate change violates human rights and duties of care.
