Public domain

The public domain comprises all creative work to which no exclusive intellectual property rights apply. Those rights may have expired, been forfeited, expressly waived, or be inapplicable. Because no one holds the exclusive rights, anyone can legally use or reference those works without permission — for example, the works of Shakespeare and Beethoven, works of the United States federal government, and much early film and photography are in the public domain. Works enter the public domain differently in different countries, most commonly when copyright expires (in many jurisdictions, 70 years after the author's death).

Definition and scope

Works in the public domain under copyright law may still be affected by other rights — trademarks, patents, publicity rights — and some jurisdictions recognise perpetual moral rights. Ideas, facts, and short phrases are generally not copyrightable and are often described as part of the public domain in a broader sense.

Value and debates

The public domain is celebrated as a shared cultural commons enabling education, scholarship, and new creativity: James Boyle called it "our largest single subsidy" for creators who build on the past. Public Domain Day (January 1) marks newly freed works each year. Debates continue over copyright term extension, the restoration of rights in some works, and the enclosure of traditional knowledge; dedication tools such as Creative Commons' CC0 allow authors to waive rights voluntarily.
