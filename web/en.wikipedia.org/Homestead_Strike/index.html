Homestead strike

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Homestead strike, also known as the Homestead steel strike, Pinkerton rebellion, or Homestead massacre, was an industrial lockout and strike at the Homestead Steel Works in the Pittsburgh-area town of Homestead, Pennsylvania, between the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers (the AA) and the Carnegie Steel Company in 1892. The strike culminated in a pitched battle on 6 July 1892 between strikers and Pinkerton agents hired to secure the plant. The final result was a major defeat for the union, and a setback for efforts to unionise steelworkers nationwide.

Background

Carnegie's works manager, Henry Clay Frick, a determined anti-unionist, sought to cut wages and break the AA, then one of the strongest unions in the country, when its contract expired at the end of June 1892. Frick fortified the plant with a three-mile fence topped with barbed wire — workers dubbed it "Fort Frick" — and, after locking out the workforce, arranged for 300 Pinkerton detectives to be brought in by barge on the Monongahela River to reopen the works with strikebreakers.

The battle of 6 July

Forewarned, thousands of strikers and townspeople met the barges as they attempted to land before dawn on 6 July. A day-long gun battle followed; at least seven workers and three Pinkerton agents were killed, and many more wounded, before the outnumbered Pinkertons surrendered and were beaten and humiliated as they were run through a gauntlet of townspeople. Days later, Governor Robert Pattison ordered some 8,500 Pennsylvania National Guardsmen to occupy the town; under military protection the mill reopened with replacement workers, and many strikers were charged with murder, riot, and treason against the Commonwealth — though none were ultimately convicted of the most serious charges.

Aftermath

The strike collapsed by November 1892. On 23 July, anarchist Alexander Berkman attempted to assassinate Frick, who survived; the attack turned much public sympathy against the strikers. Carnegie Steel operated non-union for the next four decades, and steelworkers were not effectively organised until the Steel Workers Organizing Committee campaigns of the 1930s. The Homestead defeat, together with the Pullman Strike two years later, marked the nadir of the nineteenth-century American labour movement and hardened public debate over the use of private guards and state militias in industrial disputes.
