Megiddo (Hebrew: מְגִדּוֹ) is the site of the ancient city of Megiddo, whose remains form a tell (archaeological mound) in northern Israel near the modern Kibbutz Megiddo. Situated at the western entrance to the Jezreel Valley where the ancient Via Maris trade route passed through the Carmel Ridge, Megiddo commanded one of the most strategic crossroads of the ancient Near East and was fought over for millennia — giving its name, through the Hebrew Har Megiddo ("mountain of Megiddo"), to Armageddon, the prophesied site of the final battle in the Book of Revelation.

History

The mound was occupied from the Neolithic period onward and became a major Canaanite city-state in the Bronze Age. Around 1479 BCE Pharaoh Thutmose III defeated a coalition of Canaanite kings at the Battle of Megiddo, the first battle in history recorded in credible detail, whose account was inscribed at Karnak. In the Iron Age, Megiddo became an Israelite administrative and military centre with monumental palaces, stables (long associated with Solomon), and an elaborate water system tunnelled to a spring outside the walls. King Josiah of Judah was killed at Megiddo in 609 BCE confronting Pharaoh Necho II. The city declined in the Persian period and was eventually abandoned.

Archaeology

Excavations by the Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago (1925–1939) peeled back the mound in horizontal layers, identifying some twenty strata of occupation. Finds include the Megiddo ivories, a fragment of the Gilgamesh epic, temples, and the famous "Solomonic" gates — whose dating remains central to debates over the chronology of the United Monarchy. Renewed excavations by Tel Aviv University since 1994 have refined the site's stratigraphy and revealed a massive Early Bronze Age temple. In 2005 Megiddo was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site among the Biblical Tels.

Armageddon

Because of its history of decisive battles, Megiddo became in Christian eschatology the symbolic location of the end-times conflict. The name Armageddon appears only once in the Bible (Revelation 16:16), but the site continues to draw religious interest, and a notable Roman legionary base was discovered nearby at Legio, along with one of the earliest known Christian prayer halls.
