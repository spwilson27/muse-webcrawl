Proximate cause

In law and insurance, a proximate cause is an event sufficiently related to an injury that the courts deem the event to be the cause of that injury. It is one of the two elements of causation in tort law, alongside cause in fact ("but-for" causation), and it limits liability by excluding harms considered too remote, unforeseeable, or extraordinary to be fairly attributed to the defendant.

Tests

Common law jurisdictions apply several approaches. Under the direct cause test, a defendant is liable for all consequences that flow directly from the act, even if unforeseeable. Under the foreseeability test, associated with the American judge Benjamin Cardozo's opinion in cases following Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad, liability extends only to plaintiffs and harms within the reasonably foreseeable scope of the risk.

Intervening causes

An independent intervening act that breaks the chain of causation — a superseding cause — can relieve the original actor of liability, unless the intervening act was itself foreseeable. Examples include certain acts of third parties, acts of God, and abnormal responses by the victim.
