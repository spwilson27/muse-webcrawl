Topiary

Topiary is the horticultural practice of training perennial plants by clipping their foliage and twigs to develop and maintain clearly defined shapes — spheres, cones, spirals, animals or elaborate figures. The word derives from the Latin for an ornamental landscape gardener, topiarius.

History

The art dates to Roman gardens, described by Pliny the Younger, and was revived in Renaissance Italy and France, reaching spectacular formality in the gardens of Versailles. Dutch and English gardens of the 17th and 18th centuries favoured clipped yew and box; topiary later fell from fashion with the landscape movement, then returned in Victorian and Arts and Crafts gardens.

Technique

Suitable species are evergreen, densely branched and slow growing — box, yew, privet, holly and myrtle are classics. Plants may be clipped freehand, grown through wire frames, or trained over forms stuffed with sphagnum moss. Complex figures take years of patient trimming with shears.
