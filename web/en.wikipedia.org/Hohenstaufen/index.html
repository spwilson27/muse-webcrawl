The Hohenstaufen dynasty (also spelled Hohenstauffen, and known as the Staufer) was a noble family that produced several Holy Roman Emperors and kings of Sicily between 1138 and 1254. The dynasty takes its name from Hohenstaufen Castle on a conical hill near Göppingen in Swabia, built around 1070 by Frederick I, the first Staufer duke of Swabia.

Rise

The Staufer rose through marriage to the Salian imperial family: Frederick I of Swabia married Agnes, daughter of Emperor Henry IV. Their son Conrad III became the first Hohenstaufen king of Germany in 1138, defeating the rival Welfs. His nephew Frederick I Barbarossa (emperor 1155–1190) sought to restore imperial authority in Italy and Germany, fought the Lombard League (defeated at Legnano, 1176), and drowned on the Third Crusade. Barbarossa arranged the marriage of his son Henry VI to Constance, heiress of Norman Sicily, uniting the empire with the Sicilian crown.

Frederick II and the end

Henry VI (emperor 1191–1197) briefly made the empire Europe's dominant power. After the disputed succession and the reign of the Welf Otto IV, Frederick II (emperor 1220–1250) ruled from Sicily, where his court at Palermo dazzled contemporaries; his long struggle with the papacy and the Lombard cities consumed his reign. After his death the dynasty collapsed: Conrad IV died in 1254, Manfred of Sicily was killed by Charles of Anjou at Benevento in 1266, and the last Staufer, the teenage Conradin, was executed at Naples in 1268 after the Battle of Tagliacozzo — bringing the line to an end and precipitating the Sicilian Vespers a generation later.

Legacy

The Hohenstaufen left enduring monuments — the octagonal Castel del Monte in Apulia, rebuilt cathedrals and imperial castles across Germany and Italy — and an enduring myth: legend holds that Barbarossa sleeps in the Kyffhäuser mountain, waiting to restore the empire.
