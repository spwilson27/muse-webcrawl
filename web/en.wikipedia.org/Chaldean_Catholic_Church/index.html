The Chaldean Catholic Church is an Eastern Catholic particular church in full communion with the Holy See and the worldwide Catholic Church. It follows the East Syriac liturgical tradition, celebrating the Liturgy of Addai and Mari, and is headed by the Patriarch of Baghdad of the Chaldeans.

History

The church originates in the Church of the East in Mesopotamia. A lasting union with Rome followed the consecration of Yohannan Sulaqa in 1553, after disputes over the hereditary succession of the patriarchate. The line of patriarchs at Alqosh later entered communion as well, and in 1830 the two lines were united under Patriarch Yohannan Hormizd, establishing the modern Chaldean Catholic Church.

Population and diaspora

Chaldean Catholics historically lived in northern Iraq, south-eastern Turkey, and north-western Iran. Persecution — including the Assyrian genocide of 1915 and the violence that followed the 2003 Iraq War — drove large-scale emigration. Substantial diaspora communities now exist in the United States (notably metropolitan Detroit), Australia, and Europe.

Structure

The church is organised into eparchies (dioceses) in Iraq and throughout the diaspora, governed by a synod of bishops chaired by the patriarch. Its liturgical language is Syriac, a dialect of Aramaic, with vernacular languages used increasingly in diaspora parishes.
