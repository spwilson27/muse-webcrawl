Corn whiskey

Corn whiskey is an American whiskey made primarily from corn. Under United States federal regulations, it must be distilled from a fermented mash of at least 80 percent corn and, if aged at all, aged in used or uncharred new oak containers, so that it retains more of the grain's flavour than bourbon does.

Legal definition

The Standards of Identity for Distilled Spirits define corn whisky as whisky produced at not more than 160 proof (80% alcohol by volume) from a mash of at least 80 percent corn, and stored, if stored at all, at not more than 125 proof in used or uncharred new oak. Corn whiskey bottled straight from the still without aging is sometimes sold legally today as a clear spirit, resembling the traditional "white lightning" associated with illicit moonshine.

History

Corn was the cheapest and most abundant grain on the American frontier, and corn whiskey was the dominant spirit of the early United States, produced on farms throughout the Appalachians and the South. The Whiskey Rebellion of the 1790s arose in part from a federal excise tax on such spirits. Over the nineteenth and twentieth centuries bourbon — made from a corn-rich mash but aged in new charred oak — eclipsed straight corn whiskey commercially, though small and regional brands continued to be produced.

Distinction from bourbon

Bourbon requires at least 51 percent corn and mandatory aging in new charred oak barrels, which impart colour and vanilla and caramel flavours. Corn whiskey's use of used or uncharred cooperage keeps it paler and more grain-forward.
