Mitla is an archaeological site in the state of Oaxaca, Mexico, one of the most important centres of the Zapotec civilization. Located in the modern town of San Pablo Villa de Mitla, about 44 kilometres southeast of Oaxaca City, the site's Zapotec name, Lyobaa, means "place of rest" or "place of the dead", reflected in its Nahuatl name Mictlán, land of the dead.

History and architecture

Settled for many centuries, Mitla rose to prominence after the decline of Monte Albán around 750 AD, reaching its height between roughly 750 and 1521 as a major religious centre governed by a high priest. The site is unique in Mesoamerica for its intricate stone mosaic fretwork — grecas — in which thousands of precisely cut stones are fitted without mortar into geometric patterns covering the walls of halls and tombs. Its five groups of structures include the Group of the Columns, with a great hall whose roof was supported by six massive monolithic columns, and cruciform underground tombs decorated with carved lintels.

Later history

Mitla remained inhabited at Spanish contact; the sixteenth-century church of San Pablo was built atop one of its platforms using stone from the ruins. The area, including the nearby prehistoric caves of Yagul and Mitla, was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2010, the caves providing evidence of early plant domestication in the Americas.
