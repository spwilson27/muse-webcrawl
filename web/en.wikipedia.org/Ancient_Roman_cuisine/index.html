Ancient Roman cuisine

Ancient Roman cuisine refers to the food and cooking practices of ancient Rome, from the founding of the city through the late Empire. Roman food changed greatly over the centuries, expanding from a simple diet of grain porridge and vegetables to an elaborate cuisine incorporating ingredients drawn from across the Mediterranean world and beyond. Much of what is known comes from literary sources — above all the cookbook De re coquinaria attributed to Apicius — and from archaeological evidence including Pompeii's bakeries, amphorae, and food remains.

Meals

Romans typically ate a light breakfast (ientaculum), a small midday meal (prandium), and the main meal, cena, in the late afternoon or evening. For the wealthy, the cena became a lengthy banquet with multiple courses, eaten reclining on couches in the triclinium. Poorer Romans relied on food from street stalls and thermopolia, since many apartments lacked kitchens.

Staple foods and flavours

The staples were grain (wheat for bread, earlier emmer for porridge), olive oil, and wine. A defining flavour of Roman cooking was garum (and the similar liquamen), a fermented fish sauce used much like modern soy or fish sauces. Other characteristic seasonings included honey, vinegar, lovage, rue, cumin, coriander, and silphium — later replaced by its cheaper substitute asafoetida after silphium's extinction. Dishes frequently combined sweet and sour flavours.

Exotic dishes

Literary sources describe extravagant banquets featuring dormice, flamingo tongues, ostrich, and elaborate showpieces, though such accounts partly reflect satire of luxury. Elite imports included spices such as black pepper from India, dates, and wines from across the Empire.

Legacy

Roman cuisine laid foundations for later Mediterranean food: bread, olive oil, wine, cheese, and cured meats remained central. The standardization of the grain supply (annona) and the dole of bread to Rome's citizens were also lasting political institutions.
