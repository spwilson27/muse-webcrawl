Ergotism is poisoning caused by eating grain, especially rye, contaminated with the fungus Claviceps purpurea, whose dark sclerotia ("ergots") contain potent alkaloids such as ergotamine and ergoline derivatives related to LSD. In the Middle Ages, epidemics of ergotism — known as ignis sacer, "holy fire", or St Anthony's fire — killed tens of thousands of people in Europe.

Forms and symptoms

Two main forms are described. In gangrenous ergotism, the alkaloids constrict blood vessels; sufferers feel burning pain in the limbs, which become swollen, then cold, numb, and blackened by dry gangrene, sometimes separating without bleeding. In convulsive ergotism, symptoms include muscle spasms, seizures, hallucinations, mania, and crawling sensations under the skin (formication). Both forms can be preceded by nausea, vomiting, and diarrhoea. Pregnant women may miscarry, since ergot alkaloids stimulate uterine contraction — a property long exploited by midwives and later by obstetric medicine (ergometrine).

History

Outbreaks tracked wet summers and reliance on rye bread among the poor. A French epidemic of 944 AD reportedly killed some 40,000 people; further major outbreaks occurred in 1128–29 and repeatedly through the early modern period. The Order of St Anthony, founded around 1095, ran hospitals specialising in treating victims, and pilgrims attributed cures to the saint's relics. The cause was only firmly established in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. The last large European outbreak occurred in France in 1951 at Pont-Saint-Esprit, where contaminated flour caused hundreds of poisonings and several deaths, an episode still debated by historians. Sporadic outbreaks from contaminated grain have occurred in Ethiopia and India in modern times.

Ergot and the Salem hypothesis

It has been proposed (notably by Linnda Caporael in 1976) that the symptoms of the "afflicted" girls in the Salem witch trials of 1692 were caused by convulsive ergotism from rye eaten during a cold, damp season. The hypothesis is widely discussed but disputed, as the recorded symptoms and social patterning of the accusations fit poorly with known epidemiology of ergot outbreaks.
