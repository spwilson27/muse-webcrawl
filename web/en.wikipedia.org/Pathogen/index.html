Pathogen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bacteria are among the most familiar pathogens

In biology, a pathogen (from Greek πάθος, pathos, "suffering", and -γενής, -genēs, "producer of") is any organism or agent that can cause disease in a host. In the oldest and broadest sense the term covered anything that produced suffering; today it is used mainly for infectious agents: bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa, parasitic worms (helminths) and prions.

Types

Bacterial pathogens include the agents of tuberculosis, cholera, plague and strep throat; only a minority of bacteria are harmful, most being harmless or beneficial. Viruses, obligate intracellular parasites, cause diseases from the common cold to AIDS, rabies and COVID-19. Fungal pathogens such as Candida and Aspergillus mainly threaten immunocompromised hosts, though others devastate crops and amphibian populations. Protozoan parasites cause malaria, amoebic dysentery and sleeping sickness, while helminths — tapeworms, flukes and roundworms — infect billions of people. Prions, misfolded proteins, cause transmissible spongiform encephalopathies such as Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease without any nucleic acid.

Pathogenicity and virulence

Pathogenicity is the ability of an organism to cause disease; virulence is the degree of damage it does. Pathogens deploy virulence factors — toxins, capsules, adhesins and secretion systems — to invade tissues, obtain nutrients and evade the immune system. Opportunistic pathogens harm only hosts whose defences are weakened, whereas obligate pathogens can infect healthy hosts. Transmission occurs by direct contact, droplets and aerosols, contaminated food and water, fomites, and vectors such as mosquitoes and ticks.

Host defences and control

Hosts respond with barrier defences, innate immunity and adaptive immunity — the last exploited by vaccination, which has eradicated smallpox and controlled many other diseases. Antibiotics transformed the treatment of bacterial infections from the 1940s, though antimicrobial resistance is a growing threat. The scientific study of pathogens underpins epidemiology, public health and germ theory, established in the nineteenth century by Pasteur, Lister and Koch.
