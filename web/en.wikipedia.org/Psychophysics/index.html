Psychophysics

Psychophysics is the scientific study of the relationship between physical stimuli and the sensations and perceptions they produce. Founded in the nineteenth century by Gustav Theodor Fechner, whose Elemente der Psychophysik appeared in 1860, it is one of the oldest branches of experimental psychology and remains central to the study of vision, hearing, touch, taste, and smell.

Key concepts

Central measures include the absolute threshold, the smallest intensity of a stimulus that can be detected, and the difference threshold or just-noticeable difference (JND), the smallest change in a stimulus that an observer can detect. Ernst Heinrich Weber observed that the JND is a roughly constant proportion of the stimulus intensity (the Weber fraction), a relationship formalized in Weber's law. Fechner proposed that sensation grows logarithmically with stimulus intensity (Fechner's law), while in the twentieth century S. S. Stevens argued for a power-law relationship and developed direct scaling methods such as magnitude estimation (Stevens's power law).

Methods and theory

Classic methods of measurement include the method of limits, the method of constant stimuli, and the method of adjustment. In the 1950s, signal detection theory reframed threshold measurement by separating an observer's sensitivity (d′) from their decision criterion, showing that apparent thresholds depend on expectations and payoffs as well as sensory capacity. Psychophysical methods are widely applied in audiology, optometry, product design, and human–computer interaction.
