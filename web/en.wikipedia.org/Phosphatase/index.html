Phosphatase

In biochemistry, a phosphatase is an enzyme that uses water to cleave a phosphoric acid monoester into a phosphate ion and an alcohol. Because a phosphatase enzyme catalyzes the hydrolysis of its substrate, it is a subcategory of hydrolases. Phosphatase enzymes are the opposite of phosphorylases and kinases, which add phosphate groups to substrates.

Function

Dephosphorylation by phosphatases is a central regulatory mechanism in cell signalling: protein phosphatases remove phosphate groups added by protein kinases, switching signalling proteins on or off. Serine/threonine phosphatases (such as PP1, PP2A and calcineurin) and protein tyrosine phosphatases are the major families.

Classification

Phosphatases are classified by substrate, including protein phosphatases, nucleotidases, and sugar phosphatases. Alkaline phosphatase and acid phosphatase, distinguished by their pH optima, are widely used as diagnostic markers in medicine and as reporter enzymes in molecular biology.

Clinical significance

Abnormal phosphatase activity is implicated in cancer, diabetes and immune disorders; calcineurin is the target of immunosuppressant drugs such as ciclosporin.
