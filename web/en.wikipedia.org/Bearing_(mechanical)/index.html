Bearing (mechanical)

A bearing is a machine element that constrains relative motion to only the desired motion and reduces friction between moving parts. Bearings support rotating shafts and axles in machinery of every kind, from bicycle wheels to jet engines and wind turbines, and they may be classified by the type of motion they allow, the loads they carry, and the principles by which they operate.

Types

Plain bearings (sliding or journal bearings) support a shaft on a sliding surface, often with lubrication and low-friction materials such as bronze, babbitt, or polymers. Rolling-element bearings place balls or rollers between two races: ball bearings suit lighter loads and high speeds, while roller bearings (cylindrical, tapered, spherical, or needle) carry heavier loads. Fluid bearings support the load on a thin film of pressurized liquid or gas, and magnetic bearings levitate the shaft with electromagnetic fields, eliminating contact and wear. Flexure bearings allow limited motion through bending of a flexible element.

History

Sliding bearings are ancient—used with wheels and rotary querns—and wooden roller arrangements are described from Roman times. Leonardo da Vinci sketched ball-bearing concepts around 1500, and the modern ball bearing industry grew in the late 19th century with the bicycle boom and precision grinding.

Characteristics

Important properties include load capacity (radial, axial, or combined), speed limit, friction torque, stiffness, fatigue life, and lubrication requirements. Bearing life is commonly predicted statistically: the L10 life is the operating duration that 90% of a group of identical bearings is expected to survive under stated conditions.
