Kwantung Army

The Kwantung Army, Japan's principal field force in Manchuria.

The Kwantung Army (Japanese: 関東軍, Kantōgun) was a general army of the Imperial Japanese Army, formed in 1919 to guard the Kwantung Leased Territory and the South Manchuria Railway zone in northeastern China. It grew into the largest and most prestigious command of the Japanese Army, and its unauthorized actions — above all the staging of the Mukden Incident in 1931 — drove Japan's occupation of Manchuria and the creation of the puppet state of Manchukuo.

History

The force originated as the Kwantung Garrison, established after the Russo-Japanese War to protect Japan's leasehold at the tip of the Liaodong Peninsula and the railway zone running north to Changchun. In 1919 it was reorganized as the Kwantung Army. Ambitious staff officers such as Kanji Ishiwara and Seishirō Itagaki plotted the conquest of Manchuria; on 18 September 1931 they detonated a small explosive on the railway near Mukden and blamed Chinese troops, providing the pretext for a full occupation that Tokyo had not authorized but did not reverse. The Army thereafter dominated Manchukuo, ruling through the puppet emperor Puyi.

The Kwantung Army was the parent command of the notorious Unit 731, which conducted biological warfare experiments near Harbin. In 1939 it suffered a major defeat by Soviet and Mongolian forces at Khalkhin Gol (Nomonhan). By 1945, stripped of its best units for the Pacific war, it fielded perhaps 700,000 men of poor quality. In August 1945 the Soviet Manchurian Strategic Offensive Operation crushed it in under two weeks; hundreds of thousands of its soldiers were taken prisoner and sent to Siberian labour camps.

Legacy

The Kwantung Army became a byword for the insubordination of the Japanese officer corps (gekokujō) and for the militarism that led Japan into the Second World War in Asia.
