The Inuit are a group of culturally and historically similar Indigenous peoples inhabiting the Arctic and subarctic regions of Greenland, Labrador, Quebec, Nunavut, the Northwest Territories, and Alaska (United States) and Chukotka (Russia). Inuit languages are part of the Eskimo–Aleut language family, also known as Inuit-Yupik-Unangan. Inuit Sign Language is a critically endangered language used in Nunavut.

The word "Inuit" means "the people" in Inuktitut; the singular is "Inuk". The older term "Eskimo" is considered offensive in Canada and Greenland, though it remains in use in Alaska, where it may include the related Yupik peoples.

History

Inuit ancestors of the Thule culture migrated eastward from Alaska across the Canadian Arctic beginning around 1000 CE, displacing or absorbing the earlier Dorset culture and reaching Greenland by about 1300. For centuries they lived in small, mobile groups sustained by hunting sea mammals (seals, walrus, whales), caribou, and fish, using kayaks, umiaks, dog sleds, harpoons, and snow houses (igluit) in winter camps.

Contact and modern era

Sustained European contact — through whaling, the fur trade, missionaries, and traders — transformed Inuit society from the 18th century onward. In the 20th century, Canadian government policies forced settlement into permanent communities; children were sent to residential schools, families were relocated (including the High Arctic relocation of 1953–55), and sled dogs were culled. Land-claims agreements, such as the Inuvialuit Final Agreement (1984) and the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement (1993), led to the creation of the territory of Nunavut in 1999. The Inuit Circumpolar Council (founded 1977) represents Inuit internationally across four countries.

Culture

Traditional culture centres on kinship, sharing of country food, storytelling, drum dancing, throat singing (katajjaq), and arts including soapstone and ivory carving and printmaking, for which communities such as Kinngait (Cape Dorset) are renowned. Inuit today face challenges including climate change — the Arctic is warming several times faster than the global average — food insecurity, housing shortages, and language loss, alongside revitalisation efforts in language, governance, and cultural practice.
