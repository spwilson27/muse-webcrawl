Orbital precession

Apsidal precession makes an orbit trace out a rosette rather than a closed ellipse.

Orbital precession is the gradual rotation of the orientation of an astronomical body's orbit over time. The most commonly discussed form, apsidal precession, is the slow rotation of the line of apsides — the line joining the periapsis and apoapsis (closest and farthest points) of an elliptical orbit — so that the orbit does not close on itself but traces a rosette pattern.

Causes

In Newtonian mechanics, a body orbiting a perfectly spherical mass in isolation follows a closed ellipse, as stated by Bertrand's theorem for the inverse-square law. Real systems depart from this ideal: the gravitational pull of other bodies, the oblateness of the central body, tidal effects, and relativistic corrections all perturb the orbit and cause its axis to rotate. For the Earth, the Moon and Sun drive a lunisolar precession of the planet's axis, while the planets' orbits themselves precess under mutual perturbations.

Mercury and general relativity

The most famous case is the perihelion of Mercury, whose orbit precesses by about 574 arcseconds per century. Newtonian perturbations from the other planets account for 531 of these, leaving an anomalous 43 arcseconds per century unexplained — a discrepancy noted by Urbain Le Verrier in 1859. Albert Einstein showed in 1915 that general relativity predicts exactly this extra amount, one of the theory's first great confirmations.

A related phenomenon, nodal precession, is the rotation of the orbital plane itself, changing the line of nodes.
