Mind–body dualism

Mind–body dualism is the view in the philosophy of mind that mental phenomena are non-physical, or that the mind and body are fundamentally distinct kinds of substance. It contrasts with monism, which holds that only one kind of substance exists.

Cartesian dualism

The most famous formulation is René Descartes' substance dualism, set out in his Meditations on First Philosophy (1641). Descartes held that the mind (res cogitans, a thinking thing) is immaterial and indivisible, while the body (res extensa, an extended thing) is material and divisible. He proposed that the two interact in the pineal gland.

The interaction problem

The principal objection, pressed by Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia in correspondence with Descartes, is how an immaterial mind could causally affect a material body without violating the closure of physical causation. Later dualist positions include property dualism, which denies separate substances but holds that mental properties are irreducible to physical ones, and occasionalism and parallelism, which deny direct interaction.
