Nova Scotia (Latin for "New Scotland"; Scottish Gaelic: Alba Nuadh) is one of the thirteen provinces and territories of Canada and one of the four Atlantic provinces. It is the country's second-smallest province by area, but its most populous Atlantic province. Nearly surrounded by the Atlantic Ocean, the province consists of the Nova Scotia peninsula and Cape Breton Island, along with some 3,800 coastal islands.

History

The land has been inhabited by the Mi'kmaq people for thousands of years. French settlers founded Port-Royal in 1605, among the earliest European settlements in North America, and the region became the colony of Acadia. Control alternated between France and Britain until the Treaty of Utrecht (1713) ceded peninsular Acadia to Britain; Cape Breton Island followed after the Seven Years' War. In 1755 the British expelled much of the Acadian population in the Great Expulsion. Nova Scotia was one of the four founding provinces of Canadian Confederation in 1867.

Geography and economy

Nova Scotia's capital and largest city is Halifax, a major port. The Bay of Fundy, on the province's northwest coast, has the highest tides in the world. Historically dependent on fishing, shipbuilding, and coal mining, the modern economy includes offshore energy, tourism, agriculture, information technology, and a large public and defence sector centred on Halifax.

Culture

The province has deep Scottish, Acadian, Mi'kmaq, and African Nova Scotian heritage. It is known for Celtic music and fiddling, seafood such as Digby scallops and lobster, and landmarks including Peggy's Cove and the Cabot Trail on Cape Breton Island.
