Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a blockage of an artery in the lungs by a substance that has moved from elsewhere in the body through the bloodstream (embolism). Symptoms may include shortness of breath, chest pain that worsens with breathing, cough, and coughing up blood. Signs of a blood clot in the leg, such as a red, warm, swollen, and painful leg, may also be present.

Causes

Most pulmonary emboli arise from blood clots in the deep veins of the legs or pelvis, a condition known as deep vein thrombosis (DVT). Together, PE and DVT are known as venous thromboembolism (VTE). Less commonly, the embolus consists of air, fat, amniotic fluid, tumor fragments, or foreign material. Risk factors include major surgery, trauma, prolonged immobility, cancer, obesity, smoking, hormonal contraception, and pregnancy, as well as inherited thrombophilias such as factor V Leiden.

Pathophysiology

When a clot dislodges and travels through the right side of the heart into the pulmonary arteries, it obstructs blood flow to part of the lung. The blocked region is ventilated but not perfused, creating dead space and impairing gas exchange. A large embolus, or many small ones, can increase resistance in the pulmonary circulation enough to strain or fail the right ventricle — a major cause of death in massive PE.

Diagnosis

Clinical prediction rules such as the Wells score estimate the probability of PE. In patients with low or intermediate probability, a blood test for D-dimer can rule out the diagnosis if negative. Definitive imaging is usually by CT pulmonary angiography; ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scanning is an alternative when CT contrast is contraindicated. Electrocardiography and chest radiography are generally used to exclude other causes of symptoms.

Treatment

Treatment centers on anticoagulation with agents such as low-molecular-weight heparin, warfarin, or direct oral anticoagulants such as apixaban and rivaroxaban, typically for at least three months. Life-threatening ("massive") PE with low blood pressure may be treated with thrombolytic drugs to dissolve the clot, catheter-directed therapy, or surgical embolectomy. An inferior vena cava filter may be placed when anticoagulation is impossible.

Epidemiology

About 300,000 people die of pulmonary embolism each year in the United States, and the condition is responsible for roughly 100,000 deaths annually among hospitalized patients in some estimates. The incidence rises sharply with age. Untreated, mortality has been estimated at around 30 percent, falling to under 10 percent with prompt anticoagulation.
