Kīlauea is an active shield volcano in the Hawaiian Islands, located on the southeastern flank of Mauna Loa on the island of Hawaiʻi. One of the world's most active volcanoes, it is the youngest and most active of the five volcanoes that form the island, and is protected within Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park. In Hawaiian tradition it is the home of the volcano goddess Pele.

Eruptive history

Kīlauea erupted nearly continuously from 1983 to 2018 at the Puʻu ʻŌʻō vent on its East Rift Zone — one of the longest recorded eruptions. In 2018 a major rift eruption in the lower Puna district destroyed more than 700 homes, while the summit caldera collapsed dramatically and the long-standing lava lake in Halemaʻumaʻu crater drained. After a period of quiet, summit eruptions resumed in December 2020, with repeated episodes of lava-lake activity in Halemaʻumaʻu in subsequent years.

Geology

Like all Hawaiian volcanoes, Kīlauea is fed by a mantle hotspot. Its fluid basaltic lava produces broad, gently sloping shield morphology. Gases such as sulfur dioxide create volcanic smog ("vog"). The volcano is monitored by the Hawaiian Volcano Observatory, founded in 1912 on its rim.
