Buddhahood (Sanskrit: buddhatva; Pali: buddhatta) is the state of an awakened being who, having discovered the path out of suffering (duḥkha), freely teaches it to others so they may be liberated from saṃsāra, the cycle of death and rebirth. A Buddha is one who has attained Buddhahood without the benefit of a teacher's dharma in the present lifetime, having rediscovered the truths — above all the Four Noble Truths and dependent origination — by their own effort.

Nature of a Buddha

In Buddhist doctrine a Buddha is characterised by complete awakening: the destruction of greed, hatred and delusion, perfect wisdom (prajñā) and perfect compassion (karuṇā). Classical texts enumerate special qualities of Buddhas, such as the thirty-two marks of a great man, the ten powers, and the four confidences. A Buddha is no longer subject to rebirth; upon death they enter parinirvāṇa.

Paths to Buddhahood

In Theravāda, the historical Buddha, Siddhartha Gautama (Shakyamuni), perfected himself over countless lives as a bodhisatta, fulfilling the perfections (pāramī); the ideal for most practitioners is arahantship, while Buddhahood is the rarest of destinies, reserved for those who vow to teach a rediscovered dharma in an age when it has been lost. In Mahāyāna, the bodhisattva path to Buddhahood is presented as the universal ideal: practitioners generate bodhicitta, practise the six perfections across the bodhisattva stages (bhūmis), and vow to liberate all beings. Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna doctrines of the trikāya (three bodies of a Buddha) and of an innate Buddha-nature (tathāgatagarbha) teach that Buddhahood is in some sense the true nature of all beings.

Buddhas in the traditions

Besides Shakyamuni, traditions venerate past Buddhas (such as Kāśyapa and the twenty-eight Buddhas of the Buddhavaṃsa), the future Buddha Maitreya, and, in Mahāyāna, cosmic Buddhas such as Amitābha and the Medicine Buddha, alongside celestial bodhisattvas.
