Economic calculation problem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The economic calculation problem is a criticism of using central economic planning as a substitute for market-based allocation of the factors of production. It was first proposed by Ludwig von Mises in his 1920 article "Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth" and later expanded upon by Friedrich Hayek.

Ludwig von Mises

In his analysis, Mises argued that without a market in which prices for capital goods are formed through voluntary exchange, a central planner has no rational way to compare the costs of alternative production plans. Money prices for consumer goods alone cannot convey the relative scarcity of the means of production, because in a socialist economy those means are owned in common and never actually exchanged. Rational economic calculation — deciding, for example, whether to build a bridge out of steel or stone, or to invest in one production process rather than another — would therefore be impossible beyond a small scale.

Background

Mises's 1920 article opened the socialist calculation debate. Hayek later developed the argument in terms of knowledge: prices act as signals that aggregate dispersed information held by millions of individuals, information that no central authority could collect and process in full (the "knowledge problem").

Responses

Supporters of planning, notably Oskar R. Lange and Abba Lerner, proposed a model of market socialism in which planners adjust prices by trial and error in response to shortages and surpluses, mimicking a competitive market. Other responses included proposals for calculation in kind (using physical units rather than prices), and later suggestions that advances in computing might make labour-time or optimisation-based planning feasible. Critics of the calculation problem reply that such schemes either reintroduce market prices in substance or fail to capture entrepreneurial discovery and changing consumer preferences.

Significance

The collapse of the Soviet Union and the Eastern Bloc planned economies in 1989–1991 led many economists to regard the calculation debate as having been largely settled in Mises's and Hayek's favour, although debate continues on the degree to which calculation difficulties, as opposed to incentive and political problems, explain the failures of central planning.
