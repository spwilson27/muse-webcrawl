Church–Turing thesis

FieldComputability theory
Proposed1930s
Key figuresAlonzo Church, Alan Turing

In computability theory, the Church–Turing thesis (also known as the Church thesis, Turing's thesis, or the Church–Turing conjecture) is a hypothesis about the nature of computable functions. It states that a function on the natural numbers can be calculated by an effective method if and only if it is computable by a Turing machine.

History

The thesis arose from attempts to make precise the intuitive notion of an "effective method" or algorithm. In 1936 Alonzo Church proposed identifying effectively calculable functions with the λ-definable functions of his lambda calculus, and Alan Turing independently proposed his computing machines. Kurt Gödel's general recursive functions were shown to be equivalent to both formalisms, and dozens of later models — register machines, Post systems, Markov algorithms — all turned out to define the same class of functions.

Statement and status

The thesis is not a theorem that can be proved, because it equates an informal notion (effective calculability) with a formal one (Turing computability). Rather, it is supported by the extensive equivalence results and by the absence, despite much effort, of any counterexample: no function widely accepted as effectively calculable has been shown not to be Turing-computable.

Variants

The physical Church–Turing thesis extends the claim to physical processes: every function that can be computed by any physical system can be computed by a Turing machine. The extended (or strong) Church–Turing thesis claims that any physically realisable computational device can be simulated efficiently (in polynomial time) by a probabilistic Turing machine; quantum computing has put pressure on this efficiency version, though not on the original thesis about computability itself.
