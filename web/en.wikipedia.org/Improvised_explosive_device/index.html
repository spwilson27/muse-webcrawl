Improvised explosive device

An improvised explosive device (IED) is a bomb constructed and deployed in ways other than in conventional military action, typically assembled from military ordnance, commercial explosives, or homemade explosive materials combined with an improvised initiation system. IEDs are used by insurgents, terrorists, and other non-state actors, and have also been employed by conventional forces in extremis.

Types

IEDs may be command-initiated (by wire or radio signal, including mobile phones), victim-operated (pressure plates, tripwires), time-delayed, or vehicle-borne (VBIEDs); suicide bombers carry person-borne devices. Roadside bombs were the signature example in the Iraq and Afghanistan wars. Some incorporate explosively formed penetrators capable of defeating armour.

Use in recent conflicts

IEDs caused the majority of coalition casualties in Iraq after 2003 and in Afghanistan. Their use prompted massive counter-IED investment: armoured MRAP vehicles, jammers, route-clearance robots, and dedicated organisations such as the US Joint Improvised Explosive Device Defeat Organization (JIEDDO, later JIDA).

Historical antecedents

Precursors include the booby traps and homemade bombs of earlier guerrilla wars, the devices of the Irish Republican Army, and Viet Cong booby traps. Civilian harm from IEDs remains a major concern in post-conflict regions.
