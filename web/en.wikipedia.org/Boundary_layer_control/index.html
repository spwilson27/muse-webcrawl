Boundary layer control

Field: Aerodynamics
Techniques: Blowing, suction, vortex generators
Goal: Delay flow separation, increase lift, reduce drag

Boundary layer control refers to methods of controlling the behaviour of fluid boundary layers in aerodynamics — the thin layer of air adjacent to a surface such as an aircraft wing. Its practical goals are to delay or prevent flow separation, increase lift, reduce drag and improve control effectiveness.

Methods

Techniques include blown flaps, in which high-energy air (often bled from jet engines) is blown over a wing or flap; suction, in which the low-energy boundary layer is sucked away through slots or porous surfaces; and passive devices such as vortex generators, small vanes that mix high-energy air into the boundary layer.

Applications

Boundary layer control has been used to shorten take-off and landing distances in STOL aircraft, to maintain laminar flow for drag reduction, and to keep flow attached at high angles of attack. Notable users included the Blackburn Buccaneer and various experimental laminar-flow aircraft. Complexity and maintenance costs have limited widespread adoption.
