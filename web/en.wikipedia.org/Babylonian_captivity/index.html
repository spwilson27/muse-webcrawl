"By the rivers of Babylon" — the exiles in Psalm 137 lament Jerusalem in captivity.

The Babylonian captivity or Babylonian exile was the period in Jewish history during which a large number of Judeans from the ancient Kingdom of Judah were taken captive by the Neo-Babylonian Empire under Nebuchadnezzar II and deported to Babylonia. The deportations occurred in waves, principally in 597 BCE, when King Jehoiachin and much of the court and skilled population were taken, and after the final siege and destruction of Jerusalem and Solomon's Temple in 587/586 BCE, when King Zedekiah was captured and blinded.

The exile

The exiles were settled in communities along the rivers and canals of Babylonia, such as Tel Abib on the Chebar canal, where the prophet Ezekiel ministered. Babylonian ration tablets naming "Yaukin, king of Judah" (Jehoiachin) and his sons corroborate the biblical account of the captive court. Cut off from the Temple cult, the exiles developed institutions that proved durable: emphasis on Sabbath, circumcision and dietary law, communal prayer and the study of ancestral traditions. Much of the Hebrew Bible was edited or composed during or in response to this period, and psalms such as Psalm 137 ("By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down and wept") express the exiles' grief.

Return

In 539 BCE Cyrus the Great of Persia conquered Babylon. His edict, dated to about 538 BCE and reflected in the Book of Ezra, permitted the exiles to return to Judah and rebuild the Temple. Successive waves returned under leaders such as Sheshbazzar and Zerubbabel; the Second Temple was completed in 515 BCE. Many Judeans nevertheless remained in Babylonia, forming a diaspora community that endured for over two millennia and later produced the Babylonian Talmud.

Significance

The exile is regarded as the crucible of biblical Judaism: monotheism was sharpened, the Torah traditions were consolidated, and Judaism became a portable religion of text and practice rather than one bound to a single sanctuary and king.
