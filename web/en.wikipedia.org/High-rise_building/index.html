A high-rise building is a tall building, as opposed to a low-rise or mid-rise building; the exact threshold varies by jurisdiction and context. Building codes and fire services commonly define a high-rise as a building at least 23 metres (75 feet) tall, or about seven to ten storeys — the height beyond which fires cannot be fought from the ground. The Emporis standard classed buildings of 35 metres or twelve storeys and above as high-rises, while buildings over 100–150 metres are termed skyscrapers.

History

Tall multi-storey structures existed in antiquity — Roman insulae reached five or more storeys, and the tower houses of Shibam in Yemen rose to eleven — but the modern high-rise emerged in the 19th century with the steel skeleton frame, safety elevator (Elisha Otis, 1850s), and curtain wall construction. Chicago and New York pioneered the type after the 1870s, and reinforced concrete later made high-rise housing economical worldwide, from postwar European tower blocks to the dense residential towers of East Asian cities.

Design and safety

High-rises are designed around structural systems that resist wind loads, central service cores, and stringent fire-safety requirements: protected stairwells, compartmentation, sprinklers, and firefighting lifts. Evacuation strategy, cladding fire performance, and progressive-collapse resistance have become major regulatory concerns, sharply tightened in many countries after disasters such as the 2017 Grenfell Tower fire in London.
