The TNM classification of malignant tumours (TNM) is a globally recognised standard for classifying the anatomical extent of the spread of malignant diseases (cancer). It was developed by the French surgeon Pierre Denoix between 1943 and 1952 and is maintained jointly by the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC), whose staging manuals are periodically updated.

Components

Three parameters describe the disease:

  • T (Tumour) — the size and extent of the primary tumour, graded TX (cannot be assessed), T0 (no evidence of primary tumour), Tis (carcinoma in situ), and T1–T4 of increasing size or local invasion, with site-specific definitions.
  • N (Node) — spread to regional lymph nodes: NX, N0 (no nodal metastasis), and N1–N3 by number, size, or location of involved nodes.
  • M (Metastasis) — distant metastasis: M0 (none) or M1 (present), sometimes subdivided by site (e.g. M1a, M1b, M1c).

The individual categories are combined into overall stage groups (stage 0, I, II, III, IV), which correlate with prognosis and guide treatment. Prefixes qualify the assessment: c for clinical staging before treatment, p for pathological staging after surgery, y after neoadjuvant therapy, and r for recurrent disease.

Use

TNM staging is used for most solid tumours (leukaemias and lymphomas have their own systems). It provides a common language for clinicians and cancer registries, supports decisions between surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy, and enables comparison of outcomes between institutions and clinical trials.
