Folk religion

Folk religion is a term used in religious studies and folklore studies for the diverse, localised religious beliefs and practices that exist within — and often alongside or at variance with — the official doctrines of organised religions. It encompasses such things as the veneration of local saints and spirits, household rituals, healing practices, divination, festivals, pilgrimage customs and beliefs about luck and the evil eye.

Characteristics

Folk religion is typically transmitted orally and through custom rather than scripture, is rooted in the life of a particular community or ethnic group, and tends to be syncretic, blending elements from different traditions. Examples include Chinese folk religion with its ancestor veneration and local gods, the folk Catholicism of Latin America and the Philippines blending Indigenous and Spanish practices, and survivals of pre-Christian custom in European festivals.

Study of folk religion

Scholars contrast folk or "lived" religion with official, institutional religion, though the boundary is fluid: practices once dismissed as superstition may be integrated into a tradition, and official doctrines are reshaped by popular devotion. Modern approaches generally avoid judging folk religion as a corrupted form of a "pure" faith, studying it instead as the way religion is actually experienced by most adherents.
