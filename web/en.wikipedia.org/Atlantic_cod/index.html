Atlantic cod

Scientific name: Gadus morhua
Family: Gadidae
Conservation status: Vulnerable

The Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua) is a benthopelagic fish of the family Gadidae, widely consumed by humans. It is native to the North Atlantic Ocean, from the North Carolina coast north to Greenland and east to the Barents Sea. Adults commonly grow to about 1 metre (3 ft) in length and are distinguished by a barbel on the chin, three dorsal fins, and a mottled greenish-brown coloration.

Ecology

Cod are demersal fish, living near the bottom in waters from the shoreline down to depths of about 600 m. They feed on smaller fish such as capelin and herring, as well as crustaceans and molluscs. Spawning takes place in winter and early spring; large females can release several million eggs, which float in surface waters.

Fishery and collapse

Cod has been an important economic commodity for centuries, central to the economies of Newfoundland, Iceland, and Norway, and dried salt cod (bacalao) was a staple trade good. Heavy industrial fishing in the 20th century caused severe stock declines; in 1992 Canada imposed a moratorium on the northern cod fishery off Newfoundland, putting tens of thousands out of work, and many stocks have been slow to recover. The species is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN.

As food

Cod has white, mild-flavoured flesh that flakes readily, and is used in dishes such as fish and chips. Cod liver oil is a traditional source of vitamins A and D.
