Epidemiological transition

The epidemiological transition (or epidemiologic transition) is a theory of population change that describes the long-term shift in a population's dominant causes of death and disease patterns as it develops economically and socially. Proposed by Abdel Omran in 1971, the theory links these health changes to the demographic transition from high to low birth and death rates.

As societies develop, chronic and degenerative diseases replace infections as the main causes of death

Stages

Omran described three stages: the Age of Pestilence and Famine, when mortality is high and fluctuating, dominated by epidemics and malnutrition; the Age of Receding Pandemics, when sanitation, nutrition, and medicine reduce deaths from infectious disease; and the Age of Degenerative and Man-Made Diseases, when cardiovascular disease, cancer, and lifestyle-related conditions predominate and life expectancy rises. Later authors added a fourth stage of delayed degenerative diseases, and some describe a fifth stage involving re-emerging infections.

Criticism

Critics note that the transition is not uniform: many low- and middle-income countries face a "double burden" of persistent infectious disease alongside rising noncommunicable disease, and transitions can reverse, as happened with HIV/AIDS and, in some regions, declining life expectancy.
