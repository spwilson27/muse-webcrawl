Subject–verb–object

In linguistic typology, subject–verb–object (SVO) is a sentence structure in which the subject comes first, the verb second, and the object third. English, French, Spanish, Chinese, and Swahili are prominent SVO languages, and SVO is one of the two most common word orders among the world's languages, along with subject–object–verb.

Field: Linguistic typology

Examples: English, French, Mandarin
Frequency: ~42% of languages

Properties

SVO languages tend to place prepositions before noun phrases, adjectives after nouns (though English is an exception), and auxiliaries before verbs. Word-order correlations of this kind were systematized by Joseph Greenberg in his proposed linguistic universals.

Examples

In the sentence "The cat chased the mouse," "the cat" is the subject, "chased" the verb, and "the mouse" the object. Many languages permit other orders for emphasis or questions while keeping SVO as the basic, unmarked order.

Prevalence

SVO order is dominant in Europe, sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, and creole languages, and its frequency may be growing as analytic languages spread.
