Routine activity theory

The convergence of a motivated offender, a suitable target, and the absence of a capable guardian.

Routine activity theory is a theory of crime developed by criminologists Lawrence E. Cohen and Marcus Felson, first presented in their 1979 paper "Social Change and Crime Rate Trends: A Routine Activity Approach". It is a subfield of crime prevention and environmental criminology that focuses on the circumstances in which crime occurs rather than on the characteristics of offenders.

The theory

The theory proposes that a criminal event occurs when three elements converge in time and space: (1) a motivated offender, (2) a suitable target, and (3) the absence of a capable guardian against the crime. Everyday legal activities — work, school, leisure — structure where and when these elements come together, so crime rates change as the routine patterns of society change.

Suitable targets

Felson later summarised the attractiveness of a target with the acronym VIVA: Value (the worth of the item to the offender), Inertia (its size and resistance to being moved), Visibility (how exposed it is), and Access (how easily it can be reached). A related formulation, CRAVED, describes hot products most likely to be stolen: concealable, removable, available, valuable, enjoyable, and disposable.

Influence

Routine activity theory has been highly influential in situational crime prevention, problem-oriented policing, and the analysis of "hot spots" of crime. Cohen and Felson used it to explain why crime rates in the United States rose after the Second World War even as economic conditions improved: more households were empty during the day, and consumer goods became lighter, more valuable, and more portable.

Criticism

Critics note that the theory takes offender motivation for granted and says little about how motivation arises. It has also been criticised for fitting property crime better than violent crime, although later work has applied it to violence, cybercrime, and victimisation more generally.
