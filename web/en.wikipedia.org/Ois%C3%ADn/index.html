Oisín

Oisín (pronounced roughly "uh-SHEEN"), also spelled Ossian or Osheen, is a legendary Irish poet and warrior, a member of the Fianna in the Fenian Cycle of Irish mythology. He is the son of the hero Fionn mac Cumhaill and the goddess Sadb, who had been transformed into a deer; his name means "little deer".

Tír na nÓg

The most famous tale of Oisín recounts how Niamh, a princess of Tír na nÓg, the land of eternal youth, fell in love with him and carried him away on a magical white horse across the sea. After what seemed to him three years in that land — in fact three hundred years in Ireland — Oisín longed to visit home. Niamh gave him her horse, warning him never to dismount or touch the soil of Ireland. Finding his world vanished and the Fianna long dead, Oisín fell from the horse while helping men move a stone; the moment he touched the ground, he became an ancient, blind old man and died soon after.

The Fenian Cycle

In many Fenian tales, Oisín survives into the Christian era and narrates the deeds of Fionn and the Fianna to Saint Patrick, framing texts such as the Acallam na Senórach ("Colloquy of the Ancients"). He is traditionally credited as the poet of the cycle, lending his name to poems attributed to him.

Ossian and later influence

In the 1760s the Scottish writer James Macpherson published the "Ossian" poems, purported translations of an ancient Gaelic bard modelled on Oisín. Though their authenticity was fiercely disputed, the poems became a European sensation, influencing Romantic literature and figures from Goethe to Napoleon. Oisín's story was retold by W. B. Yeats in The Wanderings of Oisin (1889).
