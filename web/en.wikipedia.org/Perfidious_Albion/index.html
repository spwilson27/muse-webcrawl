Perfidious Albion

"Albion" is an ancient poetic name for Great Britain, from the white cliffs of Dover.

Perfidious Albion is a pejorative phrase used within the context of international relations diplomacy for the United Kingdom, implying that the British state is duplicitous, treacherous, or acts in bad faith in its dealings with foreign powers. "Albion" is an ancient name for the island of Great Britain, said to derive from the Latin albus (white), referring to the white chalk cliffs of Dover.

Origins

The phrase, in French la perfide Albion, became popular in France during the Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars. It is sometimes attributed to the Marquis de Ximenez in a 1793 poem, and was repeatedly used by Napoleon Bonaparte. Earlier antecedents include a sermon by Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet in the seventeenth century referring to England's faithlessness.

Usage

The expression has been used at moments of Anglo-French tension — the Fashoda incident, the Entente Cordiale notwithstanding — and by other nations criticising British foreign policy, including during the World Wars, the Suez Crisis, and disputes over Gibraltar and the Falkland Islands. It has also been revived in commentary on Brexit. Conversely, the phrase is sometimes used ironically or proudly in Britain itself.
