Rudolf Julius Emanuel Clausius (2 January 1822 – 24 August 1888) was a German physicist and mathematician and is considered one of the central founding fathers of the science of thermodynamics. By his restatement of Sadi Carnot's principle known as the Carnot cycle, he gave the theory of heat a truer and sounder basis.

Thermodynamics

In his landmark 1850 paper "On the Moving Force of Heat", Clausius formulated the second law of thermodynamics. In 1865 he introduced the concept of entropy, defining it as the quotient of heat absorbed and temperature, and famously summarised the laws of thermodynamics: "The energy of the universe is constant. The entropy of the universe tends to a maximum."

Kinetic theory

Clausius also made foundational contributions to the kinetic theory of gases. In 1857 he introduced the concept of mean free path and gave the first largely correct molecular account of gas pressure and temperature, deriving the relation between pressure, volume and the mean square speed of molecules.

Life and career

Clausius studied at the University of Berlin and received his doctorate from the University of Halle in 1847. He held professorships at Zürich, Würzburg and Bonn. He served as a stretcher-bearer in the Franco-Prussian War and was wounded, receiving the Iron Cross.
