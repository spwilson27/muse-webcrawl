California chaparral and woodlands

The California chaparral and woodlands is a terrestrial ecoregion of the temperate Mediterranean forests, woodlands, and scrub biome, covering a large portion of the U.S. state of California and extending into northwestern Baja California in Mexico. It is characterised by a Mediterranean climate of hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters, and by plant communities adapted to drought and recurrent wildfire.

Plant communities

The ecoregion is a mosaic of communities:

  • Chaparral — dense, evergreen, sclerophyllous shrubs such as chamise, manzanita and ceanothus, many of which resprout or germinate vigorously after fire;
  • Oak woodland — open woodlands of coast live oak, blue oak, valley oak and other oaks, often mixed with foothill pine;
  • Coastal sage scrub — lower, softer-leaved shrubs such as California sagebrush and black sage near the coast;
  • Grassland — now dominated largely by introduced annual grasses, with remnants of native perennial bunchgrasses;
  • Closed-cone pine and cypress forests — small, localised stands of fire-dependent conifers such as Monterey pine and Monterey cypress.

Fauna and conservation

The ecoregion supports high levels of endemism among plants and animals, including the California gnatcatcher, wrentit, mountain lion, bobcat and numerous endemic reptiles and invertebrates. It forms part of the California Floristic Province, recognised as a global biodiversity hotspot. Major threats include urban expansion around the Los Angeles, San Diego and San Francisco Bay metropolitan areas, altered fire regimes (fires that are too frequent can convert chaparral to alien grassland), invasive species and climate change.
