Islamic dietary laws

Foods permitted under Islamic dietary law are termed halal

Islamic dietary laws are the rules of Islam governing what Muslims may eat and drink. Foods that are permitted are called halal ("lawful"), while those prohibited are called haram ("forbidden"). The principal sources of these laws are the Quran and the Sunnah (the practice of the Prophet Muhammad), interpreted by Islamic jurisprudence.

Prohibited foods

The Quran explicitly prohibits the consumption of carrion (dead animals not slaughtered), blood, pork, and animals slaughtered in the name of anyone other than God. Alcohol and other intoxicants are also forbidden. Animals that are permitted must generally be slaughtered according to the ritual method known as dhabihah: a swift cut to the throat while invoking God's name.

Categories

Islamic jurisprudence classifies actions, including eating, into categories ranging from obligatory to forbidden. Between halal and haram lie doubtful matters (shubuhat), which many Muslims avoid out of caution. Seafood is broadly permitted by most schools, though schools differ on details such as shellfish. Hunting, the meat of the People of the Book (Jews and Christians), and food eaten under necessity (starvation) are addressed in specific rulings.

Modern halal certification

In the modern food industry, halal certification bodies inspect production, processing and labelling to certify products as compliant. The global halal food market is large, and certification requirements vary between countries and certifying organizations.
