Kaapvaal Craton

The Kaapvaal Craton (also spelled Kaapvaal craton) is one of the world's oldest pieces of continental crust, a stable block of ancient basement rock underlying much of northern South Africa and parts of Botswana, Eswatini and Zimbabwe. Together with the Pilbara Craton of Western Australia, it preserves some of the only remaining crust older than 3.6 billion years on Earth.

Geology

The craton consists largely of tonalite-trondhjemite-granodiorite (TTG) gneisses intruded by granitic plutons, together with greenstone belts such as the famous Barberton Greenstone Belt, which contains some of the oldest well-preserved volcanic and sedimentary rocks known, including evidence of very early life. The craton was assembled and stabilised between about 3.7 and 2.6 billion years ago during the Archaean eon.

To its west, the Kaapvaal Craton is joined to the Zimbabwe Craton by the Limpopo Belt, a zone of highly deformed rocks formed by their collision roughly 2.6–2.0 billion years ago.

Economic importance

The craton hosts some of the richest mineral deposits on the planet. The Witwatersrand Basin, a thick sedimentary succession on its northern margin, has produced more gold than any other district in history. The Bushveld Igneous Complex, intruded into the craton about 2.06 billion years ago, contains the world's largest reserves of platinum-group elements, chromium and vanadium. Kimberlite pipes piercing the craton, such as those at Kimberley, are major sources of diamonds.
