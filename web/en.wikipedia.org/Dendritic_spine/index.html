Dendritic spine

Dendritic spine
Part ofNeuron (dendrite)
Size~0.5–2 µm long
First described bySantiago Ramón y Cajal (1888)
FunctionPostsynaptic site of most excitatory synapses

A dendritic spine (or spine) is a small membranous protrusion from a neuron's dendrite that typically receives input from a single axon at the synapse. Dendritic spines serve as storage sites for synaptic strength and help transmit electrical signals to the neuron's cell body.

Structure

Spines consist of a bulbous head connected to the dendritic shaft by a thin neck, and are rich in the cytoskeletal protein actin. They are classified by shape as thin, stubby, mushroom and branched. The spine head contains the postsynaptic density, a protein-dense region facing the presynaptic terminal that holds neurotransmitter receptors, including NMDA and AMPA receptors for glutamate.

Plasticity

Spines are highly dynamic structures that can form, grow, shrink and disappear over minutes to days. Enlargement of spine heads accompanies long-term potentiation (LTP), while shrinkage accompanies long-term depression (LTD), making spine morphology a physical correlate of learning and memory.

Clinical significance

Abnormal spine density and shape are associated with neurological and psychiatric conditions. Fragile X syndrome involves an excess of long, immature spines; schizophrenia and Alzheimer's disease are associated with spine loss in cortical and hippocampal neurons.
