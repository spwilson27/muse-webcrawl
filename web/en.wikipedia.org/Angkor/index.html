Angkor (Khmer: អង្គរ, "capital city") was the capital city of the Khmer Empire, which flourished from approximately the 9th to the 15th century AD. The city and empire produced the monumental temple complexes of Angkor, among the most important archaeological sites in Southeast Asia, extending over some 400 square kilometres near modern Siem Reap in Cambodia.

History

The Angkorian period is traditionally dated from 802, when Jayavarman II proclaimed himself universal monarch (chakravartin) and founded the empire. Successive kings built vast temple-mountains and hydraulic works: great reservoirs (barays) supported intensive rice farming for a sprawling low-density city that lidar surveys have shown to be among the largest preindustrial urban complexes on Earth. Suryavarman II built Angkor Wat in the early 12th century; Jayavarman VII built the walled city of Angkor Thom with the Bayon temple at the end of that century.

Decline

From the 14th century the capital declined under pressure from Ayutthaya, shifts from Hinduism to Theravada Buddhism, and strain on the water system from climate swings. Angkor was largely abandoned by 1431, though Angkor Wat itself was never fully deserted. French naturalist Henri Mouhot's accounts in the 1860s popularised the ruins in the West.

Tourism and conservation

Angkor Archaeological Park is Cambodia's premier tourist attraction, centred on sunrise visits to Angkor Wat, the root-entwined ruins of Ta Prohm, and the faces of the Bayon. Conservation is overseen by the APSARA Authority with international partners.
