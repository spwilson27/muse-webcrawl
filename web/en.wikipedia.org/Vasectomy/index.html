Vasectomy

Vasectomy is an elective surgical procedure for male sterilization and permanent contraception. During the procedure, the vasa deferentia — the tubes that carry sperm from the testes — are cut and tied or sealed, preventing sperm from entering the semen. The procedure does not affect testosterone production, sexual function, or the volume of ejaculate in any significant way.

Vasectomy is one of the most effective methods of birth control, with failure rates well under 1% once sterility is confirmed by a follow-up semen analysis, which is typically performed several weeks after the operation because residual sperm remain in the reproductive tract for a time.

Techniques

The conventional technique uses one or two small incisions in the scrotum to access each vas deferens. The no-scalpel vasectomy, developed in China in 1974, uses a sharp instrument to puncture the skin instead, reducing bleeding, bruising, and infection risk. Segments of the vas may be removed, and the ends may be tied, clipped, cauterized, or separated by fascial interposition to reduce the chance of reconnection.

Reversal

Vasectomy should be considered permanent, although microsurgical reversal (vasovasostomy) is often possible, with success declining as more years pass since the procedure. Sperm production continues after vasectomy; the sperm are simply reabsorbed by the body.
