Crossmodal correspondences

Crossmodal correspondences are systematic associations that most people make between features or dimensions in different sensory modalities — for example, matching high-pitched sounds with small, bright, angular objects, and low-pitched sounds with large, dark, rounded ones.

Famous demonstrations include the bouba/kiki effect, first reported by Wolfgang Köhler, in which people across cultures match the nonsense word "bouba" to a rounded shape and "kiki" to a spiky one. Other robust correspondences link sweetness with roundness, carbonation with angularity, and pitch with elevation and visual size. Unlike synaesthesia, crossmodal correspondences are shared by the general population and tend to be relative rather than absolute.

Mechanisms and applications

Proposed explanations include statistical regularities in the environment (large objects resonate at lower frequencies), shared neural coding of magnitude, and linguistic or metaphorical associations. The phenomenon is exploited in food and beverage design, packaging, and marketing, where shapes, colours, and sounds are chosen to set congruent taste expectations, and in the design of multisensory interfaces and alarms.
