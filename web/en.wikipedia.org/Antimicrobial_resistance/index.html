Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) occurs when microbes—bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites—evolve so that the medicines designed to kill them or stop their growth no longer work. Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is the best-known form. A landmark 2022 study in The Lancet estimated that bacterial AMR was directly responsible for about 1.27 million deaths in 2019 and associated with nearly 5 million deaths. The World Health Organization lists AMR among the top global public health threats.

Mechanisms

Bacteria resist antibiotics by producing enzymes that destroy the drug (e.g. beta-lactamases), altering the drug's target (as in MRSA's altered penicillin-binding protein), pumping the drug out via efflux pumps, or reducing membrane permeability. Resistance genes spread by mutation and by horizontal gene transfer through plasmids, transposons, and bacteriophages.

Causes and prevention

Resistance is accelerated by overprescribing, patients not completing courses, over-the-counter availability in some countries, and large-scale antimicrobial use in livestock production. Prevention strategies include antimicrobial stewardship programmes, vaccination, hygiene and infection control, surveillance networks such as the WHO's GLASS, and development of new antibiotics and alternatives such as phage therapy—hampered by weak commercial incentives, a problem highlighted by the UK's O'Neill Review (2016), which warned AMR could cause 10 million deaths a year by 2050 if unchecked.

Notable examples

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis, and the global spread of the resistance genes NDM-1 and mcr-1 (conferring resistance to last-resort drugs carbapenems and colistin) illustrate the problem.
