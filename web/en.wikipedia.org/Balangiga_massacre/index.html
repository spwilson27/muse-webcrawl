The Balangiga massacre — in Filipino historiography long called the Balangiga encounter — was an incident on 28 September 1901 during the Philippine–American War, in which Filipino guerrillas and townspeople of Balangiga, on the island of Samar, launched a surprise attack on Company C of the 9th United States Infantry Regiment stationed in the town. Of the 74 American soldiers, 48 were killed and most of the rest wounded — one of the worst defeats suffered by US forces in the war.

The attack

The attack was organised by the town's police chief, Valeriano Abanador, and local guerrilla leaders, and timed for a Sunday morning while most soldiers were at breakfast unarmed. At a signal — associated in tradition with the ringing of the church bells — men armed with bolos fell on the Americans while women and children who had gathered for a staged funeral the night before concealed weapons and fighters. The survivors escaped by boat to Basey.

Reprisals

American retaliation against Samar was notoriously brutal. General Jacob H. Smith ordered his forces to turn the interior of the island into a "howling wilderness", to kill everyone capable of bearing arms — reported as everyone over ten years old — and to take no prisoners; his subordinate Littleton Waller carried out a punitive campaign in which large numbers of Filipino civilians were killed and food supplies destroyed. Smith and Waller were court-martialled; Waller was acquitted, Smith convicted only of conduct prejudicial to good order and admonished. US troops seized the three Balangiga church bells as war booty.

Legacy

Balangiga remains a symbol of Filipino resistance and of the war's atrocities. After decades of Philippine requests, the United States returned the bells, which arrived in the Philippines in December 2018 and were formally handed over to Balangiga and rung again in their church.
