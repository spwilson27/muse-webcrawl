Uniting for Peace
Formal nameUnited Nations General Assembly Resolution 377 (V)
AdoptedNovember 3, 1950

Uniting for Peace is a United Nations General Assembly resolution, Resolution 377 (V), adopted on 3 November 1950. It provides that if the Security Council fails to exercise its primary responsibility for international peace and security because of a lack of unanimity among its permanent members, the General Assembly shall consider the matter immediately and may recommend collective measures, including the use of armed force when necessary.

Background

The resolution was introduced by United States Secretary of State Dean Acheson during the Korean War. The Soviet Union had ended its boycott of the Security Council in August 1950, regaining its veto; the United States sought a mechanism to prevent Soviet vetoes from paralysing the UN in future crises. The resolution passed with 52 votes in favour, five against and two abstentions, over Soviet objections that it circumvented the Charter.

Provisions

Resolution 377 states that the General Assembly may act where the Security Council is deadlocked by veto. It established procedures for convening emergency special sessions of the Assembly at short notice — at the request of any seven (later nine) members of the Security Council or a majority of member states — and created subsidiary bodies including a Peace Observation Commission and a Collective Measures Committee.

Invocations

The procedure has been used to convene a number of emergency special sessions, beginning with the Suez Crisis of 1956, which led to the creation of the first United Nations Emergency Force. Later sessions addressed the Hungarian uprising (1956), the Congo, the Six-Day War (1967), the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1980), and, in 2022, the Russian invasion of Ukraine, which produced Resolution ES-11/1 condemning the aggression.

Legal debate

Scholars have long debated the resolution's legality: supporters note the Assembly's broad powers to discuss and recommend, while critics argue only the Security Council can authorise coercive measures. Because Assembly resolutions are recommendations rather than binding decisions, the practical effect of Uniting for Peace depends on the willingness of member states to act on its recommendations.
