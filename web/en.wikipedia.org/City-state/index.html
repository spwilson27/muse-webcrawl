City-state
Definition: an independent sovereign city
Examples: Singapore, Monaco, Vatican City
Historical: Athens, Sparta, Venice, Genoa

A city-state is an independent sovereign city which serves as the center of political, economic, and cultural life over its contiguous territory. City-states have existed in many parts of the world throughout history, including in ancient Mesopotamia, the Greek world, Mesoamerica, Southeast Asia, and Renaissance Italy.

History

Some of the earliest city-states arose in Sumer in the fourth millennium BCE, where cities such as Uruk, Ur, and Lagash governed themselves and the surrounding farmland. In ancient Greece, the polis — such as Athens, Sparta, Corinth, and Thebes — became the dominant political unit, each with its own government, laws, and military.

The Phoenician cities of Tyre, Sidon, and Byblos were maritime city-states that founded colonies across the Mediterranean. In Italy, medieval and Renaissance city-states such as Venice, Florence, Genoa, and Milan became wealthy commercial and cultural centers. In what is now Germany, free imperial cities and members of the Hanseatic League exercised substantial self-government.

Modern city-states

Today, sovereign city-states are rare. Singapore, Monaco, and Vatican City are the most commonly cited examples: each is a fully independent state whose territory consists essentially of a single city. Other states, such as San Marino and Liechtenstein, are microstates but not strictly single cities.

Characteristics

City-states typically combine the functions of a city and a sovereign state: they maintain their own legal systems, defense arrangements, and foreign relations, while their economies are usually oriented toward trade, finance, or specialized services because of their limited land and resources.
