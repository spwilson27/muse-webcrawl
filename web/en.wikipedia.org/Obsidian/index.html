Obsidian is a naturally occurring volcanic glass formed when lava rich in silica cools so rapidly that atoms cannot arrange themselves into crystals. It is typically black or dark green, hard and brittle, and breaks with a distinctive conchoidal fracture that produces extremely sharp edges—finer at the molecular level than surgical steel.

Formation and distribution

Obsidian forms from high-silica (rhyolitic) lava and is found in flows and domes at places such as Yellowstone, the Aeolian Islands (Lipari), the island of Melos in Greece, Iceland, Mexico, Armenia, and Japan. Because obsidian slowly hydrates and devitrifies over geological time, almost all obsidian is younger than about 20 million years. Each source has a distinctive trace-element fingerprint, allowing archaeologists to provenance artefacts by X-ray fluorescence and similar methods.

Historical use

Obsidian was one of the most valued tool materials of the Stone Age, traded over hundreds of kilometres from the Neolithic onward—for example from Melos across the Aegean, and in Mesoamerica from sources such as Pachuca, whose green obsidian was prized by the Aztecs. It was knapped into blades, arrowheads, and mirrors; Aztec and Maya peoples used it for weapons (the macuahuitl's blades) and ritual objects. Polished obsidian mirrors were also made in the Old World, including at Neolithic Çatalhöyük.

Modern use

Today obsidian is used as a gemstone and ornamental material, in some surgical scalpels (whose edges can be far sharper than steel but are fragile), and as a dimension stone. Varieties include snowflake obsidian (with cristobalite spherulites), mahogany obsidian, and rainbow obsidian.
