Public–private partnership

A public–private partnership (PPP or P3) is a long-term contractual arrangement between a government and a private-sector entity in which the private partner finances, builds and operates infrastructure or services traditionally provided by the public sector, such as hospitals, roads, schools or water systems. Risks and rewards are shared between the partners according to the contract.

Models

Common models range from design–build contracts to build–operate–transfer concessions and arrangements in which a private consortium finances the asset and is repaid through user charges or government availability payments over 25–30 years. The United Kingdom's Private Finance Initiative, launched in 1992, became an influential template.

Arguments and criticism

Supporters argue PPPs deliver projects on time and budget, mobilise private finance and transfer construction and operating risk to the party best able to manage it. Critics note that private borrowing costs more than sovereign debt, contracts are inflexible, and profits and contingent liabilities can leave the public sector worse off; several UK inquiries reached critical conclusions about PFI value for money.
