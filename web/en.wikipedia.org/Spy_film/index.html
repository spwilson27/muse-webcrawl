Spy film

GenreAction / thriller
OriginsEarly 20th-century espionage fiction
Peak eras1960s; post-Cold War
Major franchisesJames Bond, Mission: Impossible, Bourne

The spy film, also known as the spy thriller, is a genre of film dealing with the subject of fictional espionage, whether in a realistic way or as a fantasy base. Many novels in the spy fiction genre have been adapted as films, including works by John le Carré, Ian Fleming and Len Deighton.

History

The genre emerged with early silent films about invasion scares and Great War espionage, and matured in the 1930s with Alfred Hitchcock's The 39 Steps (1935), Sabotage (1936) and The Lady Vanishes (1938). During the Second World War espionage films served propaganda purposes, and in the Cold War the genre split between glamorous fantasy and grim realism.

The Bond phenomenon

The release of Dr. No in 1962, the first James Bond film, transformed the genre, launching a wave of glossy, gadget-laden "Eurospy" imitations and establishing enduring conventions: the charismatic agent, exotic locations, elaborate villain lairs and inventive technology. Later franchises such as Mission: Impossible and the Jason Bourne series shifted the emphasis toward stunt-driven action and gritty realism.

Realist counterpoint

A parallel tradition, exemplified by adaptations of le Carré such as The Spy Who Came In from the Cold (1965) and Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy (2011), portrays espionage as morally ambiguous bureaucratic work conducted in shabby offices rather than casinos.

Conventions

Common elements include secret identities and codenames, dead drops and covert meetings, double agents and moles, interrogation and defection, and plots revolving around stolen secrets, assassinations or weapons of mass destruction.
