Streptococcus pyogenes

Streptococcus pyogenes is a species of Gram-positive, aerotolerant bacteria in the genus Streptococcus. These bacteria are extracellular, and made up of non-motile and non-sporing cocci (spherical cells) approximately 0.6–1.0 μm in diameter, tending to form chains. It is medically important as the cause of group A streptococcal infections. S. pyogenes is the chief cause of several major diseases, from mild localized infections such as pharyngitis ("strep throat") and impetigo to serious invasive infections such as necrotizing fasciitis and streptococcal toxic shock syndrome, and to post-infectious sequelae including rheumatic fever and glomerulonephritis.

S. pyogenes typically colonizes the throat, genital mucosa, rectum, and skin of healthy adults without causing disease; an estimated 5–15% of individuals carry it. It is beta-hemolytic on blood agar, producing large zones of complete hemolysis, and is classified in group A of the Lancefield grouping.

Virulence factors

The bacterium's major virulence factors include the M protein, which inhibits phagocytosis; a hyaluronic acid capsule that mimics host tissue; exotoxins such as streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins that act as superantigens; and enzymes including streptokinase, hyaluronidase, and the hemolysins streptolysin O and streptolysin S. More than 200 M types have been identified, complicating vaccine development; a 30-valent M-protein vaccine is among candidates under study.

Treatment

S. pyogenes remains universally susceptible to penicillin, which is the treatment of choice for pharyngitis; macrolides are used for patients with penicillin allergy, though resistance to them is increasingly reported. Prompt antibiotic treatment of pharyngitis helps prevent rheumatic fever.
