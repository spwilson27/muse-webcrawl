Streptococcus

Streptococcus is a genus of gram-positive spherical bacteria (cocci) that occur in pairs or chains. They are facultative anaerobes, catalase-negative, and many are members of the normal flora of the mouth, skin, intestine, and upper respiratory tract of humans and animals, while others cause significant disease. The name derives from the Greek streptos ("twisted chain") and kokkos ("berry").

Classification

Streptococci are traditionally classified by their haemolytic properties on blood agar — alpha (partial), beta (complete), or gamma (none) — and by Lancefield grouping of cell-wall carbohydrates (groups A, B, C, and so on). Important groups include:

  • Group A — Streptococcus pyogenes: causes strep throat, scarlet fever, impetigo, cellulitis, necrotizing fasciitis, and post-streptococcal complications such as rheumatic fever.
  • Group B — Streptococcus agalactiae: a cause of sepsis and meningitis in newborns and of infections in pregnant women and older adults.
  • Pneumococcus — Streptococcus pneumoniae: a leading cause of pneumonia, meningitis, sinusitis, and otitis media.
  • Viridans group and others — oral streptococci such as S. mutans, associated with dental caries, and S. bovis group organisms.

Industrial and food uses

Some streptococci are beneficial: Streptococcus thermophilus is a widely used starter culture in the production of yogurt and cheese. Other former members of the genus have been reclassified; for example, many dairy organisms are now placed in Lactococcus.
