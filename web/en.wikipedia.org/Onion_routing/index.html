Onion routing

Onion routing is a technique for anonymous communication over a computer network. In an onion network, messages are encapsulated in layers of encryption, analogous to the layers of an onion. The encrypted data is transmitted through a series of network nodes called onion routers, each of which "peels" away a single layer, revealing the data's next destination. When the final layer is decrypted, the message arrives at its destination. The sender remains anonymous because each intermediary knows only the location of the immediately preceding and following nodes.

Data is wrapped in successive layers of encryption, one removed at each relay.

How it works

A client builds a circuit through (typically) three relays chosen from a public directory. It negotiates a separate encryption key with each relay using public-key cryptography, then wraps outgoing data in three layers of encryption. The entry (guard) relay knows the client's address but not the destination; the middle relay knows neither endpoint; the exit relay knows the destination but not the client. Each relay removes only its own layer, so no single node can link sender to recipient. Circuits are usually rotated periodically.

History and Tor

Onion routing was developed at the U.S. Naval Research Laboratory in the 1990s to protect U.S. intelligence communications online, and the underlying patent was granted in 1998. A second-generation design by Roger Dingledine, Nick Mathewson and Paul Syverson became Tor, released as free software in 2003–2004. Tor supports "onion services" (formerly hidden services) that allow servers themselves to remain anonymous, reachable via .onion addresses.

Weaknesses

Onion routing does not protect against a global passive adversary able to observe traffic entering and leaving the network and correlate timing and volume (end-to-end correlation attacks). Exit traffic to unencrypted destinations can be observed or tampered with by exit-relay operators. Browser-level leaks and user behaviour can also compromise anonymity.
