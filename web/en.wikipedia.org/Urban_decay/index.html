Urban decay

Derelict housing, a common visual marker of urban decay.

Urban decay (also known as urban rot, urban death or urban blight) is the process through which a previously functioning city, or part of a city, falls into disrepair and decrepitude. It comprises various combinations of population flight, economic restructuring, abandoned buildings and property, high local unemployment, fragmented families, political disenfranchisement, crime, and a desolate and unfriendly urban landscape.

Causes

No single cause explains urban decay. Deindustrialisation removed manufacturing jobs from many inner cities in the twentieth century, while suburbanisation and motorway construction drew middle-class residents to new suburbs — a pattern often called "white flight" in the United States. Redlining by mortgage lenders starved neighbourhoods of investment, and changes in transport policy, such as the building of elevated expressways through established districts, physically fragmented communities. When the tax base shrinks, municipal services deteriorate, reinforcing the cycle of decline.

Effects and responses

Decay is associated with rising vacancy rates, vandalism, squatting, and falling property values. Responses have included urban renewal programmes, slum clearance, public housing construction, and, more recently, gentrification-led regeneration — each of which has been criticised for displacing the poor residents it claimed to help. Urban explorers and photographers frequently document decayed districts, giving rise to the controversial genre sometimes labelled "ruin porn."
