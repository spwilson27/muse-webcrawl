Jim Crow laws

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jim Crow laws were state and local statutes enacted in the Southern United States from the late nineteenth century until the mid-twentieth century that enforced racial segregation. Named after a blackface minstrel character, the laws mandated separate public facilities — schools, transport, restaurants, drinking fountains, theatres — for Black and white Americans, under the doctrine of "separate but equal" endorsed by the Supreme Court in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896).

Origins

After the brief Reconstruction era, white Southern legislatures dismantled Black political gains through segregation statutes and disenfranchisement devices — poll taxes, literacy tests, and grandfather clauses — that stripped most Black citizens (and many poor whites) of the vote. Segregation was reinforced by custom, economic coercion, and the terror of lynching.

Dismantling

The NAACP's legal campaign chipped away at the system, culminating in Brown v. Board of Education (1954), which declared school segregation unconstitutional. The civil rights movement's protests, boycotts, and freedom rides forced federal action: the Civil Rights Act of 1964 banned segregation in public accommodations and employment, and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 restored Black suffrage, effectively ending the Jim Crow regime.

Legacy

Decades of enforced inequality left deep disparities in wealth, education, and health, and the era remains central to American debates about race and law; the phrase "the New Jim Crow" has been applied by critics to mass incarceration.
