The painted lady (Vanessa cardui) is a large butterfly of the family Nymphalidae. It is one of the most widespread of all butterflies, found on every continent except Antarctica (and largely absent from South America). Its orange wings, patterned with black and white markings, make it a familiar sight in gardens, meadows and open country throughout the temperate and tropical world.

Description

Adults have a wingspan of 5–9 cm. The upperside is orange with black forewing tips marked with white spots; the underside is mottled in browns and greys with a row of small eyespots on the hindwing, providing camouflage when the wings are closed. Sexes are similar in appearance.

Migration

The painted lady is famous for its long-distance, multi-generational migration. Populations breed continuously through the year in warm regions and push poleward in spring. In Afro-European populations, butterflies breed in sub-Saharan Africa in winter, then successive generations move north through the Mediterranean into Europe as far as Scandinavia, with a return southward in autumn — a round trip of up to 12,000 km, longer than that of the monarch butterfly. Similar circuits occur in North America and Asia. In "painted lady years", such as 2009 and 2019 in Britain, arrivals number in the millions.

Life cycle and diet

Females lay small green eggs singly on host plants. The larvae feed on an exceptionally wide range of plants — more than 100 species — with thistles (Asteraceae) preferred; they construct silk tents on the leaves. After pupation in a chrysalis, adults emerge to feed on flower nectar, favouring thistles, asters and other composites. In temperate regions the species does not survive winter and must recolonise annually from the south.
