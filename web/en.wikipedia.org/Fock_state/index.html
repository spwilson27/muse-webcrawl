Fock state

In quantum mechanics, a Fock state (or occupation number state) is a quantum state that is an element of a Fock space with a well-defined, exact number of particles in each single-particle state. Named after Soviet physicist Vladimir Fock, who introduced the formalism in 1932, Fock states form the standard basis for describing systems of identical bosons or fermions with varying particle number, as in quantum field theory and quantum optics.

Notation

A Fock state is written in occupation number notation |n₁, n₂, …, nₖ⟩, where nᵢ gives the number of particles occupying the i-th single-particle mode. For bosons, occupation numbers can be any non-negative integers; for fermions, the Pauli exclusion principle restricts each nᵢ to 0 or 1. The state with all occupations zero is the vacuum state.

Creation and annihilation operators

Fock states are built from the vacuum by acting with creation operators, which add a particle to a mode, while annihilation operators remove one. These operators obey commutation relations for bosons and anticommutation relations for fermions, and their algebra underlies second quantization.

Applications

In quantum optics, Fock states of the electromagnetic field are photon number states — states of definite photon count, sharply distinct from the coherent states of a laser. Preparing high-purity Fock states in the laboratory is a benchmark for quantum control, used in tests of quantum mechanics and in quantum information processing.
