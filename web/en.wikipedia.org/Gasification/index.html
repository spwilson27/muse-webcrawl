Gasification

Gasification is a process that converts biomass- or fossil fuel-based carbonaceous materials into gases, including carbon monoxide, hydrogen and carbon dioxide, by reacting the material at high temperatures (typically above 700 °C), without combustion, via controlled amounts of oxygen and/or steam. The resulting mixture, called syngas (synthesis gas) or producer gas, can be burned for energy or used as a chemical feedstock.

Process

Inside a gasifier the feedstock passes through drying, pyrolysis, partial oxidation and reduction zones. Key reactions include the water–gas reaction (C + H₂O → CO + H₂) and the Boudouard reaction. Reactor designs include fixed-bed (updraft and downdraft), fluidised-bed and entrained-flow gasifiers, each suited to particular feedstocks and scales.

History

Gasification was used from the 19th century to produce town gas for lighting and heating from coal. During the Second World War, wood gas generators powered vehicles across fuel-starved Europe. Sasol's Fischer–Tropsch plants in South Africa gasified coal to make liquid fuels at large scale.

Applications

Modern applications include integrated gasification combined cycle (IGCC) power generation, production of hydrogen, ammonia and synthetic fuels, waste-to-energy plants, and biomass gasification for heat and power. Syngas cleaning — removal of tars, particulates and sulfur — is a central technical challenge.
