Reality–virtuality continuum

Milgram's reality–virtuality continuum, from the real environment (left) to a fully virtual environment (right), with mixed reality between.

The reality–virtuality continuum is a scale running between the entirely real and the entirely virtual, encompassing all possible variations and compositions of real and virtual objects. It was first described by Paul Milgram and Fumio Kishino in their 1994 paper "A Taxonomy of Mixed Reality Visual Displays". The region between the two extremes, where both real and virtual elements are present, is called mixed reality.

Positions on the continuum

At the left extreme lies the real environment, consisting solely of real objects viewed directly or through a window or display. Moving right, augmented reality supplements a real view with virtual content, as in a heads-up display. Further along, augmented virtuality inserts real objects — for example a live video of the user's hands — into a predominantly virtual world. At the right extreme is the virtual environment, or virtual reality, in which everything the user perceives is synthetic.

Related concepts

Milgram's taxonomy also classified displays by extent of world knowledge, reproduction fidelity and extent of presence metaphor. Later writers added the notion of a "virtuality continuum" of mediated reality, in which reality itself may also be diminished as well as augmented, and Steve Mann's concept of mediated reality (including diminished reality) extends the scheme to a two-dimensional diagram. The continuum remains a standard framing in human–computer interaction research and in the marketing of extended reality (XR) devices, although critics note that real systems are better described as experiences than as points on a single axis.
