Treaty of Edinburgh

The treaty of 1560 ended the Siege of Leith and reshaped Scotland's alliances.

Signed6 July 1560, Edinburgh
PartiesCommissioners of Elizabeth I of England; representatives of Francis II of France; the Scottish Lords of the Congregation
ContextScottish Reformation crisis; Siege of Leith
Main effectWithdrawal of French and English troops from Scotland

The Treaty of Edinburgh was a treaty concluded on 6 July 1560 between the commissioners of Queen Elizabeth I of England, the representatives of King Francis II of France, and the Scottish Lords of the Congregation. Based on the terms of the Treaty of Berwick, which had brought an English army to Scotland's aid in February 1560, it largely ended the Siege of Leith and the long French military presence in Scotland.

Terms

Both French and English forces agreed to withdraw from Scotland. Francis II and his wife Mary, Queen of Scots — who was also the French queen consort — were to cease using the arms and titles of England and Ireland, to which Mary had a dynastic claim pressed by France. The fortifications at Leith and Dunbar were to be demolished, and Scotland was to be governed by a council of nobles until a parliament could settle the country's affairs. In practice the treaty let the Protestant Lords of the Congregation take control, and the Reformation Parliament of August 1560 abolished papal authority and the Mass in Scotland.

Aftermath

The treaty ended the Auld Alliance between Scotland and France that had shaped Scottish foreign policy since 1295. It is recognised as a landmark in both Scottish and British history: French influence in Scotland collapsed, and England and Scotland began a slow convergence that led to the Union of the Crowns in 1603. Mary and Francis never ratified the treaty, objecting to its provisions on the English succession — a refusal that echoed through Elizabeth's reign.
