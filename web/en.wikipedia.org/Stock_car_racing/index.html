Stock car racing

Stock car racing is a form of automobile racing run on oval tracks and road courses measuring about 0.25 to 2.66 miles (0.4 to 4.3 km). It originally used production-model cars (hence "stock"), though modern cars are purpose-built racing machines that only resemble production vehicles in silhouette. The sport is most popular in the United States, where it is governed principally by NASCAR.

History

The sport grew out of the American South during Prohibition, when bootleggers modified cars to outrun authorities while carrying moonshine, then raced each other for sport. NASCAR was founded by Bill France Sr. in 1948, and its top series grew into one of America's most watched sports, with signature events such as the Daytona 500.

Cars and racing

Modern NASCAR Cup Series cars use a spec chassis ("Next Gen" since 2022) with body panels styled after production coupes. Races feature close pack racing on banked superspeedways, where aerodynamic drafting allows cars to run in large groups at speeds over 320 km/h (200 mph). Stock car racing also thrives at local short tracks with late model and modified divisions, and variants exist in Canada, Mexico, Europe, Brazil, and Australia.
