Black swan

The black swan (Cygnus atratus) is a large waterbird, a species of swan that breeds mainly in the southeast and southwest regions of Australia. It is mostly black-feathered, with white flight feathers visible in flight and a bright red bill with a pale band and tip. The black swan is the official bird emblem of Western Australia and appears on its flag.

Description

Black swans measure 110–142 cm in length and weigh up to about 9 kg, with a wingspan up to 2 metres. Males are slightly larger than females. Unlike many waterbirds they are not migratory in the strict sense but move nomadically in response to rainfall and water conditions.

Distribution and behaviour

The species inhabits wetlands, lakes, rivers and coastal waters, feeding largely on submerged aquatic vegetation. Pairs mate for life and both parents build the large mound nest and care for the grey-downed cygnets. Black swans were introduced to New Zealand, where they became established, and are kept ornamentally in many countries.

Cultural significance

Because Europeans long assumed all swans were white, the discovery of black swans in Australia became a famous example of the limits of inductive reasoning; the phrase "black swan event" was later popularised by Nassim Nicholas Taleb for unpredictable, high-impact occurrences.
