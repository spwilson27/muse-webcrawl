Separation of duties

Separation of duties ensures no single individual controls every step of a sensitive process.

Separation of duties (SoD), also called segregation of duties, is an internal control concept in auditing, finance and information security under which no single individual should be able to complete a critical or sensitive task — or perpetrate and conceal a fraud — on their own. The work is divided among different people or roles so that fraud or error requires collusion between two or more individuals, which is harder to arrange and easier to detect.

Examples

In accounting, the person who authorises a payment should differ from the person who processes it and from the person who reconciles the bank account; custody of assets, authorisation of transactions, and record-keeping are classic functions to separate. In information systems, developers should not be able to promote their own code into production, and system administrators should not approve their own access requests. The principle also appears in the "two-man rule" for nuclear weapons release and in dual control of vaults and safety-deposit boxes, and it underlies the four-eyes principle used in many organisations.

Implementation

Organisations implement SoD through role design, access-control policies in ERP and identity-management systems, and automated SoD conflict analysis, which flags users holding incompatible roles. The Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 made documented separation of duties over financial reporting effectively mandatory for US public companies, and auditors test it as part of internal-control assessments. In small organisations with few staff, full separation may be impractical, and compensating controls — such as owner review of reports — are used instead.

Static and dynamic separation

In role-based access control theory, static SoD prevents a user from being assigned conflicting roles at all, while dynamic SoD allows the assignment but prevents the same user from exercising both roles within a single session or transaction.
