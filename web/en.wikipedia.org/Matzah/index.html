Matzah

Matzah, matzo, or maẓẓah (Hebrew: מַצָּה, plural matzot) is an unleavened flatbread that is part of Jewish cuisine and forms an integral element of the Passover festival, during which chametz (leaven and five species of grain that have been leavened) is forbidden.

As recounted in the Torah, God commanded the Israelites to eat only unleavened bread during the first night of the Exodus from Egypt, in commemoration of their ancestors leaving Egypt in haste and not having time to wait for their bread dough to rise. Matzah also symbolises redemption and freedom, but also serves as "lechem oni" — "poor man's bread" — and as a reminder to be humble.

Religious significance

At the Passover Seder, three matzot are placed in a stack on the table. The middle matzah is broken; half of it is set aside as the afikoman, which is eaten at the end of the meal. The unleavened bread is said to symbolise how the Israelites hastily left Egypt with their bread not yet having time to rise.

Preparation

Matzah dough is made from flour and water only, and must be baked before the dough has had time to rise — under rabbinic supervision, no more than 18 minutes may elapse between the flour meeting water and the finished baking. Matzah may be soft or crisp; commercial matzah is typically a crisp, cracker-like square sheet, while handmade shmura matzah is often round.

Culinary uses

Matzah is eaten as a bread substitute during Passover and features in dishes such as matzah brei (fried matzah with eggs), matzah ball soup, and matzah pizza. Ground matzah meal is used as a flour substitute in Passover baking.
