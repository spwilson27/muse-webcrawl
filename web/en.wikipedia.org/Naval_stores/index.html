Naval stores

Naval stores are resin-based products derived from pine trees, historically essential for the construction and maintenance of wooden sailing ships. The principal products are rosin, turpentine, tar, and pitch, used for caulking hulls, waterproofing rigging and sails, and preserving timber against rot and shipworm.

History

The term dates to the age of sail, when navies and merchant fleets depended on reliable supplies. Sweden and Russia long supplied Europe; Britain, seeking colonial sources, encouraged production in North America, where the industry became central to the economy of the Carolinas and later Georgia and Florida. Workers tapped longleaf and slash pines for oleoresin ("gum"), which was distilled into turpentine and rosin. The industry in the American South relied heavily on enslaved labour before the Civil War and on exploited convict and tenant labour afterward, notably in turpentine camps.

Modern industry

As wooden ships disappeared, demand shifted: rosin is now used in paper sizing, adhesives, inks, and soldering fluxes, while turpentine serves as a solvent and chemical feedstock. Most modern production comes as a by-product of kraft pulping of pine (yielding "tall oil" products) rather than from tapped gum, with China a major gum-rosin producer.
