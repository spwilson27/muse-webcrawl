AuthorIsaiah Berlin

TypeInaugural lecture / essay
ConceptsNegative liberty; positive liberty

"Two Concepts of Liberty" is an essay by the philosopher Isaiah Berlin, first given as his inaugural lecture as Chichele Professor of Social and Political Theory at the University of Oxford in 1958. It introduced the influential distinction between negative liberty and positive liberty, and is regarded as one of the most important works of 20th-century political philosophy.

The two concepts

Negative liberty answers the question "Within what area am I left free from interference by others?" — freedom from obstruction and coercion. Positive liberty answers "What, or who, is the source of control?" — freedom to be one's own master and realise one's true self.

Berlin's argument

Berlin warned that positive liberty, though genuinely valuable, had historically been twisted to justify authoritarianism: if freedom means obedience to one's "rational" or "higher" self, rulers can claim to coerce people into freedom. He defended negative liberty as the safer foundation of liberal societies while acknowledging both concepts as authentic human ideals, reflecting his broader doctrine of value pluralism.

Influence

The distinction has structured decades of debate in political theory, prompting responses from thinkers including Gerald MacCallum, who argued liberty is a single triadic relation, and republican theorists such as Quentin Skinner and Philip Pettit, who revive freedom as non-domination.
