Empty forest

An empty forest is a forest ecosystem that appears intact — its trees standing and canopy closed — but from which most large animals have been removed, chiefly by hunting. The term and the related concept of the "empty forest syndrome" were introduced by conservation biologist Kent H. Redford in a widely cited 1992 paper, "The Empty Forest". Empty forests are found particularly in the tropical forests of Central and West Africa, Amazonia, and Southeast Asia, where commercial and subsistence hunting for bushmeat has depleted populations of primates, ungulates, large birds, and other game animals.

An apparently pristine forest may be an empty forest, stripped of its large fauna

Causes

The emptying of forests is driven mainly by hunting, both for local subsistence and for the commercial bushmeat trade that supplies urban markets. Logging roads and settlement frontiers open previously remote forests to hunters, and modern firearms and wire snares allow unsustainable harvests. Selective removal targets the largest species first — elephants, great apes, tapirs, and large frugivorous birds — often long before the forest itself is cleared.

Ecological consequences

Large animals perform ecological functions that plants depend on. Many tropical trees rely on large frugivores to swallow and disperse their seeds; when the dispersers vanish, seedling recruitment collapses beneath parent trees, gradually changing forest composition toward smaller-seeded species. Elephants and other megafauna also shape forest structure, and their loss can reduce the carbon storage of the forest as large, dense-wooded trees decline. Predators and scavengers decline in turn, cascading through the food web — a process sometimes termed "defaunation".

Detection and conservation

Empty forests are deceptive: satellite imagery and forest-cover statistics show a healthy forest, so defaunation can proceed unnoticed by measures that count only deforestation. Conservation responses include protecting source populations in strictly guarded reserves, enforcing hunting laws, providing alternative protein sources to forest communities, and monitoring animal populations with camera traps and acoustic sensors rather than vegetation cover alone.
