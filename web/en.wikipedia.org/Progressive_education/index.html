Progressive education

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Learning by doing in a progressive classroom

Progressive education, or educational progressivism, is a pedagogical movement that began in the late 19th century and has persisted in various forms to the present. It emphasises learning by doing, the integration of curriculum around themes and projects rather than isolated subjects, problem-solving and critical thinking, collaborative and social learning, and education for democratic citizenship — in contrast to traditional teacher-centred instruction based on memorisation and standardised content.

History

Precursors include Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Johann Heinrich Pestalozzi and Friedrich Fröbel, founder of the kindergarten. The movement's most influential theorist was the American philosopher John Dewey, whose Laboratory School at the University of Chicago (founded 1896) and books such as Democracy and Education (1916) and Experience and Education (1938) argued that education should be based on the child's experience and active inquiry. Other leading figures included Francis Parker, William Heard Kilpatrick, who popularised the project method, and Marietta Johnson. The Progressive Education Association, founded in 1919, promoted the movement in the United States; Maria Montessori's method developed in parallel in Europe.

Principles and criticism

Common principles include attention to individual children's interests and development, the teacher as guide rather than lecturer, integrated and experiential curricula, and assessment through observation and work portfolios rather than tests alone. Critics have charged progressive schools with neglecting systematic knowledge, rigour and discipline, and historians note that genuine progressive practice remained confined largely to private and suburban schools. Nevertheless, many of its ideas — group work, projects, formative assessment, child-centred early-years practice — were absorbed into mainstream education.
