Developmental biology is the study of the process by which animals and plants grow and develop. It encompasses the biology of regeneration, asexual reproduction, metamorphosis, and the growth and differentiation of stem cells in the adult organism. Its central question is how a single fertilised egg gives rise to the ordered complexity of a multicellular body.

Core processes

After fertilisation, the zygote undergoes cleavage divisions, gastrulation lays down the germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm), and organogenesis builds organs. Cells differentiate as gene expression is switched on and off in space and time: morphogen gradients — of proteins such as Bicoid, Sonic hedgehog and BMP — pattern tissues, while signalling pathways (Wnt, Notch, Hedgehog, TGF-β) coordinate neighbours. Conrad Waddington's "epigenetic landscape" and Lewis Wolpert's "positional information" framed modern thinking; programmed cell death (apoptosis) sculpts structures such as the digits, and in embryos of animals like the bonobo and human alike, development unfolds over months of gestation.

Genetic toolkit and evo-devo

The discovery that Hox genes pattern the body axis in flies and vertebrates alike — celebrated in the homeobox work of the 1980s — revealed a deeply conserved genetic toolkit. Evolutionary developmental biology ("evo-devo") showed that changes in the regulation of these genes explain much morphological diversity. The cartilage models that precede bone, for example, are laid down under Sox9 control.

History and applications

From Aristotle's chick observations through Roux, Driesch and Spemann's organiser experiment (1924) to the molecular era, embryology became developmental biology. Its findings underpin stem-cell medicine, regenerative therapies, understanding of birth defects and the developmental origins of diseases such as cancer.
