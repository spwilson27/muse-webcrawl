Ford Flathead V8 engine

The Flathead brought V8 power to mass-market cars from 1932.
ManufacturerFord Motor Company
Production1932–1953 (US cars)
ConfigurationSide-valve (flathead) V8, 90°
Displacement221 cu in (3.6 L) initially; later 239 and 255 cu in
Power65 hp (1932) to about 125 hp (final versions)

The Ford Flathead V8 (often called simply the "flathead Ford") is a V8 engine with a flat cylinder head design produced by the Ford Motor Company and used in Ford, Mercury, and Lincoln-Zephyr vehicles from 1932 to 1953 in the United States, and longer in some other markets and commercial applications. It was the first independently designed V8 offered in a mass-produced, affordable automobile, and it is credited with democratizing eight-cylinder performance.

Design

The engine is a side-valve ("flathead") design: the valves sit beside the pistons in the block, operated directly by a central camshaft, under a flat, detachable cylinder head. The one-piece cast-iron block was an advanced, low-cost casting for its time. The original 1932 version displaced 221 cubic inches (3.6 L) and produced 65 horsepower, rising to 85 hp by 1934 and 100 hp in postwar 239 cu in form. Known weaknesses included an exhaust layout that routed gases through the block, contributing to overheating, and modest breathing inherent to the side-valve layout.

History

Introduced with the Ford Model 18 (widely called the Ford V-8) in March 1932, the engine made Ford cars favourites of both the public and outlaws — the bank robber Clyde Barrow famously wrote to Henry Ford praising the V-8's speed. After the Second World War, the flathead powered the 1949 Fords and Mercurys that helped define early hot rod culture: its availability, simplicity, and tuning potential made it the engine of choice for hot rodders, speed-equipment pioneers, and early stock-car racers in the late 1940s and 1950s.

Replacement and legacy

Ford replaced the flathead in its US cars with the overhead-valve Y-block V8 in 1954, though flathead production continued for some years in France (notably by Simca) and for military and industrial uses. Wards named it one of the ten best engines of the 20th century. The flathead remains a centerpiece of traditional hot rod and restoration culture, supported by a substantial reproduction-parts industry.
