Carl Gustav Jung (26 July 1875 – 6 June 1961) was a Swiss psychiatrist, psychotherapist and psychologist who founded the school of analytical psychology. Jung proposed and developed the concepts of the collective unconscious, archetypes, psychological complexes, and extraversion and introversion. His work has influenced psychiatry and the study of religion, literature and related fields.

Originally a close collaborator and designated successor of Sigmund Freud, Jung broke with Freud in 1912–1913 over the nature of the unconscious and the libido, and developed his own system, which he called analytical psychology to distinguish it from Freudian psychoanalysis.

Early life and career

Jung was born in Kesswil in the canton of Thurgau, the son of a Swiss Reformed pastor. He studied medicine at the University of Basel and in 1900 joined the Burghölzli psychiatric hospital in Zurich under Eugen Bleuler, where his word-association experiments established his reputation and led him to the concept of the emotionally charged "complex". His 1902 dissertation concerned the psychology of occult phenomena, based on séances with his cousin Hélène Preiswerk.

Freud and the break

Jung began corresponding with Freud in 1906 and met him in Vienna in 1907; Freud anointed him his "crown prince", and in 1910 Jung became the first president of the International Psychoanalytical Association. Their relationship deteriorated as Jung developed the idea that the libido is a general psychic energy, not purely sexual, and that the unconscious contains a deeper, inherited layer. The publication of Psychology of the Unconscious (1912) and Jung's resignation from the Association in 1914 marked the final split. Jung then underwent a period of intense self-experimentation with his own fantasies — documented in the calligraphic Red Book — from which his mature psychology emerged.

Analytical psychology

Jung divided the psyche into the ego, the personal unconscious (repressed or forgotten material organised around complexes) and the collective unconscious, a reservoir of universal patterns called archetypes — the Self, the Shadow, the Anima and Animus, the Persona, the Wise Old Man and others — expressed in myths, dreams and religious symbolism worldwide. Therapy aims at individuation, the integration of conscious and unconscious contents into a balanced whole, using dream analysis, active imagination and the interpretation of symbols. His Psychological Types (1921) introduced the attitudes of extraversion and introversion and four functions (thinking, feeling, sensation, intuition), later adapted into the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator. Late work extended to alchemy, religion, flying-saucer lore and the concept of synchronicity, developed with the physicist Wolfgang Pauli.

Legacy

Jung died in Küsnacht in 1961. His theories remain influential in psychotherapy, literary and religious studies and popular culture, while attracting criticism from scientific psychology for their resistance to empirical falsification. His conduct in the 1930s, as president (under Nazi pressure) of the international society for psychotherapy headquartered in Germany, has been the subject of lasting scholarly debate over alleged antisemitism, which Jung denied and which historians continue to assess.
