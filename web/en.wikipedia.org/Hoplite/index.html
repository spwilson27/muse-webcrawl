Hoplite

A hoplite was a citizen-soldier of the ancient Greek city-states who fought in the phalanx formation, equipped with a large round shield (the aspis, also called a hoplon), a long thrusting spear and heavy armour. Hoplites dominated Greek warfare from roughly the 7th to the 4th century BC.

Equipment

Hoplites supplied their own kit: a bronze or linen cuirass, greaves, a Corinthian or other helmet, the double-gripped aspis — whose rim rested on the shoulder and which protected the man to one's left as well as oneself — and the dory spear, with a short sword as a secondary weapon. The full panoply could weigh 20–30 kg.

Tactics

Armies met on flat ground and advanced in close order, shields overlapping. Battle often became a pushing contest (othismos) after the spear charge. Because hoplites were farmers serving for short campaigns, battles were usually brief and decisive, and pursuit of a broken enemy was limited.

Society and decline

The hoplite class — landowners prosperous enough to afford armour — underpinned the political claims of Greek citizenry, most extremely at Sparta, where the agoge produced full-time warriors. Hoplite warfare waned as light troops, cavalry and longer spears proved decisive: Iphicrates' reforms, the Theban oblique order at Leuctra (371 BC), and finally the Macedonian sarissa phalanx of Philip II and Alexander eclipsed the classical hoplite.
