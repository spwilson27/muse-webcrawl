Fractal

A fractal is a geometric shape containing detailed structure at arbitrarily small scales, usually having a fractal dimension strictly exceeding its topological dimension. Fractals exhibit self-similarity: magnified parts resemble the whole. The term was coined by Benoit Mandelbrot in 1975 from the Latin fractus ("broken"), and fractal geometry has found applications from computer graphics to the modelling of coastlines, clouds, blood vessels and markets.

History

Self-similar curves such as the Koch snowflake (1904) and sets studied by Cantor, Peano and Hausdorff were long treated as pathological curiosities. Mandelbrot unified them in The Fractal Geometry of Nature (1982), arguing that irregular, fragmented shapes are the norm in nature and can be measured by non-integer dimensions.

Characteristics

No single definition is universally accepted, but fractals typically show fine structure at all scales, are too irregular for Euclidean description, are often generated by simple recursive rules — such as iterated function systems — and possess a fractal dimension (for example Hausdorff dimension) greater than their topological dimension. The Mandelbrot set and Julia sets arise from iterating simple complex quadratic polynomials.

In nature and technology

Approximate fractals occur in river networks, lightning, broccoli, lungs and coastlines — Mandelbrot's famous question "How long is the coast of Britain?" showed coastline length depends on the scale of measurement. Fractal compression, fractal antennas, procedurally generated landscapes in film and games, and models of turbulence and finance all exploit fractal methods.
