Penetration aid

A penetration aid (or penaid) is a device or tactic employed by a ballistic missile warhead to increase the probability of penetrating an enemy's anti-ballistic missile defences. Penaids are carried by intercontinental and submarine-launched ballistic missiles and are deployed in space or during the early reentry phase, before the warhead reaches its target.

Types

Common penetration aids include:

  • Decoys — lightweight replicas of the warhead, often metallised balloons, designed to present a radar signature similar to that of the real reentry vehicle and so overwhelm or confuse defensive radars and interceptors.
  • Chaff — clouds of small metallic dipoles that create false radar returns.
  • Jammers and radar-absorbing materials — active electronic countermeasures and coatings that reduce or distort the warhead's radar cross-section.
  • Manoeuvring reentry vehicles — warheads able to change trajectory, complicating interception.
  • Multiple warheads — MIRVs themselves act in part as saturation aids, presenting defenders with more targets than can be engaged.

History

Penetration aids were developed extensively during the Cold War as the United States and Soviet Union deployed early anti-ballistic missile systems such as Safeguard and the Moscow ABM system. The existence of effective penaids was a central argument in debates over missile defence, since relatively cheap decoys could defeat expensive interceptors. Distinguishing decoys from warheads in the vacuum of space is difficult because lightweight objects follow the same ballistic trajectories as heavy ones; the atmosphere, however, filters out light decoys during reentry, a phenomenon exploited by defenders.

Arms control treaties generally did not ban penetration aids, although counting rules for warheads were shaped by their existence.
