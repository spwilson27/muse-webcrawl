Hymn

A hymn is a type of song, and partially synonymous with devotional song, specifically written for the purpose of adoration or prayer, and typically addressed to a deity or deities, or to a prominent figure or personification. The word hymn derives from Greek hymnos (ὕμνος), which means "a song of praise". A writer of hymns is known as a hymnist, and the singing or composition of hymns is called hymnody. Collections of hymns are known as hymnals or hymn books.

Although most familiar to speakers of English in the context of Christianity, hymns are also a fixture of other world religions, especially on the Indian subcontinent. Hymns also survive from the ancient world, such as the Homeric Hymns, the Egyptian Great Hymn to the Aten, and the hymns of the Rigveda in Hinduism.

Christian hymnody

Christian hymnody derives from the singing of psalms in Jewish worship and from New Testament references to singing "psalms and hymns and spiritual songs". Hymn singing was a defining feature of the Protestant Reformation: Martin Luther wrote many hymns, including "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God". The metrical psalms of the Reformed churches, the chorales of Lutheranism, and the explosive hymn-writing of the 18th century — Isaac Watts, John Newton, and above all Charles Wesley, who wrote more than 6,000 hymns — shaped worship across the English-speaking world. Well-known hymns include "Amazing Grace", "Abide with Me", and "Joy to the World".

Structure

Hymns are usually sung to a hymn tune in a regular metre, with several stanzas sharing the same melody. Common metres include Common Metre (8.6.8.6) and Long Metre (8.8.8.8). Hymnals index hymns by metre so that texts and tunes can be combined.
