Coffea arabica

Coffea arabica, commonly known as Arabica coffee, is a species of flowering plant in the family Rubiaceae. Native to the highlands of Ethiopia and South Sudan, it is the most widely cultivated and economically important coffee species, accounting for roughly 60 percent of world coffee production.

Description

The plant is a small tree or shrub with glossy, dark green leaves and fragrant white flowers. Its fruit, the coffee cherry, turns bright red when ripe and normally contains two seeds — the coffee beans. C. arabica is an allotetraploid, the result of hybridisation between Coffea canephora (robusta) and Coffea eugenioides.

Cultivation

Arabica grows best at high altitudes (roughly 1,000–2,000 metres) in tropical climates with well-distributed rainfall. It is generally considered to produce coffee of finer flavour than robusta, with lower caffeine content, but it is more susceptible to pests and to coffee leaf rust. Major producing countries include Brazil, Colombia, Ethiopia and Honduras. Well-known cultivars include Typica, Bourbon, Caturra, Geisha and SL28.
