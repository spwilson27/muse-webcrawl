Cholecalciferol

Cholecalciferol, also known as vitamin D3, is a type of vitamin D that is made by the skin when exposed to ultraviolet light from sunlight. It is also found in some foods and can be taken as a dietary supplement. Cholecalciferol is converted in the liver to calcifediol (25-hydroxycholecalciferol), which is then converted in the kidneys to calcitriol (1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol), the hormonally active form of vitamin D.

Medical use

Cholecalciferol is used to prevent and treat vitamin D deficiency, which can cause rickets in children and osteomalacia and osteoporosis in adults. It is commonly included in multivitamin preparations and is used to fortify foods such as milk. Vitamin D plays a central role in calcium and phosphate metabolism, bone health, and the function of the immune and neuromuscular systems.

Sources

Sunlight exposure is the major natural source for most people: ultraviolet B radiation converts 7-dehydrocholesterol in the skin to pre-vitamin D3, which isomerises to cholecalciferol. Dietary sources include oily fish such as salmon and mackerel, fish liver oils, and fortified foods. Unlike ergocalciferol (vitamin D2), which comes from plants and fungi, cholecalciferol is the form produced in vertebrate skin.

Toxicity

Excessive intake of cholecalciferol can cause vitamin D toxicity, leading to hypercalcaemia with symptoms including nausea, weakness, frequent urination, and kidney damage. Because of its potency, concentrated cholecalciferol is also used as a rodenticide, causing fatal hypercalcaemia in rodents.

Chemistry

Cholecalciferol has the chemical formula C27H44O. It is a secosteroid, with a broken ring in its steroid structure. It is practically insoluble in water and soluble in fats and oils.
