The Wars of the Diadochi (from Greek Diadochoi, "Successors") were a series of conflicts fought between the generals of Alexander the Great over the division of his vast empire after his death at Babylon in 323 BC. Lasting, in several phases, until about 275 BC, they ended with the establishment of the great Hellenistic kingdoms.

Background

Alexander died leaving no adult heir: his son Alexander IV was born posthumously. A settlement at Babylon made Philip III Arrhidaeus and the infant joint kings, with Perdiccas as regent, and distributed the satrapies among the leading generals, including Ptolemy (Egypt), Antigonus (Phrygia), Lysimachus (Thrace), Seleucus and Eumenes.

Course of the wars

The struggle fell into successive wars. Perdiccas was murdered invading Egypt in 321/320 BC. Antigonus Monophthalmus came nearest to reuniting the empire before being defeated and killed at the Battle of Ipsus in 301 BC by a coalition of Lysimachus, Seleucus, Cassander and Ptolemy. The murder of Alexander IV ended the old royal line. Later fighting, including Seleucus's victory over Lysimachus at Corupedium (281 BC) and the Galatian invasions, fixed the eventual map.

Outcome

Three durable dynasties emerged: the Ptolemies in Egypt, the Seleucids in Asia, and the Antigonids in Macedon — the kingdoms that structured the Hellenistic world until the Roman conquests.
