The Guianas

The Guianas, also collectively called Guiana, are a group of three territories on the northeastern coast of South America: Guyana (formerly British Guiana), Suriname (formerly Dutch Guiana), and French Guiana, an overseas department of France. Together they form the core of the wider Guiana Shield region, which also extends into Venezuela, Brazil, and Colombia.

Geography

The Guianas lie between the Orinoco and Amazon river deltas, bounded by the Atlantic Ocean to the northeast. The region is characterized by a narrow coastal plain of fertile, partly below-sea-level polder land where most of the population lives, and a vast forested interior of the Guiana Highlands, including tepuis (table-top mountains) such as Mount Roraima and waterfalls such as Kaieteur Falls. The climate is tropical, with two rainy seasons.

History

The coast was inhabited by Arawak and Carib peoples before European colonization. From the 17th century, the Dutch, British, and French established rival plantation colonies worked by enslaved Africans and later indentured laborers from India, Java, and China. The borders were settled by treaties in the 19th century, though boundary disputes persist, notably between Guyana and Venezuela over the Essequibo region. Guyana became independent in 1966 and Suriname in 1975; French Guiana remains an integral part of France and hosts the Guiana Space Centre at Kourou, Europe's principal spaceport.

Politics and economy

The three territories are unusual in South America for their colonial languages — English in Guyana, Dutch in Suriname, and French in French Guiana — and their cultural orientation toward the Caribbean. Economies have historically depended on sugar, bauxite, gold, and timber; major offshore oil discoveries off Guyana since 2015 have transformed that country's economy, making it one of the fastest-growing in the world. The three territories cooperate through the Guiana Shield Facility on conservation of one of the world's largest remaining tracts of tropical rainforest.
