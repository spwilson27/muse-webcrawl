Jules Laforgue

Jules Laforgue (16 August 1860 – 20 August 1887) was a French poet of the Symbolist and Decadent movements, regarded as one of the inventors of free verse (vers libre) and a major influence on modernist poetry in English through T. S. Eliot and Ezra Pound. His work blends melancholy lyricism, irony, colloquial diction, and philosophical pessimism drawn from Schopenhauer and Eduard von Hartmann.

Life

Laforgue was born in Montevideo, Uruguay, to French parents; his father was a teacher and later a bank employee. The family returned to France in 1866, and Laforgue was educated at Tarbes and at the Lycée Fontanes in Paris. After failing the baccalauréat examinations for a licence, he worked as a secretary to the art critic and collector Charles Ephrussi. From 1881 to 1886 he served as French reader to the German Empress Augusta in Berlin, a comfortable post that left him time to write but which he found stifling. He married Leah Lee in England in December 1886, but by then he was already ill with tuberculosis; he died in Paris in August 1887, aged twenty-seven.

Works

Laforgue's first collection, Les Complaintes (1885), presents the poet as a Pierrot-like complainer, mixing slang, neologisms, and liturgical echoes. L'Imitation de Notre-Dame la Lune (1886) develops his lunar, Pierrot persona. His prose Moralités légendaires (1887) ironically retells myths and stories — Hamlet, Salomé, Pan, Andromeda — in a modern, deflationary style, and the posthumous Derniers vers (1890) contains his freest verse. He also translated Walt Whitman into French, helping introduce Whitman to French readers.

Legacy

Through translations and the advocacy of Pound, Laforgue's ironic, self-conscious voice shaped early modernism; Eliot acknowledged that Laforgue, together with Baudelaire, taught him the poetic possibilities of his own idiom. His combination of high lyricism and colloquial deflation remains distinctive.
