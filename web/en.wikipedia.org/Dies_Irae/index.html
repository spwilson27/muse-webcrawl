Dies Irae

Dies Irae (Latin for "Day of Wrath") is a Latin sequence hymn describing the Last Judgment, when the dead are summoned before God's throne. Probably written in the 13th century and traditionally attributed to the Franciscan Thomas of Celano, the poem opens "Dies iræ, dies illa / Solvet sæclum in favilla" ("Day of wrath and doom impending, David's word with Sibyl's blending, heaven and earth in ashes ending"). It became a prescribed part of the Roman Catholic Requiem Mass until the liturgical reforms following the Second Vatican Council.

Text and music

The poem comprises rhymed tercets in trochaic metre, building through images of the trumpet summoning the dead, the book of deeds opened, and the plea for mercy in the "Recordare" and "Lacrimosa" sections. Its plainchant melody is one of the most quoted in Western music: composers from Mozart and Berlioz to Verdi and Rachmaninoff set or alluded to it.

Musical quotations

The chant's first phrase serves as a musical emblem of death and judgment. Berlioz used it in the finale of the Symphonie fantastique; Liszt built the Totentanz on it; Saint-Saëns quoted it in Danse macabre; Rachmaninoff wove it through numerous works as a personal motto; and it recurs in countless film scores. Within Requiem settings by Mozart, Verdi, Dvořák and others, the Dies Irae sequence typically forms the dramatic centrepiece.
