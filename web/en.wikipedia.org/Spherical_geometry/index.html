Spherical geometry is the geometry of the two-dimensional surface of a sphere. It is an example of a geometry that is not Euclidean. Two practical applications of the principles of spherical geometry are navigation and astronomy.

Principles

In spherical geometry, the counterpart of a straight line is a great circle — a circle on the sphere whose centre coincides with the centre of the sphere. The shortest path between two points on the surface runs along the great circle joining them. Unlike in Euclidean geometry, there are no parallel lines: any two great circles intersect, in two antipodal points. The angles of a spherical triangle sum to more than 180°, the excess being proportional to the triangle's area (Girard's theorem): for a sphere of radius R, a triangle with angles A, B, C has area R2(A + B + C − π).

Spherical trigonometry

Spherical trigonometry studies triangles on the sphere. Key relations include the spherical law of cosines, cos c = cos a cos b + sin a sin b cos C, and the spherical law of sines, sin a / sin A = sin b / sin B = sin c / sin C, where a, b, c are side lengths measured as angles at the centre. The haversine formula, derived from these, is used to compute great-circle distances between points given their latitudes and longitudes.

History

The subject was developed in antiquity for astronomy: Menelaus of Alexandria wrote the Sphaerica in the 1st–2nd century CE, the earliest surviving work on spherical trigonometry. Medieval Islamic astronomers such as al-Battānī and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi greatly advanced it, and it remained the standard mathematics of celestial navigation into the modern era.

Relation to other geometries

Spherical geometry has constant positive curvature and is closely related to elliptic geometry. It violates Euclid's parallel postulate in the strongest way: through a point not on a given line, no line can be drawn that avoids intersecting it. On small scales, it is indistinguishable from Euclidean geometry, which is why it matters chiefly over planetary distances.
