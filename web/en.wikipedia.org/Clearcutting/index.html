Clearcutting

Clearcutting, clearfelling or clearcut logging is a forestry and logging practice in which most or all trees in an area are uniformly cut down. It is used to create even-aged stands and to harvest and regenerate species that require full sunlight, such as Douglas-fir, aspen and many pines. Along with shelterwood and seed tree harvests, it is a silvicultural system used by foresters to create and manage even-aged forests.

Practice

In a typical clearcut, all merchantable trees are removed in one operation, sometimes leaving scattered reserve trees, snags or riparian buffer strips for wildlife and water protection. The site is then regenerated naturally from seed or by planting seedlings. Clearcut sizes are regulated in many jurisdictions; in British Columbia, for example, cutblocks are generally limited to 40–60 hectares, and in parts of Europe much smaller openings are customary.

Environmental effects

Clearcutting is controversial because of its visual impact and its effects on wildlife habitat, soil erosion, stream sedimentation and carbon storage. Removing the canopy increases sunlight and temperature at the forest floor, which can favour some early-successional species while harming shade-dependent ones. Defenders argue that clearcutting mimics natural stand-replacing disturbances such as wildfire and windthrow, and that properly managed clearcuts regenerate into young forest of high habitat value.

Alternatives

Selection cutting, shelterwood systems and variable-retention harvesting retain more structure; the choice among them depends on species ecology, economics and management objectives.
