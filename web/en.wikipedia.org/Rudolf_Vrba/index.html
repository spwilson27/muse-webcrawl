Rudolf Vrba (born Walter Rosenberg; 11 September 1924 – 27 March 2006) was a Slovak Jew who escaped from the Auschwitz concentration camp in April 1944, aged 19, together with Alfréd Wetzler. The report they dictated to Jewish officials in Slovakia — the Vrba–Wetzler report — gave one of the earliest and most detailed accounts of the mass murder taking place at Auschwitz, including descriptions of the gas chambers and crematoria and estimates of the numbers killed.

Escape

Deported to Auschwitz in June 1942, Rosenberg worked in the camp's warehouses sorting the property of murdered arrivals, a position that allowed him to observe the killing process closely and memorise figures. On 7 April 1944 he and Wetzler hid in a hollowed-out woodpile outside the inner perimeter, dousing the area with petrol-soaked tobacco to defeat tracker dogs, and waited out the three-day search before cutting through the wire and walking south to Slovakia, reaching Žilina after ten days.

The report and its impact

The 32-page report was compiled in late April 1944 and reached Western Allied and neutral channels by June. Its release contributed to international pressure — including warnings from the United States and Britain, and an appeal from Pope Pius XII — on Hungary's regent Miklós Horthy, who halted the deportation of Hungarian Jews in early July 1944, sparing much of Budapest's Jewish community. Vrba later wrote the memoir I Cannot Forgive (1963), published in English as Escape from Auschwitz: I Cannot Forgive.

Later life

After the war Vrba studied chemistry in Prague, taking a doctorate, and emigrated to Israel and then Britain, where he worked for the Medical Research Council. From 1976 he was a professor of pharmacology at the University of British Columbia in Vancouver, publishing widely on diabetes research. He gave evidence at the 1985 Toronto trial of Holocaust denier Ernst Zündel. Vrba maintained, controversially, that Hungarian Jewish leaders had suppressed his report; the claim sparked a long historical debate.
