Mobius strip

The Mobius strip, or Möbius strip (also spelled Mobius band), is a surface with only one side and only one boundary curve. It is formed by taking a strip of paper, giving one end a half-twist (180 degrees), and joining the ends together. The Möbius strip is the classic example of a non-orientable surface in mathematics.

Discovery

The strip was discovered independently by the German mathematicians August Ferdinand Möbius and Johann Benedict Listing in 1858. Möbius encountered it while working on the theory of polyhedra.

Properties

A traveller walking along the centre line of the strip returns to the starting point having traversed both "sides" of the original strip without ever crossing an edge — demonstrating that the surface has just one side. Cutting a Möbius strip along its centre line yields a single longer strip with a full twist rather than two separate bands; cutting it off-centre produces two linked strips, one of them a Möbius strip. Topologically, the Möbius strip can be described as a square with one pair of opposite edges identified in opposite directions. It is a ruled surface and has Euler characteristic zero.

Applications and cultural presence

Möbius geometry appears in conveyor belts (which wear evenly on "both" sides), electronic resistors, and molecular chemistry — molecules with Möbius topology have been synthesised. The recycling symbol, designed by Gary Anderson in 1970, is based on a Möbius strip. The shape has inspired art, including works by M. C. Escher (whose Möbius Strip II shows ants crawling on the surface), architecture, and literature.
