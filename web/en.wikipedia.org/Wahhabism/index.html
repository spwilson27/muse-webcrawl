Wahhabism is an Islamic revivalist and fundamentalist movement within Sunni Islam founded by the Arabian scholar Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab (1703–1792). It is the official form of Islam in Saudi Arabia, and its adherents — who generally prefer the names Salafi or Muwahhidun ("unitarians") — regard the term "Wahhabi" as a pejorative coined by opponents.

Doctrine

The movement centres on tawhid, the absolute oneness of God. Ibn Abd al-Wahhab condemned practices he regarded as polytheistic innovations (bid'ah) and idolatry (shirk), including the veneration of saints, pilgrimages to tombs and shrines, and the celebration of the Prophet's birthday. He called Muslims back to strict adherence to the Quran and hadith as understood by the earliest generations (salaf), in the Hanbali legal tradition shaped by Ibn Taymiyyah.

History

In 1744 Ibn Abd al-Wahhab formed a pact with Muhammad ibn Saud, ruler of Diriyah: religious authority would rest with the scholar's descendants (the Al ash-Sheikh) and political rule with the House of Saud. This alliance drove the expansion of the first Saudi state across Arabia, including the sacking of Karbala in 1802 and the capture of Mecca and Medina, before Ottoman-Egyptian forces destroyed Diriyah in 1818. The movement survived through subsequent Saudi states and, after Ibn Saud's conquests of the 1920s, became the established creed of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia proclaimed in 1932.

Global influence and criticism

From the 1970s, Saudi wealth funded Wahhabi-influenced mosques, schools, and publishing worldwide, spreading the doctrine far beyond Arabia. Critics blame its intolerance for inspiring jihadist movements, while its defenders distinguish the movement from terrorism and note that Saudi authorities themselves have been targets of al-Qaeda. Since 2017, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman has pledged to return Saudi Arabia to a "moderate Islam", curbing the religious police and the movement's public role.
