Dissolution of the monasteries

The dissolution of the monasteries, occasionally referred to as the suppression of the monasteries, was the set of administrative and legal processes between 1536 and 1541 by which Henry VIII disbanded religious houses in England, Wales and Ireland, expropriated their income, disposed of their assets and provided for their former personnel.

Background

At the beginning of Henry's reign there were more than 800 religious houses in England, home to perhaps 7,000–9,000 religious men and women, and controlling an estimated income greater than that of the Crown itself. Monastic reform and suppression of individual houses had precedents in England and elsewhere in Europe, and the break with Rome in 1534 placed the English Church under royal supremacy. In 1535 Thomas Cromwell, the king's chief minister, ordered a nationwide survey, the Valor Ecclesiasticus, and a visitation of the monasteries whose reports (often exaggerated) alleged widespread corruption.

The process

The Act of Suppression of 1536 dissolved about 300 smaller houses with incomes under £200 a year. The Pilgrimage of Grace, a major rising in the north partly provoked by the closures, was suppressed, and its leaders — including several abbots — were executed. From 1538 the larger abbeys were induced or compelled to "surrender" to the Crown one by one; a second Act in 1539 confirmed the process. The last great house to fall was Waltham Abbey in March 1540.

Disposal of assets

A new government department, the Court of Augmentations, administered the seized estates. Land was sold or granted to courtiers, gentry and speculators, creating a broad class with a vested interest in the religious settlement. Lead was stripped from roofs, bells and plate were taken for the king, and many buildings were quarried for stone, leaving the ruins that still mark the English landscape. Monks, canons and nuns received pensions; some became secular clergy, but most nuns had few options open to them.

Consequences

The dissolution destroyed the monastic libraries and much of England's medieval art, though antiquaries such as John Leland rescued some books. It ended the monasteries' role in education, hospitality and poor relief, and transferred perhaps a fifth of England's landed wealth to the Crown and its purchasers. When Mary I restored papal authority in the 1550s, most former owners refused to give up their lands, and few houses were refounded.
