Intervertebral disc disease

Intervertebral disc disease (IVDD) is a condition in which the cushioning discs between the vertebrae of the spinal column degenerate, bulge or rupture, pressing on the spinal cord or nerve roots. The term is used in human medicine for disc degeneration and herniation, and is especially common in veterinary medicine, where it is a major cause of back pain and paralysis in dogs.

In humans

With ageing, intervertebral discs lose water content and height, a process called degenerative disc disease. A disc may bulge (protrusion) or its soft nucleus pulposus may herniate through a tear in the outer annulus fibrosus, compressing nearby nerves. In the lumbar spine this commonly causes sciatica — pain radiating down the leg — while cervical herniation can cause arm pain, weakness or, in severe cases, myelopathy from spinal cord compression. Most episodes improve with conservative care including exercise, physiotherapy and analgesia; surgery such as discectomy is reserved for persistent neurological deficits or intractable pain.

In dogs

In veterinary practice IVDD is classified into Hansen type I herniation, an acute extrusion seen mainly in chondrodystrophic breeds such as Dachshunds, French Bulldogs and Corgis, and type II, a slower protrusion in older, larger dogs. Signs range from back pain and reluctance to jump to ataxia and complete hind-limb paralysis with loss of bladder control. Diagnosis relies on neurological examination and imaging (MRI or CT), and treatment ranges from strict rest and medication to decompressive surgery, whose prognosis depends on whether deep pain perception is preserved.

Prevention and management

Maintaining healthy body weight, regular exercise and, in predisposed dog breeds, avoiding high-impact jumping reduce risk. Weight control is considered the single most important modifiable factor in dogs.
