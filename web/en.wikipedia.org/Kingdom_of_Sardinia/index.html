The Kingdom of Sardinia, also known as the Kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, was a state in Southern Europe from the early 18th century until the proclamation of the Kingdom of Italy in 1861. Despite its name, the kingdom's political and economic centre was not the island of Sardinia but the mainland possessions of the House of Savoy, above all Piedmont, with its capital at Turin. The kingdom also included Savoy, Nice, and (after 1815) Liguria.

History

Acquisition of Sardinia

By the Treaty of Utrecht (1713), Duke Victor Amadeus II of Savoy had received the Kingdom of Sicily. In 1720, under the Treaty of The Hague, he was obliged to exchange Sicily for the less wealthy island kingdom of Sardinia, ruled by Austria. From that date the Savoyard state took the name Kingdom of Sardinia, although the island itself long remained a neglected appendage of the mainland state.

The 18th century

Under Charles Emmanuel III and Victor Amadeus III the kingdom participated in the wars of the Polish and Austrian successions, gaining territory in Lombardy. In 1792 revolutionary France annexed Savoy and Nice, and in 1798 French troops occupied Piedmont; the royal family withdrew to Sardinia, which remained in their hands, protected by the British navy. The Congress of Vienna (1815) restored the kingdom in full and added the former Republic of Genoa, giving Piedmont a major port.

The Statuto Albertino and the Risorgimento

In March 1848, amid revolution across Europe, King Charles Albert granted the Statuto Albertino, a constitution that remained in force, after 1861, as the constitution of united Italy until 1948. The kingdom then went to war with Austria in the First Italian War of Independence (1848–49) but was defeated at Custoza and Novara; Charles Albert abdicated in favour of his son Victor Emmanuel II.

Cavour and unification

Under the premiership of Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour (from 1852), the kingdom modernised its economy, built railways, reformed its finances and entered the Crimean War to gain a seat at the peace congress. By the secret Plombières agreement of 1858, Cavour secured the alliance of Napoleon III's France. In the war of 1859 Austria was driven from Lombardy. In 1860, following plebiscites, Tuscany and the central duchies were annexed; Giuseppe Garibaldi's Expedition of the Thousand conquered Sicily and Naples, whose territories were handed to Victor Emmanuel II. In return for French support, Savoy and Nice were ceded to France.

On 17 March 1861 the parliament at Turin proclaimed the Kingdom of Italy, with Victor Emmanuel II as its first king. The Kingdom of Sardinia thereby ceased to exist as a separate state, having become the nucleus of the unified Italian kingdom.
