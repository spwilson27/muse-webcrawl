Transatlantic telegraph cable


Laying cable from the SS Great Eastern

A transatlantic telegraph cable is a submarine communications cable laid across the Atlantic Ocean to carry telegraphy between North America and Europe. The first durable transatlantic connection was achieved in 1866, transforming communication between the continents from weeks (by ship) to minutes.

Early attempts

The project was promoted by American businessman Cyrus West Field and his Atlantic Telegraph Company, with scientific advice from William Thomson (later Lord Kelvin) and others. A first cable, completed in August 1858 between Ireland and Newfoundland, allowed Queen Victoria and President James Buchanan to exchange greetings, but it failed after a few weeks when excessive voltage destroyed its insulation. Further attempts in 1865 broke mid-ocean.

Success

In July 1866 the steamship Great Eastern — by far the largest ship of her time and the only one able to carry the whole cable — successfully laid a cable from Valentia Island, Ireland, to Heart's Content, Newfoundland. The lost 1865 cable was also recovered and completed, giving two working lines. Cable construction used a copper conductor insulated with gutta-percha and armoured with iron wires.

Transatlantic cables multiplied thereafter, cutting message times and tariffs and binding together news, finance and diplomacy. They were eventually superseded for telephony by coaxial cables (TAT-1, 1956) and later by fibre-optic systems, though submarine cables remain the backbone of intercontinental data traffic.
