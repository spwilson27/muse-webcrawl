The pascal (symbol: Pa) is the unit of pressure in the International System of Units (SI). It is defined as one newton per square metre (N/m²), and is named after the French mathematician, physicist and philosopher Blaise Pascal, known for his work on hydrostatics and the behaviour of fluids.

Definition

One pascal is the pressure exerted by a force of one newton acting uniformly over an area of one square metre perpendicular to the force. In SI base units, 1 Pa = 1 kg·m−1·s−2. The unit was adopted by the General Conference on Weights and Measures in 1971.

Common multiples and usage

The pascal is a small unit for most everyday purposes. Standard atmospheric pressure is 101,325 Pa, commonly expressed as 101.325 kilopascals (kPa). Meteorologists often report atmospheric pressure in hectopascals (hPa), numerically equal to the older millibar: average sea-level pressure is about 1,013 hPa. Engineers use the megapascal (MPa) for material stress and the gigapascal (GPa) for elastic moduli and very high pressures, such as those studied in diamond-anvil-cell experiments. Blood pressure is conventionally measured in millimetres of mercury rather than pascals.

Examples

A dollar bill resting flat exerts a pressure of roughly 1 Pa. A human bite can produce pressures of the order of a megapascal at the teeth. The pressure at the centre of the Earth is estimated at about 360 GPa. Pascal's law — the principle that pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished in all directions — underlies hydraulic machinery such as lifts and brakes.
