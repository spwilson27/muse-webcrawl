Hou Jing

Hou Jing (Chinese: 侯景; pinyin: Hóu Jǐng; died 552), courtesy name Wanjing (萬景), was a Chinese military general and regicide of the Eastern Wei and Liang dynasties during the Northern and Southern dynasties period. A general first of Northern Wei and then of Eastern Wei, he defected to the southern Liang dynasty in 547 with the territory he controlled, but, suspecting that Liang's Emperor Wu would trade him back to Eastern Wei in exchange for the emperor's nephew Xiao Yuanming, he rebelled in 548—an upheaval known as the Hou Jing Disturbance (侯景之亂). He besieged and captured the Liang capital Jiankang, starving Emperor Wu to death and devastating the south, before being defeated and killed in 552.

Early career

Hou Jing was born in Shuozhou (in modern Inner Mongolia) and was of Xianbei or mixed Hu ancestry. He rose through the armies of the warlord Gao Huan, founder of Eastern Wei's power, and came to control the Henan region with an army of about 100,000 men. After Gao Huan's death in 547, distrusting Gao's son Gao Cheng, Hou surrendered his territory to Liang.

Rebellion against Liang

After Eastern Wei defeated a Liang expedition and captured Xiao Yuanming, Emperor Wu negotiated an exchange that would return Hou Jing to the north. Learning of this, Hou Jing revolted in the eighth month of 548, crossed the Yangtze, and besieged the imperial palace city (Taicheng) in Jiankang. The siege reduced the wealthy capital to famine and cannibalism; Emperor Wu, taken prisoner, died in 549, reportedly after being refused food. Hou installed and deposed puppet emperors—Xiao Gang (Emperor Jianwen) and then Xiao Dong—forcing marriages into the imperial clan, and in 551 he usurped the throne himself, declaring a dynasty called Han.

Defeat and death

Liang loyalist forces under Wang Sengbian and Chen Baxian advanced from the west and recaptured Jiankang in 552 after hard fighting, including battles on the Yangtze and at姑孰 (Gushu). Hou Jing fled east by boat and was killed by his own attendants; his body was dismembered and his head sent to Jiangling. The rebellion shattered Liang's prosperity, caused catastrophic population loss in the capital region, and fatally weakened the dynasty, which soon fragmented among Liang, Western Liang, and the rising Chen dynasty.
