Nerve agent

ClassOrganophosphorus chemical weapons
ExamplesSarin, VX, tabun, soman, Novichok agents
MechanismAcetylcholinesterase inhibition
AntidotesAtropine, pralidoxime, diazepam

Nerve agents, sometimes also called nerve gases, are a class of organic chemicals that disrupt the mechanisms by which nerves transfer messages to organs. The disruption is caused by the blocking of acetylcholinesterase (AChE), an enzyme that catalyses the breakdown of acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter. Nerve agents are among the deadliest chemical weapons.

Effects

Exposure causes constriction of the pupils (miosis), profuse salivation and sweating, convulsions, involuntary urination and defecation, and death by asphyxiation from loss of control of the respiratory muscles. Effects appear within seconds to hours depending on the agent and route of exposure. Antidotes such as atropine and pralidoxime, given promptly, can save life, and anticonvulsants such as diazepam are used to control seizures.

History

The first nerve agents — tabun, sarin, and soman (the "G-series") — were discovered in Germany in the 1930s during pesticide research, and were weaponised by Nazi Germany but not used in battle. The "V-series," including VX, was developed in Britain in the 1950s. Nerve agents were used by Iraq in the Iran–Iraq War, in the 1995 Tokyo subway sarin attack by Aum Shinrikyo, and in the Syrian civil war.

Control

Nerve agents are banned by the Chemical Weapons Convention of 1993, under which declared stockpiles were destroyed under international verification.
