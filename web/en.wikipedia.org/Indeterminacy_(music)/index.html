Indeterminacy (music)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Indeterminacy is a composing approach in which some aspects of a musical work are left open to chance or to the interpreter's free choice. The term is most often associated with the American composer John Cage, who developed chance procedures from the early 1950s, and with the wider movement of aleatoric music in post-war avant-garde composition. In an indeterminate work, the score underdetermines the sounding result, so that no two performances are identical.

Cage and chance composition

Cage's interest in chance grew from his study of the I Ching, the Chinese book of divination, which he used to make compositional decisions in the Music of Changes (1951). Strictly, Cage distinguished between chance composition, in which the score is fixed but was generated by chance operations, and indeterminacy proper, in which the work is composed so that its performance is not fully determined in advance. His most famous indeterminate work is 4′33″ (1952), in which the performer makes no intentional sounds and the "music" consists of the ambient sounds of the performance space.

To realise indeterminate works, composers developed graphic scores (drawings, shapes and spatial notation replacing conventional staves), text scores (verbal instructions, as in La Monte Young's and Yoko Ono's pieces), and mobile-form or open-form structures in which the performer chooses the order of composed fragments.

Aleatoric music in Europe

European composers including Pierre Boulez, Karlheinz Stockhausen and Witold Lutosławski explored related ideas under the term aleatoric music (from Latin alea, "dice"). Boulez's Third Piano Sonata allows the performer to choose routes through its movements; Stockhausen's Klavierstück XI (1956) presents fragments on a single large sheet to be played in an order chosen in performance; Lutosławski used "aleatory counterpoint", in which ensemble players repeat short figures independently within coordinated sections, a technique he used from Jeux vénitiens (1961) onward. The extent of freedom granted varied widely: Boulez in particular criticised pure chance as an abdication of compositional responsibility, preferring controlled choice.

Related movements

Indeterminacy overlaps with several other mid-twentieth-century currents: experimental music in the United States (Morton Feldman, Earle Brown, Christian Wolff), Cornelius Cardew and the English Scratch Orchestra, free improvisation, and later process and generative music. Its ideas also fed into conceptual art and the Fluxus movement.

Criticism and legacy

Critics questioned whether indeterminate music could support notions of authorship and quality, and whether chance procedures merely displaced compositional choice to the choice of procedure. Nevertheless, indeterminacy permanently enlarged the concept of a musical work, the role of the performer, and the materials of music — admitting silence, environmental sound and everyday action — and its influence extends through contemporary composition, sound art and popular experimental music.
