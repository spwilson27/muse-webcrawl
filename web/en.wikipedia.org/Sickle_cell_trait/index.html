Sickle cell trait

Sickle cell trait
GenotypeHeterozygous HbAS (one βS allele)
GeneHBB, chromosome 11
HaemoglobinRoughly 55–60% HbA, 35–45% HbS
Frequency~8% of African Americans; much higher in parts of sub-Saharan Africa
Key featureUsually asymptomatic; protects against severe malaria

Sickle cell trait describes a condition in which a person has one abnormal allele of the haemoglobin beta gene (is heterozygous), but does not display the severe symptoms of sickle cell disease that occur in a person who has two copies of that allele (is homozygous). Those with sickle cell trait are thus carriers of the sickle cell allele, and can pass it on to their children. Normally, a person has two copies of the β-globin gene; in sickle cell trait, one is replaced with the βS allele, resulting in a mixture of normal haemoglobin A and haemoglobin S.

Sickle cell trait affects approximately 300 million people worldwide and about 8 per cent of African Americans. It is most common in regions historically endemic for malaria — sub-Saharan Africa, parts of India, and the Mediterranean and Middle East — because carriers are substantially protected against severe Plasmodium falciparum malaria, a classic example of balancing selection.

Health implications

People with sickle cell trait are generally healthy, with normal life expectancy and normal blood counts; their red blood cells sickle only under extreme conditions of low oxygen, severe dehydration, or intense exertion. Nevertheless, trait carriers have increased risk of certain complications, including blood in the urine (haematuria) from renal papillary necrosis, impaired urinary concentrating ability, splenic infarction at high altitude, and — rarely — exertional collapse and sudden death during extreme physical training, a risk that prompted screening policies in the US military and collegiate athletics. Studies have also associated the trait with a modestly increased risk of chronic kidney disease and venous thromboembolism.

Inheritance and screening

If two carriers have a child, each pregnancy carries a 25 per cent chance of sickle cell disease (HbSS), a 50 per cent chance of the trait, and a 25 per cent chance of neither. Newborn screening programmes in the United States and several other countries identify both disease and trait incidentally, raising ethical and counselling questions about disclosure of carrier status. Genetic counselling is recommended for carriers planning families.

Sports and military policy

Following deaths of athletes with sickle cell trait during conditioning drills, the NCAA adopted testing or waiver requirements for student athletes, and the US armed forces screen recruits and apply universal precautions (hydration, rest, gradual acclimatisation) that have greatly reduced exertional deaths. Consensus guidance emphasises that carriers can safely participate in sports when such precautions are followed.
