Serif

CategoryTypography
ExamplesTimes New Roman, Garamond, Baskerville, Georgia, Caslon
ContrastSans-serif
OriginRoman capitals / Dutch typography

In typography, a serif is a small line or stroke regularly attached to the end of a larger stroke in a letter or symbol within a particular font. A typeface or font family making use of serifs is called a serif typeface (or "serifed"), whereas one without serifs is a sans-serif typeface (from French sans, "without").

Origins and etymology

Serifs derive from Roman inscriptional capitals, in which letters were first outlined with a brush and then chiselled into stone, leaving small flared terminals. The word itself may come from the Dutch schreef, meaning a line or stroke drawn with a pen. Serif typefaces proliferated after the invention of printing; early examples include the types used by Nicolas Jenson in the 1470s and Aldus Manutius.

Classification

Serif typefaces are commonly grouped into Old Style (e.g. Garamond, Caslon — low contrast, bracketed serifs), Transitional (e.g. Baskerville, Times New Roman — higher contrast, more vertical stress), Didone or Modern (e.g. Bodoni, Didot — extreme contrast, hairline serifs), and Slab serif (e.g. Rockwell, Courier — thick, block-like serifs). Slab serifs became popular for 19th-century advertising.

Use and legibility

Serif fonts have been the conventional choice for printed body text such as books and newspapers. Whether serifs actually improve reading speed or legibility has been debated for decades; research suggests familiarity and good spacing matter more than the presence of serifs themselves. On low-resolution screens, sans-serif was long preferred, but modern high-DPI displays have erased much of the difference. Style guides and brand identities often use serif typefaces to convey tradition, authority or elegance.
