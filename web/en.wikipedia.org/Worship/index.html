Worship

Worshippers gathered for communal prayer.

Worship is an act of religious devotion usually directed towards a deity or divinity. It may involve prayer, song, ritual offerings, meditation, dance, pilgrimage, and the reading of sacred texts, performed individually or by a congregation. The English word derives from the Old English weorþscipe, "worthiness" — the act of ascribing worth to the object of worship.

Forms across religions

In Christianity, worship centres on communal services involving hymns, scripture, preaching, and sacraments such as the Eucharist, though traditions differ sharply in style from liturgical to contemporary formats. In Islam, ibadah (worship) encompasses the five daily prayers (salah), fasting, almsgiving, and pilgrimage, all directed to Allah alone. Hindu worship, or puja, involves offerings of flowers, food, and light before images of deities in temples and home shrines. Buddhist practice includes chanting, prostration, and offerings before statues of the Buddha, while Jewish worship revolves around prayer, Torah reading, and observance of the Sabbath and festivals.

Anthropological perspectives

Anthropologists and sociologists have interpreted worship as reinforcing social cohesion (Émile Durkheim's theory of collective effervescence), as communication with sacred beings, and as a means of marking communal identity. Historically, worship practices were central to the authority of temples and priesthoods in ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, and Rome.

Worship and veneration

Theologians — especially in Catholic and Orthodox traditions — distinguish latria, worship due to God alone, from dulia, veneration offered to saints and holy objects, a distinction that was a major point of controversy in the Protestant Reformation and in Byzantine iconoclasm.
