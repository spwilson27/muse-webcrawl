Guerrilla art

Guerrilla art is a form of street art in which artists create and install works in public places, usually anonymously and often without permission, with the aim of provoking thought, protesting social or political conditions, or subverting advertising and official uses of public space.

Characteristics

Guerrilla art is typically anonymous, unsanctioned and ephemeral: works may be removed within hours or survive for years. Common media include stencils, stickers, posters, yarn bombing, projections and unauthorised installations or sculptures placed in streets, parks and galleries. Anonymity protects artists from prosecution for trespass or vandalism and focuses attention on the work rather than the creator.

Notable practitioners and works

The most famous guerrilla artist is Banksy, whose satirical stencils appeared from the late 1990s onward in Bristol, London and around the world. Earlier and related figures include Blek le Rat in Paris, the Guerrilla Girls — an anonymous feminist collective founded in New York in 1985 that used posters and statistics to protest sexism in the art world — and culture-jamming groups that alter billboards. Korda's portrait of Che Guevara, though not created as guerrilla art, became one of the world's most reproduced protest images in the same spirit.

Debates

Critics debate whether guerrilla art is vandalism or legitimate public expression, and cities differ in whether they remove, tolerate or protect such works. Works by celebrated artists have been cut from walls and sold at auction, raising questions about the commercialisation of an art form defined by being free and unsanctioned.
