Viewfinder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In photography, a viewfinder is what the photographer looks through to compose, and in some cases to focus, the picture. Most viewfinders are separate devices, though many cameras have a built-in viewfinder. Viewfinders allow the photographer to preview the field of view, framing and depth relationships before the exposure is made.

Types

  • Optical viewfinders are simple direct-view windows with frame lines; they are compact but suffer from parallax error at close distances.
  • Rangefinder cameras combine the viewfinder with a rangefinder focusing mechanism and bright-line frames.
  • Twin-lens reflex (TLR) cameras use a second lens to project the image onto a ground-glass focusing screen.
  • Single-lens reflex (SLR) cameras use a mirror and pentaprism so the photographer sees exactly what the taking lens sees through the same lens.
  • Electronic viewfinders (EVF), used in mirrorless cameras and video cameras, display a miniature electronic screen fed by the image sensor, allowing exposure simulation and information overlays.
  • Waist-level and ground-glass finders are found on medium- and large-format cameras.

Features

Viewfinders are commonly characterised by their magnification and coverage (the percentage of the final image area shown). Many incorporate diopter adjustment for the user's eyesight, focusing aids such as split-image prisms and microprisms, and displays of exposure information. In cinematography, the director's viewfinder is a separate tool used to scout shots and select lenses.
