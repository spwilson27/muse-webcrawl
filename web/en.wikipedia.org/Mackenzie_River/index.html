The Mackenzie River in Canada's Northwest Territories.

The Mackenzie River (Slavey: Deh-Cho, "big river"; Inuvialuktun: Kuukpak, "great river") is a river in the Canadian boreal forest. It forms, along with the Slave, Peace, and Finlay rivers, the longest river system in Canada, and includes the second largest drainage basin of any North American river after the Mississippi. The Mackenzie River flows through a vast, thinly populated region of forest and tundra in the Northwest Territories, from Great Slave Lake north to the Arctic Ocean.

The river's main stem runs 1,738 kilometres (1,080 mi). Its drainage basin of about 1.81 million square kilometres covers parts of five provinces and territories. The river is named after the Scottish explorer Alexander Mackenzie, who in 1789 travelled its length in the hope that it led to the Pacific Ocean — reaching instead the Arctic, reportedly calling it the "River of Disappointment".

Course

The Mackenzie proper begins at the western end of Great Slave Lake and flows generally north-northwest through the Mackenzie Valley, receiving major tributaries including the Liard, Great Bear, Peel, and Arctic Red rivers. Near its mouth it spreads into the Mackenzie Delta, one of the world's great river deltas and a maze of channels, lakes, and pingos, before emptying into the Beaufort Sea near Tuktoyaktuk. The river is frozen for much of the year and its spring breakup produces dramatic ice jams and flooding.

Human use

Indigenous peoples — including Dene nations along the valley and Inuvialuit of the delta — have lived along the river for thousands of years. During the fur trade era the river became a principal transport corridor for the North West Company and later the Hudson's Bay Company. Today, communities such as Fort Simpson, Norman Wells, and Inuvik depend on the river for barge transport during the short ice-free season, and ice roads cross it in winter. Oil production at Norman Wells and the proposed Mackenzie Valley Pipeline have made the river a focus of resource and environmental politics.

Ecology

The basin supports vast wetlands, including the Peace–Athabasca Delta, and provides habitat for migratory birds, beluga whales in the estuary, and fish such as inconnu and Arctic grayling. The delta's outer waters form part of a major beluga calving and feeding area.
