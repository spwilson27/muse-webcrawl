Mental map

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mental map is an individual's internal representation of their spatial environment — a cognitive map of places, routes, distances, and landmarks as the person perceives and remembers them. Mental maps are subjective: they can be distorted, incomplete, or schematic, and they reflect experience, culture, and media exposure as much as measured geography.

Research

The geographer and planner Kevin Lynch, in The Image of the City (1960), asked residents to draw sketch maps of Boston, Jersey City, and Los Angeles, and identified five elements that structure urban images: paths, edges, districts, nodes, and landmarks. Behavioral geographers later used mental maps to study migration preferences, perceived distance, and regional stereotypes — for example, the desirability surfaces drawn from students' preferences for places to live.

Applications

Mental maps inform urban planning, wayfinding design, education, and locational analysis. They also reveal systematic biases: familiar places appear larger and more detailed, distances toward liked places are underestimated, and whole regions may be pictured from maps and news imagery rather than experience.
