In probability theory, the normal distribution or Gaussian distribution is a type of continuous probability distribution for a real-valued random variable. The general form of its probability density function is f(x) = (1/(σ√(2π))) e−(x−μ)²/(2σ²). The parameter μ is the mean, and σ (the standard deviation) controls the width; the distribution is symmetric about the mean. Normal distributions are important in statistics and are often used to represent random variables whose distributions are not known.

Properties

About 68% of values drawn from a normal distribution lie within one standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within two, and about 99.7% within three — the "68–95–99.7 rule". The standard normal distribution has μ = 0 and σ = 1. Any normal random variable can be standardised by a Z-score, Z = (X − μ)/σ.

Central limit theorem

The normal distribution's prominence rests on the central limit theorem: under mild conditions, the sum or average of many independent random variables tends toward a normal distribution regardless of the original distribution. This explains why measurement errors and many natural quantities (heights, test scores) are approximately normal.

History

The distribution was first introduced by Abraham de Moivre in 1738 as an approximation to the binomial. Carl Friedrich Gauss developed it in his work on astronomical errors (1809), whence the name "Gaussian"; Adolphe Quetelet and Francis Galton later popularised its application to human characteristics and coined much of the surrounding vocabulary.

Applications

The normal distribution underlies classical hypothesis tests and confidence intervals, regression analysis, quality control, and models in finance and the natural sciences, though real-world data often show heavier tails than the normal model predicts.
