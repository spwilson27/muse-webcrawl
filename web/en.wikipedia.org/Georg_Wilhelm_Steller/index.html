Georg Wilhelm Steller

Georg Wilhelm Steller
Born: 10 March 1709, Windsheim, Germany
Died: 14 November 1746, Tyumen, Russia
Occupation: Naturalist, explorer, physician
Known for: Natural history of the North Pacific; Steller's sea cow

Georg Wilhelm Steller (10 March 1709 – 14 November 1746) was a German-born naturalist, explorer, and physician who worked in the Russian Empire. He was the first European scientist to study the natural history of Alaska and the North Pacific as the naturalist aboard Vitus Bering's Second Kamchatka Expedition (1733–1743). Several North Pacific species and features bear his name, most famously Steller's sea cow, the Steller sea lion, the Steller's jay, and the Steller's sea eagle.

The Bering expedition

Steller travelled to St. Petersburg in 1734 and joined the expedition, reaching Kamchatka in 1740. In June 1741 he sailed with Bering aboard the St. Peter toward America. The ship made landfall on Kayak Island, Alaska, in July 1741, but Steller was famously allowed only about ten hours ashore — long enough to collect plants, note a jay (later Steller's jay) whose plumage confirmed they had reached America, and record ethnographic observations. On the return voyage the expedition sighted the Shumagin Islands and the Alaska Peninsula.

Shipwreck and the sea cow

In November 1741 the St. Peter was wrecked on the island now named Bering Island in the Commander Islands. Bering and many of the crew died of scurvy during the winter; Steller's medical and naturalist skills helped the survivors, who lived on sea otters, foxes, and the giant sirenians Steller described scientifically for the first time — Steller's sea cow, a cold-water relative of the manatee up to eight metres long. The survivors built a vessel from the wreckage and reached Kamchatka in August 1742. The sea cow, hunted for meat by fur traders, was extinct by 1768, only twenty-seven years after its description.

Later years and writings

Steller spent two more years exploring Kamchatka, documenting its volcanoes, wildlife, and the Itelmen people. Recalled to St. Petersburg in 1744, he died of a fever at Tyumen in Siberia in November 1746, aged thirty-seven, never reaching the capital. His journal of the voyage and his De Bestiis Marinis ("On Marine Animals," published 1751) remain foundational works of North Pacific natural history, and his detailed descriptions preserve species — above all the sea cow — that no scientist ever studied alive again.
