Patrice Lumumba

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Patrice Émery Lumumba (2 July 1925 – 17 January 1961) was a Congolese politician and independence leader who served as the first prime minister of the Democratic Republic of the Congo from June until September 1960. A founder of the Mouvement National Congolais, he became the central figure of Congolese independence from Belgium and an enduring symbol of African nationalism and resistance to colonialism.

Independence and the Congo Crisis

At the independence ceremony on 30 June 1960, Lumumba delivered an unscheduled speech denouncing Belgian colonial rule before King Baudouin, electrifying Congolese opinion. Within days the army mutinied and the mineral-rich Katanga province seceded under Moise Tshombe with Belgian support. When the UN and the United States declined to help restore central authority, Lumumba appealed to the Soviet Union, alarming the West; declassified records later showed the CIA plotted his assassination, and President Eisenhower authorised his elimination.

Downfall and murder

In September 1960 President Joseph Kasa-Vubu dismissed Lumumba, and army chief Joseph Mobutu seized power in a coup. Lumumba was arrested while trying to reach his stronghold in Stanleyville, flown to Katanga, and on 17 January 1961 executed by firing squad under Katangese and Belgian supervision; his body was dissolved in acid. A Belgian parliamentary inquiry in 2001 acknowledged Belgium's moral responsibility, and Belgium formally apologised. In 2022 Belgium returned his only known remains — a tooth — to his family.

Legacy

Lumumba is revered across Africa and the wider world as a martyr of decolonisation; streets, universities, and monuments bear his name, and the circumstances of his death remain emblematic of Cold War intervention in the Congo, ushering in Mobutu's three-decade dictatorship.
