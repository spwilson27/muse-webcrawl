Suez Crisis

The Suez Crisis, or the Second Arab–Israeli War, was an invasion of Egypt in late 1956 by Israel, followed by the United Kingdom and France. The aims were to regain control of the Suez Canal for the Western powers and to remove Egyptian president Gamal Abdel Nasser, who had nationalised the foreign-owned Suez Canal Company that operated the canal.

After the fighting had started, political pressure from the United States, the Soviet Union and the United Nations led to a withdrawal by the three invaders. The episode humiliated the United Kingdom and France and strengthened Nasser's position.

Background

The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, was a vital shipping route. In July 1956 Nasser nationalised the canal, partly in retaliation for the withdrawal of Western funding for the Aswan Dam. Britain and France secretly conspired with Israel in the Protocol of Sèvres.

Course of the crisis

Israel invaded the Sinai on 29 October 1956. Britain and France issued an ultimatum, then bombed Egyptian airfields and landed forces around Port Said. Egypt blocked the canal by scuttling ships.

Aftermath

Under intense American and Soviet pressure, and a run on the pound sterling, Britain and France withdrew by the end of 1956; Israeli forces withdrew in early 1957. A United Nations Emergency Force was stationed in the Sinai. The crisis marked the end of Britain's role as a superpower.
