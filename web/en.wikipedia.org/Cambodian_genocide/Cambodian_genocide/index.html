The Cambodian genocide was the systematic persecution and mass killing of Cambodians by the Khmer Rouge regime under Pol Pot between 1975 and 1979. Between 1.5 and 2 million people — around a quarter of Cambodia's 1975 population of some 7.8 million — died from execution, forced labour, starvation and disease.

Background and course

After winning the civil war, the Khmer Rouge proclaimed Democratic Kampuchea and set out to purify and rebuild society from "Year Zero". Cities were emptied in days; the entire population was organised into cooperatives and subjected to exhausting agricultural labour on starvation rations. Former officials and soldiers of the Lon Nol regime were executed, as were teachers, doctors, merchants, monks and anyone deemed an intellectual — notoriously, even people who wore glasses. The regime's paranoia produced continuous internal purges run by the Santebal secret police and its chief prison, S-21 at Tuol Sleng, while mass graves — the "Killing Fields" — were dug across the country.

Victims and the legal definition

Ethnic minorities suffered disproportionately. The Vietnamese minority was virtually annihilated, and the Cham Muslim population lost perhaps a third of its people through massacres, forced assimilation and the destruction of their religious life. The UN-backed Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia (ECCC) convicted senior leaders Nuon Chea and Khieu Samphan of genocide against the Cham and the Vietnamese, alongside crimes against humanity encompassing the wider mass deaths.

Aftermath

Vietnam's invasion ended the regime in January 1979, but the Khmer Rouge continued an insurgency for two decades and Cambodia's trauma shaped its politics for generations. Memorials at Tuol Sleng and Choeung Ek, and the documentation work of the Documentation Center of Cambodia, preserve the record, while the ECCC trials, concluded in the 2010s–2020s, provided a measure of legal accountability.
