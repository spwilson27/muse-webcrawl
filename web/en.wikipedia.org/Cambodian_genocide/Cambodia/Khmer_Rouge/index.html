The Khmer Rouge (French for "Red Khmers") is the name popularly given to the communists of Cambodia and, by extension, to the regime through which they ruled the country between 1975 and 1979. The movement's political core was the Communist Party of Kampuchea, led by Pol Pot. After winning the Cambodian Civil War, the Khmer Rouge captured Phnom Penh on 17 April 1975 and renamed the country Democratic Kampuchea. In power, the regime attempted a radical transformation of Cambodian society into an agrarian, classless utopia. The result was the Cambodian genocide: mass executions, forced labour, starvation, and disease killed an estimated 1.5 to 2 million people — roughly a quarter of the population — before Vietnam invaded in late 1978 and drove the Khmer Rouge from power in January 1979.

Rise to power

The Khmer Rouge grew out of the Cambodian communist movement of the 1950s and 1960s, shaped by students radicalised in Paris, including Pol Pot, Ieng Sary, and Khieu Samphan. During the late 1960s the party launched an insurgency against Prince Norodom Sihanouk and then against the pro-American Khmer Republic of Lon Nol, which had seized power in 1970. The Vietnam War spilled into Cambodia: massive American bombing of the countryside devastated rural areas and drove recruits to the communists. Backed by North Vietnam and China and formally allied with Sihanouk in exile, the Khmer Rouge slowly encircled the capital and won the civil war in April 1975.

Democratic Kampuchea

Within days of taking Phnom Penh, the regime emptied the cities, marching the entire urban population into the countryside in a forced evacuation during which many died. Money, markets, private property, religion, and formal education were abolished; the population was organised into rural collectives and work brigades under the omnipresent authority of "Angkar" (the Organisation). The regime reset the calendar to "Year Zero". Intellectuals, professionals, former officials and soldiers, ethnic minorities such as the Cham and Vietnamese, and anyone suspected of disloyalty were targeted for execution. The S-21 prison at Tuol Sleng in Phnom Penh processed at least 14,000 prisoners, nearly all of whom were killed at the Choeung Ek extermination centre. Malnutrition and untreated disease, driven by unrealistic rice quotas and the export of food, killed many more. Estimates of the total death toll under the regime range from about 1.5 to 2 million people out of a population of roughly 8 million.

Fall and aftermath

Border clashes and purges of alleged Vietnamese agents poisoned relations with Vietnam, which invaded Cambodia on 25 December 1978 and captured Phnom Penh on 7 January 1979, installing the People's Republic of Kampuchea. The Khmer Rouge retreated to the Thai border and waged a guerrilla war for two more decades, retaining Cambodia's seat at the United Nations as part of a coalition government-in-exile. Pol Pot died in 1998 while under house arrest by his own movement, and the last Khmer Rouge forces surrendered in 1999. Decades later, the Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia (the Khmer Rouge Tribunal), established with the United Nations, convicted senior leaders including Kaing Guek Eav ("Duch"), Nuon Chea, and Khieu Samphan of crimes against humanity and genocide.
