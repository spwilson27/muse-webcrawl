Angkor Wat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Angkor Wat (Khmer: អង្គរវត្ត, "City/Capital of Temples") is a Hindu–Buddhist temple complex in Cambodia. Located on a site measuring 162.6 hectares within the ancient Khmer capital city of Angkor, it was constructed between approximately 1122 and 1150 CE by King Suryavarman II of the Khmer Empire. It is the largest religious structure in the world and one of the most important pilgrimage sites in Southeast Asia.

Architecture

Originally dedicated to the Hindu god Vishnu, the temple is designed to represent Mount Meru, home of the gods in Hindu cosmology, with its five towers symbolising Meru's peaks and its vast moat the ocean of milk. Galleries of bas-reliefs extending over hundreds of metres depict Hindu epics — the Ramayana and Mahabharata — the churning of the sea of milk, and historical scenes of Suryavarman's court and army. Unusually for a Khmer temple, Angkor Wat faces west, associated with death, leading scholars to suggest it also served as the king's funerary temple.

History

Unlike most Angkorian temples, Angkor Wat was never completely abandoned: it gradually transformed from a Hindu centre into a Theravada Buddhist monastery in the 16th century and remained in use. It was "rediscovered" for Western audiences by the French naturalist Henri Mouhot in the 1860s, whose accounts popularised the site in Europe. The temple appears on Cambodia's national flag and is the country's prime tourist attraction; together with the wider Angkor Archaeological Park, it was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1992.
