Cambodian genocide

The Cambodian genocide was the systematic persecution and murder of Cambodians by the Khmer Rouge regime under Pol Pot between 1975 and 1979. It resulted in the deaths of an estimated 1.5 to 2.2 million people — roughly a quarter of Cambodia's 1975 population of some 7.5–8 million — through execution, forced labour, starvation, and disease. The killings ended when Vietnam invaded Cambodia in December 1978 and overthrew the regime, capturing Phnom Penh on January 7, 1979.

Background

The Khmer Rouge, the Communist Party of Kampuchea led by Pol Pot, emerged from the chaos of the Cambodian Civil War and the secret American bombing of the Cambodian countryside during the Vietnam War. On April 17, 1975, Khmer Rouge forces entered Phnom Penh and immediately ordered the total evacuation of the cities, driving the entire urban population into the countryside at gunpoint. The regime renamed the country Democratic Kampuchea and proclaimed "Year Zero," abolishing money, markets, private property, religion, and formal education in pursuit of an agrarian, self-sufficient communist society.

The killings

Cambodians were divided into "base people" (peasants) and "new people" (evacuated city dwellers), the latter subjected to the harshest conditions. Ethnic and religious minorities — Vietnamese, Chinese, Cham Muslims, and Buddhist monks — were targeted for extermination, as were former officials, soldiers, teachers, intellectuals, and anyone suspected of disloyalty. Mass executions took place at sites across the country known collectively as the Killing Fields; the regime's central security prison, S-21 at Tuol Sleng in Phnom Penh, processed at least 14,000 prisoners, of whom only a handful survived. Forced labour on starvation rations, untreated disease, and a regime-driven famine accounted for a large share of the deaths.

Aftermath and justice

After 1979, the Khmer Rouge retreated to the Thai border and waged guerrilla war; Pol Pot died in 1998 without facing trial. In the 2000s Cambodia and the United Nations established the Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia (the Khmer Rouge Tribunal), which convicted S-21 commandant Kaing Guek Eav ("Duch" ) and senior leaders Nuon Chea and Khieu Samphan — the latter two of genocide against the Vietnamese and Cham — before the court's effective wind-down in the 2020s. Memorials at Tuol Sleng and Choeung Ek commemorate the victims, and the genocide remains central to Cambodian national memory.
