The Khmer Rouge (French for "Red Khmers") is the name popularly given to the followers of the Communist Party of Kampuchea (CPK) and by extension to their regime, Democratic Kampuchea, which ruled Cambodia from 1975 to 1979. Led by Pol Pot, the movement waged a civil war against the US-backed Khmer Republic, took Phnom Penh on 17 April 1975, and imposed one of the most radical social experiments of the 20th century — one that caused the deaths of an estimated 1.5 to 2 million people in the Cambodian genocide.

Rise to power

The Cambodian communist movement developed from the Indochinese Communist Party; a party was founded in 1951 and re-founded in 1960 as the Workers' Party of Kampuchea, with Pol Pot's "Paris student group" faction taking control in 1963. Rural insurgency from 1968 was massively aided by the destabilisation of the US bombing campaign and North Vietnamese use of Cambodian territory during the Vietnam War, and by Prince Sihanouk's alliance with the movement after his overthrow in 1970.

In power

The regime — run by the secretive Angkar — emptied the cities, abolished money, religion, private property and schools, and collectivised agriculture under a "Year Zero" ideology of total social revolution. Intellectuals, professionals, ethnic Vietnamese, Cham Muslims and suspected opponents were persecuted and killed in the "Killing Fields" and at security centres such as S-21. Internal purges in 1977–78, especially of the Eastern Zone, killed tens of thousands of cadres.

Fall and aftermath

War with Vietnam ended the regime in January 1979. Retreating to the Thai border, the Khmer Rouge retained Cambodia's UN seat with international backing and fought a long insurgency. The movement dissolved in 1999 after defections, the capture of Ta Mok, and Pol Pot's death in 1998. Two senior leaders, Nuon Chea and Khieu Samphan, were convicted of genocide and crimes against humanity by the Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia (ECCC) in 2018.
