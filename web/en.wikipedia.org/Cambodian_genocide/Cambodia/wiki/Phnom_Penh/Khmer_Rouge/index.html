The Khmer Rouge was the communist movement that ruled Cambodia under Pol Pot between 1975 and 1979. Taking its name from the French phrase meaning "Red Khmer", it attempted to remake Cambodia as a self-sufficient agrarian society, and in doing so caused the deaths of approximately 1.5 to 2 million people.

Origins

The movement originated in the Indochinese communist movement and took shape as the Communist Party of Kampuchea, led from 1963 by Pol Pot. It fought the government of Lon Nol in the 1970–1975 civil war, benefiting from North Vietnamese support, American bombing that radicalised the countryside, and an alliance with the exiled Prince Norodom Sihanouk. Its victory came on 17 April 1975 with the fall of Phnom Penh.

The regime

As Democratic Kampuchea, the regime emptied the cities at gunpoint, abolished currency, religion, schools and private property, and herded the population into work cooperatives. The security apparatus, centred on the S-21 prison at Tuol Sleng, tortured and executed perceived enemies, while ethnic minorities — Vietnamese, Cham, Chinese and others — suffered targeted persecution now recognised as genocide against the Cham and Vietnamese.

Downfall

Vietnam invaded in December 1978 and captured Phnom Penh on 7 January 1979. The Khmer Rouge survived as a guerrilla force along the Thai border for twenty years, even holding Cambodia's UN seat, before disintegrating following Pol Pot's death in 1998. Decades later, a hybrid UN-Cambodian tribunal convicted the S-21 chief Duch and senior leaders Nuon Chea and Khieu Samphan.
