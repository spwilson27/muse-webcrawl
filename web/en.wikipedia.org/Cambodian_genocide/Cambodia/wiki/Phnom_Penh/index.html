Phnom Penh (Khmer: ភ្នំពេញ, "Penh's Hill") is the capital and most populous city of Cambodia. It lies at the confluence of the Tonlé Sap and Mekong rivers (the Chaktomuk, "Four Faces"). Founded in the 14th century, Phnom Penh became the permanent capital in the 1860s under King Norodom and developed under French colonial rule into a city once known as the "Pearl of Asia", noted for its broad boulevards, the Royal Palace, the Silver Pagoda and Wat Phnom, the temple on the hill from which the city takes its name.

History

According to legend, the widow Penh founded a temple on the hill in 1372 for Buddha statues washed ashore in a flood. Phnom Penh became the royal capital in 1434 after the abandonment of Angkor, though the court later moved between several cities before Norodom settled permanently at Phnom Penh in 1866. The city grew rapidly in the French protectorate era and again under Prince Sihanouk in the 1950s–60s.

On 17 April 1975 the Khmer Rouge captured the city and forcibly evacuated its entire population — some two million people — into the countryside; Phnom Penh remained almost empty until the Vietnamese-backed government restored it after January 1979. The Tuol Sleng Genocide Museum (the former S-21 prison) and the Choeung Ek memorial preserve this history. Since the 1990s the city has seen rapid growth, construction and recurring challenges of flooding, traffic and inequality.

Geography and sights

Phnom Penh's landmarks include the Royal Palace and Silver Pagoda, the National Museum, the Art Deco Central Market (Psar Thmei), Wat Phnom, the Independence Monument, and the Sisowath Quay riverfront. It is served by Phnom Penh International Airport and the autonomous port on the Tonlé Sap river.
