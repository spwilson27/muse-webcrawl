Khmer Empire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Khmer Empire was a Hindu–Buddhist empire in Southeast Asia, centred on what is now Cambodia, that at its height ruled over most of mainland Southeast Asia and parts of southern China. It succeeded the earlier Chenla polity and was formally proclaimed in 802 CE when Jayavarman II declared himself a universal monarch on Phnom Kulen. The empire lasted until the fall of Angkor to the Ayutthaya Kingdom in 1431.

Rise and golden age

From its capital region at Angkor (Yasodharapura), the empire developed a sophisticated hydraulic system of reservoirs (baray) and canals that supported intensive rice agriculture and a large urban population; recent lidar surveys reveal Angkor to have been among the largest pre-industrial cities in the world, covering roughly 1,000 square kilometres. King Suryavarman II (r. 1113–1150) built Angkor Wat, and Jayavarman VII (r. 1181–1218), a Mahayana Buddhist, undertook an immense building programme including Angkor Thom, the Bayon, Ta Prohm, and Preah Khan, alongside hospitals and rest houses across the empire.

Society and religion

Khmer society was hierarchical, headed by a god-king (devaraja) and administered through a Sanskrit- and Old Khmer-inscribed bureaucracy. Hinduism, centred on Shiva and Vishnu, dominated early centuries, with Mahayana and later Theravada Buddhism progressively reshaping religious life. Trade linked the empire to China, India, and maritime Southeast Asia.

Decline

From the 13th century the empire weakened under pressure from the rising Thai kingdoms of Sukhothai and Ayutthaya, shifts in trade, strain on its water infrastructure, and possibly drought and flood cycles revealed in tree-ring records. After Ayutthaya sacked Angkor in 1431, the court moved south toward Phnom Penh, ending the Angkorian era — though Theravada monks continued to maintain Angkor Wat.
