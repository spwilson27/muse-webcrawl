Indochina

Indochina, or the Indochinese Peninsula, is the continental portion of Southeast Asia lying east of India and south of China — a name reflecting the region's historical position between, and cultural influences from, the two civilisations. It comprises the countries of Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar (Burma), Thailand, Vietnam, Peninsular Malaysia and sometimes Singapore. Geographically it is bounded by the Indian Ocean (Bay of Bengal and Andaman Sea) to the west and the South China Sea to the east.

Geography

The peninsula is traversed by great river systems flowing south from the highlands of Tibet and Yunnan: the Irrawaddy, Salween, Chao Phraya, Mekong and Red River. Their fertile deltas support dense populations and wet-rice agriculture, while mountain ranges separate the river valleys and historically channeled the region into distinct kingdoms.

History

The region's states were shaped by both Indic culture — Hinduism, Mahayana and Theravada Buddhism, Sanskrit-derived scripts — and, especially in Vietnam, by Chinese political and Confucian influence. From the mid-19th century France colonised Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos, uniting them as French Indochina (1887–1954), while Britain annexed Burma and Malaya and only Siam (Thailand) remained independent as a buffer state. French rule ended after the First Indochina War and the 1954 Geneva Accords; the following decades brought the Vietnam War (the Second Indochina War) and conflicts in Laos and Cambodia, including the Khmer Rouge genocide. Today all of the peninsula's states are members of ASEAN.

Biogeography

In biogeography, the Indochinese region is a subregion of the Indomalayan realm, rich in biodiversity, with notable species including the Indochinese tiger; parts of its forests are threatened by logging and conversion to agriculture.
