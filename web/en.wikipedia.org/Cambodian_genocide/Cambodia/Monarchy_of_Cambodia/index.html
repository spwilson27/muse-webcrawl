Monarchy of Cambodia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The monarchy of Cambodia is the constitutional monarchy of the Kingdom of Cambodia. The King of Cambodia is the head of state, reigning — but, under the 1993 constitution, not ruling — as a symbol of national unity. Cambodia's kingship is elective rather than strictly hereditary: the monarch is chosen for life by the Royal Council of the Throne from among male descendants of King Ang Duong who are at least 30 years old.

Historical overview

Cambodian kingship traces back to the early kingdoms of Funan and Chenla and reached its classical apex in the god-kings of the Khmer Empire (9th–15th centuries). After centuries of decline, the monarchy passed through a French protectorate (1863–1953). King Norodom Sihanouk led the country to independence in 1953, abdicated in 1955 to enter politics, and ruled as head of state until overthrown in 1970 by Lon Nol, who declared the Khmer Republic. The monarchy was abolished under the republic and then under the Khmer Rouge regime (1975–1979), during which much of the royal family perished.

Restoration

The 1991 Paris Peace Accords and the 1993 constitution restored the monarchy, and Sihanouk returned to the throne. On his abdication in 2004, the Royal Council of the Throne selected his son Norodom Sihamoni, a former ballet dancer and cultural ambassador, as king. The king's constitutional duties are largely ceremonial — appointing the prime minister and senior officials on advice, serving as supreme commander of the armed forces, and granting pardons — but the institution retains deep symbolic importance in Cambodia.
