Malay Archipelago


Placeholder image: Map of the Malay Archipelago

The Malay Archipelago (Malay/Indonesian: Kepulauan Melayu) is the archipelago between Mainland Southeast Asia and Australia. It has also been called the "Malay world", "Nusantara", "East Indies" (historically), and other names over time. The name was taken from the 19th-century European concept of a "Malay race", later based on the distribution of Austronesian languages.

Situated between the Indian and Pacific oceans, the archipelago of over 25,000 islands is the largest archipelago by area and fifth by number of islands in the world. It includes Brunei, East Timor, Indonesia, Malaysia (specifically East Malaysia), Papua New Guinea, and the Philippines. The term is largely synonymous with Maritime Southeast Asia.

Geography

The archipelago lies along the equator and is divided by the Wallace Line, a faunal boundary identified by naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace, which separates the ecozones of Asia and Wallacea. Wallace's 1869 book The Malay Archipelago popularised the region's natural history in the English-speaking world.

Major island groups include the Greater Sunda Islands (Borneo, Sumatra, Java, Sulawesi), the Lesser Sunda Islands, the Maluku Islands, and the Philippine Archipelago. New Guinea is often included, though it is sometimes treated separately as part of Melanesia.
