Khmer Rouge Tribunal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Khmer Rouge Tribunal, officially the Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia (ECCC), is a hybrid Cambodian–international court established in 2006 to try senior leaders and those most responsible for crimes committed during the Khmer Rouge regime of Democratic Kampuchea (1975–1979), in which an estimated 1.5 to 2 million people died.

Establishment

After years of negotiation between the Cambodian government and the United Nations — complicated by Cold War politics and the integration of former Khmer Rouge cadres into the state — an agreement was reached in 2003 and the court began work in 2006 on the outskirts of Phnom Penh. Uniquely, the ECCC sits within the Cambodian court system with mixed Cambodian and international judges and prosecutors, and victims may participate as civil parties.

Cases

In Case 001, Kaing Guek Eav ("Duch"), commandant of the S-21 prison, was convicted of crimes against humanity and grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions; his life sentence was confirmed on appeal in 2012. In Case 002, senior leaders Nuon Chea and Khieu Samphan were convicted of crimes against humanity (2014) and of genocide against the Vietnamese and Cham minorities (2018), receiving life sentences; co-accused Ieng Sary died during trial and Ieng Thirith was found unfit. Cases 003 and 004, against mid-level commanders, were mired in disputes over government opposition and were ultimately dismissed or terminated.

Legacy

The ECCC concluded its judicial work in 2022 and entered a residual phase. Despite costing over $330 million and convicting only three men, it is credited with creating an extensive historical record, engaging hundreds of thousands of Cambodians through outreach, and giving victims a public forum nearly three decades after the crimes.
