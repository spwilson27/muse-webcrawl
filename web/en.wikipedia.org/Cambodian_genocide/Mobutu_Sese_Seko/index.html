Mobutu Sese Seko

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mobutu Sese Seko Kuku Ngbendu wa za Banga (born Joseph-Désiré Mobutu; 14 October 1930 – 7 September 1997) was a Congolese politician and military officer who ruled the Democratic Republic of the Congo — renamed Zaire under his regime — from 1965 to 1997. Backed by the United States and other Western powers as an anti-communist bulwark during the Cold War, he built one of Africa's most notorious kleptocracies, amassing a personal fortune estimated in the billions while his country's economy collapsed.

Rise to power

Mobutu served in the colonial Force Publique and worked as a journalist before independence in 1960, when he became army chief of staff under Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba. During the Congo Crisis he seized power in a first coup in 1960, facilitating Lumumba's removal and eventual murder, and ruled definitively after a second coup in November 1965.

Rule

Mobutu proclaimed the ideology of authenticité, renaming the country Zaire (1971), Africanising personal and place names, and requiring citizens to adopt African names — taking his own elaborate name in 1972. His Popular Movement of the Revolution was the sole legal party, and a personality cult portrayed him as the nation's father and guide. Vast revenues from copper and cobalt were diverted to the ruling elite; his fortune, held in foreign accounts and properties, was estimated at US$5 billion or more. "Zaireanisation" of foreign businesses in the 1970s triggered economic ruin, and by the 1980s infrastructure had decayed severely.

Fall and death

With the Cold War's end, Western patronage waned and Mobutu was forced to concede multiparty reforms. Weakened by prostate cancer, he was overthrown in May 1997 by Laurent-Désiré Kabila's rebels during the First Congo War and fled into exile, dying in Rabat, Morocco, that September.
