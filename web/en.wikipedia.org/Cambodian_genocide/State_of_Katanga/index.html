State of Katanga

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The State of Katanga was a short-lived, internationally unrecognised secessionist state proclaimed on 11 July 1960 by Moise Tshombe, leader of the CONAKAT party, in the mineral-rich southern province of Katanga, days after the Congo gained independence from Belgium. Backed by Belgian mining interests — above all the Union Minière du Haut-Katanga — and white mercenaries, the state survived until January 1963, when United Nations forces ended the secession.

Secession

Katanga produced much of the Congo's export wealth, including copper, cobalt, and uranium, and its regional leaders resented both rule from Léopoldville and Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba's centralism. Following the army mutiny of July 1960, Tshombe declared independence with Belgian military and technical support. No country formally recognised Katanga, though it received sympathy from some Western governments and from apartheid South Africa and Rhodesia.

Conflict with the United Nations

The UN Security Council authorised the ONUC mission to restore Congolese territorial integrity. After the murdered Lumumba was flown to Katanga and executed there in January 1961, international pressure mounted. UN operations Rumpunch and Morthor (1961) targeted mercenaries, and Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld died in a plane crash travelling to negotiate with Tshombe. In December 1962 UN forces launched Operation Grandslam, capturing the capital Élisabethville; Tshombe fled in January 1963 and the secession ended.

Aftermath

Katanga was reintegrated into the Congo (later renamed Shaba under Mobutu). Tshombe himself briefly returned as Congolese prime minister in 1964. The Katanga episode remains a defining case of resource-driven secession and Cold War proxy conflict in Africa.
