Decolonisation of Africa


Placeholder image: Map showing dates of African independence

The decolonisation of Africa was a series of political developments in the mid-to-late 20th century in which the European colonial empires withdrew from the African continent and independent states emerged. The process began in earnest after the Second World War and peaked in 1960, the "Year of Africa", when seventeen countries gained independence.

Background

European powers had partitioned Africa at the Berlin Conference of 1884–85, in the so-called Scramble for Africa. After 1945, weakened European states faced growing nationalist movements, pressure from the United States and the Soviet Union, and the anti-colonial commitments of the newly founded United Nations.

Process

Libya gained independence in 1951, followed by Sudan, Morocco and Tunisia in 1956 and Ghana in 1957, the first sub-Saharan colony to achieve independence under majority rule. In 1960 alone, Cameroon, Togo, Senegal, Mali, Madagascar, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Somalia, Dahomey (Benin), Niger, Upper Volta (Burkina Faso), Ivory Coast, Chad, the Central African Republic, Congo, Gabon, Nigeria and Mauritania became independent.

Some transitions were negotiated and peaceful; others, such as the Algerian War (1954–62) and the Portuguese Colonial War (1961–74), were violent. The last Portuguese colonies, Angola and Mozambique, gained independence in 1975 following the Carnation Revolution. Zimbabwe followed in 1980 and Namibia in 1990; South Africa's apartheid system ended with majority-rule elections in 1994.
