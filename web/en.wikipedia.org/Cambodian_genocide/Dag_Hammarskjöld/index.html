Dag Hammarskjöld

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dag Hjalmar Agne Carl Hammarskjöld (29 July 1905 – 18 September 1961) was a Swedish economist and diplomat who served as the second Secretary-General of the United Nations from April 1953 until his death in a plane crash in 1961. Widely regarded as one of the most effective holders of the office, he was posthumously awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1961.

Early career

Born in Jönköping into a prominent family — his father Hjalmar had been Sweden's prime minister — Hammarskjöld studied law and economics at Uppsala and Stockholm, joined the Swedish civil service, and rose to senior posts in the Ministry of Finance and as deputy foreign minister, representing Sweden in early European cooperation bodies before his appointment to the UN.

Secretary-General

Hammarskjöld transformed the office into an instrument of quiet diplomacy and preventive action. He negotiated the release of American airmen held in China in 1955, pioneered UN peacekeeping with the first UN Emergency Force during the 1956 Suez Crisis, and intervened in crises in Lebanon and Laos. During the Congo Crisis of 1960–61 he deployed a large UN force (ONUC) and resisted pressure from both superpowers, asserting the independence of the international civil service.

Death and legacy

Hammarskjöld died on the night of 17–18 September 1961 when his aircraft crashed near Ndola, Northern Rhodesia (now Zambia), en route to negotiate a ceasefire with Katanga's Moise Tshombe. The cause of the crash has never been conclusively established and remains the subject of investigations and speculation. His posthumously published spiritual journal, Markings (Vägmärken), became a modern classic. U.S. President John F. Kennedy called him "the greatest statesman of our century".
