The Killing Fields (Khmer: វាលពិឃាត, veal pi-kheat) are a number of sites in Cambodia where collectively more than 1.3 million people were killed and buried by the Khmer Rouge regime during the Cambodian genocide (1975–1979), immediately after the end of the Cambodian Civil War. Analysis of 20,000 mass grave sites by the DC-Cam Mapping Program and Yale University indicates at least 1,386,734 victims of execution.

The term was popularised by Cambodian journalist Dith Pran, who escaped the regime and whose story was told in the 1984 film The Killing Fields.

History

After seizing power in April 1975, the Khmer Rouge under Pol Pot emptied Cambodia's cities and forced the population into rural labour camps. Intellectuals, professionals, ethnic Vietnamese, Cham Muslims, Buddhist monks, and anyone suspected of disloyalty were arrested, often taken to security prisons such as Tuol Sleng (S-21) in Phnom Penh, interrogated and tortured, and then transported to execution sites — the Killing Fields — where they were murdered, frequently with farm implements to save bullets, and buried in mass graves.

Choeung Ek

The best known of the sites is Choeung Ek, about 15 kilometres south of Phnom Penh, where some 8,895 bodies were exhumed from mass graves after the fall of the regime. A Buddhist memorial stupa, built in 1988, contains more than 5,000 human skulls, many showing evidence of blunt-force trauma. The site is now a memorial and museum.

Legacy

The Killing Fields have become symbols of the atrocities of the Khmer Rouge period, during which an estimated quarter of Cambodia's population died from execution, starvation, disease, and overwork. Trials of senior Khmer Rouge leaders were later conducted by the Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia (the Khmer Rouge Tribunal).
