Congo Crisis

The Congo Crisis (1960–1965) was a period of political upheaval and conflict in the Republic of the Congo (today the Democratic Republic of the Congo) following independence from Belgium on 30 June 1960. Within days the army mutinied, the mineral-rich province of Katanga seceded with Belgian backing, and Cold War rivals intervened. The crisis ended with Joseph-Désiré Mobutu's 1965 coup and the establishment of his long dictatorship; Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba was murdered in January 1961.

Origins

Belgium granted independence abruptly after riots in 1959, leaving the Congo with almost no Congolese officers or administrators. On 5 July 1960 the Force Publique mutinied against its Belgian officers; Belgium intervened militarily, and on 11 July Moïse Tshombe declared Katanga's secession, supported by Belgian mining interests (Union Minière). South Kasai also seceded.

International involvement

Lumumba appealed to the United Nations, which deployed ONUC, one of its largest early peacekeeping operations; when the UN refused to crush Katanga, Lumumba turned to the Soviet Union, alarming Washington. In September 1960 President Kasa-Vubu dismissed Lumumba, and army chief Mobutu staged a first coup. Lumumba was arrested, flown to Katanga, and executed on 17 January 1961 with Belgian complicity — a crime Belgium formally acknowledged in 2002.

End of the secession and aftermath

UN forces, after Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld died in a plane crash at Ndola in 1961 while seeking a ceasefire, finally ended Katanga's secession in January 1963. A leftist Simba rebellion in 1964 was suppressed with Belgian-American intervention (including the Stanleyville hostage rescue). On 25 November 1965 Mobutu seized power definitively, ruling the country — renamed Zaire in 1971 — until 1997.
