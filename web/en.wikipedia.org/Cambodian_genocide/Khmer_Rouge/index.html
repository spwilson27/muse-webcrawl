Khmer Rouge

The Khmer Rouge (French for "Red Khmers") was the popular name for the Communist Party of Kampuchea and the regime it imposed on Cambodia between 1975 and 1979 as Democratic Kampuchea. Led by Pol Pot, the Khmer Rouge attempted a radical agrarian-communist transformation: cities were emptied, money and markets abolished, and the population forced into rural collectives. The resulting Cambodian genocide killed an estimated 1.5 to 2 million people — roughly a quarter of the population — through execution, starvation, disease, and overwork, before Vietnam invaded and overthrew the regime in January 1979.

Rise to power

The movement grew out of Cambodian communism, radicalized by students in 1950s Paris (the "Paris Group" including Pol Pot, Ieng Sary, and Khieu Samphan). Exploiting grievances from the U.S. bombing of Cambodia and Prince Sihanouk's 1970 overthrow by Lon Nol, the Khmer Rouge won the civil war and entered Phnom Penh on 17 April 1975.

Democratic Kampuchea

The regime immediately evacuated all cities — "Year Zero" — and herded the population into agricultural cooperatives under the secretive ruling organization Angkar. Intellectuals, former officials, ethnic Vietnamese and Cham, monks, and eventually the party's own cadres were purged. At the S-21 prison (Tuol Sleng) in Phnom Penh, at least 14,000 prisoners were tortured and executed at sites such as Choeung Ek, the best known of the "Killing Fields."

Fall and aftermath

Border clashes with Vietnam led to a full Vietnamese invasion on 25 December 1978; Phnom Penh fell on 7 January 1979. The Khmer Rouge retreated to the Thai border and waged guerrilla war, controversially retaining Cambodia's UN seat through the 1980s with Western and Chinese support. The movement collapsed in the late 1990s after Pol Pot's death (1998). The UN-backed Khmer Rouge Tribunal (ECCC) later convicted senior leaders Kaing Guek Eav ("Duch"), Nuon Chea, and Khieu Samphan, formally recognizing crimes including genocide against the Cham and Vietnamese.
