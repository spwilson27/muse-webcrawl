The Cambodian genocide was the systematic persecution and killing of Cambodians by the Khmer Rouge under the leadership of Pol Pot, which ruled Cambodia as Democratic Kampuchea from 1975 to 1979. It resulted in the deaths of an estimated 1.5 to 2 million people — roughly a quarter of Cambodia's population of about 7.9 million — through execution, forced labour, starvation, and disease.

Background

In April 1975, after winning the Cambodian Civil War, the Khmer Rouge immediately evacuated all cities, including Phnom Penh, forcing millions into rural collective farms. The regime sought to create a self-sufficient, classless agrarian society, abolishing money, markets, religion, formal education, and private property.

Persecution and mass killing

The population was divided between rural "base people," who were trusted, and deported urban "new people," who were treated as enemies. Ethnic and religious minorities suffered disproportionately: most of the ethnic Vietnamese community was expelled or killed, and the Cham Muslim minority was subjected to massacres that the Khmer Rouge tribunal later recognised as genocide. Buddhist monks were defrocked or killed and most pagodas destroyed.

Mass graves known as the Killing Fields are scattered across the country; the best known is Choeung Ek outside Phnom Penh, where prisoners from the S-21 (Tuol Sleng) security prison were executed. Famine caused by forced collectivisation, irrational quotas, and the export of rice caused hundreds of thousands of additional deaths, alongside untreated disease and exhaustion.

End and legacy

Vietnam invaded Cambodia in December 1978 and overthrew the regime within weeks, establishing the People's Republic of Kampuchea. The Khmer Rouge retained Cambodia's UN seat for years as part of a Cold War alignment. Justice came decades later: the Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia (established 2006) convicted prison chief Kaing Guek Eav ("Duch") and, in 2018, ruled that Nuon Chea and Khieu Samphan had committed genocide against the Cham and Vietnamese. Cambodia commemorates the victims each year on 20 May, the Day of Remembrance.
