Norodom Sihanouk (1922–2012) was a Cambodian royal and statesman who dominated his country's political life for six decades, serving as king, prime minister, and head of state in a bewildering variety of regimes. He led Cambodia to independence from France in 1953 and remains a revered figure to many Cambodians.

King and independence

Placed on the throne by the French in 1941 at the age of 18, Sihanouk surprised his sponsors by launching a "royal crusade for independence" that won full sovereignty in November 1953. In 1955 he abdicated in favour of his father to enter politics directly, founding the Sangkum movement, which won every election of the era. As head of state from 1960 he pursued neutrality in the Cold War, balancing American, Soviet and Chinese support while suppressing both communists and the right.

Deposition, exile and the Khmer Rouge

In March 1970, while Sihanouk was abroad, Lon Nol and Sirik Matak deposed him and proclaimed the Khmer Republic. From Beijing, Sihanouk allied with his former enemies the Khmer Rouge, lending them decisive legitimacy in the countryside. After their victory in 1975 he returned as nominal head of state of Democratic Kampuchea but was held under house arrest, powerless, while several of his children died under the regime; he resigned in 1976. Released in 1979, he led a resistance coalition against the Vietnamese-backed government throughout the 1980s.

Restoration and later life

The 1991 Paris Peace Accords and UN-supervised elections restored Sihanouk as king in 1993 under a constitutional monarchy. Ill health led him to abdicate in 2004 in favour of his son Norodom Sihamoni. He spent his final years largely in Beijing, where he died in 2012; his cremation in Phnom Penh in February 2013 drew enormous crowds.
