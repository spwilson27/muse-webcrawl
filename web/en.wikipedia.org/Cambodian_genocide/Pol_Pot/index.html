Pol Pot (born Saloth Sâr; 19 May 1925 – 15 April 1998) was a Cambodian revolutionary and politician who ruled Cambodia as Prime Minister of Democratic Kampuchea between 1976 and 1979. He led the Khmer Rouge movement from 1963 until his death in 1998 and was the architect of the Cambodian genocide, in which an estimated 1.5 to 2 million people — roughly a quarter of the population — died.

Early life and rise

Born into a prosperous farming family, Saloth Sâr was educated in Phnom Penh and studied radio electronics in Paris from 1949, where he joined the French Communist Party and absorbed Marxist-Leninist ideas. Returning to Cambodia in 1953, he rose within the clandestine communist movement, becoming its leader in 1963 and fleeing to the jungle to wage guerrilla war.

Rule of Democratic Kampuchea

After the Khmer Rouge captured Phnom Penh on 17 April 1975, Pol Pot's regime attempted to create an agrarian socialist society in a single step: cities were emptied, money, markets, religion and private property were abolished, and the population was forced into collective farms. Mass executions of former officials, intellectuals, and ethnic minorities took place at sites known as the Killing Fields. The regime's radical policies, combined with purges and famine, caused mass death.

Fall and death

Vietnam invaded Cambodia in late 1978 and captured Phnom Penh in January 1979, driving the Khmer Rouge to the Thai border, from where it waged a guerrilla insurgency. Pol Pot remained a figurehead of the movement until internal purges in 1997, when he was arrested by rival Khmer Rouge commander Ta Mok and sentenced to life imprisonment by a show trial. He died in April 1998, reportedly of heart failure, before he could be handed over to an international tribunal.
