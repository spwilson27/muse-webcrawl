Two-stage-to-orbit

A two-stage launch vehicle separates its booster after the first phase of flight.

A two-stage-to-orbit (TSTO) or two-stage rocket is a launch vehicle in which two distinct stages provide propulsion consecutively in order to achieve orbital velocity. It is intermediate between a three-stage-to-orbit launcher and a hypothetical single-stage-to-orbit (SSTO) launcher.

At liftoff the first stage is at the bottom and is usually the largest, the second stage and the payload are at the top. When in flight, the first stage is jettisoned as its fuel is consumed, thereby reducing the mass of the remaining vehicle. The second stage then provides the remaining velocity needed to reach orbit.

Advantages

Compared with SSTO designs, TSTO vehicles can use mature, lower-performance engine and structural technologies while still achieving respectable payload fractions, because each stage can be optimized for its operating regime: the first stage for atmospheric flight and the second for vacuum operation. Compared with three- or four-stage vehicles, a TSTO system is simpler and has fewer separation events, reducing complexity and failure modes.

Examples

Many operational launch vehicles are two-stage designs, including the Falcon 9, Atlas V (in some configurations), and the Saturn IB. Proposed reusable TSTO systems have included air-launched and horizontal-takeoff concepts in which the first stage is a large aircraft or rocket plane that returns to the launch site.
