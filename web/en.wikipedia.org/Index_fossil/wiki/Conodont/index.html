Conodont

Conodonts (Greek kōnos, "cone", + odont, "tooth") are an extinct group of agnathan (jawless) vertebrates resembling eels, classified in the class Conodonta. For many years they were known only from their tooth-like feeding elements, microscopic phosphatic structures found abundantly in marine rocks from the Cambrian to the Triassic. Conodont elements are widely used as index fossils, and their color alteration with heating is used to gauge the thermal maturity of sedimentary rocks in petroleum geology.

Discovery and the animal

Conodont elements were first described by Christian Heinrich Pander in 1856, but the animal that bore them remained a mystery — the "conodont problem" — for over a century. In 1983, soft-bodied conodont fossils from the Lower Carboniferous of Scotland revealed an elongate, eel-like animal with large eyes, finned tail, and V-shaped muscle blocks (myomeres), identifying conodonts as early vertebrates and among the earliest known chordates with mineralized tissues.

Elements and feeding apparatus

The elements are composed of the mineral apatite and formed an apparatus in the animal's mouth or pharynx. They show microscopic wear consistent with food processing, making them among the earliest known vertebrate "teeth." Oxygen isotope analysis of conodont apatite is also used to reconstruct ancient ocean temperatures.

Extinction

Conodonts survived several mass extinctions but were finally eliminated in the end-Triassic extinction event, about 200 million years ago.
