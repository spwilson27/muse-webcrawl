Stratigraphy

Stratigraphy is a branch of geology concerned with the study of rock layers (strata) and layering (stratification). It is primarily used in the study of sedimentary and layered volcanic rocks. Stratigraphy has three related subfields: lithostratigraphy (the description of rocks by their physical characteristics), biostratigraphy (correlation by fossil content), and chronostratigraphy (the dating of rock strata and construction of the geologic time scale).

Principles

  • Law of superposition — in an undisturbed sequence, the oldest strata lie at the bottom (formulated by Nicholas Steno in 1669).
  • Principle of original horizontality — sediments are originally deposited in nearly horizontal layers.
  • Principle of lateral continuity — layers extend laterally until they thin out or are truncated.
  • Principle of cross-cutting relationships and faunal succession — later additions used to order strata and events.

History

Steno laid the foundations in the seventeenth century; William Smith applied faunal succession to map England's strata in the early nineteenth century; and Charles Lyell's Principles of Geology (1830–33) popularized uniformitarian interpretation. In archaeology, stratigraphy was adapted to the excavation of human-made deposits, where layers and their relationships (formalized in the Harris matrix) sequence artifacts and structures in time.
