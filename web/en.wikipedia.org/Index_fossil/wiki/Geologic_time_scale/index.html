Geologic time scale

The geologic time scale (GTS) is a system of chronological dating that classifies geological strata (stratigraphy) in time. It is used by geologists, paleontologists, and other Earth scientists to describe the timing and relationships of events in geologic history. The time scale is maintained by the International Commission on Stratigraphy.

Divisions

Geologic time is divided into a hierarchy of units. From largest to smallest, the principal units are:

  • Eons — the Hadean, Archean, Proterozoic, and Phanerozoic.
  • Eras — within the Phanerozoic: Paleozoic, Mesozoic, and Cenozoic.
  • Periods — e.g., Cambrian, Ordovician, Silurian, Devonian, Carboniferous, Permian; Triassic, Jurassic, Cretaceous; Paleogene, Neogene, Quaternary.
  • Epochs and ages — progressively finer subdivisions.

Development

The time scale was built up during the nineteenth century using the principles of stratigraphy — superposition, faunal succession, and cross-cutting relationships — before absolute ages could be measured. Boundary points are now defined by Global Boundary Stratotype Sections and Points ("golden spikes") at specific rock outcrops, and radiometric dating supplies numerical ages, placing the age of the Earth at about 4.54 billion years and the Cambrian explosion at roughly 541 million years ago.

Current time

The present epoch is the Holocene, beginning about 11,700 years ago after the last glacial period. A proposal to define a new epoch, the Anthropocene, reflecting significant human impact on the Earth system, has been widely debated.
