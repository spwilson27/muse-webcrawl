Ammonoidea

Ammonoids are extinct spiral-shelled cephalopods comprising the subclass Ammonoidea. They are more closely related to living coleoids (octopuses, squid, and cuttlefish) than to shelled nautiloids such as the living Nautilus. The earliest ammonoids appeared during the Devonian, and the last species were wiped out during the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event.

Ammonoids are important index fossils because of their rapid evolution, wide geographic distribution, and abundance in marine sedimentary rocks; distinct assemblages of ammonoid species characterize relatively narrow intervals of geologic time, allowing strata in different locations to be correlated.

Shell structure

The ammonoid shell is divided by septa into chambers; the animal lived in the outermost (body) chamber, and the inner chambers, collectively the phragmocone, were connected by a cord of tissue called the siphuncle and could be filled with gas to regulate buoyancy. The intricate patterns where septa meet the outer shell wall — the suture patterns — grew more complex through ammonoid evolution, progressing from simple goniatitic to ceratitic and finally to highly frilled ammonitic sutures.

Evolution and extinction

Ammonoids diversified through the Paleozoic and Mesozoic, surviving several mass extinctions only to radiate again. They flourished in the Jurassic and Cretaceous, including giant forms such as Parapuzosia over 1.5 m across and the heteromorph ammonites with uncoiled or irregularly shaped shells. The group finally became extinct at the end of the Cretaceous, 66 million years ago, along with the non-avian dinosaurs.
