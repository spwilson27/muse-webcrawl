Principle of faunal succession

The principle of faunal succession, also known as the law of faunal succession, is based on the observation that sedimentary rock strata contain fossilized flora and fauna, and that these fossils succeed each other vertically in a specific, reliable order that can be identified over wide horizontal distances. A bone from the Cenozoic era, for example, differs fundamentally from one from the Mesozoic or Paleozoic, so strata can be distinguished and correlated by their fossil contents.

History

The principle was formulated by the English engineer and geologist William Smith in the 1790s and early 1800s, while surveying for canals across England. Smith discovered that each stratum contained a unique assemblage of fossils and that the strata could be identified and ordered by those assemblages, allowing him to produce the first geological map of a whole country (1815). His insight preceded the theory of evolution; it was later explained by Darwin's work: organisms evolve and go extinct irreversibly, so fossil assemblages record a one-directional history of life.

Application

Faunal succession is the foundation of biostratigraphy and of the construction of the geologic time scale. It allows geologists to correlate strata between continents, date rocks relatively, and identify index fossils — species that are widespread but existed for only a short interval — as markers of particular intervals of time.
