Biostratigraphy

Biostratigraphy is the branch of stratigraphy which focuses on correlating and assigning relative ages of rock strata by using the fossil assemblages contained within them. The primary objective of biostratigraphy is correlation — demonstrating that a particular horizon in one geological section represents the same period of time as another horizon in a different section.

Fossils are useful for this purpose because organisms evolve irreversibly: once a species goes extinct it does not reappear, and new species evolve and spread widely in geologically short intervals. The best index fossils are abundant, widespread, easily preserved, and short-lived as species — for example ammonites in the Mesozoic, graptolites in the early Paleozoic, and planktonic foraminifera in Cenozoic marine sediments.

Biozones

Biostratigraphers divide strata into units called biozones, intervals of rock defined by their fossil content. Common types include range zones (the total stratigraphic range of a taxon), assemblage zones (defined by an association of taxa), and acme zones (intervals where a taxon is especially abundant). William Smith is considered the father of biostratigraphy for his early nineteenth-century recognition that strata could be identified by their characteristic fossils.

Applications

Biostratigraphy is fundamental to petroleum exploration, where microfossils from drill cuttings are used to date and correlate subsurface strata, and to constructing the geologic time scale. It is complemented by radiometric dating, magnetostratigraphy, and chemostratigraphy.
