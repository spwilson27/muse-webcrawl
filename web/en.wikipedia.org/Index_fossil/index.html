Index fossil

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Index fossils (also called guide fossils or indicator fossils) are fossils used to define and identify geologic periods. Index fossils must have a short vertical range in the rock record, wide geographic distribution, and rapid evolutionary turnover; moreover, distinctive features make the fossil recognisable, and abundant specimens make it likely to be found. The best index fossils are common, easy to identify at the species level, and have a broad distribution — otherwise the likelihood of finding and correctly identifying them in two sedimentary layers is poor.

The concept underlies biostratigraphy, the branch of stratigraphy that dates and correlates rock layers by their fossil content. It grows out of the principle of faunal succession, articulated by William Smith around 1800: sedimentary strata appear in a predictable order, each containing a distinctive assemblage of fossils, so strata in widely separated places can be matched by their index fossils.

Characteristics

A good index fossil has four qualities: it is distinctive (unmistakable when found), abundant, geographically widespread, and restricted to a short span of geologic time. Marine organisms that floated or swam widely — ammonoids, planktonic foraminifera, graptolites, conodonts — make excellent index fossils because ocean currents distributed them globally within geologically brief intervals, while their hard parts fossilised readily. Terrestrial fossils are generally poorer index fossils because their distribution is patchier; pollen and spores are notable exceptions because wind distributes them broadly.

Examples

  • Ammonites — rapidly evolving coiled cephalopods; the standard index fossils of Mesozoic marine rocks, with individual species sometimes spanning less than a million years, allowing fine subdivision of Jurassic and Cretaceous stages.
  • Trilobites — used extensively for Cambrian and other Palaeozoic strata; genera such as Paradoxides mark the middle Cambrian.
  • Graptolites — planktonic colonial hemichordates that define many Ordovician and Silurian zones.
  • Foraminifera — microscopic protists whose species define zones in Cretaceous and Cenozoic marine sediments, widely used in petroleum exploration (for example, Globotruncana).
  • Conodonts — tooth-like elements of early vertebrates, key index fossils for Palaeozoic and Triassic rocks and also used to estimate thermal maturity of source rocks.
  • Beech and other pollen — terrestrial sequences may be correlated by pollen zones.

Use in stratigraphy

Geologists divide the rock record into biozones — intervals characterised by particular index species or assemblages. The first appearance and last appearance of index taxa in a section define zone boundaries that can be traced between outcrops and boreholes, allowing strata to be dated relative to one another and to the geologic time scale. Radiometric dating of interbedded volcanic ash layers calibrates these biozones in absolute years. Index fossils are indispensable in subsurface geology, where oil and gas drilling recovers only small rock chips and cuttings: microfossils in the cuttings tell the driller which formation the bit is in.

Zones based on a single species (taxon-range zones), on overlapping ranges (concurrent-range zones), or on whole assemblages (assemblage zones) are all in use. The system has limits: facies control (an organism found only in one environment), provincialism (different faunas in different regions), and reworking of fossils into younger sediments can all mislead correlation if not recognised.
