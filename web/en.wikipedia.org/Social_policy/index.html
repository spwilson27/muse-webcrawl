Social policy

Social policy is the field of public policy and academic study concerned with how societies meet human needs for security, work, education, health, and wellbeing. It covers the design and effects of welfare state institutions — social security, public health services, education systems, housing, employment services, and personal social services — as well as the roles of markets, families, and voluntary organizations in welfare provision.

Scope and development

As an academic discipline, social policy (or social administration) developed in Britain with figures such as Richard Titmuss at the London School of Economics, examining how policies redistribute resources across the life course and between classes, and whether services should be universal or targeted (selective) at those in greatest need. Modern social policy analysis compares welfare regimes internationally — notably Gøsta Esping-Andersen's typology of liberal, conservative-corporatist, and social-democratic welfare states — and studies retrenchment, activation, and social investment approaches.

Key questions

Central questions include how to finance welfare sustainably amid demographic aging; how policy design affects poverty, inequality, gender relations, and labor supply; the balance between cash benefits and services; conditionality and sanctions in benefit systems; and the politics of welfare — why some programs enjoy durable public support while others are stigmatized. Social policy also engages issues of migration, childcare and long-term care, pensions reform, and the integration of health and social care.

Application

Social policy is produced not only by national governments but also by international bodies (the EU's social dimension, the ILO, the OECD, the World Bank's social protection programs) and subnational governments. Evidence-based policymaking, pilots and randomized evaluations, and distributional impact analysis are standard tools in the field.
