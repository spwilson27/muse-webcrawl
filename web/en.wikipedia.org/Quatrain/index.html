A quatrain is a type of stanza, or complete poem, consisting of four lines. Quatrains are the most common stanzaic form in European and many other poetic traditions, and they appear in poetry from ancient times to the present. A quatrain may stand alone as an epigrammatic poem or form the building block of longer works.

A Persian rubai, the most famous quatrain form

Forms and rhyme schemes

Quatrains are classified largely by their rhyme scheme:

  • ABAB — alternating rhyme, used in the heroic quatrain (iambic pentameter, also called the elegiac stanza) and in the first twelve lines of the Shakespearean sonnet, which consists of three quatrains and a couplet.
  • AABB — paired couplets, common in ballads and light verse.
  • ABBA — envelope rhyme, used in the "In Memoriam" stanza of Tennyson.
  • AABA / AAAA — the scheme of the Persian rubai, where the third line may be unrhymed.
  • Unrhymed quatrains occur in much modern and translated poetry, including many English versions of East Asian verse.

History

The quatrain is prominent in the poetry of ancient China, where the four-line jueju became a major form in the Tang dynasty. In Persia, the rubai (plural rubaiyat) reached its height with Omar Khayyam in the 11th–12th centuries; Edward FitzGerald's free English rendering, The Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam (1859), became one of the most popular poems of the Victorian era:

The Moving Finger writes; and, having writ,
Moves on: nor all thy Piety nor Wit
Shall lure it back to cancel half a Line,
Nor all thy Tears wash out a Word of it.

In Europe, quatrains structure hymns, folk ballads, the French quatrain of poets such as Nostradamus, and countless lyric poems. The form's brevity and balance make it a vehicle for meditations, proverbs, and epitaphs as well as narrative verse.
