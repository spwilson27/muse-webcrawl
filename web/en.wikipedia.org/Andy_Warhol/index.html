Andy Warhol (born Andrew Warhola Jr.; 6 August 1928 – 22 February 1987) was an American artist, film director and producer, and the leading figure of the pop art movement. His work explored the relationship between artistic expression, advertising and celebrity culture, using silkscreen printing to mass-produce images of consumer goods and famous faces.

Career

Warhol, the son of Slovak immigrants, trained in commercial art at the Carnegie Institute of Technology and became a highly successful advertising illustrator in 1950s New York. In the early 1960s he turned to fine art: his 1962 exhibition of Campbell's Soup Cans and silkscreen portraits of Marilyn Monroe, Elvis Presley and Elizabeth Taylor made him the emblematic pop artist. His studio, the Factory, became a famous gathering place for artists, musicians and socialites; his circle launched the Velvet Underground, whom he managed and produced. He developed a deadpan public persona — "I want to be a machine" — and predicted that "in the future, everyone will be world-famous for 15 minutes".

The shooting and later work

In June 1968 Warhol survived an assassination attempt by radical writer Valerie Solanas, which left him seriously injured for life. In the 1970s he became a portraitist of celebrities and wealthy patrons, co-founded Interview magazine, and in the 1980s collaborated with younger artists such as Jean-Michel Basquiat and Keith Haring. He died in 1987 from complications following gallbladder surgery.

Legacy

Warhol's works are among the most expensive paintings ever sold; his Shot Sage Blue Marilyn (1964) fetched US$195 million in 2022, a record for a 20th-century artwork at auction. The Andy Warhol Museum in Pittsburgh, his hometown, is the largest museum in North America dedicated to a single artist.
