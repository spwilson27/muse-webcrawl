Chinese Exclusion Act

The Chinese Exclusion Act was a United States federal law signed by President Chester A. Arthur on May 6, 1882, prohibiting the immigration of Chinese laborers. It was the first significant law restricting immigration into the United States and the first to bar a specific ethnic or national group.

Background

Chinese immigrants had arrived in large numbers during the California Gold Rush and later worked on the transcontinental railroad. Economic downturns in the 1870s fueled anti-Chinese sentiment, particularly in the American West, where labor organizations blamed Chinese workers for low wages. Violence and discriminatory local laws accompanied growing political pressure for exclusion.

Provisions

The Act suspended immigration of Chinese laborers, skilled and unskilled, for ten years, barred Chinese immigrants from naturalized citizenship, and required those who left the United States to obtain certificates for re-entry. It was renewed by the Geary Act in 1892, which also required Chinese residents to carry certificates of residence, and was made permanent in 1902. Subsequent amendments tightened enforcement and interrogation at stations such as Angel Island in San Francisco Bay.

Repeal and legacy

The Act was repealed by the Magnuson Act of December 17, 1943, when China was a wartime ally; the repeal set a small annual quota of 105 Chinese immigrants. Broader reforms followed with the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965. In 2011 and 2012, the U.S. Senate and House of Representatives passed resolutions expressing regret for the Exclusion Act.
