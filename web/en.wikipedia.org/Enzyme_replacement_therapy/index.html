Enzyme replacement therapy

Enzyme replacement therapy is commonly delivered by regular intravenous infusion of a manufactured replacement enzyme.

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) is a medical treatment that replaces an enzyme which is deficient or absent in the body. It is most closely associated with the lysosomal storage diseases, a group of inherited metabolic disorders in which a missing lysosomal enzyme causes undegraded substrate to accumulate in cells. The replacement enzyme is usually produced by recombinant DNA technology and administered by intravenous infusion, whereupon it is taken up by cells — typically via mannose or mannose-6-phosphate receptors — and delivered to lysosomes.

History

The concept was proposed in 1964 by Christian de Duve, and early work by Roscoe Brady and others at the US National Institutes of Health pursued enzyme infusion for Gaucher disease. Progress was initially slow because of difficulties in producing and targeting sufficient enzyme. The first commercially successful product was alglucerase (Ceredase), a placenta-derived glucocerebrosidase approved in 1991, followed by the recombinant imiglucerase (Cerezyme) in 1994.

Applications

ERT products exist for, among others:

  • Gaucher disease (imiglucerase, velaglucerase alfa, taliglucerase alfa)
  • Fabry disease (agalsidase alfa and beta)
  • Pompe disease (alglucosidase alfa and successors)
  • Mucopolysaccharidoses types I, II, IVA and VI (laronidase, idursulfase, elosulfase alfa, galsulfase)
  • Hypophosphatasia (asfotase alfa) and lysosomal acid lipase deficiency (sebelipase alfa)

Limitations

ERT does not cross the blood–brain barrier in useful amounts, so neurological manifestations of several lysosomal diseases respond poorly. Patients may develop anti-drug antibodies that reduce efficacy, and infusions can cause hypersensitivity reactions. Treatment is lifelong and extremely expensive, often costing several hundred thousand dollars per patient per year, which has raised questions about affordability and access. Alternatives and complements include substrate reduction therapy, pharmacological chaperones, haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and gene therapy.
