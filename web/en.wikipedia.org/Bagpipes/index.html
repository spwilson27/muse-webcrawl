Bagpipes are a woodwind instrument using enclosed reeds fed from a constant reservoir of air in the form of a bag. The Great Highland bagpipes are the best known variant in the Anglophone world, but bagpipes have been played for centuries throughout large parts of Europe, Northern Africa, Western Asia, and around the Persian Gulf. The term bagpipe is equally correct in the singular or the plural, though pipers usually refer to the instrument in the singular, e.g. "the pipes".

Construction

A bagpipe typically has an air supply (a blowpipe or bellows), a flexible animal-skin or synthetic bag into which air is blown and which the player squeezes to maintain pressure, a chanter with finger holes on which the melody is played, and usually one or more drones — long pipes that sound continuous fixed notes. The continuous air supply allows the bagpipe to produce an unbroken sound.

Types

Regional varieties are numerous. The Great Highland bagpipe of Scotland has three drones and a loud conical-bore chanter. Ireland's uilleann pipes are bellows-blown, quieter, and have a wider range, with keyed regulators for harmony. Spain and Portugal have the gaita; France the cornemuse and musette; Italy the zampogna; and eastern Europe and the Balkans many folk types such as the gaida.

History

Instruments with a reed pipe and an air reservoir are ancient; bagpipes are mentioned in Roman sources (Nero was said to play one) and appear across medieval Europe in art and literature. In Scotland the pipes became associated with clans, military regiments, and ceremonial occasions, and Scottish and Irish regiments spread the instrument worldwide.

Music

Highland piping includes ceòl mòr ("great music", the classical pibroch repertoire of extended theme-and-variation pieces) and ceòl beag ("little music": marches, strathspeys, and reels). Pipe bands, combining pipers with side, tenor, and bass drummers, compete internationally at events culminating in the World Pipe Band Championships in Glasgow.
