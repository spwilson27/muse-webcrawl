Bushmeat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (recreated from memory)

Bushmeat is meat from wildlife species that are hunted for human consumption, most often in parts of Africa, Asia, and Latin America. The term originally applied to the meat of African wild animals, including primates, antelope, rodents such as cane rats, pangolins, bats, and other mammals, as well as reptiles and amphibians. Bushmeat is an important source of protein and income for many rural communities, but commercial hunting at current scales threatens numerous species with extinction and is implicated in the transmission of zoonotic diseases.

Scope and species

In Central and West Africa, bushmeat commonly comes from primates, duikers and other antelope, porcupines, cane rats, and pangolins. In Asia, a wide variety of wild species are consumed, and in the Amazon and other parts of Latin America hunted wildlife includes monkeys, peccaries, tapirs, and large rodents. Estimates of the annual bushmeat harvest in the Congo Basin run to several million tonnes.

Conservation concerns

The commercial bushmeat trade has grown with logging roads opening remote forests to hunters and urban demand rising. Unsustainable hunting has caused severe declines in great ape populations and other species; conservationists describe emptied forests as the "empty forest syndrome." International trade in many affected species is restricted under CITES.

Disease risk

Handling and consuming bushmeat exposes people to zoonotic pathogens. Simian immunodeficiency viruses are believed to have crossed into humans as HIV through bushmeat hunting, and outbreaks of Ebola virus disease have been linked to handling infected carcasses. Public health campaigns in affected regions aim to reduce high-risk practices while respecting the nutritional role of wild meat.
