Mound Builders

The Mound Builders were members of various Indigenous peoples of North America who, during a roughly 5,000-year period, constructed various styles of earthen mounds for religious, ceremonial, burial, and elite residential purposes. These included the pre-Columbian cultures of the Archaic period, the Woodland period (Calusa, Adena, and Hopewell cultures), and the Mississippian culture, concentrated along the Eastern Woodlands and the great river valleys of the Mississippi and Ohio.

Cultures

The earliest mounds, such as Watson Brake in Louisiana (about 3500 BCE), were built by hunter-gatherer societies of the Archaic period. The Adena culture (c. 1000–200 BCE) and the Hopewell (c. 100 BCE–500 CE) built elaborate burial mounds and geometric earthworks, including the Great Serpent Mound. The Mississippian culture (c. 800–1600 CE) raised large platform mounds topped by temples and chiefs' houses; at Cahokia, Monks Mound rises about 30 metres and anchors the largest pre-Columbian city north of Mexico.

The "lost race" myth

In the 19th century, many American settlers and antiquarians attributed the mounds to a vanished non-Indian "lost race," a theory used to justify dispossession of Native peoples. Systematic investigations, culminating in Cyrus Thomas's 1894 report for the Bureau of Ethnology, demonstrated conclusively that the mounds were built by the ancestors of contemporary Native American peoples, a conclusion confirmed by modern archaeology.
