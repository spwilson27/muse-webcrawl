Single tax

The single tax is a proposal to replace all taxes with a single levy, most famously a tax on the unimproved value of land. It is the central policy of Georgism, the economic philosophy set out by the American economist and journalist Henry George in his 1879 bestseller Progress and Poverty. George argued that the rental value of land arises from nature and from the presence and activity of the community rather than from the landowner's efforts, so it should be collected for public revenue, while taxes on labour, capital and trade — which discourage production — should be abolished.

Arguments

Supporters contend that a land value tax is uniquely efficient: because the supply of land is fixed, taxing its value does not reduce its availability or distort economic decisions, unlike income or sales taxes. It is said to discourage speculation in idle land, encourage development, and be progressive and difficult to evade, since land cannot be hidden or moved offshore. Economists from Adam Smith and David Ricardo to Milton Friedman praised elements of the idea; Friedman called the tax on unimproved land value "the least bad tax".

History and influence

The idea had antecedents in the French Physiocrats' impôt unique and in proposals by John Locke, Thomas Paine and John Stuart Mill. George's popular movement in the 1880s–90s made him a major political figure — he nearly won the New York mayoralty in 1886 — and single-tax colonies and organisations spread internationally. Partial implementations of land value taxation exist in places such as Denmark, Estonia, parts of Australia and Pennsylvania's "split-rate" cities, though no country has adopted the full single tax.

Criticism

Critics question whether land rent alone could fund a modern state, dispute the practicality of assessing unimproved land values separately from improvements, and raise issues of fairness to those who bought land at prices reflecting untaxed rents. Others argue the theory neglects rents arising from other scarce assets, such as mineral rights, spectrum and intellectual property — extensions some modern Georgists incorporate into broader proposals.
