Silicon is a chemical element with the symbol Si and atomic number 14. It is a hard, brittle crystalline solid with a blue-grey metallic lustre, and is a tetravalent metalloid and semiconductor. Silicon is the second most abundant element in the Earth's crust (about 28% by mass), after oxygen, occurring mainly as silicon dioxide (silica) and silicate minerals.

History

Silica was known since antiquity, but the element itself was first isolated in an impure form by Jöns Jacob Berzelius in 1823, who also gave it its name, from the Latin silex (flint). Crystalline silicon was prepared by Henri Sainte-Claire Deville in 1854.

Properties

Silicon crystallizes in the diamond cubic structure. It is a semiconductor whose conductivity can be tuned by doping with elements such as boron and phosphorus — the basis of modern electronics. Silicon burns in oxygen to form silicon dioxide, and its surface naturally passivates with a thin oxide layer.

Applications

Ultra-pure silicon is the foundation of the semiconductor industry: transistors, integrated circuits, and most photovoltaic solar cells are made from it. Silicon is also used in alloys (aluminium–silicon), silicones, glass, ceramics, and as a component of Portland cement.

Occurrence

Silicon never occurs freely in nature but is abundant in sand, quartz, and most rocks. Commercial production involves reducing quartzite with carbon in an electric arc furnace.
