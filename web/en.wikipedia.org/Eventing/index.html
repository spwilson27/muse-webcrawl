Eventing

Highest governing bodyFEI (International Federation for Equestrian Sports)
PhasesDressage, cross-country, show jumping
OlympicSince 1912
Other namesThree-day eventing, horse trials, combined training

Eventing (also known as three-day eventing or horse trials) is an equestrian event in which a single horse and rider combination competes across the three disciplines of dressage, cross-country, and show jumping. The competition may run over one day (a horse trial or one-day event) or three days (a three-day event). Eventing has its roots in cavalry training, testing horses for obedience, stamina, courage, and athleticism.

Phases

Dressage opens the competition: horse and rider perform a prescribed sequence of movements in an arena, judged on precision, suppleness, and obedience. Cross-country follows, in which the pair gallops over a long outdoor course of solid, natural obstacles — logs, water complexes, ditches, and banks — against the clock. Show jumping concludes the event, testing whether the horse remains careful and athletic after the endurance phase, jumping knockable fences in an arena. Penalties from all three phases accumulate; the combination with the fewest penalties wins.

History

Eventing was introduced at the 1912 Stockholm Olympic Games, modelled on the tests required of cavalry officers' chargers. Originally restricted to military riders, it opened to civilians after the Second World War. Prestigious events include the Badminton Horse Trials and Burghley Horse Trials in England, the Kentucky Three-Day Event in the United States, and the Adelaide event in Australia — together forming the Grand Slam of Eventing along with Burghley and Kentucky.

Levels and safety

International competition is graded from one-star (CCI1*) to five-star (CCI5*) level. Because cross-country falls can be dangerous, the sport has invested heavily in safety: frangible fence technology, rider air vests, course-design standards, and extensive data collection on falls. Governing bodies continually revise rules on qualifications, so that horses and riders progress only when demonstrably ready for each level.
