Great books

The great books are those books that constitute the foundation of Western literature and belong to the Western canon. The term derives from a curriculum and movement in liberal arts education that emphasises the reading of primary texts considered essential to an educated person. The idea is associated with educators such as John Erskine of Columbia University, and later Robert Maynard Hutchins and Mortimer J. Adler at the University of Chicago.

History

John Erskine, a professor at Columbia, began teaching his General Honors course in 1920, structured around reading and discussing great books. In the 1930s and 1940s Robert Hutchins, president of the University of Chicago, and philosopher Mortimer Adler developed the idea into a full curriculum and a public movement for adult education. In 1952 Encyclopædia Britannica published the Great Books of the Western World, a 54-volume set edited by Hutchins and Adler, accompanied by the Syntopicon, an index of the "great ideas" traced through the texts. St. John's College in Annapolis and Santa Fe adopted an all-required great-books curriculum, which it retains.

Criteria

Adler argued that a great book must be worth rereading, must deal with enduring ideas and problems, and must be part of the "great conversation" among authors across centuries — each generation of writers responding to their predecessors. Typical lists begin with Homer, the Greek tragedians, Plato and Aristotle, and proceed through Virgil, Augustine, Aquinas, Dante, Chaucer, Machiavelli, Shakespeare, Cervantes, Montaigne, Descartes, Locke, Newton, Hume, Kant, Mill, Darwin, Marx, Tolstoy and Freud.

Criticism

The great-books approach has been criticised for privileging a largely European, male canon and for presenting contested questions of selection as settled. Debates intensified during the "canon wars" of the 1980s and 1990s, when universities such as Stanford revised their Western-culture requirements. Defenders respond that the lists are open to argument and revision and that the method — close reading of primary texts in seminar discussion — is separable from any fixed list.
