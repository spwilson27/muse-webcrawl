Assisted suicide

Assisted suicide, also called assisted dying or medical aid in dying depending on jurisdiction, is the practice by which a person with a terminal illness or incurable condition is provided with the means to end their own life, typically a prescription for lethal medication which the person self-administers. It is distinguished from euthanasia, in which another person performs the act.

Legal status

Assisted dying is legal under regulated conditions in a growing number of places, including Switzerland (where non-physician assisted suicide has long been permitted), the Netherlands, Belgium and Luxembourg (as part of broader euthanasia laws), Canada ("medical assistance in dying"), several U.S. states beginning with Oregon's Death with Dignity Act of 1994/1997, and the Australian states beginning with Victoria in 2017. Rules typically require adulthood, decision-making capacity, a terminal or grievous condition, repeated voluntary requests, and independent medical assessments.

Debate

Supporters ground their case in autonomy, relief of suffering and dignity. Opponents raise concerns about the protection of vulnerable people, the possibility of subtle coercion, errors in prognosis, and a "slippery slope" toward wider eligibility. Major medical bodies have historically been divided, with some adopting neutrality and others opposition.
