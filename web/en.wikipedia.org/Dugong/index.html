The dugong (Dugong dugon) is a marine mammal and the only living member of the family Dugongidae. It is one of four living species of the order Sirenia, together with the three manatees. The dugong is the only strictly herbivorous marine mammal.

Description

Dugongs have a fusiform body with no dorsal fin or hind limbs. The tail is fluked like a whale's, and the forelimbs are paddle-like flippers. Adults can reach about 3 metres (10 ft) in length and weigh up to 400 kg or more. The skin is thick and grey-brown. Males grow short tusks, modified incisor teeth, which usually erupt only in adults.

Distribution and habitat

Dugongs inhabit warm coastal waters of the Indo-Pacific, from East Africa and the Red Sea to northern Australia and the western Pacific islands. The largest population is in Australian waters, including Shark Bay and the Torres Strait. They depend on seagrass meadows in shallow, sheltered bays.

Behaviour and diet

Dugongs graze almost entirely on seagrasses, uprooting whole plants and leaving distinctive serpentine feeding trails in the meadows. They are generally solitary or found in small groups; large herds have become rare outside Australia. Females give birth to a single calf after a gestation of about 13–15 months, and calves nurse for up to two years. Dugongs can live for 70 years or more.

Conservation

The dugong is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN. Threats include habitat loss of seagrass meadows, entanglement in fishing nets, boat strikes, and hunting, which continues in some regions where dugong meat and oil are traditionally valued. The related Steller's sea cow was hunted to extinction in the eighteenth century.
