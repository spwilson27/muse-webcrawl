An essay film is a genre of non-fiction cinema that, like the literary essay, explores a subject through a personal, reflective, and often digressive voice rather than through plot or neutral documentation. Essay films typically combine documentary footage, found images, still photographs, and archive material with an intimate voiceover commentary that thinks aloud — questioning, associating, and revising itself — so that the film becomes a record of thought in progress.

Definition and history

The German artist Hans Richter first proposed the "film essay" in a 1940 text, imagining a form that could make invisible ideas visible on screen. André Bazin championed Chris Marker's Letter from Siberia (1958) as a model of the "horizontal" essay, in which meaning arises from the interaction of image and commentary rather than from either alone. Marker's Sans Soleil (1983), a meditation on memory, travel, and images narrated through letters read by a fictional cameraman, is widely regarded as the genre's masterpiece.

Other landmarks include Alain Resnais's Night and Fog (1956) on the Holocaust, the diary films of Jonas Mekas, Godard's video essays such as Histoire(s) du cinéma (1988–98), Agnès Varda's self-portraits, and the analytical works of Harun Farocki. In the digital era the form has flourished online as the video essay, ranging from scholarly film analysis to platform-native commentary.

Characteristics

Scholars such as Timothy Corrigan characterise the essay film by its subjective "I", its refusal of a single authoritative voice, its mixing of high and low material, and its tendency to make the act of thinking — rather than any settled conclusion — the true subject. The genre typically sits between documentary, fiction, and experimental film, and resists exact definition in the same spirit as the literary essay it is named for.
