Recombination (cosmology)

In cosmology, recombination refers to the epoch during which charged electrons and protons first became bound to form electrically neutral hydrogen atoms. Recombination occurred about 378,000 years after the Big Bang, at a redshift of z ≈ 1100, when the universe had cooled to roughly 3,000 K.

Process

Before recombination, the universe was an opaque plasma: photons scattered constantly off free electrons. As expansion cooled the plasma, electrons combined with nuclei — mostly hydrogen and helium. Once neutral atoms formed, photons could travel freely, an event called photon decoupling. That released radiation is observed today, redshifted and cooled to 2.725 K, as the cosmic microwave background.

Significance

Recombination marks the earliest time from which light can reach us directly, so the cosmic microwave background provides a snapshot of the universe at that epoch, studied by the COBE, WMAP and Planck satellites.
