Door-in-the-face technique

A large request that is refused, followed by a smaller target request.

The door-in-the-face technique is a compliance method commonly investigated in social psychology. It involves making a large request that the respondent will almost certainly refuse — metaphorically having the door slammed in one's face — followed by a second, more moderate request, which is the true target. People are significantly more likely to agree to the second request than if it had been asked on its own.

Original research

The technique was demonstrated by Robert Cialdini and colleagues in a 1975 experiment. Participants who were first asked to volunteer two hours a week for two years at a youth counselling centre (a request all refused) and then asked to chaperone a single two-hour zoo trip agreed about half the time — roughly three times the agreement rate of participants asked only for the zoo trip.

Explanations

The most common explanation is reciprocal concessions: the requester's retreat from the large request to the smaller one is perceived as a concession, creating social pressure to reciprocate with a concession of one's own — agreeing. Perceptual contrast may also contribute, as the second request seems smaller by comparison. The technique contrasts with the foot-in-the-door technique, which starts with a small request and escalates.

Applications

Variants of the technique are used in negotiation, sales, and fundraising, where an initial inflated demand sets an anchor against which the real offer appears reasonable.
