Aerial refueling

A tanker aircraft refuelling a fighter using a flying boom.

Aerial refueling, also referred to as air refueling, in-flight refueling (IFR), air-to-air refueling (AAR), and tanking, is the process of transferring aviation fuel from one aircraft (the tanker) to another (the receiver) while both aircraft are in flight. The two main refueling systems are probe-and-drogue, which is simpler to adapt to existing aircraft, and the flying boom, which offers faster fuel transfer but requires a dedicated operator station.

History

Early experiments in the 1920s involved transferring fuel between biplanes by hand-carried containers and hoses. In 1929 the U.S. Army Air Corps endurance flight of the Question Mark demonstrated sustained refueled flight over several days. The British company Flight Refuelling Ltd developed the looped-hose system used operationally in the 1930s and 1940s. In 1949 the U.S. Air Force B-50 Lucky Lady II completed the first non-stop flight around the world, refueled in air. The flying boom was developed by Boeing in the late 1940s, and the probe-and-drogue system was refined for carrier aviation in the 1950s. Air-to-air refuelling became central to Cold War bomber operations and to nearly all subsequent air campaigns.

Systems

In the probe-and-drogue system, the tanker trails a flexible hose with a conical drogue; the receiver flies its fixed or retractable probe into the drogue. It is used by the U.S. Navy and Marine Corps and by most other air forces. The flying boom is a rigid, telescoping tube extended by an operator ("boomer") in the tanker into a receptacle on the receiver, permitting much higher fuel flow rates; it is used primarily by the U.S. Air Force. Some tankers carry both systems, using wing-mounted hose-and-drogue pods alongside a boom.

Operations

Refueling extends range, endurance and payload: aircraft can take off with heavier weapons loads and reduced fuel, then top up in flight. Dedicated tankers such as the KC-135 Stratotanker, KC-10 Extender, KC-46 Pegasus and Airbus A330 MRTT support fighters, bombers, transports and helicopters worldwide, while smaller aircraft can act as "buddy" tankers for naval aviation.
