The Battle of Lake Regillus was a legendary early victory of the Roman Republic over the Latin League, traditionally dated to 496 BC (some sources give 499 BC). According to Roman tradition, the Latins, allied with the deposed Roman king Tarquin the Proud and his son-in-law Octavius Mamilius of Tusculum, marched to restore the monarchy. The Romans, led by the dictator Aulus Postumius Albus — who afterwards bore the surname Regillensis — defeated them beside Lake Regillus in the Alban Hills. The battle was famous in Roman memory for the reported intervention of the gods Castor and Pollux, who fought on the Roman side.

The traditional account

The fullest surviving accounts are those of Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus, written centuries after the event and embroidered with legend. The fighting was desperate: the Latin commander Mamilius led his men in person, the Roman master of the horse Titus Aebutius fought him in single combat, and both sides' commanders were wounded. In the crisis, Postumius threw the standard into the enemy ranks to rally his men and ordered the cavalry to dismount and fight on foot, which turned the battle. Tarquin the Proud, now an old man, was wounded and carried from the field; Mamilius was killed.

The Dioscuri legend

Tradition held that two handsome youths on white horses — Castor and Pollux, the Dioscuri — appeared in the Roman ranks and then were seen watering their horses at the Lacus Juturnae in the Roman Forum, announcing the victory. Postumius had vowed a temple to Castor during the battle; the Temple of Castor and Pollux in the Forum was dedicated in 484 BC and became one of Rome's most conspicuous monuments. The battle was also commemorated in the annual cavalry parade, the transvectio equitum, held on 15 July, the battle's traditional anniversary.

Historicity and aftermath

Modern historians treat the details as legend, though a real Roman–Latin conflict in the years after the expulsion of the kings is plausible. The first treaty between Rome and the Latin League, the Foedus Cassianum of 493 BC, followed soon after and is more firmly attested; whether it followed a Roman victory at Regillus or a negotiated settlement, it established Rome as an equal partner of the League and defined relations in Latium for over a century.
