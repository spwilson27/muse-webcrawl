Asiatic cheetah

StatusCritically Endangered (IUCN)
SubspeciesAcinonyx jubatus venaticus
RangeIran (formerly across Asia)

The Asiatic cheetah (Acinonyx jubatus venaticus) is a critically endangered cheetah subspecies that once ranged from the Arabian Peninsula and the Near East across Central Asia to India. Today it survives only in Iran, where a small population persists in the arid central plateau.

History

Asiatic cheetahs were famously kept and trained for hunting by Indian royalty; the Mughal emperor Akbar is said to have kept as many as a thousand. Hunting, habitat loss and the decline of prey species such as gazelles drove a long decline. The subspecies was declared extinct in India in 1952, after the last known animals were shot in 1947–48.

Surviving population in Iran

The Iranian population is estimated at only a few dozen individuals, concentrated in protected areas such as the Touran Biosphere Reserve, Miandasht Wildlife Refuge and Kavir National Park. Threats include road traffic, habitat fragmentation, poaching of prey and conflict with herders. Intensive camera-trapping and conservation programmes have documented a critically low number of breeding females.

Reintroduction to India

In 2022–2023, African cheetahs from Namibia and South Africa were released in India's Kuno National Park under Project Cheetah, an attempt to restore cheetahs to India using the African subspecies; the Asiatic subspecies itself remains confined to Iran.
