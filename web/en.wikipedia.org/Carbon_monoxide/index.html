Carbon monoxide (chemical formula CO) is a poisonous, colourless, odourless and tasteless flammable gas that is slightly less dense than air. It consists of one carbon atom and one oxygen atom joined by a triple bond. Carbon monoxide is produced by the incomplete combustion of carbon-containing fuels — in engine exhaust, faulty furnaces, wood stoves and fires — and in industry by reactions such as the water-gas reaction. It is the simplest carbon oxide.

Toxicity

Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin with an affinity roughly 240 times that of oxygen, forming carboxyhaemoglobin and progressively depriving tissues of oxygen; it also binds myoglobin and mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase. Because it is undetectable by human senses, it is sometimes called the "silent killer". Early symptoms of poisoning — headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion — mimic common illnesses; high exposures cause convulsions, coma and death. Exposure at 800 ppm can be fatal within two to three hours. Treatment is administration of 100% oxygen, often hyperbaric, to accelerate elimination. Domestic carbon-monoxide detectors are required or recommended in many jurisdictions; in the United States CO poisoning causes several hundred unintentional non-fire deaths each year.

Chemistry and uses

The molecule has an unusual electronic structure often drawn with a formal negative charge on carbon and positive charge on oxygen, consistent with its small dipole moment and its behaviour as a ligand. Carbon monoxide is an important industrial chemical: it is a component of synthesis gas used to make methanol, acetic acid (the Monsanto and Cativa processes) and aldehydes by hydroformylation; it is used in the Mond process to purify nickel; and as a strong ligand it forms metal carbonyls throughout organometallic chemistry. In the atmosphere it is removed mainly by reaction with hydroxyl radicals, with an average lifetime of a few months, indirectly affecting methane and ozone levels.

Biological role

Small quantities of carbon monoxide are produced in the body by haem oxygenase during the breakdown of haem, and the gas functions as a signalling molecule (gasotransmitter) with anti-inflammatory and vasodilatory effects — a role it shares with nitric oxide and hydrogen sulfide. Ambient background levels in clean air are typically below 0.2 ppm.
