Isotope

Term coined1913, by Margaret Todd; proposed in print by Frederick Soddy
Distinguishing featureNumber of neutrons (N)
NotationAZE, e.g. 146C

Isotopes are distinct nuclear species (nuclides) of the same chemical element. They have the same atomic number (number of protons in their nuclei) and position in the periodic table, but different nucleon numbers (mass numbers) due to different numbers of neutrons. While all isotopes of a given element have almost the same chemical properties, their atomic masses — and hence their physical properties — differ.

Terminology and notation

The term isotope comes from Greek roots meaning "same place", reflecting that isotopes occupy the same position in the periodic table. An isotope is specified by the element name followed by its mass number, as in carbon-12 or carbon-14, written symbolically as 12C and 14C.

Stability and radioactivity

Some isotopes are stable, while others — radioisotopes — undergo radioactive decay with characteristic half-lives. About 339 nuclides occur naturally, of which roughly 250 are stable. Isotopes with particular neutron-to-proton ratios are stable; the heaviest stable isotope is lead-208.

Applications

Isotopes are used as tracers in medicine and industry, in nuclear power and weapons, and in scientific dating methods such as radiocarbon dating, which exploits the decay of carbon-14. Stable isotope ratios also record past climates in ice cores and sediments.

History

Evidence for isotopes emerged from the study of radioactive decay chains in the early 1900s. Frederick Soddy received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1921 for the isotope concept, and Francis Aston's mass spectrograph confirmed that non-radioactive elements, beginning with neon, also have isotopes.
