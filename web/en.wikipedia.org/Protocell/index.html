Protocell

Fatty-acid vesicles can grow and divide, mimicking primitive cell behaviour.

A protocell (or protobiont) is a self-organised, spherical collection of lipids or other molecules proposed as a precursor to modern living cells. In origin-of-life research, protocells are hypothesised to bridge the gap between simple chemistry and biology: a membrane-bound compartment could concentrate reactants, protect an internal metabolism and, crucially, grow and divide so that its contents were passed on.

Properties sought by researchers

Experimental models, notably the fatty-acid vesicles studied by Jack Szostak and others, show that simple membranes can assemble spontaneously, take up nutrients, grow by incorporating additional fatty acids and divide under physical forces such as shear. A functional protocell model would combine three elements: a compartment (the membrane), an information-carrying polymer capable of replication (such as RNA, in the RNA world hypothesis), and a metabolism that harvests energy and materials from the environment.

Historical background

Alexander Oparin's coacervate droplets in the 1920s and Sidney Fox's proteinoid microspheres in the 1950s–60s were early protocell models. Modern work focuses increasingly on integrating replication chemistry inside vesicles and on plausible prebiotic routes to the necessary building blocks, including in hydrothermal-vent and wet–dry cycling scenarios.

Significance

Protocells are also studied in synthetic biology and astrobiology: building a minimal cell from non-living components would test theories of life's origin and sharpen criteria for recognising life elsewhere.
