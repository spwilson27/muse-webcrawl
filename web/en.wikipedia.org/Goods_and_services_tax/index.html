Goods and services tax

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A goods and services tax (GST) is a value-added tax levied on most goods and services sold for domestic consumption. The tax is paid by consumers, but it is remitted to the government by the businesses selling the goods and services. Businesses are generally able to claim credits for GST paid on their inputs, so the tax effectively falls only on the final consumer.

How it works

Under a GST, tax is collected at every stage of production and distribution, but each business in the chain claims an input tax credit for the GST it has paid, so only the value added at each stage is taxed. Many jurisdictions exempt or zero-rate essentials such as food, housing, healthcare, and education.

Countries using GST

Countries with a tax named GST include Canada, Australia (introduced in 2000 at 10%), New Zealand (introduced in 1986, now 15%), Singapore, Malaysia (2015–2018, later replaced), and India, which introduced a dual GST in 2017 unifying central and state taxes. France was the first country to implement a modern value-added tax in 1954.

Criticism

Critics argue that consumption taxes are regressive because lower-income households spend a larger share of their income. Supporters counter that they are efficient, hard to evade relative to income taxes, and can be paired with credits or exemptions to protect the poor.
