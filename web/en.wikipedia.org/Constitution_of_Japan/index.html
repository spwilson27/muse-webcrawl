The Constitution of Japan (Japanese: 日本国憲法, Nihon-koku Kenpō) is the constitution of Japan since 3 May 1947. It is often called the "Postwar Constitution" or the "Peace Constitution". It replaced the Meiji Constitution and provides for a parliamentary system of government and guarantees certain fundamental rights.

The constitution was drafted during the Allied occupation following the Second World War, principally by staff of the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, and was adopted through the amendment procedure of the Meiji Constitution by the Imperial Diet.

Key provisions

Article 1 defines the Emperor as "the symbol of the State and of the unity of the people," deriving his position from the will of the sovereign people. Article 9, the celebrated peace clause, renounces war as a sovereign right of the nation and the threat or use of force as a means of settling international disputes, and declares that land, sea, and air forces will never be maintained — a provision whose interpretation underlies the existence and expanding role of the Japan Self-Defense Forces.

Chapter III guarantees an extensive catalogue of rights, including equality under the law, freedom of thought, religion, assembly, and expression, the right to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness, and social rights such as the right to maintain minimum standards of wholesome and cultured living.

Amendment debate

Article 96 requires two-thirds majorities in both houses of the National Diet followed by a majority in a national referendum. Despite repeated proposals — particularly concerning Article 9 and the status of the Self-Defense Forces — the constitution has never been amended, making it one of the oldest unamended constitutions in the world. The 2015 security legislation reinterpreted Article 9 to permit collective self-defense, sparking continuing constitutional controversy.
