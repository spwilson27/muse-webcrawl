The Ptolemaic Kingdom (Koine Greek: Πτολεμαϊκὴ βασιλεία, Ptolemaïkḕ basileía) was an ancient Greek state based in Egypt during the Hellenistic period. It was founded in 305 BC by the Macedonian general Ptolemy I Soter, a companion of Alexander the Great, and ruled by the Ptolemaic dynasty until the death of Cleopatra VII in 30 BC.

With its capital at Alexandria — home to the Great Library and the Pharos lighthouse — the kingdom became a major center of Hellenistic culture, scholarship, and trade, and was the wealthiest of the successor states of Alexander's empire. At its territorial peak under Ptolemy III, it controlled Cyrenaica, Cyprus, parts of Syria, and coastal territories around the eastern Mediterranean.

History

Founding

After Alexander the Great's death in 323 BC, his generals (the Diadochi) divided the empire. Ptolemy took Egypt, famously securing Alexander's body for burial in Alexandria, and declared himself king in 305 BC. His dynasty practiced sibling marriage in imitation of Egyptian custom and presented itself as legitimate pharaohs to the native population while maintaining a Greek ruling elite.

Zenith and decline

Under Ptolemy II and Ptolemy III the kingdom reached its height of prosperity and power, fighting a series of Syrian Wars against the rival Seleucid Empire. From the reign of Ptolemy IV onward, dynastic strife, native revolts, and growing Roman influence weakened the state. Egypt became effectively a Roman protectorate.

Fall

Cleopatra VII, the last active ruler, allied first with Julius Caesar and then with Mark Antony. After their defeat by Octavian at the Battle of Actium in 31 BC and the fall of Alexandria, Cleopatra died in 30 BC and Egypt was annexed as a Roman province.

Government and society

The Ptolemies ran a highly bureaucratic, centrally controlled economy: royal monopolies on oil, salt, and banking, a census-based tax system, and a closed currency system underpinned the dynasty's wealth. Society was stratified, with Greeks and Macedonians holding privileged civic status above native Egyptians, though cultures blended significantly over three centuries.

Culture

Alexandria's Mouseion and Great Library made it the intellectual capital of the Greek world, attracting scholars such as Euclid, Eratosthenes, and Herophilus. The cult of Serapis, a syncretic Greco-Egyptian god promoted by Ptolemy I, exemplified the dynasty's cultural fusion.
