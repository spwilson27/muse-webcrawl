Cardiac muscle

Cardiac muscle (also called heart muscle or myocardium) is one of three types of vertebrate muscle tissue, the others being skeletal muscle and smooth muscle. It is an involuntary, striated muscle found only in the walls of the heart, where its rhythmic contractions pump blood around the body. An adult human heart beats about 100,000 times a day.

Structure

Cardiac muscle cells (cardiomyocytes) are short, branched cells, each usually containing a single central nucleus. They are joined end to end by intercalated discs containing gap junctions, which allow ions and electrical signals to pass directly between cells so that the tissue behaves as a functional syncytium. The cells are rich in mitochondria to sustain continuous aerobic work and are organised into the three layers of the heart wall: epicardium, myocardium and endocardium.

Physiology

Unlike skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle does not require external nerve input to contract: pacemaker cells of the sinoatrial node generate the cardiac action potential, which spreads through the conduction system. Its long refractory period prevents sustained tetanic contraction, which would be fatal in a pump. The autonomic nervous system and hormones such as adrenaline modulate rate and force. Damage to cardiac muscle, as in myocardial infarction, heals mainly by scarring, since cardiomyocyte regeneration is very limited.
