Hobbs Act

Hobbs Act
EnactedJuly 3, 1946
Codified18 U.S.C. § 1951
Named forRep. Sam Hobbs (D-AL)

The Hobbs Act, enacted by the United States Congress in 1946 and codified at 18 U.S.C. § 1951, prohibits actual or attempted robbery and extortion affecting interstate or foreign commerce, as well as conspiracy to do so. It is a principal federal tool for prosecuting public corruption, gang violence, and organized crime.

Origins

The Act was passed in response to the Supreme Court's decision in United States v. Local 807 (1942), which had exempted certain union conduct from the Anti-Racketeering Act of 1934. Sponsored by Representative Sam Hobbs of Alabama, the new statute targeted racketeering violence, while courts later clarified its application to legitimate union activity.

Scope and use

"Hobbs Act extortion" includes obtaining property from another, with consent, induced by wrongful use of force, fear, or under color of official right — the last category making the Act central to bribery and corruption prosecutions of public officials, as in Evans v. United States (1992). Prosecutors need show only a de minimis effect on interstate commerce. Penalties reach 20 years' imprisonment. The Supreme Court has addressed the Act in cases such as Scheidler v. National Organization for Women (2003, 2006) on its use against protest movements, Sekhar v. United States (2013) limiting "extortion", and Taylor v. United States (2016) on drug robbery prosecutions.
