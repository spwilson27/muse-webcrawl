Chiaroscuro

Chiaroscuro (Italian for "light–dark") is the use of strong contrasts between light and dark, usually bold contrasts affecting a whole composition. It is a technical term used by artists and art historians for the use of contrasts of light to achieve a sense of volume in modelling three-dimensional objects and figures. The term also names a woodcut printmaking technique using multiple blocks.

History in painting

Graduated shading developed through the Renaissance: Leonardo da Vinci explored subtle modelling (sfumato), while Caravaggio and his followers — the Caravaggisti — pushed chiaroscuro into dramatic tenebrism, with mostly dark compositions lit by a single intense source, as in The Calling of St Matthew. Rembrandt made nuanced chiaroscuro a hallmark of his paintings and etchings; Georges de La Tour lit candlelit scenes with it in France.

Printmaking and drawing

In printmaking, chiaroscuro woodcuts, developed in Germany and Italy in the early 16th century (Ugo da Carpi claimed the invention in Italy), use a line block plus tone blocks printed in different colours to simulate wash drawings. The term also applies to drawings built up with white gouache highlights on dark prepared paper.

Other media

The concept transferred to photography and cinematography, where low-key, high-contrast lighting influenced by painting — notably in film noir — is often described as chiaroscuro.
