Digital distribution

Digital distribution, also referred to as content delivery, online distribution, or electronic software distribution, among others, is the delivery or distribution of digital media content such as audio, video, e-books, video games, and other software over the internet. The term is generally used to describe distribution over an online delivery medium, such as the internet, thus bypassing physical distribution methods, such as paper, optical discs and videocassettes.

History

Software was shared digitally as early as the ARPANET era, and commercial bulletin board systems and shareware archives expanded the practice in the 1980s and 1990s. Mainstream digital distribution took off with online music stores such as Apple's iTunes Store (2003), Valve's Steam platform for games (2003), and later app stores for mobile devices, which normalised purchasing and downloading media directly to consumer hardware.

Characteristics

Digital distribution eliminates manufacturing, warehousing and retail shelf-space costs, enabling global simultaneous release and long-tail availability of niche titles. It also allows automatic patching, downloadable content and subscription access. Content is typically protected by digital rights management (DRM), and purchasers generally receive a licence rather than ownership of a physical object, raising questions about resale, preservation and the effect of storefront closures on purchased libraries.

Impact

The shift to digital delivery transformed the economics of music, film, publishing and games: streaming services largely displaced downloads for music and video, independent creators gained direct routes to audiences, and physical retailers lost share. Network bandwidth and data caps remain constraints for large downloads, and the digital divide affects who can participate fully in digital markets.
