Fever

Definition: Elevated core body temperature above the normal range due to an increase in the hypothalamic set point
Threshold: Generally ≥ 38.0 °C (100.4 °F)
Causes: Infection, inflammation, malignancy, drugs

Fever, also referred to as pyrexia, is defined as having a temperature above the normal range due to an increase in the body's temperature set point in the hypothalamus. Normal human body temperature varies through the day, but a temperature of about 38.0–38.3 °C (100.4–100.9 °F) or higher is generally considered a fever. Fever differs from hyperthermia, in which the set point is unchanged and temperature rises because heat production overwhelms heat loss.

Mechanism and symptoms

Fever is usually triggered by pyrogens — substances such as bacterial endotoxins and cytokines (interleukin-1, interleukin-6, TNF-α) — which stimulate prostaglandin E2 production in the hypothalamus, raising the set point. While the set point is being raised the person feels cold and may shiver (rigors); when the fever breaks, sweating and flushing occur. Associated symptoms include headache, muscle aches, dehydration, and malaise. Fever is a component of the acute-phase response and appears to aid the immune system by enhancing some immune functions and inhibiting growth of some pathogens.

Causes and management

The most common cause is infection — viral, bacterial, or otherwise — but fever also occurs with inflammatory diseases, cancers, tissue injury, and certain drugs. Fever in young infants, very high fevers, prolonged fevers, and fevers of unknown origin warrant medical evaluation. Antipyretics such as paracetamol (acetaminophen) and ibuprofen lower the set point and relieve discomfort, though there is debate about whether routinely suppressing mild fever is beneficial. Febrile seizures can occur in a small proportion of children between six months and five years of age.
