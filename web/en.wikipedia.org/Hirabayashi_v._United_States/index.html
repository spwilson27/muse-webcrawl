Hirabayashi v. United States, 320 U.S. 81 (1943), was a case in which the Supreme Court of the United States held that the application of curfews against members of a minority racial group was constitutional. Gordon Hirabayashi, an American citizen of Japanese ancestry and a student at the University of Washington, had deliberately violated the military curfew imposed on Japanese Americans on the West Coast and failed to report for relocation during the Second World War.

Decision

In a unanimous opinion by Chief Justice Harlan F. Stone, the Court upheld Hirabayashi's curfew conviction, reasoning that the war powers of the federal government permitted distinctions based on ancestry in the face of a threatened invasion. The Court avoided ruling on the exclusion conviction. The decision, a companion to Yasui v. United States and a precursor to Korematsu v. United States (1944), has since been widely condemned as part of the jurisprudence that enabled the incarceration of Japanese Americans.

Later developments

In the 1980s, evidence emerged that the government had suppressed intelligence reports contradicting claims of military necessity. Through a writ of coram nobis, Hirabayashi's convictions were vacated by the federal courts in 1986–87. Hirabayashi was posthumously awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2012.
