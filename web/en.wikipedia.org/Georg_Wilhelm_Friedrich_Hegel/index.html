Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (27 August 1770 – 14 November 1831) was a German philosopher and an important figure of German idealism. His work spans logic, metaphysics, aesthetics, history, religion and politics, and his comprehensive system — in which reality is understood as the self-unfolding of Geist (Spirit/Mind) toward self-knowledge and freedom — made him one of the most influential, and contested, thinkers of the modern era.

Life

Born in Stuttgart, Hegel studied theology at the Tübinger Stift alongside Hölderlin and Schelling. After working as a tutor, he became a lecturer at Jena, where he completed The Phenomenology of Spirit (1807) as Napoleon's army fought the Battle of Jena nearby. He later served as rector of a gymnasium in Nuremberg, then as professor at Heidelberg, and from 1818 held the prestigious philosophy chair at the University of Berlin, where his lectures drew large audiences. He died in 1831, probably of cholera.

Philosophy

Hegel's method is dialectical: concepts and historical forms develop through internal contradictions that are overcome — preserved and cancelled at once — in richer unities, a process he calls Aufhebung (sublation). The Phenomenology of Spirit traces consciousness from sense-certainty through self-consciousness (including the famous master–slave dialectic and the "unhappy consciousness") to Absolute Knowing. His mature system divides into the Science of Logic, the Philosophy of Nature, and the Philosophy of Spirit, culminating in art, religion and philosophy as forms of Absolute Spirit. In the Elements of the Philosophy of Right (1820) he presented his political philosophy: freedom is actualised in the institutions of family, civil society and the rational state.

Influence

After his death his followers split into Right and Left (Young) Hegelians; Karl Marx turned Hegel's dialectic toward materialism, making Hegel a root of Marxism, while Kierkegaard, and later existentialism, phenomenology, critical theory and communitarianism all reacted to or drew upon him. Analytic philosophy began partly in revolt against British Hegelianism, though Hegel's reputation has revived in recent decades.
