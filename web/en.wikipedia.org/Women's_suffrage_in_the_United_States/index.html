Women's suffrage in the United States

Women's suffrage in the United States was achieved through a decades-long movement culminating in the ratification of the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920, which prohibited denying the right to vote on account of sex. Before the amendment, many states — especially in the West — had already granted women full or partial voting rights.

Origins

The movement formally began at the Seneca Falls Convention of 1848, where Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott issued a Declaration of Sentiments modeled on the Declaration of Independence. Activists such as Susan B. Anthony and Sojourner Truth linked suffrage to abolition and women's broader legal rights.

State-by-state progress and splits

Wyoming Territory enfranchised women in 1869 and kept the right upon statehood in 1890; Colorado, Utah, and Idaho followed in the 1890s. After the Civil War the movement split over the Fifteenth Amendment, reuniting in 1890 as the National American Woman Suffrage Association. In the 1910s a more militant wing, Alice Paul's National Woman's Party, picketed the White House and endured imprisonment and hunger strikes.

The Nineteenth Amendment

Congress passed the amendment in 1919, and Tennessee became the 36th and deciding state to ratify on August 18, 1920. In practice, many Black, Native, and Asian-American women remained disenfranchised by Jim Crow laws, citizenship exclusions, and state barriers until the Voting Rights Act of 1965 and other reforms.
