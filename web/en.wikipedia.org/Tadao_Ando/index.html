Tadao Ando (安藤 忠雄, Andō Tadao, born 13 September 1941) is a Japanese architect, regarded as one of the most influential figures in contemporary architecture. Self-taught, he is known for his minimalist use of exposed cast-in-place concrete, his emphasis on light, geometry, and nature, and an approach often described as critical regionalism. He received the Pritzker Architecture Prize in 1995.

The Church of the Light (1989) in Ibaraki, Osaka, one of Ando's signature works

Early life and training

Ando was raised in Osaka and worked as a boxer and truck driver before deciding to become an architect. Lacking formal training, he educated himself through travel and study, visiting buildings by Le Corbusier, Frank Lloyd Wright, Louis Kahn, and Alvar Aalto in Europe, Africa, and the United States in the 1960s. He founded his practice, Tadao Ando Architect & Associates, in Osaka in 1969.

Career and style

His breakthrough work was the Azuma House (1976) in Osaka, a stark concrete box that turned inward around a courtyard. International recognition followed with works such as the Church of the Light (1989), the Water Temple (1991), the Pulitzer Arts Foundation in St. Louis, the Modern Art Museum of Fort Worth, and the Chichu Art Museum on Naoshima, built largely underground. Ando's buildings are characterized by smooth concrete surfaces formed with meticulous craftsmanship, dramatic cuts of light, and a dialogue between solid geometry and the natural elements of wind, water, and sky.

Honors

In addition to the Pritzker Prize, Ando has received the Praemium Imperiale and the Order of Culture of Japan. He has held professorships at Yale, Columbia, Harvard, and the University of Tokyo.
