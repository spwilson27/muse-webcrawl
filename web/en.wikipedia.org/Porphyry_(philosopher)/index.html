Porphyry (philosopher)

Porphyry of Tyre (c. 234 – c. 305 AD) was a Neoplatonic philosopher born in Tyre, Phoenicia (modern Lebanon). He was the student of Plotinus, whose works he edited and published as the Enneads, and the author of works on philosophy, religion, and vegetarianism that were highly influential in late antiquity and the Middle Ages.

Life

Born Malchus, he studied in Athens under the philologist Longinus before joining Plotinus in Rome in 263. Suffering from depression, he withdrew to Sicily on Plotinus's advice, later returning to Rome, where he taught and wrote prolifically. His works survive in Greek, Latin, and Arabic transmission.

Works

Porphyry's Isagoge, a short introduction to Aristotle's Categories, became the standard logic textbook for over a thousand years in the Greek, Latin, Syriac, and Arabic worlds. In it he posed the famous questions about genera and species — whether they exist in reality or only in thought — that framed the medieval problem of universals. His "Tree of Porphyry" diagrammed the classification of substance by differentiae.

His treatise On Abstinence from Animal Food is a major ancient defence of vegetarianism. His fifteen-book Against the Christians was a systematic critique of Christianity, later burned by imperial decree; its contents are known partly from the refutations it provoked. He also wrote a Life of Plotinus, a Life of Pythagoras, commentaries on Plato and Aristotle, and works on Homer and astrology.
