Edward Gibbon (8 May 1737 – 16 January 1794) was an English historian and Member of Parliament, best known for The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, published in six volumes between 1776 and 1789. The work is celebrated for its ironic, elegant prose, its use of primary sources, and its sceptical treatment of religion, and it remains a landmark of English historical writing.

Life

Gibbon was educated briefly at Magdalen College, Oxford, but left after converting to Catholicism in 1753; his father sent him to Lausanne, Switzerland, where he returned to Protestantism and acquired the French-influenced classical education that shaped his style. In Lausanne he fell in love with Suzanne Curchod, later the mother of Madame de Staël, but the match was forbidden by his father. On a visit to Rome in 1764, seated amid the ruins of the Capitol while friars sang vespers in the Temple of Jupiter, he conceived the idea of writing the history of the city's decline. He sat in Parliament from 1774, almost always voting silently with the government.

The Decline and Fall

The first volume appeared in 1776 to acclaim, though its fifteenth and sixteenth chapters — attributing the empire's fall partly to the otherworldly influence of early Christianity — provoked fierce controversy. The concluding volumes, completed in Lausanne in 1787, carried the narrative to the fall of Constantinople in 1453. Gibbon's ironic detachment, mastery of detail and balanced periods made the work a classic, though his thesis has been much revised by later scholarship. His Memoirs, published posthumously, are regarded as a classic of autobiography.
