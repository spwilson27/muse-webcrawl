Psychological resilience

Psychological resilience is the ability of an individual to cope with a crisis, adapt to stress and adversity, and return to a pre-crisis state of functioning. Resilient people are able to use their skills and strengths to respond to life's challenges, which can include trauma, tragedy, threats or significant sources of stress such as family problems, health problems or workplace and financial stressors.

Background

Research on resilience grew out of developmental psychology studies of children who thrived despite poverty, parental mental illness or abuse, notably Emmy Werner's longitudinal Kauai study. Ann Masten described resilience as "ordinary magic" arising from common adaptive systems — secure attachment, cognitive skills, supportive relationships — rather than rare personal qualities.

Factors

Factors associated with resilience include positive emotions and optimism, problem-solving ability, a sense of purpose, social support, and physical health. Resilience is now generally viewed as a dynamic process rather than a fixed trait, varying across contexts and over the lifespan.

Interventions

Psychological therapies, mindfulness training, and programmes in schools and the military aim to strengthen resilience. Critics caution against framing resilience as purely an individual responsibility when adversity is driven by structural conditions.
