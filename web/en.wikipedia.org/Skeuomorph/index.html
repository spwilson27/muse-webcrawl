Skeuomorph

Skeuomorph Etymology: Greek skeuos (vessel/tool) + morphē (shape)
Fields: Archaeology; industrial design; user interface design

A skeuomorph is a derivative object that retains ornamental design cues from structures that were necessary in the original, even when the new material or technology makes them redundant. The term was coined in the nineteenth century from the Greek words for vessel or tool and shape, and was first used in archaeology and the decorative arts to describe, for example, pottery vessels imitating the rivets and forms of metalwork.

In digital design

Skeuomorphism became widely discussed in user interface design, where it denotes interfaces that imitate real-world counterparts: a notes app resembling a yellow legal pad, a bookshelf rendered in wood, or a floppy-disk save icon. Early versions of Apple's iOS under Steve Jobs and Scott Forstall were strongly skeuomorphic, using rich textures such as stitched leather and green felt to make unfamiliar touch interfaces approachable.

Decline and debate

From about 2013, with Microsoft's Metro design language and Apple's iOS 7 redesign under Jony Ive, mainstream interface design shifted toward "flat design", discarding realistic textures in favour of typography, colour and simple geometry. Defenders of skeuomorphism argue that familiar physical metaphors aid learnability and affordance; critics contend they add visual clutter and constrain design to outdated metaphors. Many interfaces retain functional skeuomorphs, such as the trash-can icon and the camera shutter sound.
