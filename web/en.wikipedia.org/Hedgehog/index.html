Hedgehog

A hedgehog is a spiny mammal of the subfamily Erinaceinae, in the eulipotyphlan family Erinaceidae. There are seventeen species of hedgehog in five genera found throughout parts of Europe, Asia, and Africa, and in New Zealand by introduction. There are no hedgehogs native to Australia and no living species native to the Americas, although the extinct genus Amphechinus was once present in North America.

Description

Hedgehogs are easily recognised by their spines, which are hollow hairs stiffened with keratin; unlike porcupine quills, they are neither barbed nor readily detached. A hedgehog has roughly 5,000–7,000 spines. When threatened, the animal rolls into a tight ball, tucking its head, feet, and tail inside and presenting only spines to a predator. Hedgehogs are nocturnal, have poor eyesight but keen senses of smell and hearing, and feed mainly on invertebrates, making them a traditional friend of gardeners. They are also relatively resistant to snake venom.

Life history

Depending on the species, pregnancy lasts 30 to 60 days; litters average three to five hoglets in larger species. Many species hibernate through the winter, and desert species may aestivate. Hedgehogs also perform a curious behaviour called self-anointing, spreading frothy saliva over their spines after encountering strong smells.

Relations with humans

Hedgehogs appear in the folklore and literature of many cultures, from ancient Greece to Beatrix Potter's Mrs. Tiggy-Winkle. Some species, especially the African pygmy hedgehog, are kept as exotic pets. The European hedgehog has declined sharply in parts of its range, prompting conservation concern.
