Federal Radio Commission

The Federal Radio Commission (FRC) was a United States government agency created by the Radio Act of 1927 to regulate radio broadcasting. It replaced the largely ineffective licensing regime administered by the Department of Commerce under Secretary Herbert Hoover. In 1934 the FRC was succeeded by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) under the Communications Act of 1934.

Creation and mandate

By the mid-1920s the American airwaves were chaotic: hundreds of stations broadcast on overlapping frequencies, interfering with one another. The Radio Act of 1927 established the principle that the electromagnetic spectrum belonged to the public, and that broadcasters could use it only under temporary licenses issued in the "public interest, convenience, and necessity." The five-member commission was empowered to assign frequencies, set power limits and hours of operation, and deny license renewals.

General Order 40

The commission's most consequential act was General Order 40 (1928), a sweeping reallocation of the AM band. It created clear channels reserved for high-power stations, regional and local channels, and removed many small, educational, and nonprofit broadcasters from the air in favor of large commercial network affiliates. Critics charged that the reallocation privileged commercial broadcasting in the United States, a structure that endured for decades.

Notable actions

The FRC refused to renew the licenses of controversial broadcasters, most famously Kansas station KFKB of "goat gland" doctor John R. Brinkley and Los Angeles evangelist Robert P. Shuler. In the Brinkley case the courts upheld the commission's authority, establishing that programming content could be considered in licensing decisions without constituting censorship under the First Amendment.

Succession

The Communications Act of 1934 abolished the FRC and transferred its functions, along with regulation of telephone and telegraph, to the newly created FCC. The licensing framework and public-interest standard created in the FRC era remain the basis of American broadcast regulation.
