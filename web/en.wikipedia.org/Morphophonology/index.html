Morphophonology

Morphophonology
FieldLinguistics
InterfacesMorphology, phonology
Key notionsAlternation, allomorph, underlying form

Morphophonology (also morphophonemics or morphonology) is the branch of linguistics that studies the interaction between morphological and phonological or phonetic processes. Its primary focus is on the sound changes that take place in morphemes (minimal meaningful units) when they combine to form words.

Alternations

Morphophonological phenomena are usually described as alternations: a single morpheme appears in different phonological shapes (allomorphs) depending on its context. A classic English example is the plural suffix, realised as /s/ in cats, /z/ in dogs, and /ɨz/ in horses. Another is the alternation between electric /k/ and electricity /s/, a historical velar softening conditioned by the following vowel.

Levels of analysis

Analyses differ in how abstract the underlying forms are taken to be. Structuralist morphophonemics posited morphophonemes — abstract units standing for sets of alternating phonemes. Generative phonology, beginning with Chomsky and Halle's The Sound Pattern of English (1968), treated many morphophonological alternations as the output of ordered phonological rules applying to underlying forms identical or close to one of the surface variants. Later theories such as lexical phonology organise these rules into levels tied to morphological structure.

Types of alternation

  • Phonologically conditioned — the shape of a morpheme is determined by neighbouring sounds, as with the English plural.
  • Morphologically conditioned — the alternation depends on grammatical category, as in strong-verb vowel changes (sing/sang/sung).
  • Lexically conditioned — alternations restricted to particular words, such as the /f/–/v/ alternation in leaf/leaves but not chief/chiefs.
