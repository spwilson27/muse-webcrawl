James II (14 October 1633 – 16 September 1701), also James VII of Scotland, was King of England, Scotland, and Ireland from 1685 until he was deposed in the Glorious Revolution of 1688. He was the last Catholic monarch of England, Scotland, and Ireland.

Early life and accession

The younger son of Charles I, James escaped to the continent during the English Civil War and served in the French and Spanish armies. After the Restoration he was a capable Lord High Admiral. His conversion to Catholicism became public in the 1670s, provoking attempts in Parliament to exclude him from the succession, but he succeeded his brother Charles II in 1685 and easily defeated the Monmouth Rebellion.

Reign and deposition

James alarmed the Protestant establishment by promoting Catholics in the army and universities, asserting a royal power to suspend laws, and issuing Declarations of Indulgence granting religious toleration — seen as a route to Catholic supremacy. The birth of a Catholic heir, James Francis Edward, in June 1688 displaced his Protestant daughter Mary in the succession. Leading politicians invited Mary's husband, William of Orange, to intervene; William landed with an army in November 1688, James's support collapsed, and he fled to France. Parliament declared he had abdicated.

Exile and legacy

James attempted a comeback in Ireland, but was defeated at the Battle of the Boyne in 1690. He lived thereafter at Saint-Germain-en-Laye under Louis XIV's protection. The settlement after 1688 — the Bill of Rights and parliamentary supremacy — reshaped the British constitution; his descendants, the Jacobites, pressed the Stuart claim for decades.
