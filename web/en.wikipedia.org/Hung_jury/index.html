Hung jury

Hung jury
Also called: Deadlocked jury
Consequence: Mistrial
Used in: Jury systems, chiefly the United States

A hung jury, also called a deadlocked jury, is a jury that cannot agree upon a verdict after extended deliberation and is unable to reach the required unanimity or supermajority. A hung jury generally results in a mistrial, after which the case may be retried, dismissed, or resolved by plea agreement.

Procedure

When jurors report deadlock, a judge may urge further deliberation, in the United States often through an Allen charge, which encourages jurors in the minority to reconsider without surrendering honestly held beliefs. If deadlock persists, the judge declares a mistrial. Because no verdict was reached, retrial does not violate double jeopardy protections.

Frequency and causes

Studies in the United States find hung juries in a small percentage of jury trials, varying by jurisdiction. Causes include weak or conflicting evidence, juror nullification, credibility disputes and strongly held individual views. Unanimity rules — required in US federal criminal cases and, since Ramos v. Louisiana (2020), in state criminal cases — affect the likelihood of deadlock.

Other jurisdictions

Many common-law jurisdictions now accept majority verdicts after a period of deliberation, making formal hang outcomes rarer. Where majority verdicts are unavailable, a hung jury has similar consequences to those in the US.

Notable examples

The first criminal trial of Bill Cosby in 2017 ended with a hung jury; he was convicted at retrial (later overturned on other grounds). Several trials in the 1990s of the officers accused in widely publicised cases also deadlocked on some counts.
