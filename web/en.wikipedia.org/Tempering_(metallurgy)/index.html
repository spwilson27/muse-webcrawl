Tempering (metallurgy)

Tempering
ProcessHeat treatment of hardened steel
Typical range150–650 °C, below the critical temperature
PurposeTrade some hardness for increased toughness

Tempering is a heat treatment process applied to ferrous alloys, most commonly quenched steel, in which the metal is reheated to a temperature below its lower critical point and then cooled, in order to achieve a desired balance of hardness, strength, and toughness. Freshly quenched steel, consisting largely of martensite, is extremely hard but brittle; tempering relieves internal stresses and allows carbon to precipitate as fine carbides, making the steel less brittle.

Process

The workpiece is held at temperature for a period — often one to two hours per 25 mm of thickness — then cooled in air. The outcome depends on temperature: low tempering temperatures (150–250 °C) preserve most hardness for cutting tools, while higher temperatures (450–650 °C) produce softer, much tougher steel for springs and structural parts. Historically, smiths judged temperature by the oxide "tempering colors" that form on polished steel, running from pale straw through blue.

Stages and effects

As temperature increases, martensite first loses carbon to form transition carbides, retained austenite decomposes, and finally cementite particles coarsen, progressively reducing hardness and increasing ductility. Certain alloy steels suffer "tempered martensite embrittlement" if held in specific temperature ranges, which metallurgists avoid by composition control or rapid cooling. Differential tempering allows a single part — such as a sword blade — to be hard at the edge and tough at the spine.
