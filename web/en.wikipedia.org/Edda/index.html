Edda

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Edda is a term used for two medieval Icelandic works that are the principal sources for Norse mythology and skaldic poetry: the Poetic Edda, a collection of anonymous Old Norse poems, and the Prose Edda, a handbook of poetics written by Snorri Sturluson around 1220. The etymology of the word is disputed; it may derive from a word meaning "great-grandmother" or from Oddi, the Icelandic farm where Snorri was educated.

Poetic Edda

The Poetic Edda survives chiefly in the Codex Regius, a manuscript written in Iceland around 1270 and rediscovered in 1643 by Bishop Brynjólfur Sveinsson. Its poems fall into mythological lays — including the Völuspá, a seeress's account of the creation and destruction of the world, and the Hávamál, wisdom sayings attributed to Odin — and heroic lays about figures such as Sigurd the dragon-slayer and the Burgundian kings, linking Scandinavian tradition to Germanic heroic legend.

Prose Edda

Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda was composed as a manual for poets, since skaldic verse depended on allusions to pagan myth. It consists of a prologue euhemerising the gods, the Gylfaginning (a dialogue recounting the myths), the Skáldskaparmál (on poetic diction, quoting many verses) and the Háttatal (a catalogue of verse forms). It preserves myths attested nowhere else, though Snorri wrote two centuries after Iceland's conversion to Christianity.

Influence

The Eddas profoundly influenced later literature, from Scandinavian ballads to Wagner's Ring cycle and J. R. R. Tolkien's legendarium.
