Vedic period

The Vedic period, or the Vedic age (c. 1500 – c. 500 BCE), is the period in the late Bronze Age and early Iron Age of the history of India when the Vedic literature, including the Vedas, was composed in the northern Indian subcontinent, between the end of the urban Indus Valley civilisation and a second urbanisation, which began in the central Indo-Gangetic Plain c. 600 BCE.

Overview

The Vedas were composed by Indo-Aryan peoples in the Punjab and upper Ganges region. The early Vedic period (c. 1500–1000 BCE) was pastoral and tribal, centred on the Ṛgveda's hymns to gods such as Indra and Agni, cattle wealth, and chariot warfare. The later Vedic period (c. 1000–500 BCE) saw the emergence of settled agriculture, kingdoms such as Kuru and Pañcāla, iron technology, and increasingly elaborate sacrificial ritual described in the Brāhmaṇas.

Society and religion

Society was organised around kin groups and gradually developed the varṇa hierarchy of priests (brāhmaṇa), rulers and warriors (kṣatriya), commoners (vaiśya), and servants (śūdra). Religious practice centred on fire sacrifice (yajña), performed by priests to sustain the cosmic order (ṛta). Toward the end of the period, the early Upaniṣads shifted attention inward, introducing concepts of ātman, brahman, karma, and saṃsāra that reshaped Indian religion.

Legacy

The Vedic period laid the foundations of Hinduism and of classical Indian culture. Its literature, preserved orally with extraordinary fidelity for millennia, remains the oldest Indo-European religious corpus still in living use. The period's end overlaps with the rise of Buddhism and Jainism and the second urbanisation of the Ganges plain.
