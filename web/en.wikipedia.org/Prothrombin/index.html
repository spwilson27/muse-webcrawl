Prothrombin, or coagulation factor II, is a glycoprotein produced in the liver that circulates in blood plasma as an inactive precursor (zymogen) of the enzyme thrombin. It is one of the vitamin K–dependent clotting factors. During blood coagulation, prothrombin is cleaved by the prothrombinase complex—factor Xa, factor Va, calcium ions, and phospholipid—to form thrombin (factor IIa).

Function

Thrombin is the central enzyme of clotting: it converts soluble fibrinogen into fibrin strands, activates platelets and factors V, VIII, XI, and XIII, and thereby stabilises the growing clot. Thrombin also has anticoagulant roles when bound to thrombomodulin, activating protein C.

Clinical significance

The prothrombin time (PT) test measures how long plasma takes to clot after tissue factor is added, and is used to monitor therapy with warfarin, commonly reported as the INR. A common inherited variant, the prothrombin G20210A mutation in the F2 gene, raises plasma prothrombin levels and increases the risk of venous thrombosis. Deficiency of prothrombin, whether inherited or acquired through liver disease or vitamin K deficiency, causes a bleeding tendency. Direct thrombin inhibitors such as dabigatran act downstream by blocking thrombin itself.
