Pi

The number π (pi) is a mathematical constant that is the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, approximately equal to 3.14159. It has been represented by the Greek letter π since the mid-18th century, and is also called Archimedes' constant. Pi is an irrational number, meaning it cannot be expressed as a simple fraction, and it is also transcendental, so its decimal representation never ends and never settles into a repeating pattern.

Definition

Pi is defined in Euclidean geometry as the ratio of a circle's circumference C to its diameter d: π = C/d. The ratio is the same for all circles. Equivalently, π is the ratio of a circle's area to the square of its radius. Pi appears in countless formulae in mathematics and physics, including Euler's identity, eiπ + 1 = 0.

History

Ancient Egyptian and Babylonian mathematicians used approximations of π. Around 250 BC, Archimedes computed upper and lower bounds by inscribing and circumscribing polygons, obtaining 223/71 < π < 22/7. Chinese mathematician Zu Chongzhi later found 355/113, accurate to six decimal places. Indian mathematician Madhava developed infinite series for π in the 15th century. In 1706 William Jones first used the symbol π, and Leonhard Euler popularised it from 1737.

Computation

Modern algorithms, such as the Chudnovsky algorithm, have computed π to over 100 trillion digits. Because π is irrational, such computations never terminate; they serve mainly as tests of supercomputer performance and numerical methods.

Applications

Beyond geometry, π appears in trigonometry, complex analysis, probability theory (for example in the normal distribution), number theory, and physics, including Heisenberg's uncertainty principle and Einstein's field equations.
