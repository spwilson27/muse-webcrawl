Chronic wasting disease

Chronic wasting disease (CWD)
Cause: Misfolded prion protein
Hosts: Deer, elk, moose, reindeer, sika deer
First identified: 1967 (captive mule deer, Colorado)
Human cases: None confirmed

Chronic wasting disease (CWD) is a transmissible spongiform encephalopathy — a prion disease — affecting cervids: deer, elk, moose, and reindeer. Infected animals gradually lose body condition and develop behavioural changes, excessive salivation and thirst, and loss of fear of humans; the disease is invariably fatal, with no treatment or vaccine.

Transmission and spread

CWD prions are shed in saliva, urine, and faeces and persist for years in soil, where they can bind to clay particles and remain infectious. The disease was first recognised in captive mule deer in Colorado in 1967 and identified as a prion disease in the late 1970s. It has since spread through wild and farmed cervids across most U.S. states and several Canadian provinces, and has been detected in South Korea (via imported elk) and, since 2016, in Norway, Sweden, and Finland — the first cases in Europe.

Human health and management

No case of CWD in humans has been confirmed, and epidemiological studies have not linked it to human prion disease; nevertheless, health agencies advise against eating meat from infected animals, and hunters in affected areas are urged to test harvested deer. Management relies on surveillance, culling, carcass-movement restrictions, and bans on feeding and baiting that concentrate animals. Comparisons with "mad cow disease" (BSE), which did jump to humans as variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, keep CWD under close scientific scrutiny.
