Utilitarianism

Utilitarianism is a family of normative ethical theories that prescribe actions that maximise happiness and well-being for the affected individuals. It is a form of consequentialism: the moral worth of an action is determined by its outcome. The classic formulation — "the greatest happiness of the greatest number" — was developed by Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill.

History

Bentham's Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789) proposed a felicific calculus weighing pleasures and pains by intensity, duration, certainty and extent. Mill's Utilitarianism (1863) refined the theory, distinguishing higher and lower pleasures and defending liberty as instrumentally essential to happiness. Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics (1874) gave the doctrine its most rigorous classical treatment.

Forms

Act utilitarianism judges each act by its consequences; rule utilitarianism judges rules by the consequences of their general acceptance. Preference utilitarianism, associated with Peter Singer, maximises the satisfaction of preferences rather than pleasure. Two-level utilitarianism, developed by R. M. Hare, combines intuitive moral rules for everyday decisions with critical consequentialist thinking for hard cases.

Criticisms

Critics argue that utilitarianism can justify violating individual rights, demands more impartiality than human motivation allows, and faces problems of interpersonal comparison and the demandingness objection. Defenders reply that plausible versions accommodate rights-protecting rules and that common-sense morality is less impartial only because of predictable human limitations.

Influence

Utilitarian reasoning underlies cost-benefit analysis, much of welfare economics, animal-welfare advocacy and the effective altruism movement.
