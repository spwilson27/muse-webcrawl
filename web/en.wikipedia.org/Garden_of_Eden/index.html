Garden of Eden

In Abrahamic religions, the Garden of Eden (Hebrew: גַּן־עֵדֶן, gan-ʿĒḏen) or Garden of God is the paradise described in the Book of Genesis as the home of Adam and Eve, the first man and woman, before their expulsion for disobeying God's command.

Biblical narrative

According to Genesis 2–3, God planted the garden "eastward in Eden", placed Adam there to tend it, and formed Eve as his companion. A river flowed out of Eden and divided into four heads: Pishon, Gihon, Tigris (Hiddekel) and Euphrates. In the garden stood the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, whose fruit God forbade the pair to eat. Tempted by the serpent, Eve ate and gave to Adam; the two were expelled, and cherubim with a flaming sword guarded the way back to the tree of life.

Location hypotheses

The mention of the Tigris and Euphrates has led many to place Eden in Mesopotamia, near the head of the Persian Gulf; other proposals include Armenia and, in Latter Day Saint theology, Missouri. Most scholars treat Eden as mythological rather than historical geography, and compare it with the Sumerian concept of Dilmun and other ancient Near Eastern paradise traditions.

Religious interpretations

In Judaism and Christianity the expulsion is linked with the Fall of man and original sin. In Islam, Eden (Jannat ʿAdn) is associated with the paradise promised to the righteous. The garden is a frequent motif in medieval and Renaissance art, literature from Milton's Paradise Lost onward, and the English word "paradise" derives via Greek paradeisos, used for the garden in the Septuagint.
