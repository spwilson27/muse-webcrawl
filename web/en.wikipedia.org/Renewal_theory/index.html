Renewal theory

Renewal theory is the branch of probability theory that generalizes the Poisson process to arbitrary holding times. A renewal process is a sequence of events occurring at times determined by independent and identically distributed non-negative random variables, called interarrival or holding times. Much of renewal theory is devoted to determining the probability distribution of the number of renewals occurring by a given time, and to the limiting behaviour of the process over long horizons.

A renewal process restarts stochastically at each event.

Key results

The expected number of renewals by time t, the renewal function m(t), satisfies the renewal equation, a Volterra integral equation involving the interarrival distribution. The elementary renewal theorem states that as time grows, the expected number of renewals per unit time approaches the reciprocal of the mean interarrival time:

m(t) / t → 1 / E[X] as t → ∞.

The key renewal theorem, developed by William Feller and others, describes the asymptotic behaviour of solutions to the renewal equation and underlies results such as the limiting distribution of the age and residual life of a component — leading to the so-called inspection paradox, in which a randomly sampled interarrival interval is on average longer than a typical one.

Applications

Renewal theory, and its extension to renewal-reward processes, is applied in reliability engineering, inventory control, queueing theory, maintenance scheduling, actuarial science, and the analysis of equipment replacement policies. It was central to mid-twentieth-century operations research and remains a standard part of stochastic processes courses.
