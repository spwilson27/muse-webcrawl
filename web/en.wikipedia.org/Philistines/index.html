The Philistines (Hebrew: Pəlištīm) were an ancient people who settled on the south coast of Canaan from the 12th century BC. Their territory, known as Philistia, comprised the five city-states of Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Ekron and Gath, ruled by lords (seranim). The name "Palestine" derives from them.

Origins

Egyptian inscriptions of Ramesses III at Medinet Habu list the "Peleset" among the Sea Peoples defeated around 1175 BC. Archaeology shows a new material culture arriving in the coastal plain at that time — locally made Mycenaean-style pottery of Aegean type, hearth buildings and Aegean-style figurines — and a 2019 ancient-DNA study of burials at Ashkelon found southern-European-related ancestry in early Iron Age infants, consistent with a migration from the Aegean world or its fringes.

History

The Philistines were the principal rivals of ancient Israel during the period of the Judges and the early monarchy; biblical narratives feature Samson's exploits among them, the capture of the Ark of the Covenant, Goliath of Gath, and David's service under Achish. Expanding inland at times, they were gradually subdued by the Israelite kingdom, then dominated successively by Egypt, Assyria and Babylon. In 604 BC Nebuchadnezzar II destroyed Ashkelon and exiled its population, after which the Philistines disappear as a distinct people from the historical record, though the regional name endured.

Modern usage

From the 19th century, the word "philistine" acquired a figurative meaning — a person indifferent or hostile to art, culture and intellectual pursuits — popularised by the writer Matthew Arnold.
