Miller–Urey experiment

The Miller–Urey experiment was a chemical experiment conducted in 1952 by Stanley Miller under the supervision of Harold Urey at the University of Chicago, simulating conditions thought to exist on the early Earth to test the chemical origin of life.

Method

A closed apparatus circulated water, methane, ammonia, and hydrogen — representing the early atmosphere — past an electrical discharge simulating lightning. After about a week, analysis showed that amino acids, the building blocks of proteins, had formed spontaneously.

Significance

Published in 1953, the experiment was the first experimental support for the Oparin–Haldane hypothesis of abiogenesis and showed that organic compounds could arise under prebiotic conditions. Later reanalyses of Miller's preserved samples found even more amino acids than originally reported.

Criticism

Later geochemistry suggested the early atmosphere may have been less reducing than the mixture used, prompting alternative scenarios such as hydrothermal vents, though variants of the experiment with other gas mixtures still yield organic compounds.
