Conte philosophique

A conte philosophique (French for “philosophical tale”) is a literary genre of the Enlightenment: a fictional story, often in the form of a novella or short novel, that conveys philosophical ideas through narrative, irony and satire rather than abstract treatise. The form allowed authors to criticise religion, politics and received ideas at arm's length, frequently under the cover of exotic or fantastic settings.

Voltaire

The genre is inseparably associated with Voltaire, whose Zadig (1747), Micromégas (1752) and above all Candide (1759) exemplify the form. In Candide, the naive hero's travels through a world of war, disaster and cruelty satirise Leibnizian optimism and the doctrine that this is “the best of all possible worlds.”

Other examples

Denis Diderot's Jacques the Fatalist and Montesquieu's Persian Letters (1721) employ related devices of estrangement, while Samuel Johnson's Rasselas (1759) is an English analogue. The conte philosophique influenced later philosophical fiction and the modern novella of ideas.

Characteristics

Typical features include swift, episodic plots; ironic narrators; dialogues debating metaphysics, tolerance and happiness; and endings that counsel modest practical wisdom — Voltaire's famous injunction to “cultivate our garden.”
