Meadow
Habitat typeGrassland dominated by herbaceous plants
Typical usesHay production, grazing, conservation
Related habitatsPasture, prairie, steppe, savanna

A meadow is an open habitat or field vegetated by grasses, herbs and other non-woody plants. Meadows may occur naturally, but many familiar temperate meadows are created and maintained by human activity such as mowing for hay or grazing by livestock, which prevents shrubs and trees from taking over.

Types

Traditional hay meadows are cut once or twice a year and are often rich in wildflowers. Wet meadows occur on seasonally flooded ground near rivers and coasts, while alpine meadows lie above the treeline in mountain regions. In North America, meadow vegetation intergrades with tallgrass prairie.

Ecology

Because they are kept open by cutting or grazing, meadows support a distinctive community of sun-loving plants and animals, including numerous butterfly species, grassland birds and pollinating insects. Species-rich traditionally managed meadows have declined sharply with agricultural intensification — through reseeding, fertilizer use and conversion to silage — making the surviving fragments a conservation priority in many countries.

Human use

Beyond hay and pasture, meadows are valued for recreation, landscape character and ecosystem services such as flood mitigation and carbon storage. Deliberate "meadow gardens" and urban wildflower plantings imitate the habitat on a small scale.
