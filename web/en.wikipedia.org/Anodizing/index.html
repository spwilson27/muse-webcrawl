Anodizing

Dyed anodized aluminium — the porous oxide layer accepts dyes before sealing

Anodizing is an electrolytic passivation process used to increase the thickness of the natural oxide layer on the surface of metal parts, most commonly aluminium. The name derives from the fact that the part to be treated forms the anode electrode of an electrolytic cell. Anodizing increases resistance to corrosion and wear, and provides a porous layer that can absorb dyes or improve adhesion of paints and glues.

Process

The metal part is immersed in an acidic electrolyte bath (commonly sulfuric, chromic, or phosphoric acid) and an electric current is passed through it. Oxygen released at the anode surface reacts with the metal to build a controlled layer of metal oxide, typically 5–25 micrometres for decorative work and up to 100 micrometres for "hard anodizing". The resulting oxide is integral to the metal, not an applied coating, so it cannot peel.

Types and uses

Standards distinguish Type I (chromic acid), Type II (sulfuric acid, decorative), and Type III (hardcoat) anodizing. Anodized finishes are used on aircraft parts, architectural aluminium, cookware, consumer electronics casings, and sporting goods. Titanium and niobium can also be anodized to produce interference colours without dyes, used in jewellery and medical implants. Magnesium, zinc and titanium alloys are also anodized, though less commonly.
