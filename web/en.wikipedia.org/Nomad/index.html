Nomad

Nomadism
DefinitionA lifestyle characterised by regular movement between places rather than permanent settlement
Main typesPastoral nomadism, hunter-gatherer nomadism, peripatetic (itinerant) nomadism
Notable peoplesBedouin, Mongols, Maasai, Sámi, Romani, Tuareg
PopulationAn estimated 30–40 million pastoral nomads worldwide (early 21st century)

A nomad is a member of a community without a fixed habitation who regularly moves to and from the same areas. Such groups include hunter-gatherers, pastoral nomads (owning livestock), tinkers and trader nomads. In the twentieth century, the population of nomadic pastoral tribes slowly decreased, reaching an estimated 30–40 million nomads in the world as of 2022.

Nomadic hunting and gathering — following seasonally available wild plants and game — is by far the oldest human subsistence method known. Pastoralists raise herds of domesticated livestock, driving or accompanying them in patterns that normally avoid depleting pastures beyond their ability to recover. Nomadism is also the traditional form of seasonal transhumance of the Sámi people, who keep reindeer herds, on the borderlands of northern Scandinavia and Russia.

Characteristics

Nomads are distinguished from other mobile groups by the collectivity of their movement: the whole community — families, elders, children and possessions — travels together, usually along established routes between seasonal pastures or camps. Anthropologists such as Anatoly Khazanov have emphasised that nomadism is rarely a wholly self-sufficient adaptation; pastoral nomads have historically depended on exchange with sedentary agricultural societies for grain, metal goods and other products they do not produce themselves.

Pastoral nomads

Pastoral nomads move with herds of sheep, goats, cattle, camels, horses, yaks or reindeer. Famous pastoral peoples include the Bedouin of Arabia, the Mongols of the Central Asian steppe, the Maasai of East Africa, the Tuareg of the Sahara, and the Kazakh and Kyrgyz herders of Central Asia. The Mongol nomads under Genghis Khan built the largest contiguous land empire in history in the thirteenth century, demonstrating the military power that mounted pastoral societies could project.

Hunter-gatherers

Nomadic hunter-gatherer bands — such as the San of the Kalahari, Aboriginal Australian groups, and various peoples of the Arctic and Amazon — move in response to the seasonal availability of wild foods. This was the universal human way of life before the Neolithic Revolution introduced settled farming roughly 10,000–12,000 years ago.

Peripatetic nomads

Peripatetic nomads live by offering services — tinkering, metalwork, entertainment, fortune-telling or trade — to sedentary populations, moving from place to place as demand requires. The Romani people, who migrated from northern India over a thousand years ago, are the best-known example, along with Irish Travellers and various itinerant artisan castes of South Asia.

History and decline

The twentieth century saw sustained pressure on nomadic peoples from nation-states seeking to settle them: enclosure of grazing lands, new national borders crossing migration routes, collectivisation in the Soviet Union (which caused catastrophic famine among Kazakh nomads in the 1930s), and settlement programmes in the Middle East, China and Africa. Climate change and desertification have further strained pastoral livelihoods in the Sahel and Central Asia.

Modern nomads

The term "nomad" has been extended metaphorically to people such as "digital nomads" — remote workers who travel while working online — and to military or diplomatic families who relocate frequently, although these usages differ fundamentally from traditional communal nomadism.
