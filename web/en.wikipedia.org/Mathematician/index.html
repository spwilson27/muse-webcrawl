Mathematician

A mathematician is someone who uses an extensive knowledge of mathematics in their work, typically to solve mathematical problems. Mathematicians are concerned with numbers, data, quantity, structure, space, models and change.

History

One of the earliest known mathematicians was Thales of Miletus (c. 624–546 BC), credited with the first deductive proofs in geometry. Euclid, Archimedes and Apollonius developed Greek mathematics, which was preserved and extended by scholars of the Islamic world such as al-Khwarizmi, from whose name the word "algorithm" derives. Figures such as Fibonacci helped transmit these ideas to Europe, and from the Renaissance the discipline professionalised: Newton and Leibniz founded calculus, Euler dominated 18th-century analysis, and Gauss, Riemann and Hilbert shaped much of the modern field.

Work

Mathematicians divide broadly into pure (theoretical) mathematicians, who advance mathematical knowledge for its own sake, and applied mathematicians, who use mathematical methods in science, engineering, business and computing. Professional mathematicians usually hold a doctoral degree, publish research in peer-reviewed journals, and work in universities, government or industry. Pure research centres on proving theorems; its results are published as proofs whose correctness is checked by the community.

Prizes and recognition

The most prestigious awards in mathematics include the Fields Medal, awarded every four years to mathematicians under 40, the Abel Prize, and the Wolf Prize. There is no Nobel Prize in Mathematics, a fact often remarked upon; the Abel Prize was established in part to fill that gap.

Quotations and culture

G. H. Hardy, in A Mathematician's Apology (1940), famously described mathematics as a creative art and a young person's game. The profession has long struggled with the under-representation of women, despite major contributions by figures such as Emmy Noether, Sofia Kovalevskaya and Maryam Mirzakhani, the first woman to win the Fields Medal.
