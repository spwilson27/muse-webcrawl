James Wolfe (2 January 1727 – 13 September 1759) was a British Army officer known for his training reforms and remembered chiefly for his victory over the French at the Battle of the Plains of Abraham in Quebec in 1759, in which he was killed. The battle decided the fate of New France and of Canada in the Seven Years' War.

Early career

Wolfe was born in Westerham, Kent, the eldest son of a lieutenant general. He received his first commission aged 13 and served in Flanders during the War of the Austrian Succession, fighting at Dettingen (1743). In the Jacobite rising of 1745 he fought at Falkirk and Culloden. He afterwards served in Scotland and the Netherlands, and became known as a reforming officer who emphasized marksmanship, discipline and training; his standing orders influenced the light infantry tactics later used in America. In 1758 he distinguished himself as a brigadier at the siege of Louisbourg under Jeffrey Amherst.

Quebec campaign

In 1759 Wolfe was given command of the expedition against Quebec City. After a frustrating summer of failed attacks along the Beauport shore and a bombardment that reduced much of the town, he gambled on landing at night upriver at L'Anse-au-Foulon. On the morning of 13 September 1759 his army scaled the cliffs and formed up on the Plains of Abraham. In the ensuing short battle the French under the Marquis de Montcalm were routed; both commanders were mortally wounded. Wolfe, told of the victory as he lay dying, is said to have replied, "Then I die happy." Quebec surrendered five days later.

Legacy

Wolfe's body was returned to England and buried at Greenwich. He became a hero of the British Empire; Benjamin West's painting The Death of General Wolfe (1770) is one of the most famous history paintings of the era. Monuments to Wolfe stand in Westminster Abbey, at Quebec and at his birthplace in Westerham.
