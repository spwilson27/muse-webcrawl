Yoruba (native name Èdè Yorùbá) is a language spoken in West Africa, principally in southwestern and central Nigeria, as well as in parts of Benin and Togo. It is one of the most widely spoken Niger–Congo languages and is native to the Yoruba people. Yoruba is a tonal language with three-level tone distinctions and an isolating grammatical structure.

Phonology and tone

Yoruba has seven oral vowels and a set of nasal vowels. Tone is phonemic: high, mid, and low tones distinguish word meanings, and written Yoruba marks tones with diacritics, along with underdots on certain letters such as ẹ, ọ, and ṣ.

Literature and standardisation

A standard literary form developed in the nineteenth century, influenced by the work of the bishop and linguist Samuel Ajayi Crowther, who compiled a Yoruba grammar and translated the Bible. Yoruba has a rich literary tradition in poetry, prose, and theatre, and is used in education, broadcasting, and publishing across Nigeria.

Dialects

Numerous dialects are spoken across Yorubaland, broadly grouped into northwestern, northeastern, central, and southeastern varieties, largely mutually intelligible with the standard language.
