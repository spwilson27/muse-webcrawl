Dog food

Dog food is food specifically formulated for consumption by dogs and other related canines. Although dogs are facultative carnivores with omnivorous adaptations — including an ability to digest starch — commercial dog foods are formulated to meet nutritional standards for protein, fat, vitamins, and minerals over a dog's life stages.

History

Before the mid-19th century, dogs were fed largely on table scraps, grains, and offal. In the 1860s James Spratt, an American entrepreneur, introduced the first commercially prepared dog biscuit in England, made from wheat, vegetables, beetroot, and beef blood. Canned horse meat followed in the United States after the First World War, and extruded dry kibble became dominant after the Second World War, driven by post-war pet population growth and pet-food marketing that recast dogs as family members.

Types

Commercial dog food is sold as dry kibble, wet (canned) food, semi-moist products, and refrigerated or freeze-dried formulations. Specialty diets include puppy, senior, weight-management, and veterinary therapeutic foods. Raw feeding and home-cooked diets remain subjects of debate among veterinarians because of nutritional balance and pathogen risks.

Regulation and standards

In the United States, the Association of American Feed Control Officials (AAFCO) publishes nutrient profiles and feeding-trial protocols; labels stating "complete and balanced" must meet these standards. Dog food recalls — notably the 2007 melamine contamination crisis — have periodically raised questions about ingredient sourcing and oversight.
