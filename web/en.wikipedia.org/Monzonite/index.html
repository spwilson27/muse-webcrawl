Monzonite

Monzonite is an igneous intrusive rock, formed by slow cooling of underground magma. It is composed of roughly equal amounts of plagioclase and alkali feldspar, with less than 5% quartz by volume. Biotite, hornblende, pyroxene and small amounts of other minerals may also be present. When quartz content exceeds 5%, the rock is classified as quartz monzonite; with more alkali feldspar it grades into syenite, and with more plagioclase into diorite. The volcanic equivalent of monzonite is latite.

Classification

In the QAPF diagram used by the International Union of Geological Sciences to classify plutonic rocks, monzonite occupies field 8, between syenite and diorite: quartz constitutes less than 5% of the QAP (quartz, alkali feldspar, plagioclase) fraction, and alkali feldspar makes up between 35% and 65% of the total feldspar content.

Name and occurrences

The name comes from the Monzoni range in the Val di Fassa, in the Dolomites of Trentino, Italy, where the rock was described in the 19th century. Monzonites occur in many igneous provinces worldwide. Notable examples include the monzonitic intrusions of the Oslo Rift in Norway (where related rocks are called larvikite in their coarse, iridescent form), parts of the White Mountain magma series of New Hampshire, and monzonites associated with porphyry copper mineralization in several cordilleran districts, including deposits in British Columbia and the southwestern United States.

Formation

Monzonitic magmas are commonly interpreted as products of fractional crystallization of more mafic (basaltic or shoshonitic) magmas, or of partial melting and mixing processes in continental arc and post-collisional settings. Their intermediate composition — between mafic and felsic end members — makes them characteristic of subduction-related and alkaline magmatic suites.
