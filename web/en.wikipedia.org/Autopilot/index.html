Autopilot

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Autopilot controls on a flight deck

An autopilot is a system used to control the path of a vehicle without requiring constant manual control by a human operator. Autopilots do not replace human operators; they assist them in controlling the vehicle, allowing the operator to focus on broader aspects of operations. The term is most closely associated with aircraft flight control systems, but autopilots are also used in spacecraft, missiles, ships, and — by extension — in driver-assistance systems in cars.

History

The first aircraft autopilot was developed by the Sperry Corporation in 1912, using gyroscopic stabilisation; Lawrence Sperry's dramatic 1914 demonstration, walking along the wing while the system kept the aircraft level, made the technology famous. Wiley Post used an autopilot on his 1933 round-the-world flight. Modern airliner autopilots are integrated with flight management systems and can control the aircraft from shortly after takeoff through autoland in poor visibility.

Operation

Aircraft autopilots typically control pitch, roll, and yaw through servos acting on the flight controls, guided by inertial and satellite navigation inputs, with autothrottle managing engine thrust. Certification categories (CAT I–III) govern the minimum visibility in which autoland may be used. Outside aviation, marine autopilots steer vessels by compass or GPS heading, and automotive systems such as Tesla Autopilot provide partial driving automation while still requiring driver supervision.
