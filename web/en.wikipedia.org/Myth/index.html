Myths are traditional sacred narratives explaining the world and human experience.

A myth is a traditional story, especially one concerning the early history of a people or explaining a natural or social phenomenon, and typically involving supernatural beings or events. In folkloristics, myths are sacred narratives — often set in a primordial age — that explain how the world, its inhabitants, and cultural practices came into being.

Definitions and functions

Scholars distinguish myths from legends (set in historical time, about human heroes) and folktales (told for entertainment and understood as fiction). Proposed functions of myth include explaining natural phenomena, justifying rituals and social institutions, encoding moral values, and providing psychological archetypes. Thinkers from Euhemerus and Malinowski to Lévi-Strauss and Joseph Campbell have offered competing accounts of what myths do and mean.

Types of myths

  • Creation myths describe the origin of the world and humanity.
  • Etiological myths explain the origin of a custom, name or feature.
  • Eschatological myths concern the end of the world (e.g. Ragnarök).
  • Hero myths follow the deeds of culture heroes and founders.

Modern usage

In everyday speech "myth" often simply means a false belief — a usage scholars avoid, since it misrepresents the role of myth in the cultures that hold it. Comparative mythology studies recurring motifs across traditions, from flood narratives to dying-and-rising gods.
