Caribbean literature

Caribbean literature is the literature of the territories of the Caribbean region, written in English, French, Spanish, Dutch, and Caribbean creole languages. Shaped by the shared history of colonisation, slavery, indenture and migration, it explores themes of identity, language, exile and the recovery of suppressed histories, and it has achieved worldwide recognition, including two Nobel laureates in Literature.

Development

Early Caribbean writing includes slave narratives and the poetry of the 18th and 19th centuries, but a distinct literature emerged in the 20th century. In the Anglophone Caribbean the "Windrush generation" — George Lamming (In the Castle of My Skin), Samuel Selvon (The Lonely Londoners), and the BBC's Caribbean Voices programme — brought island writing to London audiences. Derek Walcott of Saint Lucia, author of the epic poem Omeros, won the Nobel Prize in 1992; V. S. Naipaul of Trinidad, who won in 2001, is a more controversial figure in the region.

Themes and languages

Writers such as Jamaica Kincaid, Maryse Condé of Guadeloupe, Édouard Glissant of Martinique (theorist of créolité and the "poetics of relation"), Kamau Brathwaite of Barbados (champion of "nation language"), Earl Lovelace, Merle Hodge and Marlon James have broadened the canon. Francophone négritude, Spanish-language Caribbean modernism, and oral traditions — calypso, dub poetry and storytelling — continually feed the written literature. Much Caribbean literature is now produced by the diaspora in London, Toronto and New York while remaining centred on the islands.
