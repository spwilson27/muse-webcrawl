The solar cycle, also known as the sunspot cycle, is the roughly 11-year quasi-periodic change in the Sun's magnetic activity, measured most visibly by the number of sunspots on its surface. Over each cycle, activity rises from a quiet solar minimum to a solar maximum of frequent sunspots, flares, and coronal mass ejections, then declines again; the Sun's magnetic polarity reverses each cycle, giving a full magnetic (Hale) cycle of about 22 years. Note: this is distinct from a solar eclipse, an unrelated alignment phenomenon.

History

Heinrich Schwabe announced the sunspot periodicity in 1843 after years of daily observations; Rudolf Wolf devised the relative sunspot number and reconstructed cycles back to 1755. The 70-year Maunder Minimum (c. 1645–1715), when sunspots nearly vanished, coincided with the coldest part of the Little Ice Age, prompting long-running study of possible solar influences on climate.

Mechanism

The cycle is driven by a dynamo in the Sun's interior: differential rotation stretches poloidal magnetic field into toroidal field (the omega effect), while rising, twisting flux loops regenerate the poloidal field (the alpha effect). Sunspots emerge where concentrated flux breaks the surface, migrating toward the equator as the cycle progresses (Spörer's law), a pattern displayed in the butterfly diagram.

Effects

At solar maximum, total solar irradiance is about 0.1% higher, ultraviolet output varies much more, and geomagnetic storms—capable of disrupting satellites, radio, and power grids—become more frequent. Solar cycle 25 began in December 2019 and proved stronger than many predictions.
