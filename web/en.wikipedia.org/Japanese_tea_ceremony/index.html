Japanese tea ceremony

The Japanese tea ceremony (茶道, sadō/chadō, "the way of tea") is a choreographed ritual of preparing and serving powdered green tea (matcha) together with traditional sweets. Rooted in Zen Buddhism, it was given its classic form by the tea master Sen no Rikyū in the 16th century, emphasising the principles of harmony, respect, purity and tranquillity.

Other names: Sadō, chadō, chanoyu

Key figure: Sen no Rikyū (1522–1591)

Principles: Wa (harmony), kei (respect), sei (purity), jaku (tranquillity)

History

Tea was introduced from China, and by the Muromachi period tea gatherings became a refined pursuit of the shogunal court. Murata Jukō began the turn toward rustic simplicity (wabi-cha), perfected by Rikyū under the patronage of Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi. Rikyū's descendants founded the three main schools — Omotesenke, Urasenke and Mushakōjisenke — which still teach the tradition today.

The gathering

A formal gathering (chaji) may last four hours and include a kaiseki meal, charcoal-laying, and both thick tea (koicha) and thin tea (usucha). Guests enter the tea house through a garden path (roji), purify their hands, and admire the scroll and flowers in the alcove (tokonoma). Every movement — the folding of the cloth, the turning of the bowl — follows prescribed forms (temae), and the concept of ichi-go ichi-e, "one time, one meeting", teaches that each encounter is unique and unrepeatable.
