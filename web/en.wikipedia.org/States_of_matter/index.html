State of matter

States of matter
Classical statesSolid, liquid, gas, plasma
Modern statesBose–Einstein condensate, fermionic condensate, superfluid, quark–gluon plasma, and others

In physics, a state of matter is one of the distinct forms in which matter can exist. Four states are observable in everyday life: solid, liquid, gas, and plasma. Changes between states — such as melting, freezing, evaporation, condensation, sublimation, and ionization — are called phase transitions and are driven by changes in temperature and pressure, which alter the energy and arrangement of the constituent particles.

The classical states

Solid. Particles are held in a fixed, ordered arrangement by strong intermolecular forces; a solid has a definite shape and volume and resists compression.

Liquid. Particles remain close together but can flow past one another; a liquid has a definite volume but takes the shape of its container.

Gas. Particles are far apart and move freely; a gas expands to fill its container and is highly compressible.

Plasma. At very high temperatures, atoms are stripped of electrons, producing a mixture of ions and free electrons. Plasma is the most abundant state of ordinary matter in the universe, composing stars and much of the interstellar medium.

Other states

Many further states exist under extreme conditions or at very low temperatures. Cooling a dilute gas of bosons to near absolute zero produces a Bose–Einstein condensate, in which the atoms occupy a single quantum state. Related low-temperature phases include superfluids and fermionic condensates. At extremely high energies, quarks and gluons form quark–gluon plasma, thought to have filled the universe in its first microseconds and recreated in heavy-ion colliders. Inside neutron stars, matter may exist as degenerate matter. Liquid crystals, glasses, and magnetic phases such as ferromagnetism are also classified among the states of matter.
