Coelacanth

Coelacanths are an ancient group of lobe-finned fish (Sarcopterygii) in the class Actinistia. They were thought to have become extinct in the Late Cretaceous, around 66 million years ago, but were rediscovered in 1938 off the coast of South Africa. Two extant species are known: the West Indian Ocean coelacanth (Latimeria chalumnae) and the Indonesian coelacanth (Latimeria menadoensis). Coelacanths are often called "living fossils".

Rediscovery

On 22 December 1938, museum curator Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer found a strange blue fish among the catch of a trawler near East London, South Africa. Ichthyologist J. L. B. Smith identified it as a coelacanth and named it Latimeria chalumnae. A second population was found around the Comoro Islands in 1952, and the Indonesian species was identified from a Sulawesi fish market in the 1990s.

Biology

Coelacanths are large, slow-moving predators reaching about 2 m and 90 kg. Their paired fins are fleshy and lobed, moving in an alternating pattern reminiscent of tetrapod walking. They possess a three-lobed tail, a hinged skull joint, a fat-filled vestigial lung, and an electrosensory rostral organ. Females give birth to live young after a gestation estimated at several years, and individuals may live for many decades, perhaps close to a century.

Evolution and conservation

Coelacanths belong to the lobe-finned fishes, the group from which tetrapods evolved, but they are not the direct ancestors of land vertebrates; lungfish are closer relatives. Both living species are threatened — the West Indian Ocean coelacanth is critically endangered — with small populations vulnerable to bycatch in deep-set nets.
