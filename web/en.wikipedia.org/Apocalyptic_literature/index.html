Apocalyptic literature

Apocalyptic literature is a genre of prophetical writing that developed in post-Exilic Jewish culture and was popular among millennialist early Christians. Apocalypse (ἀποκάλυψις) is a Greek word meaning "revelation", "an unveiling or unfolding of things not previously known and which could not be known apart from the unveiling".

Characteristics

As a genre, apocalyptic literature details the authors' visions of the end times as revealed by an angel or other heavenly messenger. The genre is characterised by symbolic imagery, a dualistic worldview of cosmic struggle between good and evil, deterministic history moving toward a final judgement, and pseudonymous authorship attributed to ancient worthies such as Enoch, Daniel, or Ezra.

Jewish apocalypses

The canonical Jewish example is the Book of Daniel (chapters 7–12), written in the 2nd century BCE during the persecution under Antiochus IV Epiphanes. Non-canonical works include 1 Enoch, 2 Enoch, 4 Ezra, 2 Baruch, and the Apocalypse of Abraham. These texts typically interpret contemporary suffering as the birth pangs of a coming age of divine vindication.

Christian apocalypses

The Book of Revelation, traditionally attributed to John of Patmos, is the only apocalypse in the New Testament canon, with its visions of the seven seals, trumpets, and bowls, the beast, and the New Jerusalem. Other early Christian apocalypses, such as the Apocalypse of Peter and the Shepherd of Hermas, were widely read but not canonised. The genre continued through the Sibylline Oracles and medieval visionary literature.
