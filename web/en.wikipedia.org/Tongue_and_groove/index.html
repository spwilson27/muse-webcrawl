Tongue and groove

Tongue and groove is a method of fitting similar objects together, edge to edge, used mainly with wood, in flooring, parquetry, panelling, and similar constructions. Tongue and groove joints allow two flat pieces to be joined strongly together to make a single flat surface. Before plywood became common, tongue and groove boards were also used for sheathing buildings and to construct concrete formwork.

A strong joint, the tongue and groove joint is widely used for re-entrant angles. The effect of wood shrinkage is concealed when the joint is beaded or otherwise moulded. In expensive cabinet work, glued dovetail and multiple tongue and groove are used.

Methods

Each piece has a slot (the groove or dado) cut all along one edge, and a thin, deep ridge (the tongue) on the opposite edge. The tongue projects a little less than the depth of the groove. Two or more pieces thus fit together closely. The joint is usually glued. The pieces may be assembled without glue, the original method being to nail down one board at a time while installing flooring.

Originally, tongue and groove boards were made with hand planes: a tongue plane for the tongue and a matching groove plane. Modern methods use moulding machines or spindle moulders with router cutters.

Applications

Flooring

Tongue and groove flooring is common in both solid wood and engineered wood flooring. The interlocking edges keep boards aligned and reduce gaps caused by seasonal expansion and contraction.

Panelling

Wood panelling and wainscoting often use tongue and groove boards with a decorative bead or V-groove at the joint.

Decking

Some decking systems use tongue and groove profiles to create a smooth surface with concealed fasteners.
