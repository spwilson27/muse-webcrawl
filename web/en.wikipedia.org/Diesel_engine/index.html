Diesel engine

A diesel engine ignites fuel by compression heating alone.

The diesel engine is an internal combustion engine in which ignition of the fuel is caused by the elevated temperature of air in the cylinder due to mechanical compression — so-called compression ignition. This contrasts with spark-ignition engines (such as petrol engines), which use a spark plug. The engine is named after its inventor, the German engineer Rudolf Diesel, who patented the design in 1892 and demonstrated a working engine in 1897.

Operation

In the four-stroke diesel cycle, air alone is drawn into the cylinder and compressed to a high ratio (typically 14:1 to 25:1), heating it to around 550 °C or more. Near the top of the compression stroke, fuel is injected as a fine spray directly into the hot air and ignites spontaneously. Diesel engines have no throttle plate on the intake, so power is controlled by the quantity of fuel injected, and they run with a large excess of air, which contributes to their high efficiency and strong torque at low speeds.

Efficiency and fuels

Diesel engines achieve the highest thermal efficiency of any practical combustion engine: large two-stroke marine diesels exceed 50 percent. They burn diesel fuel, a middle distillate of petroleum, and can also run on biodiesel and various fuel oils.

Applications

Diesels power most trucks, buses, freight trains, ships, agricultural and construction machinery, military vehicles, and standby generators, and a substantial share of passenger cars, especially in Europe. Their disadvantages include higher nitrogen oxide and particulate (soot) emissions — addressed by modern exhaust after-treatment such as particulate filters and selective catalytic reduction — and, historically, noise and poor cold starting, mitigated by glow plugs and common-rail direct injection.
