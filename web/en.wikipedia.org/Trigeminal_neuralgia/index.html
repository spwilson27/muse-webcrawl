Trigeminal neuralgia

SpecialtyNeurology, neurosurgery
SymptomsBrief, intense, electric-shock-like facial pain
Usual onsetAfter age 50
First-line treatmentCarbamazepine

Trigeminal neuralgia (TN), also called tic douloureux, is a long-term pain disorder that affects the trigeminal nerve, the nerve responsible for sensation in the face and motor functions such as biting and chewing. It causes episodes of severe, sudden, shock-like pain in one side of the face that last from a few seconds to two minutes.

Signs and symptoms

The pain typically affects the cheek, jaw, teeth, gums or lips, usually on one side only. Attacks can be triggered by light touch, chewing, speaking, brushing the teeth or even a breeze. Episodes may occur in clusters over weeks or months, followed by pain-free remissions. The condition is considered one of the most painful disorders known.

Causes

Most cases are caused by compression of the trigeminal nerve root, usually by a nearby artery or vein, leading to demyelination. Other cases are associated with multiple sclerosis, tumours, or structural abnormalities.

Treatment

The anticonvulsant carbamazepine is the established first-line treatment; oxcarbazepine is a common alternative. For patients who do not respond to or cannot tolerate medication, surgical options include microvascular decompression, which separates the offending blood vessel from the nerve, and ablative procedures such as radiofrequency rhizotomy, balloon compression and stereotactic radiosurgery (Gamma Knife).
