Generalized quantifier

In formal semantics, a generalized quantifier is an expression that denotes a set of sets, following the standard treatment of noun phrase quantification. The theory, developed in the 1970s and 1980s by Jon Barwise and Robin Cooper and others, extends the notion of a quantifier from logic — such as "for all" (∀) and "there exists" (∃) — to arbitrary determiners of natural language, such as every, some, no, most, few, and exactly three.

Basic idea

A determiner phrase such as "every student" is interpreted as the set of all sets that contain every student: that is, the set of properties (subsets of the domain) that hold of all students. The sentence "every student sleeps" is then true if and only if the set of sleepers is one of the sets in that family. In type-theoretic terms, a generalized quantifier has type ⟨⟨e,t⟩,t⟩: it takes a set (the predicate) and returns a truth value.

Properties

Generalized quantifier theory studies properties of determiners across languages, such as conservativity — the observation that "Det A B" depends only on the intersection of A and B — and monotonicity, which governs patterns of inference and the licensing of negative polarity items. The framework descends from the work of Andrzej Mostowski on generalized quantifiers in logic and from Richard Montague's treatment of English quantification.

Applications

The theory is a cornerstone of formal semantics in linguistics and is used in analyses of quantification, plurality, and mass nouns, as well as in computational semantics.
