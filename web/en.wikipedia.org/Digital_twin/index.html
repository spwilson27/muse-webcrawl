Digital twin

A digital twin is a digital model of an intended or actual real-world physical product, system, or process that serves as an effectively indistinguishable digital counterpart of it for practical purposes, such as simulation, integration, testing, monitoring, and maintenance. The concept spans the object, the processes applied to it, and the data and communication channels linking the digital and physical versions.

History

The idea of pairing a physical asset with a virtual model traces to NASA's practice of running detailed ground-based simulations of spacecraft — famously used during the Apollo 13 rescue in 1970. Michael Grieves formalised the concept for product lifecycle management in the early 2000s, and NASA popularised the term "digital twin" around 2010–2012 in roadmaps for vehicle simulation. The growth of the Internet of Things and cheap sensors made live-updated twins practical.

Types and applications

Twins range from component twins (a single part such as a turbine blade), through asset and system twins, to process and city-scale twins used in urban planning. Manufacturers use them for design, predictive maintenance and remaining-life estimation; energy companies model wind farms and grids; hospitals experiment with patient and workflow twins; and building operators use them to optimise energy use. A twin that only runs offline simulations is sometimes distinguished from one receiving continuous real-time data.

Technologies

Digital twins draw on sensor telemetry, physics-based simulation, computer-aided design models, and machine learning. Standards and commercial platforms from industrial and cloud software vendors provide the data integration. Challenges include model fidelity, interoperability between vendors, data quality, cybersecurity of the connected physical asset, and deciding how much detail a twin needs for its intended purpose.
