Myrddin Wyllt


Labelled image: Myrddin Wyllt, the wild man of the woods

Myrddin Wyllt (Welsh: [ˈmərðɪn ˈwɨɬt], "Myrddin the Wild", also called Merlinus Caledonensis in Latin sources) is a figure in Welsh legend. A prophet and madman of the Old North (Yr Hen Ogledd) in the sixth century, he is said to have been driven insane by the horrors of the Battle of Arfderydd (c. 573) and to have fled into the Caledonian forest, where he lived as a wild man uttering prophecies. He is one of the principal sources for the later literary figure of Merlin.

Legend

According to the medieval Welsh poems attributed to him — including the Afallenau ("The Apple-trees"), the Oianau ("The Greetings") and the Hoianau — Myrddin fought at Arfderydd on the side of Gwenddoleu, a king of the Britons of the North. When Gwenddoleu was slain, Myrddin lost his reason and wandered the forest of Celyddon, lamenting his lord and prophesying to an apple tree and a little pig. The poems survive in the Black Book of Carmarthen and the Red Book of Hergest.

Relationship to Merlin and Lailoken

Geoffrey of Monmouth drew on the Myrddin tradition in creating Merlin, though Geoffrey's Merlin Ambrosius is largely a separate, conflated figure. Myrddin Wyllt is also closely paralleled by the Scottish wild man Lailoken, who appears in the hagiography of Saint Kentigern; the two figures are widely regarded as variants of the same legendary tradition, in which the wild prophet is eventually reconciled with, or persecuted by, the saint.
