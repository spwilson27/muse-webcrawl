The Motion Picture Association (MPA) is an American trade association representing the major film studios of the United States, the mini-major Amazon MGM Studios, and the streaming service Netflix. Founded in 1922 as the Motion Picture Producers and Distributors of America (MPPDA) and known as the Motion Picture Association of America (MPAA) from 1945 until September 2019, its original goal was to ensure the viability of the American film industry. In addition, the MPA established guidelines for film content which resulted in the creation of the Production Code in 1930. This code, also known as the Hays Code, was replaced by a voluntary film rating system in 1968, administered by the Classification and Rating Administration (CARA).

More recently, the MPA has advocated for the motion picture and television industry, with the goals of promoting effective copyright protection, expanding market access, and combating the illegal copying and distribution of copyrighted works, notably through anti-piracy initiatives.

History

The association was founded in 1922, in the aftermath of several scandals in Hollywood, with former Postmaster General Will H. Hays as its first president. Hays sought to improve the industry's public image and led efforts to censor objectionable content through the Production Code, adopted in 1930 and enforced from 1934.

Jack Valenti, a former advisor to President Lyndon B. Johnson, served as president from 1966 to 2004. In 1968 Valenti introduced the MPAA film rating system, which replaced the Production Code and remains, in modified form, in use today: G, PG, PG-13, R, and NC-17. The PG-13 rating was introduced in 1984 following controversy over violent content in PG-rated films.

Members

As of the 2020s the MPA's members are the five major Hollywood studios — Paramount Pictures, Sony Pictures, Universal Pictures, Walt Disney Studios, and Warner Bros. — along with Amazon MGM Studios and Netflix.

Film rating system

The MPA film rating system is a voluntary scheme under which films are rated by the Classification and Rating Administration. Ratings are advisory for parents and are enforced by most theatre chains as a matter of policy. The system is also a well-known example of industry self-regulation.
