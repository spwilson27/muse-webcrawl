Global commons

Global commons is a term typically used to describe international, supranational, and global resource domains in which common-pool resources are found — resources shared by all humanity and lying beyond the jurisdiction of any single state. Classic examples include the high seas, the atmosphere, Antarctica, and outer space, and by extension the climate system, the deep seabed, and cyberspace.

Legal frameworks

Several global commons are governed by international treaties. The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea treats the deep seabed beyond national jurisdiction ("the Area") and its minerals as the "common heritage of mankind"; the Outer Space Treaty of 1967 declares space the province of all mankind; the Antarctic Treaty System of 1959 reserves the continent for peaceful scientific use; and the 2023 High Seas Treaty (BBNJ Agreement) created a framework for marine protected areas on the high seas.

Governance challenges

Because global commons are open to all but owned by none, they face the "tragedy of the commons": overfishing, atmospheric pollution, and orbital debris accumulate because individual actors gain the benefits of use while costs are shared. Scholars such as Elinor Ostrom have studied how institutions, norms, and treaties can govern shared resources sustainably, while debates continue over equity between developed and developing nations in exploiting and protecting these domains.
