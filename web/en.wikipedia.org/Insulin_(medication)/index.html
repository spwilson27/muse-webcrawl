Insulin is a medication used in the treatment of diabetes. As a pharmaceutical, insulin is any of several preparations of the protein hormone insulin used to treat high blood glucose in conditions such as type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, gestational diabetes, and diabetic complications including diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic states. It is also used along with glucose to treat high blood potassium levels. Typically it is given by injection under the skin, but some forms may also be used by injection into a vein or muscle. The inhaled form is no longer widely available in most markets.

Types

Insulin preparations differ chiefly in their onset and duration of action:

  • Rapid-acting analogues (insulin lispro, aspart, glulisine): onset about 15 minutes, duration 3–5 hours.
  • Short-acting (regular insulin): onset about 30 minutes, duration 5–8 hours.
  • Intermediate-acting (NPH insulin): onset 1–2 hours, duration up to 18 hours.
  • Long-acting analogues (glargine, detemir, degludec): onset 1–2 hours, duration up to 24–42 hours, used as basal insulin.

Mechanism

Insulin binds to the insulin receptor on cell surfaces, promoting glucose uptake into skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, stimulating glycogenesis and lipogenesis, and suppressing hepatic glucose output and lipolysis. In the absence of sufficient insulin (or response to it), blood glucose rises and the body may break down fat, producing ketones.

Adverse effects

The most common adverse effect is hypoglycemia (low blood sugar), which can cause sweating, tremor, confusion and, in severe cases, seizures or coma. Repeated injections can cause lipodystrophy at injection sites. Weight gain is common when therapy is intensified.

History

Insulin was isolated from pancreatic extracts at the University of Toronto in 1921 by Frederick Banting, Charles Best, James Collip and John Macleod; it was first used in a human patient, Leonard Thompson, in January 1922. Animal-source insulin from cattle and pigs was used for decades before recombinant human insulin became available in 1982, followed by insulin analogues engineered for faster or longer action.

Society and culture

Insulin is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines. Its price and accessibility — particularly in the United States — have been the subject of sustained public debate.
