Riemann hypothesis

In mathematics, the Riemann hypothesis is the conjecture that the Riemann zeta function has its zeros (other than the trivial zeros at the negative even integers) only on the critical line — that is, at complex numbers s with real part Re(s) = 1/2. It was proposed by Bernhard Riemann in 1859 and remains unproven, generally regarded as the most important unsolved problem in pure mathematics.

The zeta function

The Riemann zeta function is defined for Re(s) > 1 by the convergent series:

ζ(s) = Σn=1..∞ n−s = Πp prime (1 − p−s)−1

The product over primes (Euler product) ties ζ(s) directly to the distribution of prime numbers. Riemann showed ζ(s) extends by analytic continuation to the whole complex plane except a simple pole at s = 1, and satisfies a functional equation relating ζ(s) to ζ(1 − s).

Consequences

The hypothesis is equivalent to the strongest possible error term in the prime number theorem: it would imply that π(x), the count of primes below x, approximates the logarithmic integral li(x) with error of order √x · log x. Hundreds of results in number theory are proven conditionally "on the Riemann hypothesis", including sharp bounds on gaps between primes and the verification of the weak Goldbach conjecture for sufficiently large numbers (later proven unconditionally by Helfgott in 2013).

Progress

Hardy proved in 1914 that infinitely many zeros lie on the critical line; Selberg showed a positive proportion do; Levinson and later Conrey raised the proportion above 40%. Computers have verified the first ten trillion zeros. Analogues of the hypothesis for zeta functions over function fields were proven by André Weil and Pierre Deligne as part of the Weil conjectures. Hilbert included the problem eighth on his famous list of 1900, and the Clay Mathematics Institute offers US$1 million for its solution.

Attempted proofs

Proposed proofs are announced regularly and invariably found flawed. Notable approaches include spectral theory (the Hilbert–Pólya conjecture that the zeros correspond to eigenvalues of a self-adjoint operator), connections to random matrix theory (the Montgomery–Dyson correspondence observed at the Institute for Advanced Study in 1972), and Lee–Yang-type results in statistical physics.
