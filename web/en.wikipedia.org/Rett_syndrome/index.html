Rett syndrome

Rett syndrome (RTT) is a genetic disorder that typically becomes apparent after 6–18 months of age in females. The underlying causes are de novo mutations in the X-linked gene MECP2, which encodes methyl-CpG binding protein 2. Symptoms include impairments in language and coordination, intellectual disability, loss of purposeful hand use with repetitive hand movements (such as wringing), slowed head growth, seizures and breathing abnormalities.

Signs and course

Development usually appears normal for the first 6–18 months. A period of stagnation and then rapid regression follows (stage II), with loss of speech and hand skills. In stage III ("pseudostationary") some communication and social interest may return, while stage IV brings motor deterioration, scoliosis and loss of ambulation. Because the causative gene is X-linked, males with severe mutations rarely survive infancy, making Rett syndrome predominantly a condition of girls and women.

Genetics

In 1999, Huda Zoghbi's laboratory identified mutations in MECP2 as the cause. The protein binds methylated DNA and regulates gene expression in neurons. Atypical and variant forms involve genes such as CDKL5 and FOXG1. Mouse models have shown that restoring MeCP2 function can reverse many symptoms, a finding that motivates gene-therapy research.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by genetic testing; Rett syndrome was removed from the autism spectrum disorders in DSM-5 and is classified as a distinct genetic condition. There is no cure; management is multidisciplinary — physiotherapy, communication support, anti-seizure medication and monitoring for cardiac (long QT) and breathing issues. In March 2023 the U.S. FDA approved trofinetide (Daybue), the first drug specifically approved for Rett syndrome.

History

The Austrian paediatrician Andreas Rett first described the condition in 1966; it gained international recognition after Bengt Hagberg and colleagues published an English-language account in 1983.
