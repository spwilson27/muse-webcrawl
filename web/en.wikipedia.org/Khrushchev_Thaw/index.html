Khrushchev Thaw

The Khrushchev Thaw (Russian: Хрущёвская оттепель) was a period in the 1950s and early 1960s when the Soviet Union, under First Secretary Nikita Khrushchev, loosened political repression and censorship following the death of Joseph Stalin in 1953. The period is named after Ilya Ehrenburg's 1954 novel The Thaw.

Beginnings

After Stalin's death in March 1953, a collective leadership briefly headed the USSR before Nikita Khrushchev emerged as the dominant figure. Lavrentiy Beria, head of the secret police, was arrested and executed in 1953, and millions of prisoners were released from the Gulag system of labour camps in a series of amnesties.

The Secret Speech

The defining moment of the Thaw came on 25 February 1956, when Khrushchev delivered his famous "Secret Speech" to a closed session of the 20th Congress of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union. In it, he denounced Stalin's crimes, the mass arrests and deportations, and the creation of a personality cult. Although the speech was officially secret, its contents spread throughout the Eastern Bloc and beyond, triggering shock and unrest, notably the Polish October and the Hungarian Revolution of 1956.

Cultural thaw

Censorship was relaxed and Soviet culture experienced a flowering. Writers such as Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, whose novella One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich (1962) depicted life in a labour camp, were permitted to publish. Journals like Novy Mir under Aleksandr Tvardovsky published bolder work, and foreign literature, films, and music became more accessible. The 1957 World Festival of Youth and Students in Moscow brought tens of thousands of foreign visitors to the Soviet capital.

Limits and end

The Thaw was never complete: Khrushchev could turn sharply conservative, as in his 1962 tirade against abstract art at the Manege exhibition, and the regime crushed the Hungarian uprising with tanks. The Thaw effectively ended with Khrushchev's ouster in October 1964, after which Leonid Brezhnev's leadership gradually tightened controls again, though it never returned to full Stalinist terror.

Legacy

The Thaw reshaped Soviet society, giving a generation — the "shestidesyatniki", the people of the sixties — a taste of relative freedom that influenced Soviet culture and dissent for decades. It is generally regarded as the first step in the long process of de-Stalinisation.
