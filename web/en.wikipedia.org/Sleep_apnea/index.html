Sleep apnea (sleep apnoea) is a sleep disorder in which breathing repeatedly stops and starts during sleep. Episodes, called apneas, last at least ten seconds and may occur dozens or hundreds of times a night, fragmenting sleep and lowering blood oxygen levels.

Types

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), the most common form, occurs when the muscles at the back of the throat relax and the airway narrows or closes, despite continued breathing effort. Central sleep apnea arises when the brain fails to send proper signals to the breathing muscles, and is associated with heart failure and opioid use. Some patients have a combination of both.

Symptoms and risks

Signs include loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, gasping during sleep, morning headaches, unrefreshing sleep and excessive daytime sleepiness. Risk factors include obesity, a thick neck, alcohol use, smoking and family history. Untreated apnea is associated with hypertension, heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and accidents caused by drowsiness.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is confirmed by polysomnography (a sleep study) or home sleep testing, and severity is graded by the apnea–hypopnoea index. The mainstay of treatment for moderate to severe OSA is continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), which keeps the airway open with pressurised air delivered through a mask. Alternatives include oral appliances that reposition the jaw, weight loss, positional therapy and, in selected cases, surgery or hypoglossal nerve stimulation.
