Memphis was the ancient capital of Inebu-hedj, the first nome of Lower Egypt, and one of the oldest and most important cities in ancient Egypt. Located near the modern village of Mit Rahina, south of Cairo at the apex of the Nile Delta, it stood at the strategic junction of Upper and Lower Egypt and served as a royal residence and administrative center for much of the Old Kingdom.

History

Tradition attributes the city's founding to the pharaoh Menes, who united Egypt around 3100 BC. Memphis was the cult center of the creator god Ptah, whose great temple, Hut-ka-Ptah, gave rise through Greek to the name "Egypt". The city's necropolises — Saqqara, Giza, Abusir, and Dahshur — contain the pyramids of the Old Kingdom kings. Memphis remained a major city through the New Kingdom and later periods until it declined with the rise of Alexandria.

Archaeology

Because the ruins lie in a populated area and the ancient city was built largely of mudbrick, relatively little survives above ground. Notable remains include the colossal statue of Ramesses II and a large alabaster sphinx, displayed in an open-air museum at Mit Rahina. In 1979 the site was inscribed as part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing "Memphis and its Necropolis – the Pyramid Fields from Giza to Dahshur".
