An Indian reservation in the United States is an area of land held in trust by the federal government for a federally recognised Native American tribe, whose members generally exercise tribal sovereignty within its bounds. There are 326 such reservations, varying from vast territories such as the Navajo Nation to tiny parcels of a few acres.

History

The reservation system developed through treaties in the 18th and 19th centuries as the United States expanded westward, confining tribes to defined lands. The Indian Removal Act of 1830 led to forced relocations such as the Trail of Tears. The Dawes Act of 1887 allotted communal reservation land to individuals and opened "surplus" land to settlers, drastically reducing the tribal land base, before the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 ended allotment.

Governance and law

Tribal governments provide services, enact laws and operate courts on reservations, though jurisdiction is a complex patchwork involving tribal, federal and sometimes state authority, shaped by statutes such as Public Law 280 and Supreme Court decisions. The Bureau of Indian Affairs administers the federal trust responsibility.

Contemporary issues

Reservations face persistent challenges including poverty, infrastructure deficits and fractionated land ownership, alongside economic development through gaming under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988, renewable energy, and cultural revitalisation programmes.
