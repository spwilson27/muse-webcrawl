In statistics, the Wald test (named after Abraham Wald) assesses constraints on statistical parameters based on the weighted distance between the unrestricted estimate and its hypothesised value under the null hypothesis, where the weight is the precision of the estimate. Intuitively, the larger this weighted distance, the less likely it is that the constraint is true.

While the finite sample distributions of Wald tests are generally unknown, it has an asymptotic distribution under the null hypothesis, which is the chi-squared distribution, assuming that the estimator is asymptotically normally distributed. This fact can be used to determine statistical significance for the statistic.

Formulation

For a scalar parameter θ with unrestricted estimate θ̂ and standard error se(θ̂), the Wald statistic for testing H₀: θ = θ₀ is W = (θ̂ − θ₀)² / Var(θ̂), which under the null is asymptotically distributed as a chi-squared random variable with one degree of freedom. The square root of the single-restriction Wald statistic follows an asymptotic standard normal distribution, and equals the familiar "z-statistic" reported by most regression software. The multivariate version tests several restrictions jointly using the estimated covariance matrix of the parameters.

Advantages and limitations

Together with the likelihood ratio test and the Lagrange multiplier (score) test, the Wald test forms the classical trinity of tests based on maximum likelihood estimation. Its chief advantage is convenience: it requires only the unrestricted model to be estimated. Its disadvantages include dependence on the parameterization of the model — the statistic is not invariant to nonlinear reparameterization — and poor finite-sample behaviour in some settings, such as the Hauck–Donner effect in logistic regression, where very large coefficients produce deceptively small Wald statistics.
