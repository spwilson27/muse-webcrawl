Ghalib

Mirza Asadullah Baig Khan (27 December 1797 – 15 February 1869), better known by his pen name Ghalib, was an Urdu and Persian poet of the Mughal era. Regarded as the greatest poet of the Urdu language, he is celebrated for his ghazals, which explore love, loss, faith, and the human condition with wit and philosophical depth.

Life

Ghalib was born in Agra into a family of Turkic aristocratic descent and was raised in Delhi after his father's early death. He began composing poetry in his teens, writing first in Persian, which he considered his more serious medium, and later in Urdu. He married at a young age and settled in Delhi, where he spent most of his life attached, loosely and often unhappily, to the declining court of the last Mughal emperor, Bahadur Shah Zafar.

Financial difficulties, disputes over his pension from the British authorities, and personal tragedies — none of his children survived infancy — marked much of his life. He lived through the Indian Rebellion of 1857, which devastated Delhi and ended the Mughal court; his letters from this period are an important literary record of the catastrophe.

Poetry

Ghalib's Urdu divan (collected poems) is relatively small, but intensely influential. His ghazals are known for their compressed, ambiguous imagery, their questioning of religious orthodoxy, and their blend of melancholy and irony. He also wrote celebrated letters in Urdu, which helped shape modern Urdu prose with their conversational, intimate style.

Legacy

Though his genius was contested in his lifetime, Ghalib's reputation grew steadily after his death. His tomb in Delhi is a place of pilgrimage for poetry lovers, and his verses are quoted constantly in South Asian popular culture, music, and film. The critic Ralph Russell helped introduce his work to English readers in the twentieth century.
