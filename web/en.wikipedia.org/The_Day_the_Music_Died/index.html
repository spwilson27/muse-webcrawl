The Day the Music Died

"The Day the Music Died" is the name given to 3 February 1959, when American rock and roll musicians Buddy Holly, Ritchie Valens and "The Big Bopper" J. P. Richardson were killed, along with pilot Roger Peterson, in a plane crash near Clear Lake, Iowa.

The crash

The musicians were on the gruelling Winter Dance Party tour of the Midwest in buses with broken heating. After a show at the Surf Ballroom in Clear Lake, Holly chartered a four-seat Beechcraft Bonanza to fly to Moorhead, Minnesota. Valens won a coin toss with guitarist Tommy Allsup for his seat; Richardson, suffering from flu, took the place of bassist Waylon Jennings. The plane crashed into a cornfield shortly after takeoff in snow and darkness; all four aboard died. The accident investigation blamed pilot error and weather.

The name

Don McLean coined the phrase in his 1971 song "American Pie", an eight-and-a-half-minute elegy in which "the day the music died" recurs as a symbol of lost American innocence. The crash site, marked by a monument and a large replica of Holly's glasses, hosts annual memorial concerts at the Surf Ballroom.

Legacy

The deaths of Holly (22), Valens (17) and Richardson (28) shocked the young rock and roll world and are often taken to mark the end of its first era, before the British Invasion. The story is dramatised in the films The Buddy Holly Story (1978) and La Bamba (1987).
