Nichiren Buddhism is a branch of Mahayana Buddhism based on the teachings of the thirteenth-century Japanese monk Nichiren (1222–1282) and on the Lotus Sutra, which Nichiren regarded as the supreme expression of the Buddha's teaching. Its central practice is the chanting of the mantra Nam-myoho-renge-kyo ("devotion to the Mystic Law of the Lotus Sutra"), called the daimoku, before a calligraphic mandala known as the Gohonzon.

Nichiren's teaching

Nichiren proclaimed his teaching in 1253, holding that in the degenerate age of the Latter Day of the Law (mappō), only the Lotus Sutra — and specifically the invocation of its title — could lead all people to enlightenment. In his treatise Risshō Ankoku Ron (1260) he blamed Japan's disasters on the nation's adherence to what he considered inferior teachings, provoking persecution: he was twice exiled (to Izu and to Sado Island) and narrowly escaped execution at Tatsunokuchi in 1271.

Doctrine

Key concepts drawn from the Lotus Sutra and the Tiantai (Tendai) tradition include the "three thousand realms in a single moment of life" (ichinen sanzen) and the mutual possession of the Ten Worlds, teaching that Buddhahood is inherent in every person and every moment. The Gohonzon, inscribed by Nichiren from 1279, embodies the enlightenment he attained.

Schools

After Nichiren's death his disciples formed several lineages; today the largest groups are Nichiren-shū, Nichiren Shōshū, and the lay organisation Soka Gakkai (with its international arm SGI), which has spread Nichiren Buddhism worldwide and is known for its emphasis on chanting, study, and propagation (shakubuku).
