The Sicilian Expedition was an Athenian military expedition to Sicily, which took place during the period from 415 BC to 413 BC, during the Peloponnesian War. The expedition was a catastrophic failure for the Athenians, who suffered heavy losses in both fighting men and ships. It severely weakened Athens, both militarily and financially, and is often considered a turning point in the war, which ended in Athens' defeat in 404 BC.

The main source for the expedition is Thucydides, who covered it in Books 6 and 7 of his History of the Peloponnesian War, regarded as among the masterpieces of ancient historiography. Donald Kagan called the expedition "the greatest and most terrible disaster ever to befall an Athenian army".

Background

Athens and Sparta had been at peace under the Peace of Nicias since 421 BC, though skirmishing continued. In 416 BC envoys from the Sicilian city of Segesta appealed to Athens for help against Selinus and Syracuse, offering money and exaggerating their resources. The Athenian assembly, swayed by the ambitious Alcibiades and against the caution of Nicias, voted to send a large force under Alcibiades, Nicias, and Lamachus. Nicias's attempt to deter the assembly by demanding an enormous armament backfired: the Athenians enthusiastically voted the larger force.

The expedition

The fleet that sailed in the summer of 415 BC comprised 134 triremes and thousands of hoplites — one of the largest forces ever dispatched by a Greek city. Shortly after reaching Sicily, Alcibiades was recalled to Athens to face charges of sacrilege connected with the mutilation of the Herms; he escaped and defected to Sparta. Lamachus was killed in battle in 414 BC, leaving the ailing Nicias in sole command. The Athenians besieged Syracuse, building a circumvallation across the Epipolae plateau, but Spartan assistance under Gylippus arrived in time to rally the Syracusans, who built counter-walls and progressively trapped the Athenians.

A reinforcement fleet under Demosthenes arrived in 413 BC but failed to reverse the situation; a bold night attack on Epipolae ended in confusion and heavy loss. After the Athenians lost control of the Great Harbour in a decisive naval battle, their attempted retreat overland disintegrated. The army was destroyed at the river Assinarus, and the survivors — some 7,000 men — were imprisoned in the stone quarries of Syracuse, where many died; Nicias and Demosthenes were executed.

Aftermath

The disaster shattered Athenian military strength and prestige, emboldened Sparta to renew the war with Persian financial support, and encouraged Athens' subject allies to revolt. Although Athens fought on for another nine years, it never fully recovered from the loss of tens of thousands of soldiers and sailors and two entire fleets.
