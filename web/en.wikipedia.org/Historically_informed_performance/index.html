Historically informed performance

Historically informed performance (also referred to as period performance, authentic performance, or HIP) is an approach to the performance of classical music which aims to be faithful to the approach, manner and style of the musical era in which a work was originally conceived. It is based on two key aspects: the application of the stylistic and technical aspects of performance, known as performance practice; and the use of period instruments which may be reproductions of historical instruments that were in use at the time of the original composition, or original instruments from that era.

History

The modern early music revival began in the early twentieth century with figures such as Arnold Dolmetsch and Wanda Landowska, who championed the harpsichord and historical instruments. From the 1950s and 1960s, pioneers including Nikolaus Harnoncourt, Gustav Leonhardt, and Sigiswald Kuijken founded period-instrument ensembles and recorded Baroque and Classical repertory using historical performance practices. Conductors such as Roger Norrington, John Eliot Gardiner, and Trevor Pinnock later extended the approach to Beethoven, Mendelssohn, and even Brahms and Wagner.

Practices

HIP performers draw on treatises, tutors, and other period sources for evidence about tempo, articulation, ornamentation, vibrato, improvisation, and tuning systems such as meantone temperament. String players use gut strings and Baroque bows; ensembles adopt the pitch standards of the time (often A = 415 Hz for Baroque music); and keyboard works are played on harpsichords or fortepianos rather than modern pianos.

Reception and criticism

The movement transformed mainstream performance, and many modern-instrument orchestras now adopt some historically informed practices. Critics — most famously Richard Taruskin — have argued that "authenticity" is partly a modern aesthetic construction, and that performers can never fully escape the tastes of their own time. Adherents respond that HIP is valued less for literal reconstruction than for the freshness and clarity it brings to familiar repertory.
