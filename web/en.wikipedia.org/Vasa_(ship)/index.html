Vasa (ship)

Vasa or Wasa is a Swedish warship built between 1626 and 1628. The ship sank after sailing roughly 1,300 m (1,400 yd) into her maiden voyage on 10 August 1628. She fell into obscurity after most of her valuable bronze cannon were salvaged in the 17th century, until she was located again in the late 1950s in a busy shipping lane just outside Stockholm harbour. The ship was salvaged with a largely intact hull in 1961.

Construction and sinking

Commissioned by King Gustav II Adolf during the Thirty Years' War, Vasa was richly decorated, with hundreds of carved sculptures, and carried 64 bronze cannon on two gun decks. Design changes during construction — including an extra gun deck demanded by the king — left her dangerously top-heavy, with too little ballast and a freeboard so low that water poured through the open lower gunports when she heeled in a gust of wind. Despite a stability test that visibly alarmed officials, she was allowed to sail and sank within sight of the city.

Salvage and preservation

Amateur archaeologist Anders Franzén relocated the wreck in 1956. On 24 April 1961 she was raised in a widely watched operation, bringing up thousands of artefacts and the remains of crew members. Conservators sprayed the timbers with polyethylene glycol for nearly two decades to prevent the waterlogged oak from cracking as it dried.

The Vasa Museum

Since 1990 the ship has been displayed in the purpose-built Vasa Museum on Djurgården in Stockholm, Sweden's most visited museum. Vasa is the world's best-preserved 17th-century ship and a unique source for the study of naval warfare, shipbuilding, and everyday life of the period.
