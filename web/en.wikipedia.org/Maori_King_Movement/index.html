Māori King Movement

The Māori King Movement, known in Māori as the Kīngitanga, is an indigenous movement in New Zealand that began in the 1850s with the election of a Māori king. It was established to preserve Māori land and authority in the face of rapid European settlement, and to provide a unified leadership able to negotiate with the British Crown as an equal.

Foundation

After a series of meetings among central North Island tribes, the respected Waikato chief Pōtatau Te Wherowhero accepted the kingship in 1858 and was crowned at Ngāruawāhia. His son Tāwhiao succeeded him and led the movement through the New Zealand Wars, the invasion of the Waikato in 1863, and the subsequent confiscation of Kīngitanga lands.

Later history

Successive monarchs — Mahuta, Te Rata, Korokī, Te Atairangikaahu (reigned 1966–2006), and Tūheitia (2006–2024) — each combined the traditional responsibilities of the movement with engagement in national politics and Māori development. In 2024, Ngā Wai hono i te Pō, daughter of Tūheitia, became the eighth holder of the office and the second woman. The movement's home is Tūrangawaewae Marae in Ngāruawāhia, and it is closely associated with the Tainui confederation of tribes.
