Safari

A game drive in East Africa, the heartland of the modern safari.

A safari (from Swahili safari, "journey", from Arabic safar, "to travel") is an overland journey, usually in Africa, undertaken to observe or hunt wild animals. Historically associated with big-game hunting in the colonial era, the term today most often denotes wildlife-viewing tourism in national parks and game reserves.

History

The safari tradition developed in 19th-century East and southern Africa, where expeditions combined hunting, exploration, and trade, employing local guides, porters, and trackers. Figures such as Frederick Selous became famous as professional hunters, and clients included Theodore Roosevelt, whose 1909 Smithsonian expedition collected thousands of specimens. Ernest Hemingway's stories The Snows of Kilimanjaro and The Short Happy Life of Francis Macomber helped fix the safari in popular imagination. The distinctive khaki clothing, pith helmets, and the "safari jacket" entered Western fashion from this milieu.

Modern safaris

Today most safaris are photographic: visitors travel in open vehicles with guides to see the "Big Five" — lion, leopard, elephant, rhinoceros, and buffalo — a list that originated with hunters ranking the most dangerous game on foot. Premier destinations include the Serengeti and Ngorongoro in Tanzania, the Maasai Mara in Kenya, Kruger National Park in South Africa, and the Okavango Delta in Botswana. Safari tourism provides major funding for conservation and local communities, though its distribution and environmental footprint are debated. Ecotourism variants include walking safaris, mobile tented camps, and gorilla trekking in Rwanda and Uganda.
