Convict leasing

Convict leasing was a system of forced penal labour practised historically in the Southern United States, under which states leased prisoners to private railways, mines, and plantations. The system began during Reconstruction after the Emancipation Proclamation (1863) and the passage of the Thirteenth Amendment (1865), whose exception clause permits involuntary servitude as punishment for a crime.

Operation

Under Black Codes and vagrancy laws, large numbers of African Americans were arrested for minor or fabricated offences and leased to private employers. Lessees paid the state for the prisoners' labour and assumed responsibility for their food, housing, and guarding, but had little financial incentive to preserve prisoners' lives, since replacement convicts were cheap. Working conditions in mines, turpentine camps, and railroad construction were notoriously brutal, and mortality rates were extremely high — in some camps reaching double-digit percentages per year.

Abolition

Investigative reporting, labour-union opposition (convinced free workers were undercut), and public scandals gradually undermined the system. Florida abolished convict leasing after the 1923 death of Martin Tabert, a young white prisoner whose flogging provoked national outrage; Alabama, the last state to end formal leasing, did so in 1928. Historians such as Douglas Blackmon have described convict leasing as "slavery by another name". See also penal labour.
