Theotokos (Greek: Θεοτόκος, romanized: Theotókos) is a title of Mary, mother of Jesus, used especially in Eastern Christianity. The usual Latin translations are Dei Genitrix or Deipara ("Mother of God" / "God-bearer"). Common English translations are "Mother of God" and "God-bearer". The title has been in use since the 3rd century and was dogmatically affirmed at the Third Ecumenical Council, held at Ephesus in 431.

Meaning and theology

The title is a compound of two Greek words, Θεός ("God") and τόκος ("childbirth, offspring"). Its theological significance rests on the doctrine of the Incarnation: because Jesus Christ is, from conception, one person who is fully God and fully man, his mother may properly be called the mother of God — not in the sense that she is the origin of God's eternal nature, but because she bore the person who is God incarnate. The title thus safeguards the unity of Christ's person against teaching that divided him into two separate subjects.

History

Early Christian writers including Origen, Athanasius of Alexandria, and Gregory of Nazianzus used the term. The controversy erupted in 428 when Nestorius, Patriarch of Constantinople, objected to the popular title, preferring Christotokos ("Christ-bearer"). Cyril of Alexandria defended Theotokos as essential to orthodox Christology. The Council of Ephesus (431) condemned Nestorius and affirmed the title, a settlement later confirmed at the Council of Chalcedon (451). The dispute contributed to the schism between the imperial church and the Church of the East.

Usage and devotion

In the Eastern Orthodox Church, Theotokos is the most common way of referring to Mary, and she is honored with hymns, icons, and feasts (notably the Dormition). Icons of the Theotokos and Child — of the Hodegetria, Eleusa, and other types — are central to Byzantine and Orthodox art. In Roman Catholic usage, "Mother of God" is a solemn title, celebrated on the Solemnity of Mary, Mother of God (January 1), and the doctrine was reiterated at the Second Vatican Council. Many Protestant traditions historically accepted the title in its Christological sense, though it is less commonly used.
