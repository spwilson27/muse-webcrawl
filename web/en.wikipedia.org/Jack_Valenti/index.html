Jack Joseph Valenti (5 September 1921 – 26 April 2007) was an American political advisor and lobbyist who served as president of the Motion Picture Association of America (MPAA) for 38 years, from 1966 to 2004. He is best known for creating the MPAA film rating system in 1968, which replaced the restrictive Hays Code.

Early life and career

Born in Houston, Texas, to Italian immigrant parents, Valenti served as a bomber pilot in the United States Army Air Forces during World War II, flying 51 combat missions. After the war he co-founded the Houston advertising agency Weekley & Valenti. His firm worked on John F. Kennedy's 1960 campaign, and Valenti was in the presidential motorcade in Dallas on 22 November 1963; he appears in the famous photograph of Lyndon B. Johnson being sworn in aboard Air Force One, standing behind the new president.

White House service

Valenti became the first special assistant to President Johnson, serving from 1963 to 1966 as a close and famously loyal aide — he once said he slept better at night knowing Johnson was his president. In 1966, at Johnson's urging, he resigned to head the MPAA.

MPAA presidency

Valenti's first major act was to abolish the Production Code and, in 1968, introduce the voluntary film rating system with its original categories G, M (later PG), R, and X; PG-13 was added in 1984 and NC-17 replaced X in 1990. He became Hollywood's chief Washington lobbyist, a fixture of congressional hearings on copyright, piracy, and new technologies. In 1982 he memorably testified that "the VCR is to the American film producer and the American public as the Boston strangler is to the woman home alone." In his later years he championed digital rights management and anti-piracy measures. He retired in 2004, succeeded by Dan Glickman.

Later life

Valenti published his memoir, This Time, This Place, in 2007. He died in Washington, D.C., on 26 April 2007 of complications from a stroke, and was buried at Arlington National Cemetery.
