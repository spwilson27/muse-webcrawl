Focalization

Focalization is a term coined by the French literary theorist Gérard Genette in his work Narrative Discourse (1972) to describe the perspective through which a narrative is presented — the distinction between who speaks (the narrator) and who sees (the focalizer). In a story, the events, characters and settings are all filtered through some perceptual or cognitive position, and focalization names that filtering.

Genette's typology

Genette proposed three kinds of focalization. In zero focalization (non-focalized narrative), the narrator knows more than any character — the classical "omniscient" narrator. In internal focalization, the narrative is restricted to what one character (fixed), several characters in turn (variable), or the same event retold from several viewpoints (multiple) can perceive and know. In external focalization, the narrator says less than the characters know, presenting only outward behaviour, as in Hemingway's short fiction and behaviourist novels.

Development and critique

The narratologist Mieke Bal revised the concept by distinguishing the focalizer (the subject of focalization) from the focalized (its object), and by allowing focalization to be delegated to characters even within a narrator's discourse. Focalization has become a standard tool in the analysis of prose fiction, film (where it interacts with camera point of view) and other narrative media.
