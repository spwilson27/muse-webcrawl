Tidal power

Tidal power or tidal energy is a form of renewable electricity generation that converts the energy of the tides into useful power. Unlike most renewables it is highly predictable, since tides follow the predictable motions of the Moon and Sun. The kinetic and potential energy of tidal currents and tidal ranges can be captured by tidal stream generators (underwater turbines), tidal barrages, tidal lagoons, and dynamic tidal power schemes.

Methods

Tidal stream generators work much like underwater wind turbines, driven by fast tidal currents at constricted sites such as sounds and headlands. Tidal barrages are dams built across estuaries: as the tide rises and falls, water flows through turbines built into the barrage, generating power on the ebb, the flood, or both. The first large barrage was the Rance Tidal Power Station in France (240 MW, opened 1966), long the world's largest until surpassed by South Korea's Sihwa Lake Tidal Power Station (254 MW, 2011). Tidal lagoons are enclosed pools built out from the coast that generate in the same way while avoiding blocking an entire estuary.

Advantages and limitations

Tidal energy is predictable decades in advance, has a high energy density (water is far denser than air), and produces no direct greenhouse gas emissions. However, suitable sites with large tidal ranges or strong currents are limited, capital costs are high, and barrages can alter estuarine ecosystems, sediment transport and fish migration. Total installed capacity worldwide remains small — only a few hundred megawatts — although tidal stream projects such as MeyGen in Scotland's Pentland Firth have begun to demonstrate commercial-scale arrays.
