Frederick Sanger (13 August 1918 – 19 November 2013) was a British biochemist who won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry twice — one of only a handful of people to receive two Nobel Prizes in the same category. He determined the complete amino acid sequence of insulin and later developed the dideoxy ("Sanger") method of DNA sequencing that powered the Human Genome Project.

Insulin sequencing

Working in Cambridge in the 1940s and 1950s, Sanger developed methods using the reagent fluorodinitrobenzene to identify N-terminal amino acids and to break proteins into analysable fragments. In 1955 he published the full sequence of bovine insulin — the first protein ever sequenced — proving that proteins have definite, genetically determined structures. The work earned him the 1958 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

DNA sequencing

At the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology, Sanger turned to nucleic acids. After contributing to the sequencing of RNA molecules, his group published in 1977 the "plus and minus" and then the dideoxy chain-termination method of DNA sequencing. Using it, they produced the first complete genome of an organism — the bacteriophage φX174 — and later the 16,569-base human mitochondrial genome. Sanger shared the 1980 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Walter Gilbert and Paul Berg.

Legacy

Sanger sequencing remained the standard technology for three decades and underpinned the sequencing of the human genome. The Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute near Cambridge, opened in 1993, is named in his honour. Sanger declined a knighthood but accepted the Order of Merit.
