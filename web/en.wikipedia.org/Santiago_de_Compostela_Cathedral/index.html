The Cathedral of Santiago de Compostela (Galician: Catedral de Santiago de Compostela) is a Roman Catholic cathedral in the city of Santiago de Compostela in Galicia, northwestern Spain. According to tradition, it enshrines the tomb of the apostle Saint James the Great, whose relics were said to have been discovered there in the early 9th century. It is the destination of the Camino de Santiago (Way of St James), one of the most important pilgrimage routes in Christendom.

Architecture

The present church, begun in 1075 under Bishop Diego Peláez and King Alfonso VI, is a masterpiece of Romanesque architecture: a cruciform basilica with an ambulatory and radiating chapels designed to receive crowds of pilgrims. Master Mateo's Pórtico de la Gloria (1188), a sculptural programme of more than 200 figures, fills the western entrance. Later centuries added the Gothic cloister and, in the 18th century, the exuberant Baroque Obradoiro façade, flanked by the towers of the Bells and the Ratchet, which fronts the Praza do Obradoiro.

Pilgrimage and liturgy

Pilgrims traditionally embrace the statue of St James behind the high altar, visit the crypt, and attend the Pilgrim's Mass, at which the enormous Botafumeiro — a 53-kilogram thurible swung by eight men on ropes — is used on solemnities. Those who walk at least the final 100 kilometres may receive the Compostela certificate. In Holy Years, when the feast of St James (25 July) falls on a Sunday, the Holy Door is opened and pilgrim numbers swell; the cathedral and the old town were declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1985.
