RecQ helicase

RecQ helicases unwind DNA at replication forks and repair intermediates using energy from ATP hydrolysis.

RecQ helicase is a family of helicase enzymes important in genome maintenance. They are found in all kingdoms of life and function as ATP-dependent DNA helicases that unwind duplex DNA in the 3′ to 5′ direction. Members of the family are often described as "guardians of the genome" because they suppress inappropriate recombination, resolve stalled replication forks and DNA repair intermediates, and help maintain telomeres.

Family members

The family is named for the Escherichia coli RecQ protein, a component of the RecF recombination pathway. Budding and fission yeasts each have one or two RecQ homologues (Sgs1 and Rqh1 respectively). Humans have five: RECQL1, BLM, WRN, RECQL4 and RECQL5.

Functions

RecQ helicases act on a range of branched DNA substrates, including replication forks, Holliday junctions, D-loops and G-quadruplex structures. In partnership with topoisomerase III and scaffold proteins (the BTR complex in humans: BLM–TOP3A–RMI1–RMI2), they dissolve double Holliday junctions into non-crossover products, thereby preventing sister-chromatid exchanges and loss of heterozygosity. They also promote replication fork restart and the repair of DNA double-strand breaks by homologous recombination.

Clinical significance

Mutations in three human RecQ genes cause rare autosomal recessive disorders marked by genomic instability, cancer predisposition and features of premature ageing: mutations in BLM cause Bloom syndrome, in WRN cause Werner syndrome (adult progeria), and in RECQL4 cause Rothmund–Thomson syndrome, RAPADILINO and Baller–Gerold syndrome. These disorders demonstrate the family's essential role in suppressing the chromosomal rearrangements that lead to cancer.
