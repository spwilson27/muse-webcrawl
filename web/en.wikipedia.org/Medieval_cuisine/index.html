Medieval cuisine

Medieval cuisine includes foods, eating habits, and cooking methods of various European cultures during the Middle Ages, a period roughly spanning the 5th to the 15th century. During this period, diets and cooking changed less than they did in the early modern period that followed, when those changes helped lay the foundations for modern European cuisines.

Overview

Cereals remained the most important staple during the early Middle Ages as rice was introduced late, and the potato was only introduced in 1536, with a much later date for widespread consumption. Barley, oats, and rye were eaten by the poor, while wheat was generally more expensive. These were consumed as bread, porridge, gruel, and pasta by all of society's members. Cheese, fruits, and vegetables were important supplements to the cereal-based diet of the lower orders, while meat was more expensive and generally more prestigious. Game, a type of meat acquired from hunting, was common only on the tables of the nobility. The most prevalent butcher's meats were pork, chicken, and other domestic fowl; beef, which required greater investment in land, was less common.

Spices and seasonings

Spices were among the most luxurious products available in the Middle Ages, and the most common were black pepper, cinnamon, cumin, nutmeg, ginger, and cloves. They were imported from Asia and Africa and were extremely expensive. Herbs were also grown and used in cooking, including sage, parsley, and mint. Sauces were thickened with bread or almonds, and the taste of the wealthy palate favoured a combination of sweet and sour flavours.

Drinks

Wine was consumed widely, especially in southern Europe, while ale and beer were staples in the north; mead and cider were also drunk. Milk was mostly processed into butter and cheese. Because water quality was often poor, alcoholic drinks were a safer everyday choice.

Meals and etiquette

The main meal of the day was usually eaten around midday. Aristocratic households staged elaborate multi-course banquets with pageantry, while peasants ate simply from wooden bowls. Forks were little used in northern Europe until late in the period; food was eaten with spoons, knives, and fingers, and trenchers of stale bread served as plates.
