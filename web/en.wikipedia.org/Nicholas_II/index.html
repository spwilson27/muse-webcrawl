Nicholas II (Russian: Nikolai II; 18 May 1868 – 17 July 1918) was the last Emperor of Russia, ruling from 1894 until his abdication in 1917 during the February Revolution. His reign saw the transformation of Russia from one of the great powers into an economically and militarily struggling state, marked by the Russo-Japanese War, Bloody Sunday and the Revolution of 1905, and Russia's costly involvement in the First World War.

Nicholas was devoted to his wife Alexandra and their five children; the haemophilia of their son, the Tsarevich Alexei, brought the mystic Grigori Rasputin into the imperial circle, damaging the monarchy's prestige. After abdicating in March 1917, Nicholas and his family were held under house arrest, exiled to Tobolsk and then Yekaterinburg, where they were murdered by Bolshevik guards on the night of 16–17 July 1918.

Reign

His coronation in 1896 was marred by the Khodynka Tragedy, in which nearly 1,400 people were crushed in a stampede. Defeat by Japan in 1904–05 and the shooting of peaceful petitioners on Bloody Sunday (January 1905) triggered revolution, forcing Nicholas to issue the October Manifesto creating the elected Duma — though the Fundamental Laws of 1906 restored much of his autocratic power. Prime Minister Pyotr Stolypin's reforms were cut short by his assassination in 1911. In 1915 Nicholas took personal command of the army in the First World War, linking the dynasty to military failure; Alexandra and Rasputin's influence in Petrograd deepened the crisis until Rasputin's murder in December 1916 and Nicholas's abdication weeks later.

Death, canonisation, and legacy

The family's remains were recovered near Yekaterinburg and identified by DNA testing; most were reburied in St. Peter and Paul Cathedral, St. Petersburg, in 1998. In 2000 the Russian Orthodox Church canonised Nicholas and his family as passion bearers. Historians debate his character — personally dutiful but widely judged ill-suited to the demands of governing a modernising empire in crisis.
