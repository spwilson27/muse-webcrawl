Uncodified constitution

An uncodified constitution is a type of constitution in which the fundamental principles of government are not set out in a single authoritative written document. Instead, the constitution is found in a variety of sources, including statutes, court judgments, treatises, and long-established conventions and practices.

Examples

The best-known example is the constitution of the United Kingdom, which has evolved over centuries and comprises Acts of Parliament, common law, constitutional conventions, and authoritative works such as Magna Carta (1215), the Bill of Rights 1689, and the Acts of Union. New Zealand and Israel are also commonly cited as having uncodified constitutions; Israel's constitutional framework rests on its Basic Laws, and New Zealand's on statutes such as the Constitution Act 1986 together with conventions and the Treaty of Waitangi.

Characteristics

Uncodified constitutions are generally flexible: in the United Kingdom, Parliament can change constitutional arrangements by ordinary legislation, since no higher-order law entrenches them. Critics argue this offers less protection for rights than a codified, entrenched constitution, while defenders value its adaptability and evolutionary character.
