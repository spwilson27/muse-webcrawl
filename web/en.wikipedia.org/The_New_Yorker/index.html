The New Yorker

The New Yorker is an American magazine featuring journalism, commentary, criticism, essays, fiction, satire, cartoons, and poetry. Founded by Harold Ross and Jane Grant, its first issue appeared on 21 February 1925. Published by Condé Nast, it appears weekly for most of the year and is renowned for its illustrated covers, literary fiction, and rigorous fact-checking.

History

Harold Ross edited the magazine from its founding until his death in 1951, shaping its urbane voice; his successors were William Shawn (1952–1987), Robert Gottlieb, Tina Brown, and David Remnick. The first cover introduced the monocled dandy Eustace Tilley, who has reappeared on anniversary issues ever since. Under Shawn, the magazine published landmark long-form journalism, including John Hersey's "Hiroshima", which filled an entire issue in 1946, and Rachel Carson's Silent Spring in 1962.

Content and influence

Regular features include "The Talk of the Town", Shouts & Murmurs humour pieces, cultural criticism, and single-panel cartoons by artists such as Charles Addams, Saul Steinberg, and Roz Chast. Its fiction has featured writers from J. D. Salinger and John Updike to Alice Munro. The magazine has won numerous National Magazine Awards and Pulitzer Prizes and remains a benchmark for literary journalism.
