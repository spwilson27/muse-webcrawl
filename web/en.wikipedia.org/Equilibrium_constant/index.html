Equilibrium constant

The equilibrium constant of a chemical reaction is the value of its reaction quotient at chemical equilibrium, the state approached by a dynamic system after sufficient time has elapsed so that its composition shows no measurable tendency to change. For a reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the constant is K = ([C]c[D]d) / ([A]a[B]b), where the bracketed terms are activities (approximated by concentrations) at equilibrium.

Interpretation

A large K (≫ 1) means the equilibrium lies to the right — products predominate — while a small K (≪ 1) means reactants predominate. K depends on temperature but not on the initial amounts of reactants. It is connected to the standard Gibbs free energy change by ΔG° = −RT ln K, linking thermodynamics to the observable position of equilibrium.

Variants

Specialized constants include the acid dissociation constant Ka (and its negative logarithm pKa), the base dissociation constant Kb, the solubility product Ksp, formation constants for complexes, and for gas-phase reactions Kp, expressed in partial pressures. The temperature dependence of K is given by the van 't Hoff equation, and shifts in equilibrium under changed conditions are predicted qualitatively by Le Chatelier's principle.

History

The law of mass action, from which the equilibrium constant follows, was formulated by Cato Guldberg and Peter Waage in the 1860s.
