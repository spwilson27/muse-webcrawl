Sewn boat

A sewn boat under construction, its planks laced together with fibre cordage.

A sewn boat is a type of wooden vessel whose hull planks are fastened together by stitching — with rope, cord or other fibrous material passed through holes along the plank edges — rather than by metal nails or rivets. Sewn construction is ancient and widespread, appearing in traditions around the Indian Ocean, Southeast Asia, Oceania, Scandinavia and the Mediterranean.

Construction

Planks are typically edge-joined, caulked with fibre and resin or oil, and lashed to frames or to each other with cordage of coir (coconut fibre), palm fibre or similar materials. The resulting hull is lighter and more flexible than a nailed one, able to flex in surf and over reefs, and repairable with local materials. The flexibility comes at the cost of requiring periodic re-stitching and careful maintenance.

Historical examples

The Bronze Age Dover Boat and the Ferriby boats from Bronze Age Britain were sewn with yew withies. Ancient Egyptian vessels, including the Khufu ship buried beside the Great Pyramid, were assembled with rope lashings. In the Indian Ocean, sewn dhows such as the Omani beden and the belitung-type ships carried trade for centuries; the ninth-century Belitung wreck found in Indonesia was a sewn vessel. The lashed-lug boats of Southeast Asia and the Pacific, and early Norse craft preceding iron-riveted clinker construction, belong to the same broad family of techniques. Some traditions survive today, and reconstructions such as the Jewel of Muscat, a ninth-century-style sewn ship sailed from Oman to Singapore in 2010, have demonstrated the method's seaworthiness.
