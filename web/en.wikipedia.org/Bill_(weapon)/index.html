Bill (weapon)

TypePolearm
Place of originEngland
In serviceMedieval period – 17th century

The bill is a polearm weapon used by infantry in medieval Europe. Derived from the agricultural billhook, the military bill combined a hooked chopping blade with a spike and sometimes a projecting point, mounted on a long wooden shaft. It was a characteristic weapon of English armies from the medieval period into the 16th century.

Design

The bill's head typically featured a broad, curved cutting edge, a top spike for thrusting, and rearward projections or flukes that could be used to pull riders from horses or hook limbs and armour. English bills varied in form; the "black bill" and "brown bill" were distinguished by the finish of the metal.

Use in warfare

Bills were used alongside pikes and longbows. Massed billmen could deliver powerful chopping blows against armoured opponents, and the weapon was famously effective at battles such as Flodden in 1513. The bill remained in service with militias and in naval boarding actions into the 17th century.

Related weapons

The bill is related to other agricultural-tool-derived polearms including the glaive, halberd, and the Italian roncone.
