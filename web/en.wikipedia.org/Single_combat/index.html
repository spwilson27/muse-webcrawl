Champions deciding a battle by single combat, a recurrent theme in epic literature.

Single combat is a duel between two single combatants which takes place within the context of a larger battle or war, often between the champions of opposing armies. It is one of the oldest traditions in warfare and heroic literature, in which the fate of armies, kingdoms, or disputes might be decided by the outcome of a fight between two individuals, sparing mass bloodshed — at least in theory.

In ancient traditions

The Hebrew Bible describes the fight between David and the Philistine champion Goliath. In Homer's Iliad, duels between Paris and Menelaus and between Achilles and Hector structure the narrative. Roman legend celebrated the triple combat of the Horatii and Curiatii, and early Roman history preserves stories of champions such as Marcus Valerius Corvus. In the Irish epic Táin Bó Cúailnge, the hero Cú Chulainn defends Ulster by fighting a series of single combats at a ford.

Functions

Single combat served as spectacle and morale display, as a ritualized substitute for battle, and as a demonstration of divine favour. In practice, battles often proceeded regardless of the outcome. Ritual single combat was also institutionalised as trial by combat in medieval European law, and echoes survive in the duel of honour.

Later instances

The Byzantine and Ottoman worlds saw ceremonial duels between champions, and Japanese history records formal duels between samurai such as the famous 1612 encounter between Miyamoto Musashi and Sasaki Kojirō. By the era of gunpowder warfare, battlefield single combat had effectively disappeared, though it persisted in literature and later in film.
