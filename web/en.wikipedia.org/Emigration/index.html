Emigration

Emigration is the act of leaving a resident country or place of residence with the intent to settle elsewhere, permanently or for a long period. Conversely, immigration describes the movement of people into one country from another. A person who emigrants is called an emigrant, while the same person is an immigrant in their destination country. Emigration is one component of human migration, alongside immigration and internal migration.

Causes

People emigrate for many reasons, commonly described as "push" and "pull" factors. Push factors include poverty, unemployment, political persecution, war, natural disasters, and lack of opportunity. Pull factors include higher wages, better education, political stability, family reunification, and improved quality of life. Economic motives have historically been the most common driver, but refugees fleeing conflict and persecution form a significant share of modern emigration.

History

Large-scale emigration has occurred throughout history. The nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw mass emigration from Europe to the Americas, Australia, and other settler colonies, driven by population growth, industrialisation, famine (such as the Irish Potato Famine), and the availability of cheap steamship passage. Tens of millions left Europe between 1815 and 1914. After the Second World War, emigration patterns shifted, with significant flows from former colonies to Europe and from developing to developed countries worldwide.

Effects

Emigration affects both sending and receiving societies. Sending countries may lose skilled workers — the so-called "brain drain" — but also benefit from remittances sent home by emigrants, which in many countries exceed foreign aid. Return migration and diaspora networks can transfer skills, capital, and ideas back to countries of origin. Emigrants themselves often face challenges of cultural adaptation, language acquisition, and legal status in their new homes.

Legal aspects

The right to leave any country, including one's own, is recognised in Article 13 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. However, states retain broad authority to control entry, so emigrants must generally satisfy the immigration law of their destination. Some states have historically restricted emigration, imposing exit visas or penalties on those who leave.
