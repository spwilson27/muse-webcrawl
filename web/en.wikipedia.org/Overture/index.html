Overture

An overture (from French ouverture, "opening") is instrumental music played at the beginning of an opera, oratorio, ballet, play or other extended work. In the 19th century the term also came to denote an independent, single-movement orchestral piece, the concert overture.

History

Early operas opened with brief sinfonias. In the 1650s Jean-Baptiste Lully standardised the French overture: a slow, dotted-rhythm opening, a faster fugal section, and a return of the slow music. The Italian overture or sinfonia favoured a fast–slow–fast scheme and evolved toward the classical symphony. Composers such as Gluck and Mozart made the overture foreshadow the drama's mood and themes.

Forms

Many Romantic opera overtures are medleys of the opera's principal melodies; Rossini's overtures (to The Barber of Seville, William Tell) became concert favourites in their own right. The independent concert overture includes Mendelssohn's The Hebrides, Brahms's Academic Festival and Tragic overtures, and Tchaikovsky's 1812 Overture and Romeo and Juliet. In musical theatre the overture traditionally previews the show's songs.
