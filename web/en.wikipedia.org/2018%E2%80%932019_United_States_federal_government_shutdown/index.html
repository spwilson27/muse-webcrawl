2018–2019 United States federal government shutdown

The 2018–2019 United States federal government shutdown lasted from 22 December 2018 to 25 January 2019 — 35 days, the longest shutdown in American history, surpassing the 21-day shutdown of 1995–96. It arose from a dispute over President Donald Trump's demand for $5.7 billion in federal funds for a wall on the Mexico–United States border, which the Democratic-controlled House of Representatives refused to appropriate. Roughly 800,000 federal employees were affected, about 380,000 of them furloughed and the rest working without pay.

TSA officers were among the essential employees who worked without pay.

Background

Trump had campaigned on building a border wall. In December 2018, after first signalling he would accept a Senate-passed funding bill without wall money, he reversed course under pressure from conservative commentators and House hardliners, announcing on television that he would be "proud to shut down the government for border security". Funding for nine departments, including Homeland Security, Justice, and the Interior, lapsed at midnight on 22 December.

Effects

Federal employees missed two paychecks; many sought loans, charity food banks, and second jobs. Transportation Security Administration officers calling in sick strained airport security, and on 25 January staffing shortages caused delays and a ground stop at New York's LaGuardia Airport. National parks overflowed with trash and suffered vandalism; tax-refund processing, food inspections, and Coast Guard pay were disrupted. The Congressional Budget Office estimated the shutdown cost the economy about $11 billion, of which roughly $3 billion was never recovered.

Resolution

On 25 January 2019 Trump agreed to a three-week continuing resolution without wall funding, reopening the government while a bipartisan conference committee negotiated border security funding. The eventual compromise appropriated $1.375 billion for border fencing — far less than demanded — and Trump simultaneously declared a national emergency to redirect other funds to wall construction, provoking legal challenges that continued for years. Congress subsequently passed the Government Employee Fair Treatment Act, guaranteeing retroactive pay after future shutdowns.
