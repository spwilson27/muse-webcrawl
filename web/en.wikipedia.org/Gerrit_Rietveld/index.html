Gerrit Thomas Rietveld (Dutch pronunciation: [ˈɣɛrɪt ˈtoːmɑs ˈritfɛlt]; 24 June 1888 – 25 June 1964) was a Dutch furniture designer and architect, one of the principal members of the Dutch artistic movement De Stijl. He is famous for the Red and Blue Chair and for the Rietveld Schröder House, which is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Early life and furniture

Rietveld was born in Utrecht in 1888, the son of a joiner. He left school at eleven to work in his father's workshop and later apprenticed as a jeweller's draughtsman and studied architecture in evening classes. In 1917 he opened his own furniture workshop in Utrecht. There, in 1918, he designed the chair later known as the Red and Blue Chair: built from simple beech laths and plywood panels, it was painted around 1923 in De Stijl's primary colours of red, blue, and yellow with black — becoming one of the most famous chairs of the 20th century and a three-dimensional manifesto of De Stijl principles.

Rietveld joined De Stijl in 1919, the year the movement's members, including Theo van Doesburg and Piet Mondrian, were redefining art as an arrangement of elementary forms and primary colours.

Rietveld Schröder House

In 1924, Truus Schröder-Schräder, a Utrecht widow with whom Rietveld collaborated closely, commissioned him to design a house on the edge of the city. The Rietveld Schröder House is generally regarded as the only building fully realised according to De Stijl principles: its white planes, primary-coloured linear elements, and corner windows dissolve the boundary between inside and outside, while the upper floor can be transformed from an open space into separate rooms by sliding and revolving partitions. Schröder lived there until her death in 1985; the house is now a museum and was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2000.

Later work

Rietveld was a founding member of the Congrès Internationaux d'Architecture Moderne (CIAM) in 1928. His later designs moved toward inexpensive, industrially produced furniture and social housing, including the Zig-Zag Chair (1932–34), the crate furniture designed during the Depression, and row houses in Utrecht and Vienna. After the Second World War his work was widely honoured; major commissions included the Dutch pavilion at the Venice Biennale (1954) and the Sonsbeek Pavilion in Arnhem (1955). He died in Utrecht in 1964, the day after his 76th birthday. The academy of art in Amsterdam, the Gerrit Rietveld Academie, bears his name.
