English cuisine

English cuisine encompasses the cooking styles, traditions, and recipes associated with England. It has distinctive attributes of its own, but also shares much with wider British cuisine, partly thanks to the importation of ingredients and ideas from the Americas, China, and India during the time of the British Empire and as a result of post-war immigration.

Traditional dishes

Well-known English dishes include the full breakfast (eggs, bacon, sausage, beans, toast, and often black pudding, mushrooms, and tomatoes), fish and chips, the Sunday roast of meat with Yorkshire pudding and vegetables, shepherd's pie and cottage pie, bangers and mash, steak and kidney pie, and the Cornish pasty. Afternoon tea with sandwiches, scones with clotted cream and jam, and cakes is a famous English institution associated with the 19th century. Puddings such as sticky toffee pudding, spotted dick, and trifle, often served with custard, are characteristic desserts.

History

English cooking was documented early in works such as The Forme of Cury (c. 1390), compiled for the court of Richard II, and later in Hannah Glasse's The Art of Cookery Made Plain and Easy (1747) and Mrs Beeton's Book of Household Management (1861). Industrialization, rationing during and after the Second World War, and the rise of convenience foods gave English food an international reputation for plainness in the later 20th century, though this has been countered by a revival of interest in regional and seasonal cooking, farmers' markets, and gastropubs.

Regional specialities and influences

Regional products include Cheddar, Stilton, and Wensleydale cheeses, Melton Mowbray pork pies, Bakewell pudding, and Cumberland sausage. Curry, introduced via Britain's connections with India, has become so embedded that dishes such as chicken tikka masala are sometimes described as British national dishes, and cities such as Birmingham are known for Balti cuisine.
