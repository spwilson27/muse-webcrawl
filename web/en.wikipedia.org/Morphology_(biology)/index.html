Morphology (biology)

[Image: Comparative anatomy of mammal limbs]
FieldBiology
Coined byJohann Wolfgang von Goethe (1790s)
Related fieldsAnatomy, taxonomy, evolutionary biology

Morphology is the branch of biology that deals with the form and structure of organisms and their specific structural features. It includes aspects of outward appearance (shape, structure, colour, pattern, size) — external morphology, or eidonomy — as well as the form and structure of internal parts such as bones and organs — internal morphology, or anatomy. The term was coined by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe in the 1790s and developed independently by Karl Friedrich Burdach around 1800.

Divisions

Morphology is divided into comparative morphology, which analyses patterns of structure across organisms and underlies taxonomy and phylogenetics; functional morphology, which studies the relationship between structure and function; and experimental morphology, which investigates the external factors affecting form during development.

Morphology and evolution

Homologous structures — such as the forelimbs of mammals, alike in plan despite differing in function — provide key evidence of common descent, whereas analogous structures reveal convergent evolution. Morphological data long formed the main basis for classifying organisms and reconstructing phylogenies; today they are combined with molecular data in systematics.

Methods

Traditional morphology relied on dissection and observation. Modern methods include morphometrics (quantitative analysis of shape), computed tomography, electron microscopy, and developmental genetics, which has revealed the genetic toolkit — such as Hox genes — governing body plans across animals.
