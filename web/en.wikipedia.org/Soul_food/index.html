Soul food

OriginSouthern United States; African-American cuisine
Term popularised1960s, during the civil rights and Black Pride movements
Staple dishesFried chicken, collard greens, black-eyed peas, cornbread, macaroni and cheese, chitterlings

Soul food is the ethnic cuisine of African Americans in the Southern United States. It developed from the foodways of enslaved Africans, who combined West African ingredients and techniques — okra, black-eyed peas, rice, one-pot cooking — with foods available in the Americas, including corn, pork and leafy greens, making nourishing meals from the meagre rations and discarded cuts allotted under slavery.

History

Enslaved cooks transformed ingredients such as chitterlings (pig intestines), hog jowls, ham hocks and oxtails into flavourful dishes, and seasoned greens with smoked meats. After emancipation and during the Great Migration, the cuisine spread to Northern and Western cities, where restaurants became community institutions. The term "soul food" gained currency in the 1960s, when "soul" was a proud marker of Black culture, and cookbooks such as The Ebony Cookbook and works by Edna Lewis celebrated and refined the tradition.

Typical dishes and discussion

Classic dishes include fried chicken, smothered pork chops, catfish, collard, mustard and turnip greens, candied yams, hushpuppies, grits, red beans and rice, peach cobbler and sweet tea. Because traditional soul food can be high in fat, salt and calories, contemporary chefs have developed lighter interpretations, and the cuisine's contributions to American food are increasingly recognised in fine dining.
