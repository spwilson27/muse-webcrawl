The cosmic microwave background (CMB, also cosmic microwave background radiation, CMBR) is thermal radiation left over from the time of recombination in the hot early universe. It fills the sky nearly uniformly with an almost perfect blackbody spectrum at a temperature of 2.725 kelvin, and it is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the Big Bang model.

The Planck satellite's map of CMB temperature anisotropies across the whole sky.

Origin

About 380,000 years after the Big Bang, the universe cooled enough (to roughly 3,000 K) for protons and electrons to combine into neutral hydrogen at recombination. Photons, no longer constantly scattered by free electrons, decoupled and travelled freely through space. Expanding space has since stretched their wavelengths by a factor of about 1,100, shifting their light into the microwave band and cooling the radiation to 2.725 K. The CMB is thus a snapshot of the universe's infancy — the oldest light that can be observed.

Discovery

The radiation was predicted in 1948 by Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman working with George Gamow. In 1964 Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson at Bell Labs stumbled on a persistent excess noise in their horn antenna at Holmdel, New Jersey, which a Princeton group under Robert Dicke identified as the predicted relic radiation; Penzias and Wilson received the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Anisotropies and cosmology

The CMB is not perfectly smooth: tiny temperature fluctuations of about one part in 100,000, first mapped by the COBE satellite in 1992 and later measured precisely by WMAP and Planck, are the seeds from which galaxies grew under gravity. Their statistical pattern determines the universe's age (13.8 billion years), geometry (spatially flat), and composition (about 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter, 68% dark energy). The CMB is also polarised, and its faint B-mode patterns are a target in the search for primordial gravitational waves from cosmic inflation.
