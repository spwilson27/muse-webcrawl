The House of Orange-Nassau is the reigning royal house of the Kingdom of the Netherlands. It descends from William I of Orange ("William the Silent"), who united the German county of Nassau with the southern French principality of Orange — inherited in 1544 from his cousin René of Chalon — and went on to lead the Dutch Revolt against Spanish rule.

Stadtholders

For two centuries the princes of Orange served, on and off, as stadtholders (chief executives and military commanders) of the Dutch Republic. William the Silent was assassinated in 1584; his sons Maurice and Frederick Henry built the Republic into a great power. After stadtholderless periods under the regent regime of Johan de Witt, William III of Orange (1650–1702) restored the office in the crisis year 1672 and in 1689 also became King of England, Scotland and Ireland in the Glorious Revolution with his wife Mary II.

Monarchy

After the Napoleonic era the Netherlands became a kingdom: William I was proclaimed sovereign prince in 1813 and King of the Netherlands (including Belgium until 1830) in 1815. His descendants William II and William III reigned as constitutional monarchs. From 1890 to 2013 the throne passed through three queens — Wilhelmina, Juliana and Beatrix — before Willem-Alexander succeeded on 30 April 2013. The Peace of Utrecht (1713) had earlier settled the Orange succession disputes following William III's death.

Legacy

The colour orange — from the principality's name — became the Dutch national colour, worn by sports fans and used by the national football team. The House's name survives in places worldwide, from the Orange Free State in South Africa to Orange County, California and New York.
