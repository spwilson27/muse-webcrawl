Laser printing is an electrostatic digital printing process that produces high-quality text and graphics by passing a laser beam back and forth over a negatively charged photoreceptor drum to define a differentially charged image. It is the dominant technology for office printing, valued for speed, precision, and low cost per page.

Process

The process, adapted from xerography, proceeds in stages: the drum is uniformly charged; a laser (or LED array) discharges the points corresponding to the image; fine dry toner particles are attracted to the charged image areas; the toner is transferred to paper; and heat and pressure in the fuser unit melt the toner permanently into the sheet. Colour laser printers repeat the cycle for cyan, magenta, yellow, and black toners.

History

Electrophotography was invented by Chester Carlson in 1938 and commercialized in photocopiers by Xerox. At Xerox PARC in the late 1960s and early 1970s, Gary Starkweather combined the copier with a laser scanner, producing experimental printers such as EARS and, commercially, the Xerox 9700 (1977). The Hewlett-Packard LaserJet of 1984, built around a Canon engine, brought laser printing to the desktop, and with the Apple LaserWriter of 1985 it helped spark the desktop publishing revolution.

Characteristics

Laser printers typically offer resolutions of 600–2400 dpi and are faster and more economical for volume text printing than inkjet printers, though inkjets have traditionally had advantages in photographic colour work. Toner cartridges, maintenance, and the identification dots ("printer steganography") embedded by colour machines have all attracted comment and study.
