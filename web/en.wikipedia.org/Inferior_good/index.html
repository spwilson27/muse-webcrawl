Inferior good

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In economics, an inferior good is a good whose demand decreases when consumer income rises, and increases when income falls — the opposite of a normal good. The term describes the relationship between income and demand, not necessarily the quality of the product, though inferior goods are often cheaper, lower-quality alternatives that consumers abandon as they become wealthier.

Examples

Commonly cited examples include instant noodles, generic or store-brand groceries, margarine (relative to butter), bus travel (relative to car ownership), and second-hand goods. A good can be inferior for some consumers and normal for others; classification depends on income range and available substitutes.

Income effect

When income rises, the income elasticity of demand for an inferior good is negative: consumers switch expenditure toward preferred substitutes. Demand curves for inferior goods therefore shift inward as incomes grow. Retailers specialising in discount goods often see sales surge during recessions, when demand for inferior goods rises.

Giffen goods

A Giffen good is a special, rare type of inferior good whose demand rises as its price rises, violating the ordinary law of demand: the income effect of a price increase on a staple with no close substitutes outweighs the substitution effect. Historically suggested examples include bread and rice among very poor households.
