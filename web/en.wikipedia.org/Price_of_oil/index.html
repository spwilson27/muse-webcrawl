Price of oil

The price of oil, or oil price, generally refers to the spot price of a barrel of benchmark crude oil — a reference price for buyers and sellers of crude oil such as West Texas Intermediate (WTI), Brent Crude, OPEC Reference Basket, Dubai Crude, and Urals oil. Oil prices are determined by global supply and demand, futures markets, geopolitical events, and the production decisions of major exporters, especially OPEC and its allies (OPEC+).

Long-run crude oil prices, with major shocks in 1973, 1979, 2008, and 2020.

History

Oil prices were relatively stable under the posted-price system dominated by the major oil companies until the early 1970s. The 1973 oil crisis, triggered by an OPEC embargo during the Yom Kippur War, quadrupled prices, and the 1979 energy crisis following the Iranian Revolution pushed them higher still. Prices collapsed in the mid-1980s, spiked during the 1990–91 Gulf War, and climbed through the 2000s to a peak near $147 per barrel in July 2008 before crashing in the financial crisis. A shale-driven supply glut caused another collapse in 2014–2016. In April 2020, pandemic demand destruction briefly drove WTI futures into negative territory. Prices surged again in 2022 following Russia's invasion of Ukraine.

Determinants

Short-run prices respond to inventories, refinery outages, weather, speculation, and geopolitical risk, while long-run prices reflect production costs, reserve depletion, technological change (such as hydraulic fracturing), and the pace of the energy transition. Because demand for oil is price-inelastic in the short run, small supply disruptions can produce large price movements.

Effects

Oil price shocks have been linked to recessions in importing countries, inflation, and large international wealth transfers between exporters and importers. Conversely, low prices strain the budgets of oil-exporting states.
