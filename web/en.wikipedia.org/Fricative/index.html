Fricative

Fricatives are consonants produced by forcing air through a narrow channel made by placing two articulators close together, creating turbulent airflow. Examples in English include /f/, /v/, /s/, /z/, /ʃ/ (as in "ship"), /ʒ/ (as in "measure"), /θ/ (as in "thin"), /ð/ (as in "this"), and /h/.

Types

Fricatives are classified by their place of articulation: bilabial, labiodental, dental, alveolar, postalveolar, retroflex, palatal, velar, uvular, pharyngeal, and glottal. They may be voiced or voiceless. The strongly hissing alveolar and postalveolar fricatives are often grouped as sibilants, which have distinctive high-amplitude, high-frequency acoustics. Grooved tongue shape channels air toward the teeth in sibilants, while non-sibilants such as /f/ and /θ/ are quieter and more diffuse.

Occurrence

Fricatives are among the most common consonant types in the world's languages, though some languages lack them entirely; many Australian Aboriginal languages traditionally have no fricatives. Rotokas, spoken in Papua New Guinea, is also noted for lacking fricatives. In the International Phonetic Alphabet, fricatives occupy dedicated rows of the consonant chart, arranged from the front of the mouth to the glottis.
