Hydroboration is a chemical reaction in which a hydrogen–boron bond is added across a carbon–carbon or carbon–heteroatom double or triple bond. The reaction produces organoborane compounds that are versatile intermediates in organic synthesis. Hydroboration was systematically developed by Herbert C. Brown, who received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1979 in part for this work.

Hydroboration–oxidation

In the widely used hydroboration–oxidation reaction, an alkene reacts with borane (usually as BH3·THF) to give a trialkylborane, which is then oxidized with alkaline hydrogen peroxide to an alcohol. The overall transformation is the syn addition of water across the double bond with anti-Markovnikov regiochemistry: the hydroxyl group ends up on the less substituted carbon atom. This complements acid-catalysed hydration, which follows Markovnikov's rule.

Mechanism

The addition is concerted, proceeding through a four-membered cyclic transition state in which the boron and hydrogen add to the same face of the alkene (syn addition). The boron atom, being the larger and more electrophilic partner, adds preferentially to the less sterically hindered carbon, which determines the regiochemistry of the final alcohol.

Reagents

Bulky dialkylboranes such as disiamylborane (bis(1,2-dimethylpropyl)borane) and 9-borabicyclo[3.3.1]nonane (9-BBN) give enhanced regioselectivity with terminal alkenes and allow selective monohydroboration of dienes and alkynes. Hydroboration of alkynes with hindered boranes gives vinylboranes, which can be protonated to give cis-alkenes or used in cross-coupling reactions such as the Suzuki–Miyaura coupling.
