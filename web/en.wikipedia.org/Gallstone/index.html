Gallstone

A gallstone is a stone formed within the gallbladder or bile ducts from concretions of bile components. The condition is called cholelithiasis. Most gallstones are composed mainly of cholesterol; others are pigment stones of bilirubin. Gallstones are very common — affecting roughly 10–15% of adults in Western countries — and most cause no symptoms, but they can produce severe pain (biliary colic) and complications when they block a duct.

Signs and symptoms

Stones that stay in the gallbladder are usually silent. If a stone lodges in the cystic duct, the result is biliary colic: intense pain in the right upper abdomen or epigastrium, often after a fatty meal, radiating to the back or right shoulder. Blockage can progress to cholecystitis, while stones in the common bile duct cause obstructive jaundice, cholangitis or gallstone pancreatitis.

Causes and diagnosis

Cholesterol stones form when bile contains more cholesterol than its bile salts and lecithin can dissolve, promoted by gallbladder stasis. Risk is classically summarized as the "four Fs" — female, forty, fertile, fat — though genetics, diet, haemolysis and certain drugs also contribute. Diagnosis is usually by ultrasound, which shows stones with high accuracy.

Treatment

Asymptomatic stones generally need no treatment. For symptomatic disease, removal of the gallbladder (cholecystectomy), usually laparoscopic, is definitive. Stones in the bile duct may be extracted endoscopically by ERCP. Dissolution therapy with ursodeoxycholic acid is an option for small cholesterol stones in patients unfit for surgery.
