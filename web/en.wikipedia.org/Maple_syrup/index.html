Maple syrup is a syrup made from the sap of maple trees, principally the sugar maple (Acer saccharum), red maple, and black maple. It is produced almost exclusively in northeastern North America, where cold winters and thawing springs create the freeze–thaw cycles that drive sap flow. Canada, and especially the province of Quebec, produces the large majority of the world's supply; Vermont is the largest producing U.S. state.

Production

In late winter and early spring, producers drill tapholes into mature maples and collect the clear, slightly sweet sap (about 2% sugar) in buckets or through networks of plastic tubing to a sugar house. The sap is concentrated by reverse osmosis and boiling in evaporators until it reaches the legal density of about 66% sugar — roughly 40 litres of sap yield one litre of syrup. Indigenous peoples of the northeastern woodlands originated the practice, teaching it to European settlers, who introduced metal kettles and later flat-pan evaporators.

Grades and uses

Canada and the United States harmonized grading in 2015 into classes from "Golden / Delicate Taste" through "Very Dark / Strong Taste"; earlier-run syrup is lighter, and flavour intensifies through the season. Maple syrup is used on pancakes, waffles, and French toast, and as an ingredient in baking and confectionery, including maple sugar, maple butter, and taffy poured on snow (tire d'érable).

Economics

Quebec's producers market most syrup through a federation that operates a strategic reserve — famously the target of the "Great Canadian Maple Syrup Heist" of 2011–2012, in which some 3,000 tonnes of syrup worth about C$18 million were stolen. Imitation "pancake syrups" flavoured with corn syrup are legally distinguished from pure maple syrup in both countries.
