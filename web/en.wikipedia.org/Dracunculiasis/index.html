Dracunculiasis

Extracting a Guinea worm with a stick.

Dracunculiasis, also called Guinea-worm disease, is a parasitic infection caused by the nematode Dracunculus medinensis. People are infected by drinking water containing copepods (water fleas) carrying the worm's larvae. About a year later the mature female worm, up to a metre long, emerges through a painful skin blister, usually on the leg, and sufferers often immerse the limb in water, releasing larvae and continuing the cycle.

Eradication

There is no drug or vaccine; treatment consists of slowly winding the emerging worm around a stick over days or weeks, a technique depicted since antiquity and possibly the origin of the rod of Asclepius symbol. Prevention relies on filtering drinking water, treating water sources, and health education. An eradication campaign led by the Carter Center since 1986 has reduced cases from an estimated 3.5 million per year in 20 countries to only a handful of human cases annually, making dracunculiasis a candidate to become the second human disease eradicated after smallpox, though infections in dogs in Chad have complicated the endgame.
