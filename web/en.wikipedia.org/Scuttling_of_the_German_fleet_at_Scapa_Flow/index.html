Scuttling of the German fleet at Scapa Flow

The Imperial German Navy's High Seas Fleet was scuttled by its own crews at the British naval base of Scapa Flow in the Orkney Islands of Scotland on 21 June 1919. The scuttling was ordered by the German commander, Rear Admiral Ludwig von Reuter, who believed — wrongly — that the armistice ending the First World War had expired and that the British were about to seize the ships.

Background

Under the terms of the Armistice of 11 November 1918, the bulk of the High Seas Fleet — 74 ships, including 11 battleships and 5 battlecruisers — was interned at Scapa Flow pending a decision on its fate in the peace negotiations at Versailles. The ships were manned by skeleton German crews while Allied negotiators disputed whether they should be surrendered, divided among the victors, or sunk.

The scuttling

On the morning of 21 June 1919, with the British guard force largely away on exercises, von Reuter sent a pre-arranged signal and the crews opened seacocks and watertight doors. Over the following hours 52 of the 74 interned ships sank; British forces managed to beach or save the remainder. Nine German sailors were shot and killed by the British while abandoning ship — among the last deaths of the First World War.

Aftermath

The Allies were outraged but also privately relieved, since the sinking removed the problem of dividing the fleet. Germany was required to hand over additional ships and material in compensation. Most of the wrecks were raised and broken up in one of the largest marine salvage operations ever undertaken during the 1920s and 1930s; seven wrecks remain on the seabed and are popular with divers. Steel from the wrecks, formed before atmospheric nuclear testing, is prized as low-background steel for sensitive scientific instruments.
