Bertolt Brecht

Eugen Berthold Friedrich Brecht (10 February 1898 – 14 August 1956), known professionally as Bertolt Brecht, was a German theatre practitioner, playwright and poet. He is best known as the founder of "epic theatre", a form that used the Verfremdungseffekt ("alienation effect") to encourage audiences to think critically rather than identify emotionally with characters.

Career

Brecht rose to prominence in the Weimar Republic with plays such as Baal and the hugely successful The Threepenny Opera (1928), written with composer Kurt Weill. A committed Marxist, he fled Nazi Germany in 1933 and spent years in exile in Scandinavia and the United States, writing many of his greatest plays, including Mother Courage and Her Children, The Life of Galileo, The Good Person of Szechwan and The Caucasian Chalk Circle.

Berliner Ensemble

After the Second World War Brecht settled in East Berlin, where in 1949 he founded the Berliner Ensemble theatre company with his wife, Helene Weigel. His productions there defined his theories in practice and toured internationally to great acclaim.

Legacy

Brecht is widely regarded as one of the most influential theatre figures of the twentieth century, and his works continue to be performed worldwide.
