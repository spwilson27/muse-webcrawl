Ironclad warship

An ironclad is a steam-propelled warship protected by iron or steel armor plates, constructed during the mid-19th century as a response to the development of explosive shells. The first seagoing ironclad, the French Gloire (1859), and Britain's HMS Warrior (1860) rendered wooden warships obsolete and launched a naval arms race.

Origins

Armored floating batteries proved themselves in the Crimean War at the Battle of Kinburn (1855), where French batteries withstood Russian fire while destroying the fort. Combined with the devastation of wooden ships by explosive shells at Sinop (1853), this convinced navies that armor was essential.

Hampton Roads

The most famous ironclad action took place on 8–9 March 1862 during the American Civil War, when the Confederate CSS Virginia sank two Union wooden warships before dueling the Union's USS Monitor — the first battle between ironclads — to a draw. Monitor-type vessels, with revolving gun turrets, and casemate ironclads like Virginia defined the era's designs.

Development

Designs proliferated: broadside ironclads, central-battery ships, turret ships, and coastal monitors. Armor grew thicker — from 4.5 inches of wrought iron to compound and steel armor — while rifled muzzle- and breech-loading guns grew in caliber. By the 1890s the all-steel, uniformly armed "pre-dreadnought" battleship had replaced the heterogeneous ironclad fleets.
