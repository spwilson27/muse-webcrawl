Dissolution of Czechoslovakia

Date1 January 1993
LocationCzechoslovakia
ResultCzechoslovakia split into the Czech Republic and Slovakia

The dissolution of Czechoslovakia (Czech: Rozdělení Československa; Slovak: Rozdelenie Česko-Slovenska), which took effect on 1 January 1993, was the self-determined partition of the federal republic of Czechoslovakia into the independent countries of the Czech Republic and Slovakia. Although the events are sometimes called the Velvet Divorce, by analogy with the peaceful Velvet Revolution of 1989, the split was decided by political elites without a referendum.

Background

Czechoslovakia was created in 1918 from the former lands of Austria-Hungary. After the Second World War it came under communist rule; the federation of Czech and Slovak socialist republics was formalised in 1969. Following the Velvet Revolution, disputes emerged over the name of the state (the "Hyphen War"), the distribution of federal powers, and the pace of economic reform.

Negotiations and split

After elections in June 1992, Czech premier Václav Klaus of the Civic Democratic Party and Slovak premier Vladimír Mečiar of the Movement for a Democratic Slovakia negotiated the division. On 25 November 1992 the Federal Assembly passed the constitutional act ending the federation. Assets were divided roughly two to one in proportion to population. On 1 January 1993 the two republics became independent states, both admitted to the United Nations as new members, and both later joined the European Union in 2004.

Legacy

The dissolution is widely regarded as a model of peaceful state partition. Relations between the two countries remain exceptionally close, with citizens retaining broad mutual rights. Public opinion polling at the time showed that majorities in both republics would have preferred to preserve a common state, a fact often cited by historians.
