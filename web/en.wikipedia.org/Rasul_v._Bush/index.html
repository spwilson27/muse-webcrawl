Rasul v. Bush, 542 U.S. 466 (2004), was a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States holding that the federal court system has jurisdiction to hear petitions for habeas corpus brought by non-citizen detainees held at the Guantanamo Bay detention camp in Cuba. The case was brought on behalf of detainees including Shafiq Rasul, a British citizen, and was consolidated with Al Odah v. United States.

Background

Following the September 11 attacks and the invasion of Afghanistan, the United States transferred hundreds of captured men to its naval base at Guantánamo Bay, designating them "enemy combatants". The government argued that because the base lay outside the sovereign territory of the United States, federal courts lacked jurisdiction to review the detentions. Lower courts accepted that argument, relying on Johnson v. Eisentrager (1950).

Decision

Writing for a 6–3 majority, Justice John Paul Stevens held that the federal habeas statute, 28 U.S.C. § 2241, conferred jurisdiction because the United States exercises "complete jurisdiction and control" over Guantánamo Bay under its lease with Cuba. The Court distinguished Eisentrager and concluded that the detainees were entitled to invoke the courts to challenge the legality of their detention. Justice Antonin Scalia dissented.

Subsequent developments

Congress responded with the Detainee Treatment Act of 2005 and the Military Commissions Act of 2006, which attempted to strip habeas jurisdiction; the Court later held in Boumediene v. Bush (2008) that detainees retain a constitutional habeas privilege. The Rasul petitioners themselves were repatriated to the United Kingdom in 2004 and released without charge.
