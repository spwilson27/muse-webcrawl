An antigen is a molecule, molecular structure, or foreign substance that can be bound by an antigen-specific antibody or B-cell antigen receptor; the term originally derives from "antibody generator". Antigens may be proteins, polysaccharides, lipids, or nucleic acids, on pathogens, allergens, tumour cells, or — in autoimmunity — the body's own tissues.

Epitopes and recognition

Antibodies bind discrete regions of an antigen called epitopes (antigenic determinants), which may be linear sequences or conformational structures. B cells recognise native antigen directly, whereas T cells recognise short peptide fragments presented on cell surfaces by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules — class I MHC presents peptides from intracellular proteins and class II MHC presents peptides from extracellular material taken up by antigen-presenting cells.

Types

Exogenous antigens enter the body from outside (e.g. microbes, pollen); endogenous antigens are generated within cells, including viral proteins in infected cells and tumour antigens. Autoantigens are normal self proteins targeted in autoimmune disease. Small molecules called haptens become antigenic only when attached to a carrier protein. Superantigens, produced by some bacteria and viruses, cross-link MHC and T-cell receptors and provoke massive, non-specific T-cell activation.

Uses

Antigens are the basis of vaccines, which present pathogen antigens to prime immune memory, and of diagnostic antigen tests that detect pathogen molecules directly (e.g. rapid antigen tests for influenza and SARS-CoV-2).
