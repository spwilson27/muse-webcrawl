Scottish poetry

Scottish poetry is poetry written by Scottish poets in English, Scots, and Scottish Gaelic. Scotland has a poetic tradition reaching back to the early Middle Ages, in the Brythonic, Gaelic, and later Scots and English languages.

Early poetry

The earliest surviving Scottish poetry includes Brythonic works such as the Gododdin attributed to Aneirin, and Gaelic verse composed in the classical bardic tradition. In the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries the "makars" — William Dunbar, Robert Henryson, Gavin Douglas, and others — produced a flourishing court poetry in Middle Scots, including Henryson's Testament of Cresseid and Douglas's Eneados, the first complete translation of Virgil's Aeneid into a northern European vernacular.

Eighteenth century

After the Union of 1707, Allan Ramsay and Robert Fergusson revived vernacular Scots verse, paving the way for Robert Burns (1759–1796), whose songs and poems — "Auld Lang Syne", "Tam o' Shanter", "To a Mouse" — made him Scotland's national poet and a figure of worldwide fame.

Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

The nineteenth century saw popular poets such as William McGonagall alongside more accomplished voices. In the twentieth century the Scottish Renaissance under Hugh MacDiarmid transformed Scots poetry, while Sorley MacLean revolutionised Gaelic verse. Post-war poets including Edwin Morgan, Scotland's first national poet (Makar), Liz Lochhead, and Jackie Kay carried Scottish poetry to new audiences.
