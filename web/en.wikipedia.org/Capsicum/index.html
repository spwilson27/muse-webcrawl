Capsicum (/ˈkæpsɪkəm/) is a genus of flowering plants in the nightshade family Solanaceae, native to the Americas. Its species are cultivated worldwide for their fruit, commonly called peppers—including chili peppers, bell peppers, and many others. The fruit's pungency, when present, comes from capsaicinoids, principally capsaicin.

Domesticated species

Five species were domesticated in pre-Columbian times: Capsicum annuum (bell peppers, jalapeños, cayenne), C. frutescens (tabasco, bird's eye), C. chinense (habanero, Scotch bonnet, and the world's hottest peppers), C. baccatum (ají peppers of South America), and C. pubescens (rocoto, with distinctive black seeds). Archaeological evidence indicates cultivation in the Americas for at least 6,000 years, and peppers spread worldwide rapidly after the Columbian exchange, transforming cuisines from Hungary (paprika) to Korea (gochugaru) and Sichuan.

Cultivation and use

Peppers are grown as annuals in temperate climates and perennials in the tropics. Heat is measured in Scoville heat units: bell peppers are 0 SHU, jalapeños roughly 2,500–8,000 SHU, habaneros up to 350,000 SHU, and bred varieties such as the Carolina Reaper exceed 1.6 million SHU. Peppers are eaten fresh, dried, smoked (chipotle), ground into spices, and fermented into hot sauces; they are also rich in vitamin C and vitamin A.
