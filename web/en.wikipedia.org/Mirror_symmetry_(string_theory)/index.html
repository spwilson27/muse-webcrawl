Mirror symmetry (string theory)

In algebraic geometry and theoretical physics, mirror symmetry is a relationship between pairs of Calabi–Yau manifolds. Two geometrically distinct Calabi–Yau manifolds are said to be mirror partners if they give rise to identical physics when used as the extra-dimensional geometry in a string theory compactification. The relationship exchanges certain geometric features of one manifold with complementary features of its partner—most famously the Hodge numbers h1,1 and h2,1, which count Kähler and complex structure deformations respectively.

History

The phenomenon was first noticed in the late 1980s in work by Lance Dixon, and by Wolfgang Lerche, Cumrun Vafa, and Nicholas Warner, who observed that conformal field theories could arise from two different manifolds. Brian Greene and Ronen Plesser constructed explicit mirror pairs in 1990 using orbifold techniques. A landmark application came in 1991, when Philip Candelas, Xenia de la Ossa, Paul Green, and Linda Parkes used mirror symmetry to compute the number of rational curves of each degree on the quintic threefold—an enumerative geometry result (such as 2,875 lines and 609,250 conics on a generic quintic) that mathematicians had been unable to obtain by classical methods.

Impact

Mirror symmetry transformed enumerative algebraic geometry, spurring rigorous reformulations such as Maxim Kontsevich's homological mirror symmetry, which relates the derived category of coherent sheaves on one manifold to the Fukaya category of its mirror, and the SYZ conjecture of Strominger, Yau, and Zaslow, which seeks a geometric explanation via dual torus fibrations. It remains one of the deepest links between string theory and pure mathematics.
