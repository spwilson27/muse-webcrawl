The Kronia was an ancient Greek festival held in honour of Cronus, the Titan father of Zeus, celebrating the harvest and recalling the mythical Golden Age when Cronus ruled and humans lived without toil or hierarchy. In Athens it was celebrated on the twelfth day of the month Hekatombaion, the first month of the Attic calendar, corresponding roughly to July.

Customs

The festival was marked by feasting in which social distinctions were temporarily relaxed: masters and slaves (or masters and servants) dined together, echoing the equality of the Golden Age. Ancient sources describe a holiday atmosphere of licence, noise and merrymaking, with slaves given leave from work. The Roman festival of Saturnalia, held for Saturn — the Roman counterpart of Cronus — shared this theme of role reversal and is often compared with the Kronia.

Observance and evidence

The Kronia is attested in Athens and in cities such as Olympia, Thebes, Lebadeia and on Rhodes and Samos. In Athens the festival centred on the sanctuary of Cronus and Rhea near the Olympieion. Literary references appear in authors including Plutarch and the comic poets, and the chronicler Philochorus described masters feasting with their farm slaves after the harvest.
