The FitzGerald dynasty is a Hiberno-Norman noble and aristocratic dynasty of Cambro-Norman origin that arrived in Ireland during the Norman invasion of 1169–71 and became one of the most powerful families in the country for more than 700 years. Its two great branches, the Earls of Kildare and the Earls of Desmond, dominated Leinster and Munster respectively, and the family motto Crom a Boo ("Crom forever") reflects its enduring influence.

Origins

The family descends from Maurice FitzGerald, Lord of Lanstephan, a Norman-Welsh lord whose sons and grandsons took part in the invasion of Ireland under Strongbow. The name FitzGerald means "son of Gerald", from Gerald of Windsor, castellan of Pembroke Castle, whose descendants intermarried with Welsh royalty — Maurice's mother was Nest ferch Rhys, daughter of the last king of Deheubarth. From their Irish grants the FitzGeralds expanded into one of the great "Old English" families, becoming, in the phrase applied to the Kildare branch, "more Irish than the Irish themselves" (Hiberniores Hibernis ipsis).

The Kildare branch

The senior branch held the earldom of Kildare from 1316. Under Garret Mór, the 8th Earl ("the Great Earl"), and his son Garret Óg, the Kildare FitzGeralds effectively governed Ireland as Lords Deputy for the English crown in the late 15th and early 16th centuries. Their power ended with the rebellion of Silken Thomas in 1534–35 and the execution of Thomas and his uncles at Tyburn. The earldom was later restored, and in 1766 the head of the family was created Duke of Leinster, the premier duke in the Peerage of Ireland.

The Desmond branch

The junior branch, the Earls of Desmond, ruled a large palatine lordship in Munster from the southwest. The Desmond FitzGeralds frequently clashed with the Ormond Butlers and the crown; the Desmond Rebellions (1569–73 and 1579–83) against Elizabeth I ended in devastation, the death of the last earl, Gerald FitzGerald, in 1583, and the confiscation and plantation of Munster.

Legacy

The family name survives widely in Ireland, and descendants include figures in Irish and British public life. The dynasty's castles, including Maynooth and Kilkea, and its long record as kingmakers, rebels, and statesmen make it central to the history of medieval and early modern Ireland.
