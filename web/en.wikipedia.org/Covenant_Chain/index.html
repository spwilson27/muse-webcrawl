Periodc. 1677 – late 18th century
CentreAlbany, New York
SymbolismChain of friendship, "brightened" at treaty councils

The Covenant Chain was a series of alliances and treaties developed during the seventeenth century between the Iroquois Confederacy (Haudenosaunee) and the English colonies of North America, beginning with agreements between the Five Nations and New York in 1677. It became the central framework of Anglo-Iroquois diplomacy for a century.

Diplomacy

Relations were maintained through treaty councils, usually at Albany, conducted with wampum belts and the metaphor of a chain linking the parties together — first described as rope, then iron, then silver — which had periodically to be "brightened" (renewed) so that it would not rust or break. The chain provided for trade, mutual defence, and the handling of disputes and murders under agreed procedures, and it later extended to other Indigenous nations and colonies.

Decline

The Covenant Chain strained during the Seven Years' War and effectively ended with the American War of Independence, when the Confederacy split and most nations sided with Britain. Its diplomatic forms — condolence councils, wampum exchange, and chain imagery — influenced early United States treaty practice, beginning with the 1778 Treaty of Fort Pitt.
