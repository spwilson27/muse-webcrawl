Human cloning

Human cloning is the creation of a genetically identical copy of a human. The term is generally used to refer to artificial human cloning, which is the reproduction of human cells and tissue; it does not refer to the natural conception and delivery of identical twins. Two commonly discussed types are therapeutic cloning, which creates embryonic stem cells matched to a patient, and reproductive cloning, which would create a cloned human being.

Status of the science

Reproductive cloning has been achieved in many mammal species, beginning with Dolly the sheep in 1996, using somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT). No verified case of a cloned human being exists, and claims by groups such as Clonaid in 2002 were never substantiated. In 2013 researchers reported deriving human embryonic stem cells by SCNT, a milestone for therapeutic cloning, though induced pluripotent stem cells have since reduced interest in that route.

Ethical and legal debate

Human reproductive cloning is widely opposed on grounds of safety, human dignity, identity, and the welfare of the resulting child, and is prohibited by law in many countries and by international declarations, including the United Nations Declaration on Human Cloning (2005). Therapeutic cloning and related stem-cell research are regulated differently across jurisdictions and remain the subject of ethical and religious debate.

In culture

Cloning has been a recurring theme in science fiction, from Aldous Huxley's Brave New World to films such as The Island and novels like Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go, often used to explore questions of identity and individuality.
