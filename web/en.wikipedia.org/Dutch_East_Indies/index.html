The Dutch East Indies (Dutch: Nederlandsch-Indië; Indonesian: Hindia Belanda) was a Dutch colony consisting of what is now Indonesia. It was formed in 1800 from the possessions of the Dutch East India Company (VOC), which the Dutch government nationalised, and expanded over the 19th and early 20th centuries to the borders of present-day Indonesia. The colony was one of the most valuable in the world, exporting spices, coffee, sugar, rubber, tin, and oil.

History

The Dutch East India Company, chartered in 1602, built a trading empire in the archipelago, founding Batavia (now Jakarta) in 1619. When the VOC went bankrupt, its assets were taken over by the Dutch state on 1 January 1800. After a British interregnum during the Napoleonic Wars (1811–1816), Dutch rule was restored. The Java War (1825–1830) under Prince Diponegoro ended with Dutch control consolidated over Java. From 1830 the Cultivation System (Cultuurstelsel) forced Javanese farmers to devote part of their land to export crops, generating large surpluses for the Dutch treasury at great social cost.

During the later 19th century the Dutch extended control over the outer islands through wars and treaties, including the long Aceh War (1873–1904). The colony reached its greatest extent in the 1920s, encompassing Sumatra, Java, Borneo (Kalimantan) except for British North Borneo, Sulawesi, the Moluccas, Bali and the Lesser Sunda Islands, and Dutch New Guinea.

Administration

The colony was governed by a Governor-General based in Batavia (and later partly at Buitenzorg, now Bogor), advised by the Council of the Indies. A people's council, the Volksraad, was established in 1918 with limited powers and an increasingly Indonesian membership. Administration relied heavily on the indigenous aristocracy (priyayi) as regents within a system of indirect rule.

Economy

After the Cultivation System was phased out, the Liberal Policy and private enterprise expanded sugar, coffee, tea, tobacco, rubber, and palm-oil plantations. The discovery of oil in Sumatra and Borneo made the colony strategically vital; the Royal Dutch Shell company originated in part from East Indies oil ventures. The Ethical Policy, proclaimed in 1901, nominally sought to improve native welfare through education, irrigation, and emigration programmes, though spending remained modest.

Society

Colonial society was legally stratified: Europeans, "Foreign Orientals" (mainly Chinese and Arabs), and "Natives" were subject to different legal systems. Education in Dutch was restricted to a small elite, among whom an Indonesian nationalist movement grew in the early 20th century, with organisations such as Budi Utomo (1908), Sarekat Islam, and Sukarno's Indonesian National Party (1927).

End of the colony

Japan occupied the Indies from 1942 to 1945. On 17 August 1945, two days after Japan's surrender, Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta proclaimed Indonesian independence. The Netherlands attempted to restore colonial rule, fighting the Indonesian National Revolution until, under international pressure—particularly from the United States—the Dutch transferred sovereignty to the Republic of the United States of Indonesia on 27 December 1949. Dutch New Guinea remained under Dutch control until 1962.
