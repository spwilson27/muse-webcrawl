Wilderness Act

The Wilderness Act of 1964 (Public Law 88-577) is a landmark piece of American conservation legislation, signed into law by President Lyndon B. Johnson on September 3, 1964. Written largely by Howard Zahniser of The Wilderness Society, the Act established the legal definition of wilderness in the United States and created the National Wilderness Preservation System (NWPS).

Provisions

The Act famously defines wilderness as "an area where the earth and its community of life are untrammeled by man, where man himself is a visitor who does not remain." It initially protected 9.1 million acres (about 37,000 km²) of federal land in 54 areas. Designated wilderness areas are generally closed to motorized and mechanized equipment (including bicycles), roads, and commercial development, though existing grazing and some other uses were grandfathered.

Growth of the system

Subsequent acts of Congress have expanded the system enormously. The Eastern Wilderness Areas Act of 1975 extended protection to smaller, previously disturbed lands in the eastern United States, and the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 added more than 56 million acres in a single stroke. Today the NWPS encompasses more than 800 areas covering over 111 million acres, administered by the Forest Service, National Park Service, Bureau of Land Management, and Fish and Wildlife Service. Only Congress can designate new wilderness areas.
