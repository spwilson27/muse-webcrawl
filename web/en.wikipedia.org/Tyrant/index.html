Tyrant

A tyrant of ancient Greece, in a later artistic depiction.

A tyrant (from Greek τύραννος, tyrannos) is, in modern usage, an absolute ruler who seizes or holds power unconstitutionally and governs oppressively, without legal restraint. In its original Greek sense, the term simply denoted a sole ruler who had taken power by unorthodox means — often with popular support — and was not necessarily pejorative; only later did it acquire its enduring connotation of cruelty and arbitrary rule.

Ancient Greece

Tyrannies flourished in many Greek city-states during the seventh and sixth centuries BC, as aristocracies were displaced by strongmen backed by rising commercial classes and hoplite soldiers. Notable tyrants include Cypselus and Periander of Corinth, Polycrates of Samos, and Peisistratos of Athens, whose rule combined public works and cultural patronage with firm control. The word's negative turn is associated with figures such as Phalaris of Acragas and with later philosophical reflection: Plato treated tyranny as the worst form of government, and Aristotle analysed the tyrant's methods in the Politics.

Later usage

Through Roman, medieval and early modern thought, "tyranny" became the standard charge against rulers perceived to violate law and custom, used to justify resistance and, in celebrated cases, tyrannicide — from the Roman assassins of Caesar to early modern resistance theory. In present-day political language the term is largely rhetorical, applied to dictators and authoritarian rulers generally, while political science prefers categories such as dictatorship and totalitarianism.
