Cable car (railway)

A cable car, in railway terms, is a type of rail vehicle hauled by a cable that moves continuously at a constant speed, driven by a stationary engine. The car grips and releases the cable to start and stop. Cable railways flourished in cities in the late 19th century, most famously in San Francisco, whose cable car system survives as a working landmark. The term is sometimes confused with aerial cable cars (gondola lifts) and with funiculars, in which two cars are directly attached to opposite ends of the same cable and counterbalance each other.

History

The first successful street cable railway was Andrew Smith Hallidie's Clay Street Hill Railroad in San Francisco, opened in 1873. Hallidie's key invention was a detachable grip that allowed a car to clamp onto the moving cable running in a conduit beneath the street. Cable traction spread rapidly to cities including Chicago, Melbourne, Dunedin, London and Lisbon, because it was cleaner and more powerful than horse-drawn trams and could climb steep grades. Most systems were replaced by electric streetcars after the 1890s, which were cheaper to operate.

Operation

A powerhouse drives an endless loop of steel cable through conduits in the street at a constant speed (about 9.5 mph / 15 km/h in San Francisco). The gripman operates a lever controlling the grip that clamps the cable; releasing the grip and applying brakes stops the car. Because the cable never stops, operators must take care at crossings and terminals, where cars are turned on turntables or loops.
