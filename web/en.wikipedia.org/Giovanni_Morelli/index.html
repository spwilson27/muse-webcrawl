Giovanni Morelli

Giovanni Morelli (25 February 1816 – 28 February 1891) was an Italian art critic and political figure who developed an influential method of connoisseurship for attributing Old Master paintings. Writing partly under the pseudonym "Ivan Lermolieff", he reshaped the study of Italian Renaissance painting in the late nineteenth century.

Born in Verona, Morelli studied medicine and anatomy in Germany and Switzerland before turning to art history and politics; he served as a deputy and later a senator in the parliament of the Kingdom of Italy.

The Morellian method

Morelli argued that attributions should rest not on the most obvious features of a painting — which copyists and pupils could imitate — but on minor, habitual details rendered almost unconsciously by the artist: the shapes of ears, hands and fingernails, the folds of drapery, and similar marginal motifs. By systematically comparing these details, he reassigned the authorship of many famous works in European galleries, publishing his findings in works on the Borghese and Doria-Pamphilj galleries in Rome and on Italian painters more generally.

His approach influenced Bernard Berenson, who developed it further, and has often been compared with the contemporary rise of other "evidential" methods — Sigmund Freud, who cited Morelli, compared the method to psychoanalysis, and it has been likened to the detective work of Sherlock Holmes in the writings of Carlo Ginzburg.

Legacy

Though some of his attributions have been overturned and the method is now combined with documentary research and technical analysis, Morelli is regarded as a founder of modern scientific connoisseurship and of the detailed cataloguing of Italian collections.
