Amos Nathan Tversky (16 March 1937 – 2 June 1996) was an Israeli cognitive and mathematical psychologist. With Daniel Kahneman he founded the study of cognitive biases through the "heuristics and biases" research programme and developed prospect theory, work that reshaped psychology, behavioural economics and decision science.

Amos Tversky, cognitive psychologist and co-creator of prospect theory.

Early life and education

Tversky was born in Haifa, then in Mandatory Palestine, to a veterinarian father and a mother who later served in the Knesset. He served as a paratrooper in the Israel Defense Forces, reaching the rank of captain and receiving a decoration for bravery. He studied psychology and philosophy at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem and earned his PhD at the University of Michigan in 1965, then taught at the Hebrew University before joining Stanford University in 1978.

Work with Kahneman

Beginning in the late 1960s, Tversky and Daniel Kahneman investigated how people judge probabilities and make decisions under uncertainty. Their 1974 Science paper identified three heuristics — representativeness, availability, and anchoring and adjustment — each producing predictable biases in judgment. Their 1981 paper on framing showed that logically equivalent descriptions of a choice (in terms of lives saved versus lives lost, for example) reverse people's preferences.

Prospect theory

In 1979 Tversky and Kahneman published prospect theory in Econometrica, presenting experimental evidence that people evaluate outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point, are more sensitive to losses than to equivalent gains (loss aversion), and weight probabilities non-linearly. The paper became one of the most cited in economics and founded the field of behavioural economics. A revised version, cumulative prospect theory, followed in 1992.

Other contributions and legacy

Tversky also worked on similarity and feature matching, choice theory, support theory of probability judgment, and systematic errors in intuition — famously demonstrating the "conjunction fallacy" with the Linda problem. He received a MacArthur Fellowship in 1984 and was elected to the U.S. National Academy of Sciences. He died of melanoma in 1996, aged 59. Because Nobel Prizes are not awarded posthumously, the 2002 economics prize went to Kahneman alone; Kahneman himself said the work had been joint. Michael Lewis's book The Undoing Project (2016) recounts their partnership.
