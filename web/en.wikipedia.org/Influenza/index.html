Influenza

Influenza, commonly known as the flu, is an infectious disease caused by influenza viruses. Symptoms range from mild to severe and often include fever, runny nose, sore throat, muscle pain, headache, coughing, and fatigue. These symptoms begin one to four days after exposure to the virus (typically two days) and last for about two to eight days.

Virology

There are four types of influenza virus: A, B, C, and D. Influenza A and B cause seasonal epidemics in humans; influenza A viruses are classified by their hemagglutinin (H) and neuraminidase (N) surface proteins, such as H1N1 and H3N2, and have animal reservoirs, notably wild aquatic birds. Genetic changes through antigenic drift produce new seasonal strains, while antigenic shift can generate pandemic viruses.

Transmission and prevention

The virus spreads through droplets from coughs and sneezes, aerosols, and contact with contaminated surfaces. Annual influenza vaccination is the primary method of prevention; hand hygiene and masks also reduce transmission. Antiviral drugs such as oseltamivir may be used for treatment and prophylaxis in some cases.

Epidemiology

Seasonal influenza causes an estimated 3–5 million cases of severe illness and 290,000–650,000 respiratory deaths worldwide each year. Pandemics, such as the 1918 Spanish flu, the 1957 and 1968 pandemics, and the 2009 swine flu pandemic, have caused much larger burdens.
