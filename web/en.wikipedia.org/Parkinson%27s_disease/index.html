Parkinson's disease (PD) is a long-term neurodegenerative disorder that primarily affects movement. It results mainly from the death of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra of the midbrain. The disease was described by the English physician James Parkinson in his 1817 Essay on the Shaking Palsy.

Signs and symptoms

The cardinal motor symptoms are tremor (often a resting "pill-rolling" tremor), rigidity, slowness of movement (bradykinesia), and postural instability leading to falls. Non-motor symptoms are common and may precede motor signs by years: loss of smell, constipation, REM sleep behaviour disorder, depression and anxiety; dementia frequently occurs in advanced stages.

Cause and pathology

The cause is unknown in most cases and is thought to involve a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental factors. Pathologically, affected neurons contain Lewy bodies — aggregates of the protein alpha-synuclein. Mutations in genes such as LRRK2 and GBA account for a minority of cases.

Treatment

There is no cure, but symptoms can be managed. The mainstay is levodopa, a dopamine precursor, often combined with carbidopa; other options include dopamine agonists, MAO-B inhibitors, and deep brain stimulation for selected patients with motor complications. Exercise and multidisciplinary care improve quality of life. Parkinson's is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer's disease, affecting more than 10 million people worldwide.
