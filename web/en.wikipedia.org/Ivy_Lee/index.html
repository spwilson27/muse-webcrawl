Ivy Lee

Ivy Lee (1877–1934)
Born: 16 July 1877, Rockmart, Georgia, US
Died: 9 November 1934, New York City, US
Occupation: Public relations pioneer, journalist

Ivy Ledbetter Lee (July 16, 1877 – November 9, 1934) was an American publicity expert and consultant regarded as a founder of modern public relations, alongside Edward Bernays. He is credited with developing the press release as a standard practice and with advising major corporations and philanthropists on dealing openly with the press.

Career

After Princeton and work as a newspaper reporter, Lee co-founded the publicity firm Parker and Lee in 1904. In 1906 he issued his famous "Declaration of Principles," pledging that his material supplied to the press would be accurate and promptly verifiable — a founding statement of professional PR ethics. He worked for the Pennsylvania Railroad, pioneered crisis communication after a 1906 rail accident, advised the anthracite coal operators during the 1902 strike's aftermath, and became a long-time adviser to John D. Rockefeller Jr., helping rehabilitate the Rockefeller image after the Ludlow Massacre of 1914 — though his methods, including minimizing damaging facts, drew criticism that earned him the nickname "Poison Ivy."

Legacy

Lee's counsel that corporations should tell their story candidly rather than rely on secrecy helped professionalize public relations; later historians have noted the gap he sometimes left between principle and practice. He died in 1934 while under congressional scrutiny for publicity work on behalf of the German conglomerate IG Farben.
