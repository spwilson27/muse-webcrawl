Year Without a Summer

The eruption of Mount Tambora in 1815 darkened skies and cooled the globe in 1816.

The Year Without a Summer was 1816, which saw severe climate abnormalities that caused average global temperatures to decrease by 0.4–0.7 °C (0.7–1.3 °F). Summer temperatures in Europe were the coldest on record between 1766 and 2000, resulting in major food shortages across the Northern Hemisphere. Evidence suggests that the anomaly was predominantly a volcanic winter event caused by the massive 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora in the Dutch East Indies (modern-day Indonesia), the largest eruption in recorded human history. The year also fell within the Dalton Minimum, a period of unusually low solar activity.

Effects

In North America, killing frosts struck New England in every month of the summer: snow fell in Albany and Maine in June, ice formed on lakes in Pennsylvania in July and August, and crops failed across the region, contributing to westward migration. In Europe, incessant rain ruined harvests in Ireland, Wales, England, Germany, and Switzerland; grain prices soared, bread riots and demonstrations spread, and the ensuing famine and typhus epidemics made 1816–17 one of the worst subsistence crises of the 19th century. In Asia, the monsoon was disrupted; floods devastated the Yangtze valley and frosts killed crops in southern China, while in India a disturbed monsoon may have helped spread a new cholera strain from Bengal around the world.

Cultural consequences

  • Confined indoors by rain at the Villa Diodati in Switzerland, Lord Byron, Percy Bysshe Shelley, and Mary Shelley held a ghost-story contest: Mary Shelley began Frankenstein, and Byron wrote the apocalyptic poem "Darkness".
  • Dramatic, ash-tinted sunsets are thought to have influenced the skies in the paintings of J. M. W. Turner and Caspar David Friedrich.
  • High oat prices and the suffering of horses are sometimes credited — in a debated story — with spurring Karl Drais to invent his wooden Laufmaschine (1817), a forerunner of the bicycle.
  • Failed corn crops in New England pushed farmers westward, accelerating settlement of the American Midwest.

Cause

The April 1815 eruption of Tambora ejected vast quantities of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, where sulfate aerosols veiled the planet and reflected sunlight for several years. A smaller but significant eruption of Mayon in 1814 likely preconditioned the cooling. The event remains a benchmark for understanding volcanic climate forcing and modern society's vulnerability to such eruptions.
