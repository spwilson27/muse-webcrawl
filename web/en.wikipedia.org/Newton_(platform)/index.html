Newton (platform)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Newton is a discontinued platform for personal digital assistants (PDAs) developed by Apple Computer and released in 1993. The first device, the MessagePad, was one of the earliest PDAs and popularised the very term "personal digital assistant", which Apple's CEO John Sculley coined. Newton devices ran the Newton OS, featured ARM processors, and were navigated with a stylus on a touch-sensitive screen, making them ancestors of later devices such as the iPhone and iPad.

Features

The Newton platform's signature technology was its handwriting recognition, which in early models was widely mocked for errors — famously parodied in the comic strip Doonesbury and in The Simpsons ("Beat up Martin") — but improved substantially in Newton OS 2.0. The system stored information in object-oriented databases called "soups" and included an early form of artificial intelligence that could, for example, interpret "Call John tomorrow" into a scheduled call, and recognise shapes drawn freehand, converting them to clean geometry.

Development and cancellation

Newton began in 1987 as an ambitious project for a "Knowledge Navigator". Devices were manufactured from 1993 to 1998, including the MessagePad series, the eMate 300 (a clamshell device for education), and licensed clones. Despite critical praise for later models, sales were modest, and after Steve Jobs returned to Apple he discontinued the platform in February 1998 to focus the company's resources. The Newton retained a devoted user community for decades afterward, and several of its concepts — mobile handwriting input, intelligent assistants, and instant-on handheld computing — anticipated mainstream products of the following decades.
