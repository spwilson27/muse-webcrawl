Hausa language

Hausa
Native name: Harshen Hausa
Region: Northern Nigeria, Niger
Speakers: tens of millions (native and second-language)
Family: Afroasiatic → Chadic → West Chadic
Writing: Latin (Boko), Arabic (Ajami)

Hausa (Harshen Hausa) is a Chadic language spoken by the Hausa people, mainly in northern Nigeria and Niger, and as a lingua franca across much of West Africa. It is the most widely spoken of the Chadic languages and one of the largest languages of Africa, with tens of millions of native speakers and many more who use it as a second language in trade and broadcasting.

Classification and distribution

Hausa belongs to the West Chadic branch of the Chadic family within the Afroasiatic phylum. Its heartland is the Hausa Kingdoms region of what is now northern Nigeria and southern Niger, with significant speaker communities in Ghana, Cameroon, Chad, Sudan, and elsewhere through trade and migration. Major dialect areas include Kano (widely regarded as standard), Sokoto, Zaria, Katsina, and the dialects of Niger.

Phonology and grammar

Hausa is a tonal language with two basic tones, high and low, and a falling contour. It has a rich consonant system including ejective and implosive consonants such as /ƙ/, /ɗ/, and /ɓ/. Nouns have grammatical gender (masculine and feminine), and plurals are formed by a variety of internal changes and suffixes. The verb system marks aspect (completive, continuous, subjunctive) largely through combinations of person-aspect pronouns and tone.

Writing systems

Hausa has been written for centuries in Ajami, an adaptation of the Arabic script. In the twentieth century a Latin-based orthography called Boko became official in Nigeria and is used in education, newspapers, and government; it distinguishes special letters ɓ, ɗ, ƙ, and ƴ. BBC, Voice of America, and other broadcasters run Hausa-language services.
