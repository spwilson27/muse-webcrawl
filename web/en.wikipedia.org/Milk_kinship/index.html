Milk kinship

Milk kinship is a form of kinship created by breastfeeding: a child nursed by a woman who is not its biological mother becomes her "milk child", and the nurse's own children become the child's "milk siblings". The institution is best known from Islamic law, where it is called raḍāʿa (رضاعة), but forms of milk kinship existed historically across the Middle East, the Balkans, Central Asia and parts of Europe.

In Islam

The Quran (4:23) prohibits marriage to one's milk mother and milk sisters, placing milk relations within the prohibited degrees of kinship, and hadith literature sets conditions — traditionally a minimum number of sucklings (often five) within the child's first two years. Milk kinship therefore created marriage impediments and mahram status equivalent in important respects to blood kinship. It was used socially and politically: children were fostered out to Bedouin wet nurses (the Prophet Muhammad himself was nursed by Halimah bint Abi Dhuayb), and milk ties could bind families and even dynasties in alliance.

Wider history and anthropology

Comparable institutions appear in medieval Christian Europe, in Balkan and Caucasus fosterage customs, and in Shi'a and Sunni jurisprudence with differing details. Anthropologists, notably Peter Parkes, have studied milk kinship as a widespread strategy of alliance and child circulation across the "milk kinship belt" from the Balkans to Bengal. Contemporary questions include the status of donor human milk banks, where anonymous pooled milk complicates the creation and documentation of milk kinship for observant Muslim families, prompting some banks to adopt single-donor recording systems.
