Titans

In Greek mythology, the Titans (Greek: Τιτᾶνες) were the pre-Olympian gods: the twelve children of the primordial parents Uranus (Sky) and Gaia (Earth), with six male and six female Titans — Oceanus, Coeus, Crius, Hyperion, Iapetus and Cronus; Theia, Rhea, Themis, Mnemosyne, Phoebe and Tethys. Led by Cronus, the Titans ruled during the mythical Golden Age until they were overthrown by Zeus and the Olympians in the ten-year war called the Titanomachy, after which most were imprisoned in Tartarus.

Genealogy

Hesiod's Theogony gives the canonical account. Uranus, fearing and hating his children, hid them away inside Gaia, who fashioned a sickle of flint and persuaded Cronus, the youngest Titan, to castrate his father. Cronus then ruled with his sister-wife Rhea; their children included Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades, Poseidon and Zeus. Warned that he would be overthrown by his own son as he had overthrown Uranus, Cronus swallowed each child at birth, until Rhea hid the infant Zeus in Crete and tricked Cronus with a stone wrapped in swaddling clothes.

Titanomachy

Grown to adulthood, Zeus forced Cronus to disgorge his siblings and led them in war against the Titans. The war lasted ten years; Zeus prevailed after freeing the Cyclopes — who forged his thunderbolts — and the Hecatonchires, the hundred-handed giants, from Tartarus. The defeated Titans were cast into Tartarus and guarded by the Hecatonchires; Atlas, a second-generation Titan, was condemned to hold up the sky. Some Titans had sided with Zeus or stayed neutral — Oceanus, Themis and Mnemosyne among them — and were not imprisoned.

Later tradition

In Orphic traditions the Titans took on a darker role: they dismembered the infant god Dionysus (Zagreus), and humankind was said to have sprung from their ashes, inheriting both Titanic wickedness and a spark of the divine. The word "titanic" entered modern languages meaning enormous or titanic in scale; the element titanium and Saturn's largest moon Titan are named for them.
