Communicating sequential processes

Communicating sequential processes (CSP) is a formal language for describing patterns of interaction in concurrent systems. It was introduced by Tony Hoare, first in a 1978 journal article and fully in his 1985 book Communicating Sequential Processes. In CSP, a system is a composition of processes that run independently and interact solely by passing messages over named channels, synchronising on communication events.

Model

CSP uses a small algebra of operators: prefix (an event followed by a process), choice between processes, and parallel composition with synchronisation on shared events. Its mathematical semantics — traces, failures, and divergences models — allow engineers to prove properties such as deadlock freedom of specifications before implementation.

Influence

CSP directly inspired the occam programming language for transputers and underlies formal verification tools such as FDR (Failures-Divergences Refinement). Its philosophy — share memory by communicating, rather than communicate by sharing memory — was famously adopted by the Go programming language, whose goroutines and channels echo CSP, and it also influenced Erlang's actor-based concurrency and the π-calculus.

Applications

CSP has been used to specify and verify safety-critical systems in rail, aerospace, and microprocessor design, including verifying aspects of the T9000 transputer.
