The University of Chicago Press is the largest and one of the oldest university presses in the United States. Founded in 1890 as part of the University of Chicago, it publishes scholarly books and journals across the humanities, social sciences, and natural sciences, and is best known to the general public as the publisher of The Chicago Manual of Style, first issued in 1906.

History

The Press was among the founding members of the Association of American University Presses (1937). Initially a printer of academic documents for the university, it began publishing scholarly monographs under director Gordon J. Laing in the early 1900s and grew substantially in the mid-20th century, publishing landmark works such as Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962) and translations of classic texts.

Publishing program

The Press publishes around 300 new books and more than 80 scholarly journals per year, with a backlist of several thousand titles. Its journals division, founded in 1882 with the Journal of Political Economy's predecessors, includes prominent titles in economics, sociology, and astrophysics (including The Astrophysical Journal). The Chicago Distribution Center, opened in 1991, provides distribution services for over one hundred client publishers.

The Chicago Manual of Style

First published in 1906 as a style sheet for the Press's own compositors, The Chicago Manual of Style has grown into a comprehensive reference for authors and editors, now in its 18th edition (2024). Its citation systems — notes-bibliography and author-date — are widely used in the humanities and social sciences.
