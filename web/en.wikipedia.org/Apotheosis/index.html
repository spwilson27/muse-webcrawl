Apotheosis

Apotheosis (from Ancient Greek apotheōsis, "deification") is the glorification of a subject to divine levels, and commonly the treatment of a human being, any other living thing, or an abstract idea in the likeness of a deity. The term has meanings in theology, in art, and in figurative usage for the highest point of achievement or exaltation.

Ancient world

In Greek tradition, heroes such as Heracles were said to have been raised to Olympus after death. In ancient Rome, the state formally deified deceased emperors — beginning with Julius Caesar and Augustus — in a practice tied to the imperial cult; the Senate voted the honour, and temples and priests served the divus. Pharaohs in ancient Egypt were considered divine in life, while in Mesopotamia a few kings, such as Naram-Sin, claimed godhood.

In art and culture

Apotheosis is a frequent subject of Baroque ceiling painting, glorifying rulers, saints, and heroes ascending into heaven; Constantino Brumidi's The Apotheosis of Washington (1865) in the dome of the United States Capitol adapts the motif to a republican founder. In literature and criticism, "apotheosis" has come to mean the culmination or ideal example of something, as in "the apotheosis of the novel".
