Free market

A free market is an economic system in which the prices of goods and services are determined by the open market and by consumers, through the laws of supply and demand, with little or no government intervention or regulation. In a free market, the forces of supply and demand are free from intervention by a government or other authority, other than the enforcement of property rights and contracts.

Concept

The idea is associated with Adam Smith's metaphor of the "invisible hand", by which individuals pursuing their own interest may unintentionally promote the public good. Classical and neoclassical economists argue that, under conditions of competition and complete information, free markets allocate resources efficiently — an idea formalised in the fundamental theorems of welfare economics. The term laissez-faire describes the policy of minimal state interference associated with free markets.

Criticism and limits

Critics point to market failures — externalities, public goods, information asymmetries, and monopolies — as reasons why purely free markets may produce inefficient or inequitable outcomes. Economists therefore debate the appropriate degree of regulation, taxation, antitrust enforcement, and social welfare provision surrounding markets. Most modern economies are mixed economies combining market allocation with varying measures of state intervention.

History

Advocacy of free markets grew with the repeal of the British Corn Laws in 1846, the spread of free trade in the nineteenth century, and the liberalisations of the late twentieth century associated with figures such as Milton Friedman and Friedrich Hayek.
