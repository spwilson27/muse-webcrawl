Monoamine oxidase (MAO) is a family of flavin-containing enzymes that catalyse the oxidative deamination of monoamines, including the neurotransmitters serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine, as well as dietary amines such as tyramine. The enzymes sit in the outer membrane of mitochondria in most cell types and help terminate the action of monoamine signalling molecules after their reuptake (see monoamine transporter).

Isoforms

Humans have two isoforms, MAO-A and MAO-B, encoded by adjacent genes on the X chromosome. MAO-A preferentially deaminates serotonin, melatonin, norepinephrine, and epinephrine, while MAO-B preferentially acts on phenethylamine and benzylamine; both act on dopamine and tyramine. MAO-A is prominent in the gut, liver, and placenta, while MAO-B predominates in platelets and certain brain glia, and its levels rise with age.

Clinical significance

Drugs that inhibit these enzymes — monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) — were among the first antidepressants; non-selective irreversible MAOIs such as phenelzine and tranylcypromine remain in use for treatment-resistant depression, while selective MAO-B inhibitors such as selegiline and rasagiline are used in Parkinson's disease. Non-selective MAO inhibition carries a risk of hypertensive crisis when tyramine-rich foods (such as aged cheeses) are eaten, because gut MAO-A normally detoxifies dietary tyramine. Genetic variation in MAO-A, especially low-activity variants interacting with childhood adversity, has been widely studied in relation to aggression and antisocial behaviour.
