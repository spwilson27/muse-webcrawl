Public Law 280

Public Law 280 (Pub. L. 83-280; 67 Stat. 588), enacted by the United States Congress in 1953, is a federal statute that transferred to certain states jurisdiction over civil and criminal matters arising in Indian country, displacing the federal and tribal jurisdiction that had previously governed most reservation crime. It is considered one of the central laws of the federal "termination era" of Native American policy.

Provisions

The law granted six states — California, Minnesota (except the Red Lake Reservation), Nebraska, Oregon (except the Warm Springs Reservation), Wisconsin, and later Alaska — mandatory criminal jurisdiction and civil adjudicatory jurisdiction over Indian country within their borders. Other states were permitted to opt in by legislation or constitutional amendment, and several did so in whole or in part. Importantly, the statute transferred jurisdiction without providing federal funding to support the assumption of those duties, and it did not give states civil regulatory authority: the Supreme Court held in Bryan v. Itasca County (1976) that the civil grant was adjudicatory only, not a general power to tax or regulate.

Amendments and criticism

The Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968 amended the law to require tribal consent before any further state assumption of jurisdiction, and permitted states to retrocede jurisdiction back to the federal government. Critics, including many tribal governments, argue that Public Law 280 complicated law enforcement on reservations, created jurisdictional confusion, and was imposed without tribal consent; studies have associated it with under-policing in some affected communities. The Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 allowed tribes in Public Law 280 states to request a return of federal concurrent jurisdiction.
