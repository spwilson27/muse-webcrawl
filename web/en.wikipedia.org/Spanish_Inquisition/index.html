The Spanish Inquisition was a tribunal established in 1478 by the Catholic Monarchs, Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile, to maintain Catholic orthodoxy in their kingdoms. Unlike the medieval papal inquisitions, it operated under the control of the Spanish crown and lasted, with interruptions, until 1834.

Origins

The tribunal was founded amid suspicion of conversos — Jews who had converted to Christianity, often under pressure — who were accused of secretly practising Judaism. Pope Sixtus IV authorised the monarchs to appoint inquisitors; the first auto-da-fé was held in Seville in 1481. Tomás de Torquemada became the first Grand Inquisitor and organised the institution under the Council of the Supreme Inquisition (la Suprema).

Activities

The Inquisition policed heresy, blasphemy, bigamy, superstition and prohibited books. Its procedures included secret denunciations, lengthy imprisonment, torture under regulated conditions, and public autos-da-fé at which sentences were proclaimed. After 1492 it accompanied the expulsion of the Jews from Spain, and in the 16th and 17th centuries it turned against Moriscos (converted Muslims), the small Protestant communities, and suspected illuminists and witches — the latter prosecuted less ferociously than in much of Europe. Modern historians estimate the number executed over its 356 years in the low thousands, far below the figures of the "Black Legend", though its wider effect of censorship and fear was profound.

Abolition

Weakened under Enlightenment influence and suppressed during the Napoleonic period and the Liberal Triennium, the Inquisition was definitively abolished in 1834.
