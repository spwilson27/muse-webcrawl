Electron hole


When an electron leaves its bond, the vacancy — a hole — can migrate through the crystal

In physics, chemistry and electronic engineering, an electron hole (usually simply called a hole) is a quasiparticle denoting the lack of an electron at a position where one could exist in an atom or atomic lattice. Since in a normal atom or crystal lattice the negative charge of the electrons is balanced by the positive charge of the atomic nuclei, the absence of an electron leaves a net positive charge at the hole's position.

Holes in semiconductors

In a semiconductor crystal, thermal energy or doping can promote an electron from the valence band to the conduction band, leaving behind a hole. Neighbouring valence electrons can move into the vacancy, so the hole effectively migrates through the material in the opposite direction. Holes behave as mobile positive charge carriers with their own mobility and effective mass, and they contribute to electrical conduction alongside electrons.

Semiconductors doped with electron-accepting impurities (such as boron in silicon) have holes as their majority carriers and are called p-type; those doped with electron donors (such as phosphorus) have electrons as majority carriers and are n-type. The junction between p-type and n-type material is the basis of diodes, transistors and solar cells.

Evidence

The positive sign of the Hall coefficient in many semiconductors, opposite to that expected for electrons, was historically key evidence for hole conduction. The concept also appears in electrochemistry and in the theory of insulators, where a hole may localise on an atom or bond.
