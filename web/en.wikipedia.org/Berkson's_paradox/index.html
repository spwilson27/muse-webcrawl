Berkson's paradox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Berkson's paradox, also known as Berkson's bias, collider bias or the Berkson fallacy, is a result in conditional probability and statistics which is often found to be counterintuitive and hence a veridical paradox. It is an example of selection bias: when a sample is drawn from a subpopulation defined by two (or more) variables, a spurious correlation can appear between those variables even when none exists in the population as a whole.

Original example

The paradox is named after Joseph Berkson, who described it in 1946 in the context of hospital-based case-control studies. Suppose a researcher studies patients in a hospital and looks at two diseases. A person enters the sample if they have either disease A or disease B (or both). Even if A and B are independent in the general population, among hospitalized patients they will tend to appear negatively associated: knowing a patient has disease A makes it less "necessary" that they also have disease B to explain their presence in the hospital.

Explanation

In causal-graph terms, Berkson's paradox arises when conditioning on a common effect (a "collider") of two variables, which opens a non-causal association between them. The same mechanism can bias studies conducted on selected samples — for instance, samples of hospitalized patients, published papers, or admitted students — and it is a common illustration of why correlation in a selected sample need not reflect correlation in the population.
