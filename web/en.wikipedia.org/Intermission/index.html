Intermission

Also calledInterval (British English); entr'acte (historical)
Typical length15–20 minutes in theatre and opera; none for most films in North America
PurposesAudience rest, refreshments, set and costume changes, concession sales

An intermission, also known as an interval in British English, is a recess between parts of a performance or production, such as a theatrical play, opera, concert, or film screening. It should not be confused with an entr'acte, a piece of music written to be performed during an intermission, or with an intermezzo.

The intermission gives audiences a break and venues a major source of concession revenue.

Purpose

Intermissions serve several functions. They give the audience a chance to rest, visit the restroom, and buy refreshments — concession sales during intermission are a significant revenue source for many venues. Backstage, the break allows for set changes, costume changes, and rest for performers and musicians. Dramaturgically, playwrights and librettists traditionally structure works around the interval, placing a climax or revelation immediately before it.

By medium

Theatre and opera

Most plays and operas include one intermission of about 15 to 20 minutes, typically after the first act. Some long works, such as Wagner's operas, have two. A convention in many theatres is a warning bell or chime, followed by the house lights dimming, to signal the end of the break.

Concerts

Orchestral concerts conventionally divide the programme into two halves separated by an interval of about 20 minutes, often with a shorter work before the break and a symphony after it.

Film

In the early sound era, long films were routinely shown with an intermission. Roadshow presentations of epics such as Lawrence of Arabia (1962) and 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968) included overture, intermission, and entr'acte music. The practice largely disappeared from North American and European cinemas by the 1980s, but remains standard in India, where mainstream films are written with an "interval point" — a dramatic beat designed to hold the audience through the break.
