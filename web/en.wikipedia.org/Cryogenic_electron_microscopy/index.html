Cryogenic electron microscopy

Cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) is an electron microscopy technique in which samples are studied at cryogenic temperatures, typically after being vitrified — flash-frozen so rapidly that water forms amorphous ice rather than crystals. It allows biological specimens to be imaged in a near-native, hydrated state without staining or chemical fixation.

Single-particle analysis

In single-particle cryo-EM, images of many thousands of identical molecules in random orientations are aligned and averaged computationally to reconstruct a three-dimensional structure, increasingly at near-atomic resolution. The "resolution revolution" of the 2010s, driven by direct electron detectors and improved software, made cryo-EM a central method of structural biology.

Recognition

The 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank and Richard Henderson "for developing cryo-electron microscopy for the high-resolution structure determination of biomolecules in solution". Cryo-EM has since determined structures of ribosomes, membrane proteins, viruses and amyloid fibrils, aiding drug discovery.
