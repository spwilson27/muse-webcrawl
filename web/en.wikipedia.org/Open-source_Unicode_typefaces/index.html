Open-source Unicode typefaces

A small number of typefaces aim at broad coverage of the Unicode standard, providing glyphs for most of the world's writing systems in a single font. Because developing such coverage is an enormous undertaking, many of the most complete efforts are open-source Unicode typefaces, produced by communities, universities, and companies and released under free licences such as the SIL Open Font License. These fonts are essential for multilingual publishing, minority-language support, linguistics, and operating systems that must render text in any script.

Notable typefaces

TypefaceNotes
Noto fontsGoogle's family ("no tofu") aiming to cover all Unicode scripts; split into many per-script fonts, with the CJK versions branded Noto Sans/Serif CJK.
DejaVu fontsDerived from Bitstream Vera; long the default on many Linux systems, with wide Latin, Greek, Cyrillic, and symbol coverage.
GNU FreeFontPart of the GNU Project; FreeSerif, FreeSans, and FreeMono families covering many blocks of Unicode.
GNU UnifontA bitmap font covering the entire Basic Multilingual Plane with a glyph for every code point, widely used as a fallback.
Liberation fontsMetric-compatible with common proprietary fonts; good Latin/Greek/Cyrillic coverage.
SIL fonts (Charis, Gentium, Doulos)From SIL International, designed for linguistics and minority languages, with extensive diacritic support.
JunicodeFor medievalists, covering the Medieval Unicode Font Initiative recommendations.
Code2000An early shareware pan-Unicode font by James Kass, influential in the 2000s.

Coverage challenges

Unicode encodes well over 140,000 characters in more than 160 scripts. Han unification means East Asian ideographs dominate the count, and most "pan-Unicode" fonts still omit large areas or rely on fallback stacking, in which a system substitutes glyphs from several fonts. Complex-script shaping additionally requires OpenType intelligence, not just glyph outlines, so projects such as HarfBuzz and SIL's Graphite technology complement the fonts themselves. Licence freedom matters because these fonts must be bundled with free operating systems and embedded in documents.
