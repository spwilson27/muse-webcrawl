Dislocation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In materials science, a dislocation or Taylor's dislocation is a linear crystallographic defect or irregularity within a crystal structure that contains an abrupt change in the arrangement of atoms. The movement of dislocations allows atoms to slide over each other at low stress levels and is known as glide or slip. The crystalline order is restored on either side of a glide dislocation but the atoms on one side have moved one position.

Types

Edge dislocations

An edge dislocation can be visualized as an extra half-plane of atoms inserted into a crystal. The atoms on one side of the dislocation line are displaced relative to those on the other side. The stress field around an edge dislocation has both tensile and compressive components.

Screw dislocations

A screw dislocation can be visualized as a helical ramp of atomic planes twisting around the dislocation line. Screw dislocations are harder to visualize but can be pictured as cutting a crystal partway and sliding one side by one lattice spacing parallel to the cut.

Mixed dislocations

Most dislocations found in real materials are mixed, having both edge and screw components along different parts of the dislocation line.

Burgers vector

The magnitude and direction of the lattice distortion caused by a dislocation are expressed by the Burgers vector, obtained by tracing a closed circuit around the dislocation line and measuring the closure failure. The Burgers vector is perpendicular to the dislocation line for an edge dislocation and parallel to it for a screw dislocation.

Movement and multiplication

Dislocation motion is central to plastic deformation. Dislocations move most easily on closely packed planes and in closely packed directions, called slip systems. New dislocations are generated during deformation, for example at Frank–Read sources, so dislocation density can increase by many orders of magnitude during cold working. Interactions between dislocations impede their motion and cause work hardening.

History

The theory of dislocations was developed in 1934 independently by Egon Orowan, Michael Polanyi, and G. I. Taylor to explain why the observed strength of metals was far lower than the theoretical strength of a perfect crystal.
