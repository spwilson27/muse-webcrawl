GIUK gap

RegionNorth Atlantic Ocean
Named forGreenland – Iceland – United Kingdom
SignificanceAnti-submarine warfare chokepoint

The GIUK gap (pronounced often as "gee-yook") is an area in the northern Atlantic Ocean forming a naval chokepoint between the open ocean and the Norwegian and North Seas. The name is an acronym for Greenland, Iceland, and the United Kingdom, the three land masses bounding the passages between them: the Denmark Strait, the Iceland–Faroe gap, and the Faroe–Shetland channel.

Cold War role

During the Cold War the gap was a principal focus of NATO anti-submarine warfare. Soviet Northern Fleet submarines based on the Kola Peninsula had to pass through it to reach the Atlantic shipping lanes. NATO monitored the gap with the SOSUS underwater hydrophone network — listening stations feeding facilities such as Naval Air Station Keflavik in Iceland — together with maritime patrol aircraft and attack submarines. Britain's RAF and Royal Navy and the US Navy's P-3 Orion fleets were heavily tasked to the barrier.

Post–Cold War

Monitoring declined after 1991; the US left Keflavik in 2006. Renewed Russian submarine activity in the 2010s revived interest: the US Navy re-established its Second Fleet in 2018 with responsibility for the North Atlantic, P-8 Poseidon patrol aircraft returned to Icelandic bases on rotation, and the UK's P-8 fleet at RAF Lossiemouth was assigned in part to gap surveillance.

Strategic significance

Analysts describe the gap as a gate through which Russian submarines — including ballistic missile submarines protecting the Barents Sea bastion and attack submarines threatening transatlantic reinforcement routes — must pass, making it one of the most closely watched maritime chokepoints in the world.
