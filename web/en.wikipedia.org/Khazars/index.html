Khazars

The Khazars were a confederation of Turkic peoples who, in the late 6th century CE, established a major commercial empire covering the southeastern part of modern European Russia, southern Ukraine, Crimea, and Kazakhstan. Their polity, the Khazar Khaganate, was one of the great powers of the early medieval steppe, acting as a buffer between the Arab Caliphate and the peoples of the north.

History

Emerging from the breakup of the Western Turkic Khaganate, the Khazars established their state by the mid-7th century, with capitals at Balanjar and later Atil on the Volga delta. They fought a long series of wars with the Umayyad and Abbasid Caliphates in the Caucasus, halting Arab expansion northward. Khazaria grew rich on trade along the Volga and the Silk Road, taxing goods moving between the Baltic, Byzantium, and the Islamic world.

Religion

The Khazar ruling class famously converted to Judaism, probably in stages during the 8th and 9th centuries — a conversion attested in the "Khazar Correspondence" exchanged between the Spanish Jewish courtier Hasdai ibn Shaprut and King Joseph of Khazaria. The general population remained religiously mixed, including pagans, Muslims, and Christians, with separate judges for each community's law.

Decline

The khaganate declined in the 10th century under pressure from the Rus' and the Pechenegs. Sviatoslav I of Kiev destroyed Atil in 968–969, and Khazaria faded from history by the 11th century. The extent of any genealogical connection between the Khazars and later Jewish populations remains a subject of scholarly and popular debate.
