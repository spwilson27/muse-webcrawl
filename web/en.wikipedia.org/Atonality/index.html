Atonality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


An atonal score excerpt

Atonality in its broadest sense is music that lacks a tonal center, or key. In this sense it includes music written from about 1908 onwards, and in particular the works of the Second Viennese School — Arnold Schoenberg, Anton Webern, and Alban Berg — in the years before the adoption of the twelve-tone technique.

History

The term "atonal" was coined in the early 20th century, often by critics hostile to the new music. Schoenberg himself disliked the term, preferring "pantonal", as he felt his music did not negate tonality but rather extended its possibilities. Landmark atonal works include Schoenberg's Three Piano Pieces, Op. 11 (1909), Erwartung (1909), and Pierrot Lunaire (1912).

Free atonality and twelve-tone music

The period of "free atonality" gave way in the early 1920s to Schoenberg's twelve-tone technique, which orders all twelve notes of the chromatic scale in a row so that no single pitch serves as a center. Later theorists distinguish atonal music organized by other principles, such as set theory.

Criticism and debate

Musicologists continue to debate the scope of the term; some apply "atonal" only to music lacking any pitch hierarchy, while others use it more loosely for music outside the major-minor system. Composers associated with atonality include Charles Ives (in some works) and later figures influenced by the Second Viennese School.
