The Dominican Order, officially the Order of Preachers (Latin: Ordo Praedicatorum, abbreviated OP), is a mendicant religious order of the Catholic Church founded by the Castilian priest Saint Dominic and approved by Pope Honorius III in December 1216. Founded to preach the Gospel and combat heresy — particularly the Cathars of southern France — the order placed an unusual emphasis on intellectual formation, and its members are called Dominicans or Black Friars (in England), after the black cloak worn over their white habit.

History

Dominic established the first community at Toulouse in 1215 and sent friars to Paris and Bologna, seat of the great universities, where the order flourished. Dominicans produced the medieval church's greatest scholastic theologians, including Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas, author of the Summa Theologiae. Members also served prominently — and controversially — as inquisitors in the medieval Inquisition. In the colonial era, Dominicans such as Bartolomé de las Casas defended the rights of indigenous peoples of the Americas.

Structure

The order includes friars (priests and brothers), contemplative nuns, apostolic sisters, and lay fraternities, governed by a Master of the Order elected for a nine-year term. Study remains central to the Dominican charism, summed up in the motto contemplare et contemplata aliis tradere — to contemplate and to hand on the fruits of contemplation.
