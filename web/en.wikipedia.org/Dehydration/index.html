Dehydration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physiology, dehydration is a lack of total body water, with an accompanying disruption of metabolic processes. It occurs when free water loss exceeds free water intake, usually due to exercise, disease, or high environmental temperature. Mild dehydration can also be caused by immersion diuresis, which may increase risk of decompression sickness in divers.

SymptomsThirst, dry mouth, dizziness, fatigue, decreased urine output
CausesDiarrhea, vomiting, burns, fever, sweating, insufficient intake
TreatmentOral rehydration therapy, intravenous fluids
SpecialtyEmergency medicine, nephrology

Signs and symptoms

Early signs of dehydration include thirst, dry mouth, headache, and dark-coloured urine. As fluid loss progresses, patients may develop dizziness, rapid heartbeat, low blood pressure, poor skin turgor, confusion, and in severe cases delirium, unconsciousness, and shock. Most causes of dehydration can be tied to diarrheal illness, vomiting, fever, excessive sweating, or failure to drink enough fluids.

Classification

Dehydration is classified by the tonicity of the fluid lost: hypotonic (primarily sodium loss), hypertonic (primarily water loss), and isotonic (equal water and sodium loss). Severity is graded by percentage of body weight lost — mild at roughly 1–2 percent, moderate at 5–6 percent, and severe at 7–10 percent or more.

Treatment

For mild to moderate dehydration, oral rehydration therapy with water and electrolytes is the treatment of choice; oral rehydration solution exploits glucose-sodium co-transport in the gut and has saved millions of lives in cholera and other diarrheal diseases. Severe dehydration requires rapid intravenous fluid replacement, typically with isotonic saline or lactated Ringer's solution.

Prevention

Prevention involves drinking sufficient fluids during exercise, hot weather, and illness, and prompt replacement of losses from diarrhea or vomiting, especially in infants, the elderly, and people with chronic illness.
