Trinity Sunday

Trinity Sunday celebrates the Christian doctrine of the Trinity: God the Father, God the Son, and God the Holy Spirit.
Observed byWestern Christians (Roman Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist, and others)
DateFirst Sunday after Pentecost
SignificanceCelebration of the Holy Trinity

Trinity Sunday is the first Sunday after Pentecost in the Western Christian liturgical calendar, and celebrates the Christian doctrine of the Trinity — the three Persons of God: the Father, the Son (Jesus Christ) and the Holy Spirit. In the Eastern Orthodox Church, Pentecost itself is also called Trinity Day, and the Sunday after Pentecost is observed as the Sunday of All Saints.

History

Devotion to the Trinity grew through the early and medieval Church, and votive Masses of the Trinity were celebrated on various days. Thomas Becket, Archbishop of Canterbury, is traditionally credited with promoting the observance: he was consecrated bishop on the Sunday after Pentecost in 1162 and ordered that the day be kept in honour of the Holy Trinity in his province. In 1334 Pope John XXII extended the feast to the whole Western Church and fixed it on the first Sunday after Pentecost.

Observance

In the Roman Catholic Church, Trinity Sunday is a solemnity, the highest rank of feast day. Its liturgy focuses on the mystery of the Trinity; the Athanasian Creed was traditionally recited at Prime on this day. In the Anglican tradition, the long season after Trinity Sunday — often simply called "Trinity" — stretches through the summer and autumn until Advent; the number of Sundays is counted as "Sundays after Trinity". Lutheran and Methodist churches likewise observe the day as the Feast of the Holy Trinity, after which the Sundays are counted as Sundays after Pentecost or in "Kingdomtide".

In the Church of England's traditional calendar, Trinity Sunday gives its name to the third term of the academic year at Oxford and Cambridge universities (Trinity term) and to the Trinity term of the legal year in England and Wales.
