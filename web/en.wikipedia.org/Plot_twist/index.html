Plot twist

A twist radically changes the direction or expected outcome of a plot.

A plot twist is a literary technique that introduces a radical change in the direction or expected outcome of the plot in a work of fiction. When it happens near the end of a story, it is often referred to as a twist ending. Plot twists may change the reader's perception of the preceding events, reveal that a character's identity or motives were other than they seemed, or reframe the entire narrative.

Mechanisms

Twists are achieved by several means. An unreliable narrator may deceive the audience, as in much detective fiction. Anagnorisis, or recognition, is the classical device in which a character discovers a hidden identity — Oedipus's recognition of his own guilt is Aristotle's prime example. Peripeteia is a sudden reversal of fortune. Other mechanisms include the withheld revelation (information the narrative has concealed), the false protagonist, the revelation that events were a dream or simulation, and the discovery that two characters are one, related, or have exchanged roles. Writers typically prepare twists with foreshadowing and clues that appear innocent on first reading, so that the twist feels surprising yet inevitable in retrospect.

Examples

Famous twists include the identity of the murderer in Agatha Christie's The Murder of Roger Ackroyd (1926), the reveal in the film The Empire Strikes Back (1980) that Darth Vader is Luke Skywalker's father, the nature of the protagonist's reality in The Sixth Sense (1999) and Fight Club (1999), and the ending of Planet of the Apes (1968). O. Henry's short stories popularised the ironic twist ending in prose fiction.

Reception

A twist that contradicts established facts or depends on information no audience could possess is often criticised as a cheat; the term "twist" is also used pejoratively for gimmicky reversals. The cultural prominence of twists has shaped marketing and reviewing practices, including spoiler warnings designed to preserve the surprise for new audiences.
