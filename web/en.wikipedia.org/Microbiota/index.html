Microbiota
ComponentsBacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses, protists
HabitatsGut, skin, soil, oceans, plant roots
Related termMicrobiome (the community plus its genes/environment)

Microbiota are the range of microorganisms that live in a specific environment, including bacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses and other microscopic life. The term is used for communities in environments as varied as the human body, soil, the oceans and the surfaces of plants. The microbiome is often used for the collective genomes of the microbiota, or for the community together with its habitat.

Human microbiota

The human body hosts trillions of microorganisms, with the largest community in the gut. The gut microbiota aids digestion, synthesises vitamins such as B12 and K, trains the immune system and protects against pathogens. Its composition is shaped by birth mode, diet, antibiotics and environment, and disruptions (dysbiosis) have been linked with conditions including inflammatory bowel disease, obesity and allergies. Research projects such as the Human Microbiome Project have catalogued the microbes of healthy adults.

Plant and soil microbiota

Plants host microbiota on roots (the rhizosphere), leaves and internal tissues that influence nutrient uptake, growth and resistance to disease. Soil microbiota drive nutrient cycling, decomposing organic matter and fixing nitrogen, and are central to soil health and agriculture.

Methods of study

Because most microbes cannot easily be cultured, microbiota are studied chiefly through DNA sequencing: marker-gene surveys such as 16S rRNA sequencing, and shotgun metagenomics, which sequences all DNA in a sample.
