The Battle of Passchendaele, also known as the Third Battle of Ypres, was a campaign of the First World War fought by the Allies against the German Empire from 31 July to 10 November 1917 on the Western Front in Belgium. The British Expeditionary Force under Field Marshal Sir Douglas Haig sought to break out of the Ypres Salient, seize the Passchendaele ridge and ultimately the Belgian coast and its U-boat bases. Exceptionally wet weather — the heaviest rain in decades — turned the shell-churned battlefield, whose drainage had been destroyed by bombardment, into a swamp in which men and animals drowned. The ruined village of Passchendaele was finally taken by Canadian troops in November. The battle became a byword for the futility and horror of trench warfare.

Background and plan

After the failures of 1915–16, Haig proposed a Flanders offensive to wear down the German army, exploit the success of the Battle of Messines in June 1917, and clear the coast. The plan was approved despite reservations in the British government. Preliminary bombardment of unprecedented weight — over four million shells — opened in mid-July.

The battle

The infantry assault began on 31 July (Battle of Pilckem Ridge) with modest gains, after which rain began. Through August, General Gough's Fifth Army made repeated costly attacks in the mud. In September, command of the main effort passed to General Plumer's Second Army, whose methodical "bite-and-hold" operations — limited advances behind creeping barrages, as at Menin Road (20 September), Polygon Wood (26 September) and Broodseinde (4 October) — achieved a series of successes in drier weather. The rain returned in October; attacks at Poelcappelle and the First Battle of Passchendaele (12 October, a disaster for the New Zealand Division, its worst day in the country's military history) foundered in the swamp. The Canadian Corps under General Arthur Currie then took over and, in four deliberate attacks between 26 October and 10 November, captured Passchendaele village and the ridge.

Outcome and legacy

The Allies gained about 8 km (5 mi) of ground at a cost of roughly a quarter of a million casualties on each side; the strategic objectives — the coast ports and U-boat bases — were never approached. Historians remain divided: traditional accounts condemn Haig's persistence in impossible conditions, while revisionists emphasise the battle's attritional effect on an increasingly strained German army, which could less afford its losses in 1917 than the Allies. "Passchendaele" and "the mud of Flanders" entered the language as emblems of the Great War's suffering, commemorated at the Menin Gate and Tyne Cot Cemetery.
