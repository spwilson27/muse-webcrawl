Rhyton


A rhyton with a zoomorphic terminal

A rhyton (plural rhytons or rhyta) is a roughly conical drinking container, usually terminating in the shape of an animal's head or horn, from which liquid — typically wine — flowed out of a small hole at the bottom tip. The word comes from the Greek rhyton, connected with the verb "to flow". Rhytons were used in drinking rituals and feasts across the ancient world, from the Bronze Age Aegean and Anatolia to Achaemenid Persia and the Eurasian steppe.

Form and use

A rhyton cannot be set down when full, since the hole in its tip lets the liquid escape; the drinker either drank the entire contents in one draught or stopped the hole with a finger, and in some ceremonial uses the stream was directed into another vessel or onto the ground as a libation. Many rhytons end in the forepart (protome) of an animal — a bull, ram, horse, lion, griffin or stag — while others are shaped as human or mythological figures.

History and distribution

Early examples come from Minoan Crete, including stone and faience rhytons from palaces such as Knossos. The form flourished in Achaemenid Persia, where gold and silver rhytons were prestige objects of courtly culture, and spread widely through Greek, Thracian, Scythian and later Parthian and Sasanian art. Thracian hoards such as the Panagyurishte Treasure include elaborate gold rhytons. The type persisted into the early medieval period in the Near East and influenced drinking horns of northern Europe.
