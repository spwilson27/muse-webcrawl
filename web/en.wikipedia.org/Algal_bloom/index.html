Algal bloom

Causes: Excess nutrients (eutrophication), warm water, sunlight
Effects: Oxygen depletion, toxins, dead zones

An algal bloom or algae bloom is a rapid increase or accumulation in the population of algae in freshwater or marine water systems. It is often recognized by the discoloration of the water — green, red, or brown — resulting from the high density of pigmented cells. Blooms may occur naturally, but their frequency and severity have increased worldwide due to nutrient pollution.

Causes

Blooms are fuelled by an abundance of nutrients, particularly phosphorus and nitrogen from agricultural runoff, wastewater, and fertilizers — a process called eutrophication. Warm temperatures, strong sunlight, and calm or slow-moving water further promote growth. In the ocean, upwelling currents can trigger natural blooms that underpin marine food webs.

Harmful algal blooms

Some blooms are harmful: certain cyanobacteria and dinoflagellates produce toxins (such as microcystins and saxitoxins) that poison fish, shellfish, marine mammals, and people. "Red tides" caused by dinoflagellates like Karenia brevis afflict the Gulf of Mexico, while cyanobacterial blooms have repeatedly threatened drinking water — Toledo, Ohio, shut down its water supply in 2014 after toxins appeared in Lake Erie. When bloom biomass decays, bacterial decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen, creating hypoxic "dead zones" that suffocate aquatic life.

Management

Long-term control focuses on reducing nutrient loading from farms and wastewater treatment plants. Monitoring by satellite and in-situ sampling provides early warning for fisheries and water utilities, and climate change — warming waters and altering rainfall — is expected to make blooms more frequent.
