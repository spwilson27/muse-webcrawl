Legal aid

Legal aid is the provision of legal assistance to people who are otherwise unable to afford legal representation or access to the court system. It is regarded in many jurisdictions as central to ensuring access to justice, by providing advice, assistance and representation to those without sufficient means, and in criminal cases is often a constitutional or human-rights entitlement.

History

Charitable and court-appointed representation for the poor has ancient roots, but modern publicly funded legal aid emerged in the twentieth century. In the United States, the Supreme Court's decision in Gideon v. Wainwright (1963) held that the Sixth Amendment requires states to provide counsel to criminal defendants who cannot afford a lawyer, leading to the creation of public defender systems. Federally funded civil legal aid followed with the Legal Services Corporation in 1974. In England and Wales, the Legal Aid and Advice Act 1949 created a comprehensive scheme, since administered (now by the Legal Aid Agency) subject to means and merits tests, and substantially cut back by reforms in 2012.

Models of delivery

  • Staff model — salaried lawyers employed by a public defender office or legal aid agency handle cases.
  • Judicare / panel model — private lawyers take cases and bill the state per matter.
  • Pro bono and clinics — private practitioners volunteer services, and law-school clinics and community law centres provide free advice, often in civil fields such as housing, immigration, family and welfare benefits.

Debates

Legal aid systems face recurring tension over funding, eligibility thresholds, and quality. Critics of cuts argue they produce "advice deserts", raise rates of unrepresented litigants, and can be false economies when unresolved legal problems escalate; governments respond that budgets are finite and aid must be targeted at the most serious cases. International human rights law, including Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights, guarantees legal assistance in criminal cases where the interests of justice require it and the defendant lacks sufficient means.
