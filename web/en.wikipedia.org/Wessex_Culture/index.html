Wessex culture
Period: Early Bronze Age
Dates: c. 2000–1400 BC
Region: Southern Britain (Wessex)
Type site: Wiltshire

The Wessex culture is the predominant prehistoric culture of central and southern Britain during the early Bronze Age, originally defined by archaeologist Stuart Piggott in 1938. It is known principally from its rich burials under round barrows on the chalk downlands of Wiltshire, Dorset, and Hampshire, in the region later known as the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of Wessex.

Burials

The culture is divided into Wessex I and Wessex II phases. Its graves — such as the Bush Barrow near Stonehenge — contained exceptionally rich goods: gold ornaments including lozenge-shaped plaques, bronze daggers with ornate hilts, amber and jet necklaces, and faience beads suggesting trade links with the Mycenaean world and the Baltic. Inhumation predominated in Wessex I, giving way to cremation in cemeteries of the Deverel–Rimbury type in Wessex II.

Interpretation

The wealth of the burials was once taken to indicate a chiefdom or aristocracy controlling trade through the Stonehenge–Avebury monumental landscape. Modern scholarship treats the "culture" label more cautiously, regarding the rich graves as expressions of a wide exchange network rather than a single unified society.
