Friedrich Ebert (4 February 1871 – 28 February 1925) was a German politician of the Social Democratic Party (SPD) who served as the first President of Germany from 1919 until his death in 1925. A trained saddler who rose through the trade-union movement, Ebert became leader of the SPD in 1913 and guided the party through the First World War and the German Revolution of 1918–19.

Early life

Ebert was born in Heidelberg, the seventh of nine children of a master tailor. After an apprenticeship as a saddler he travelled as a journeyman, joined the SPD in 1889, and became a trade-union organiser and journalist in Bremen. In 1905 he moved to Berlin as a party secretary and was elected to the Reichstag in 1912. On the death of August Bebel in 1913, Ebert became party chairman jointly with Hugo Haase.

Revolution and chancellorship

During the First World War Ebert supported the party's Burgfrieden (political truce) policy, a stance that split the SPD in 1917. On 9 November 1918, amid military collapse and revolution, Chancellor Prince Max of Baden handed the chancellorship to Ebert, who proclaimed — against his own preference — the German Republic through Philipp Scheidemann's famous announcement. As head of the Council of the People's Deputies, Ebert struck an agreement with the army high command (the Ebert–Groener pact) and ordered elections to a National Assembly.

Presidency

The Weimar National Assembly elected Ebert the first Reichspräsident in February 1919. His presidency was marked by crisis: the Kapp Putsch of 1920, political assassinations, hyperinflation in 1923, and repeated reliance on presidential emergency decrees under Article 48 of the Weimar Constitution, which he invoked 136 times. Ebert sought to defend the young republic against extremes of left and right.

Ebert died in office on 28 February 1925 of appendicitis whose treatment had been delayed. He was succeeded by Paul von Hindenburg. The Friedrich Ebert Foundation, affiliated with the SPD, is named in his honour.
