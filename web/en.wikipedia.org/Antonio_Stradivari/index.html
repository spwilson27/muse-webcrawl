Antonio Stradivari

Born1644, Cremona, Lombardy
Died18 December 1737 (aged 92–93), Cremona
OccupationLuthier
Known forStradivarius violins, violas, and cellos

Antonio Stradivari (1644 – 18 December 1737) was an Italian luthier of Cremona and the most celebrated maker of stringed instruments in history. Over a career of more than seventy years he produced some 960 instruments — violins, violas, cellos, guitars, and harps — of which roughly 650 survive. His Latinised surname, Stradivarius, and the label borne by his instruments have become bywords for supreme craftsmanship; the finest examples, such as the 1716 "Messiah" and the 1715 "Alard," command prices in the tens of millions of dollars at auction.

Life

Stradivari was born in Cremona, then under Spanish rule, probably in 1644, and likely apprenticed in the workshop of Nicolò Amati, the leading Cremonese maker of the day; labels on his earliest violins (from 1666) describe him as Amati's pupil. In 1680 he purchased a house with a workshop on the Piazza San Domenico in Cremona, where he worked for the rest of his long life, assisted in later years by his sons Francesco and Omobono. He married twice and died in 1737, at over ninety years of age, still active at the bench.

Work and "golden period"

Stradivari's early instruments follow Amati's patterns. In the 1690s he experimented with a larger "long pattern" model, then returned around 1700 to a broadened grand pattern that defines his golden period (c. 1700–1725), producing instruments of exceptional power, brilliance, and finish, including the "Betts" (1704), "Alard" (1715), and "Messiah" (1716) violins and the "Davidoff" and "Duport" cellos. Much research has been devoted to his varnish, wood selection (spruce and maple, often from the Fiemme Valley), and arching, though no single "secret" has been identified; modern double-blind studies have found that elite players sometimes cannot distinguish his instruments from fine modern ones.

Legacy

About 650 Stradivari instruments survive and are played by leading soloists, frequently on loan from foundations and collectors. Record auction prices include the "Lady Blunt" violin (sold in 2011 for £9.8 million for Japanese tsunami relief) and the "da Vinci, ex-Seidel" (sold in 2022 for $15.3 million). The Museo del Violino in Cremona preserves his workshop tools, moulds, and drawings, and Cremona's violin-making tradition is inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
