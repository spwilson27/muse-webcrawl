Universal health care

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A public hospital ward illustrating publicly funded care — placeholder]

Universal health care (also called universal health coverage, universal coverage, or universal care) is a health care system in which all residents of a particular country or region are assured access to health care. It is generally organized around providing either all residents or only those who cannot afford care on their own with either health services or the means to acquire them, with the ultimate goal of improved health outcomes.

Models

Single-payer

In a single-payer system, a single public agency handles health care financing while care delivery remains largely in private hands. Canada's Medicare system is a prominent example: each province operates a public insurance plan that pays for medically necessary services.

Social health insurance

Countries such as Germany use competing non-profit "sickness funds" financed by compulsory payroll contributions from employers and employees, a model originating with Otto von Bismarck's legislation of the 1880s.

National health service

The United Kingdom's National Health Service (NHS), founded in 1948 under Aneurin Bevan, is funded through general taxation and most services are provided by publicly owned facilities, free at the point of use.

Mixed systems

Many countries combine public funding with private insurance and delivery. Australia, France, and the Netherlands use hybrids in which a public core is supplemented by regulated private insurers.

History

Germany introduced the first national social health insurance system in 1883. After the Second World War, universal systems were established across Western Europe, and the World Health Organization has promoted universal health coverage as a central goal, reflected in the Sustainable Development Goals. The United States remains the most prominent developed country without a universal system, relying on a mix of employer-sponsored insurance, private markets, and public programs such as Medicare and Medicaid.

Debates

Supporters argue that universal coverage improves population health, removes financial barriers to care, and can control costs through administrative simplicity. Critics point to waiting times, rationing, and the tax burden in some systems. Comparative studies generally find that countries with universal systems achieve comparable or better health outcomes at substantially lower per-capita spending than the United States.
