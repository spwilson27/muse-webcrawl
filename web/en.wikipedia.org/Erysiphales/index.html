The Erysiphales are an order of ascomycete fungi, containing the single family Erysiphaceae, whose members are known collectively as the powdery mildews. They are obligate parasites of flowering plants: they cannot complete their life cycle away from a living host, and together they attack some 10,000 species of angiosperms, including major crops such as wheat, barley, grapevine, cucurbits, and roses.

Biology

Powdery mildews grow mainly on the surface of leaves and stems as a white, powdery mat of mycelium. Specialized feeding structures called haustoria penetrate the host's epidermal cells to extract nutrients without killing them immediately. The powdery appearance comes from vast numbers of asexual spores (conidia) produced in chains; unlike most fungi, these spores can germinate without free water, so powdery mildews thrive in warm, dry weather with humid nights — the opposite of most leaf pathogens.

Sexual reproduction produces small spherical fruiting bodies called chasmothecia (formerly cleistothecia), which bear sacs (asci) of ascospores and often carry distinctive branched appendages used in identification. These structures help the fungi survive winter or dry seasons on plant debris and bark.

Importance

Powdery mildews are among the most common and economically damaging plant diseases. Cereal powdery mildew (Blumeria graminis) and grape powdery mildew (Erysiphe necator) require extensive fungicide use and continuous breeding of resistant crop varieties. Control relies on resistant cultivars, sulphur and synthetic fungicides, and cultural practices that improve air circulation.
