Sisal

Sisal Species: Agave sisalana
Origin: Southern Mexico (Yucatán)
Major producers: Brazil, Tanzania, Kenya
Uses: Twine, rope, carpets, dartboards, paper

Sisal is a species of flowering plant native to southern Mexico, Agave sisalana, and the stiff fibre obtained from its leaves. The plant is widely cultivated in tropical countries for fibre production. The name sisal derives from the port of Sisal in Yucatán, from which the fibre was historically shipped.

Cultivation

Sisal plants consist of a rosette of sword-shaped leaves, typically 1.5 to 2 metres long, each ending in a sharp spine. Plants are propagated from bulbils or suckers and reach harvestable maturity in about three to five years. Leaves are harvested by hand or machine, and the fibre is extracted by decortication, in which the leaf is crushed and beaten to remove the pulp, after which the fibre is washed, dried in the sun and brushed.

Uses

Sisal fibre is traditionally used for twine, especially baler twine for agriculture, as well as rope, sacks, carpets, matting, and the cores of wire ropes. The best dartboards are made from compressed sisal fibre, into which darts penetrate and partially self-heal. Sisal is also used as a reinforcement in composite materials, in speciality paper, and increasingly in geotextiles and automotive components as a natural alternative to synthetic fibres and fibreglass.

Production

Brazil is the world's largest producer of sisal, followed by Tanzania and Kenya; Mexico, Madagascar, China and Haiti also produce significant quantities. World production declined sharply in the late twentieth century as polypropylene twine replaced sisal in agriculture, but demand has partially recovered with interest in biodegradable natural fibres.
