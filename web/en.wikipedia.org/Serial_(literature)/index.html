Serial (literature)

A serial novel published in monthly parts

In literature, a serial is a printing or broadcasting format by which a single larger work, often a work of narrative fiction, is published in smaller, sequential installments. The installments may be known as numbers, parts or fascicles, and may be released either as separate publications or within sequential issues of a periodical publication, such as a magazine or newspaper.

Serialization became a dominant mode of publishing fiction in the nineteenth century, when the high cost of books and the rise of cheap periodicals made part-publication commercially attractive. Charles Dickens popularized the format in Britain with works such as The Pickwick Papers (1836–37), and many Victorian novels first reached readers in monthly or weekly parts before appearing in book form.

History

Early examples of serialized publication appeared in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, but the format flourished after the 1830s. Dickens's success with The Pickwick Papers, issued in twenty monthly parts, demonstrated that a novel could be sold profitably in shilling installments. Other major novelists who published serially include William Makepeace Thackeray, George Eliot, Anthony Trollope, Thomas Hardy and Fyodor Dostoevsky, whose novels appeared in Russian journals such as The Russian Messenger.

In France, the roman-feuilleton appeared at the foot of newspaper pages; Alexandre Dumas's The Count of Monte Cristo and The Three Musketeers were both serialized in 1844. In the United States, magazines such as Harper's Weekly and The Atlantic Monthly serialized novels by authors including Mark Twain and Henry James.

Characteristics

Serial publication shaped the structure of the works themselves. Authors writing in installments often ended each part with a cliffhanger or revelation to sustain reader interest, and the need to maintain a regular rhythm of incident influenced pacing and subplot structure. Because later installments were written while earlier ones were already in print, authors sometimes adjusted storylines in response to sales figures or reader reaction.

Modern serials

Although the Victorian part-issue largely disappeared in the twentieth century, serialization persists in comic books, manga published in weekly anthologies, online fiction platforms, and episodic digital publishing. Television drama adopted the serial form in soap operas and later in prestige drama series with continuing story arcs.
