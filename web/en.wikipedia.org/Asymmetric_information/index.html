Asymmetric information

Asymmetric information, also known as "information failure," occurs in a transaction when one party has more or better information than the other. Such imbalance can distort markets, causing good products to be driven out or preventing mutually beneficial trades. The concept is central to information economics.

Adverse selection

In his 1970 paper "The Market for Lemons," George Akerlof showed that if buyers cannot distinguish good used cars from defective "lemons," they will pay only an average price, driving good cars out of the market. This hidden-information problem before a transaction is called adverse selection.

Moral hazard

Moral hazard arises after a transaction, when one party can take hidden actions — for example, an insured person behaving more recklessly, or an employee shirking — because the other party cannot observe them.

Responses

Markets and institutions respond with signalling (such as education credentials or warranties), screening (such as insurance menus or interviews), regulation and disclosure requirements, reputation, and third-party certification. Akerlof, Michael Spence, and Joseph Stiglitz shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for their analyses of markets with asymmetric information.
