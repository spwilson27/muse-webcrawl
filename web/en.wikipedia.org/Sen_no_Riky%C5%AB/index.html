Sen no Rikyū
Born: 1522, Sakai, Japan
Died: 21 April 1591, Kyoto
Occupation: tea master
Known for: perfecting wabi-cha

Sen no Rikyū (千利休, 1522 – 21 April 1591), also known simply as Rikyū, is considered the historical figure with the most profound influence on the Japanese tea ceremony (chanoyu), particularly the tradition of wabi-cha, which he brought to its mature form.

Life and work

Rikyū was born in Sakai, a prosperous port city with a strong tea culture, and studied under the tea masters Kitamuki Dōchin and Takeno Jōō. He served as tea master first to Oda Nobunaga and then to Toyotomi Hideyoshi, becoming a close adviser with considerable political influence. He refined the austere aesthetic of wabi: small rustic tea houses (including the famous two-tatami Tai-an at Myōki-an), rough Raku tea bowls, bamboo utensils and a stripped-down etiquette emphasising harmony, respect, purity and tranquillity.

Death and legacy

In 1591, for reasons still debated — pride, political friction, or disputes over a wooden statue of himself placed at a temple gate — Hideyoshi ordered Rikyū to commit seppuku, which he did on 21 April 1591. His descendants founded the three schools of tea (Omotesenke, Urasenke and Mushakōjisenke) that still dominate the practice, and his influence extends across Japanese aesthetics, garden design and architecture.
