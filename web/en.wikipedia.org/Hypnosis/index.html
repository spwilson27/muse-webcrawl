Hypnosis is a human condition involving focused attention (the selective attention/sustained attention dichotomy), reduced peripheral awareness, and an enhanced capacity to respond to suggestion. It is usually induced by a procedure known as a hypnotic induction, typically involving a series of preliminary instructions and suggestions. Theories explaining what occurs during hypnosis fall into two broad groups: "state" theories, which treat hypnosis as an altered state of consciousness, and "non-state" theories, which explain hypnotic phenomena through ordinary psychological processes such as role-taking, expectation, and focused attention.

History

Hypnosis developed out of the 18th-century practice of mesmerism, associated with Franz Anton Mesmer's theory of "animal magnetism". The Scottish surgeon James Braid rejected the magnetic theory in the 1840s, coined the terms "hypnotism" and "hypnosis", and reinterpreted the phenomenon in physiological and psychological terms. Later in the 19th century, Jean-Martin Charcot at the Salpêtrière and Hippolyte Bernheim of the Nancy School disputed whether hypnosis was a pathological or a normal phenomenon. In the 20th century, Clark L. Hull's experimental work and Milton H. Erickson's indirect, permissive techniques reshaped research and clinical practice.

Applications

Clinical hypnosis (hypnotherapy) is used as an adjunct in managing pain, irritable bowel syndrome, anxiety, and some habit disorders; evidence of effectiveness varies by condition. Hypnotisability — the degree to which a person responds to hypnotic suggestions — is a stable, measurable trait assessed by standardised scales such as the Stanford Hypnotic Susceptibility Scales. Stage hypnosis exploits selection of highly suggestible volunteers and social pressure for entertainment. Popular claims that hypnosis can recover accurate "repressed memories" or compel behaviour against a person's will are not supported by evidence.
