Monopole antenna

A monopole antenna is a class of radio antenna consisting of a straight rod-shaped conductor, often mounted perpendicularly over some type of conductive surface, called a ground plane. The driving signal from the transmitter is applied between the lower end of the monopole and the ground plane, or, in the common quarter-wave monopole, the antenna is fed at its base against ground.

History

The monopole antenna was invented in 1895 by radio pioneer Guglielmo Marconi, who discovered that if he attached one terminal of his transmitter to a wire suspended in the air and the other to Earth, he could transmit for longer distances. For this reason it is sometimes called a Marconi antenna.

Radiation pattern

The monopole radiates an omnidirectional pattern in the plane perpendicular to its axis: radio waves are emitted with equal power in all azimuthal directions, with power falling off toward the vertical axis. Below the ground plane, radiation is negligible. A quarter-wave monopole over a perfect ground has the same pattern in the upper hemisphere as a half-wave dipole in free space, with twice the field strength for the same power.

A quarter-wave monopole with ground radials, widely used for mobile and broadcast antennas.

Impedance

The feedpoint impedance of a quarter-wave monopole over an infinite perfect ground is half that of a half-wave dipole — about 36.5 ohms — making it a convenient match to common 50-ohm and 75-ohm feed lines. Longer monopoles, such as the 5/8-wave type, offer slightly higher gain but require matching networks.

Applications

Monopoles are used for most omnidirectional radio services in the VHF and UHF bands: mobile phones' internal antennas, FM radio towers (as mast radiators), vehicle whip antennas, and handheld radios. At low frequencies, T-antennas and inverted-L antennas serve as practical shortened monopoles.
