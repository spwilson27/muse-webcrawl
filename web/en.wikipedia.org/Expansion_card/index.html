Expansion card

In computing, an expansion card (also called an expansion board, adapter card, peripheral card, or accessory card) is a printed circuit board that can be inserted into an electrical connector, or expansion slot, on a computer's motherboard to add functionality to the computer system. One edge of the card holds the contacts that fit exactly into the slot; a metal bracket often provides external ports.

Common types

Frequent expansion cards include graphics cards (GPUs), sound cards, network interface cards (Ethernet and Wi-Fi), storage controllers (RAID and additional SATA/NVMe interfaces), TV tuners, and USB or serial/parallel port adapters. Historically, modems and dedicated physics or capture cards were also common. As more functions were integrated onto motherboards and CPUs, the need for many types of expansion cards declined, leaving the graphics card as the dominant consumer expansion card.

Standards

Expansion bus standards evolved from the S-100 bus and the IBM PC's ISA bus through Micro Channel, EISA, VESA Local Bus, and PCI, to AGP for graphics, and finally PCI Express (PCIe), which uses serial point-to-point lanes and remains the standard interface; cards and slots come in ×1, ×4, ×8, and ×16 lane widths. Laptops and small devices use smaller form factors such as Mini PCIe and M.2, while external expansion is provided by standards like ExpressCard historically and Thunderbolt today.
