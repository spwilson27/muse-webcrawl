Weregild

Weregild (also wergild, wergeld, or weregeld; literally "man price") was a monetary value placed on every human being and every piece of property in the Salic Code and other Germanic legal codes. If property was stolen, or a person was injured or killed, the guilty party paid the weregild as restitution to the victim's family or to the owner of the property.

Purpose and scale

The institution provided an alternative to the blood feud: a kin group that accepted the weregild renounced vengeance, and payment restored legal peace. Amounts varied strictly by social rank — the weregild of a king or nobleman was many times that of a common freeman, and the price of a slave was the lowest. Early English law codes, beginning with that of Æthelberht of Kent (c. 602), specified tariffs for killings and for individual injuries to nearly every part of the body. A related but distinct payment, the bōt, compensated for lesser offences, while the wīte was a fine paid to the king or lord.

Decline

As royal justice grew stronger in the later Middle Ages, homicide increasingly became a plea of the Crown punishable by death or outlawry, and composition payments faded from use, though echoes of the idea survive in modern wrongful-death damages.
