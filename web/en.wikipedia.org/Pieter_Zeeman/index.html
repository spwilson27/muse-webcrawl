Pieter Zeeman

Pieter Zeeman (25 May 1865 – 9 October 1943) was a Dutch physicist who shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1902 with Hendrik Lorentz for his discovery of the Zeeman effect, the splitting of spectral lines in the presence of a magnetic field.

The Zeeman effect

In 1896, working at Leiden, Zeeman observed that the spectral lines of a light source placed in a strong magnetic field broaden and split into polarized components. Lorentz's classical electron theory explained the effect and allowed an early estimate of the electron's charge-to-mass ratio, shortly before J. J. Thomson's identification of the electron. The "anomalous" Zeeman splitting patterns that defied classical explanation later became key evidence for electron spin and quantum mechanics. Astronomers use the effect to measure magnetic fields in the Sun and other stars.

Career

Zeeman became professor at the University of Amsterdam in 1900 and directed its physics laboratory, rebuilt as the Zeeman Laboratory in 1923. His precision experiments included measurements of the speed of light in moving media, testing special relativity.
