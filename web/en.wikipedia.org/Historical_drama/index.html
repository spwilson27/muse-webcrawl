Historical drama

A historical drama (also period drama, and in theatre a history play) is a work of drama, film or television set in an earlier historical period, whose plot draws on real events and persons or on fictional characters placed within a documented past. The genre is characterised by its investment in period detail — costume, sets, manners and speech — and by the tension it maintains between historical fidelity and dramatic licence.

History

Historical drama is among the oldest dramatic forms: Shakespeare's English history plays (Henry V, the Richard III tradition) and his Roman plays established durable models of the genre, mixing documented events with invented scenes and speeches. The 19th century cultivated historical opera and the historical novel's stage adaptations; Walter Scott's novels shaped both. Cinema took up the genre early — from Intolerance (1916) and The Birth of a Nation (1915) through the studio-era epics of Cecil B. DeMille — and television later made the period serial a staple, from I, Claudius (1976) to Downton Abbey and The Crown.

Approaches and debate

Works range from near-documentary reconstructions to "heritage" dramas prized for their pictorial evocation of the past and to openly revisionist treatments that use history to comment on the present. Historians frequently debate the accuracy of popular productions — compressed timelines, composite characters, invented dialogue — while defenders argue that emotional and structural truth justify departures from the record. Wartime and biographical films (Schindler's List, 12 Years a Slave, Oppenheimer) are prominent modern subgenres, and the historical epic remains one of the most decorated categories at major film awards.

Related forms

Closely related genres include the costume drama, emphasising dress and setting over documented events; the swashbuckler; the East Asian forms jidaigeki (Japanese period drama) and Chinese historical television series; and alternate history, which departs from the record deliberately at a point of divergence.
