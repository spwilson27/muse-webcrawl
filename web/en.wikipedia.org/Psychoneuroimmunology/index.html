Psychoneuroimmunology

Psychoneuroimmunology (PNI) is the study of interactions between psychological processes, the nervous system and the immune system. The field examines how stress and emotions influence immune function and susceptibility to disease, and conversely how immune activity, through cytokines and inflammation, affects mood and cognition.

History

The field is usually dated to Robert Ader and Nicholas Cohen's 1975 demonstration of classically conditioned immunosuppression in rats, which showed that the brain and immune system communicate. Ader coined the term psychoneuroimmunology in 1980. Earlier clinical observations, including George Solomon's work on "psychoimmunology" in the 1960s, anticipated the field.

Findings

Chronic stress is associated with slower wound healing, reduced vaccine responses and reactivation of latent viruses, mediated partly by the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and sympathetic nervous system. Immune activation can produce "sickness behaviour" — fatigue, social withdrawal and low mood — and chronic inflammation is implicated in some cases of depression. PNI findings have informed behavioural interventions for patients with cancer, HIV and autoimmune conditions, though claims in popular mind–body literature sometimes exceed the evidence.
