Huguenots

The Huguenot cross, symbol of the French Reformed Protestants known as Huguenots.

The Huguenots were French Protestants affiliated with the Reformed (Calvinist) tradition during the 16th and 17th centuries. At their peak they comprised perhaps ten percent of the French population. Persecuted by the Catholic monarchy, they fought the French Wars of Religion, gained limited toleration under the Edict of Nantes in 1598, and were suppressed again when Louis XIV revoked the edict in 1685, prompting a mass exodus.

Origins

Reformed ideas spread in France from the 1520s, influenced by John Calvin, a Frenchman exiled in Geneva. The origin of the name "Huguenot" is uncertain; it may derive from a conflation of German Eidgenosse ("confederate") with other terms. The first national synod of the Reformed Church of France met in Paris in 1559.

Wars of Religion

Between 1562 and 1598, France was convulsed by civil wars between Catholic and Huguenot factions. The worst atrocity was the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of 1572, in which thousands of Huguenots were killed in Paris and the provinces. The wars ended when the Protestant Henry of Navarre converted to Catholicism and, as Henry IV, issued the Edict of Nantes.

Revocation and diaspora

Louis XIV's Edict of Fontainebleau (1685) outlawed Protestantism and unleashed the dragonnades against remaining Huguenots. An estimated 200,000 to 400,000 fled to England, the Netherlands, Prussia, Switzerland, South Africa, and North America, taking skills in textiles, finance, and watchmaking that enriched their host countries. Those who stayed formed the clandestine "Church of the Desert." Protestantism was legalized again during the French Revolution.
