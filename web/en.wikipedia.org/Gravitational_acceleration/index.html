Gravitational acceleration

In physics, gravitational acceleration is the acceleration of an object in free fall within a vacuum, caused by the gravitational field of a massive body. It is commonly denoted by g. Near the Earth's surface its value is approximately 9.81 m/s², and the conventional standard value, gn, is defined as exactly 9.80665 m/s².

Newton's law

Under Newtonian gravity, the acceleration at a distance r from a mass M is g = GM/r², where G is the gravitational constant. Because it depends only on the attracting mass and the distance, all objects at the same location accelerate equally in vacuum regardless of their own mass — a fact demonstrated by Galileo's falling-body experiments and, famously, by astronaut David Scott dropping a hammer and a feather together on the Moon in 1971.

Variation on Earth

The measured value of g varies over the Earth's surface, from about 9.78 m/s² at the equator to about 9.83 m/s² at the poles, owing to the Earth's rotation and its oblate shape, and decreases with altitude. Local geology produces small anomalies exploited in gravimetry and prospecting.

Other bodies

Surface gravity differs widely across the Solar System: about 3.71 m/s² on Mars, 1.62 m/s² on the Moon, and roughly 24.8 m/s² on Jupiter. The unit "g" derived from Earth's gravity is also used to express accelerations in aviation, engineering and spaceflight.
