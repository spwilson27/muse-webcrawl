Common-law marriage

Legal conceptMarriage without formal ceremony or license
OriginEnglish common law
Recognized inA minority of US states; Scotland (historically); some Commonwealth jurisdictions in limited forms

Common-law marriage, also known as non-ceremonial marriage or informal marriage, is a legally recognized marriage in which a couple is considered married despite never having obtained a marriage license or participated in a formal ceremony. Instead, the parties agree to be married, cohabit, and present themselves to the community as spouses.

Informal marriage in earlier common-law tradition

Elements

Where recognized, the typical requirements are: (1) a present agreement between the parties to be married; (2) cohabitation; and (3) holding out to the public as a married couple. Contrary to a persistent myth, there is generally no minimum period of cohabitation — living together for a set number of years does not by itself create a common-law marriage. Once validly formed, a common-law marriage is legally identical to a ceremonial marriage and can only be ended by divorce or death.

History

The concept descends from medieval English practice, in which marriages could be contracted by mutual consent without clergy. The Marriage Act 1753 largely abolished informal marriage in England and Wales, though the doctrine survived in parts of the British Empire and in the American colonies, where distance from clergy and courts made it practical.

United States

Most US states have abolished common-law marriage, but a small number — including Colorado, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Rhode Island, Texas, and the District of Columbia — still permit it, subject to statutory conditions. Under the Full Faith and Credit Clause, a valid common-law marriage formed in one state is generally recognized in others even if those states do not allow such marriages to be formed within their borders.

Elsewhere

Scotland retained "marriage by cohabitation with habit and repute" until its abolition in 2006. Various other jurisdictions recognize analogous institutions — such as de facto relationships in Australia and New Zealand — though these typically confer rights without treating the couple as formally married.
