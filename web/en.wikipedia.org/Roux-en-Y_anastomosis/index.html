Roux-en-Y anastomosis

TypeSurgical reconstruction
Named afterCésar Roux (1857–1934), Swiss surgeon
Used inGastric bypass, gastrectomy, biliary and pancreatic surgery

In general surgery, a Roux-en-Y anastomosis, less commonly termed an end-to-side enteroenterostomy, is a reconstruction in which the end of one segment of intestine is joined to the side of another, forming a Y-shaped configuration. It is named after the Swiss surgeon César Roux, who described the technique in the 1890s for use after gastrectomy.

Configuration

The bowel is divided, and the distal (downstream) end — the Roux limb or alimentary limb — is brought up and anastomosed end-to-side to the stomach remnant, oesophagus, bile duct, or pancreas, depending on the operation. The proximal end carrying bile and pancreatic secretions — the biliopancreatic limb — is anastomosed side-to-side further down the Roux limb, creating the "foot" of the Y (the jejunojejunostomy). Separating the two junctions allows food to bypass the secretions until they mix downstream and prevents bile reflux into the stomach or oesophagus.

Uses

The Roux-en-Y configuration is used in a variety of operations:

  • Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, one of the most common bariatric (weight-loss) operations, in which a small gastric pouch is connected to a Roux limb of jejunum, bypassing most of the stomach and the duodenum.
  • Reconstruction after total or partial gastrectomy for gastric cancer or peptic ulcer disease.
  • Hepaticojejunostomy and drainage procedures for biliary obstruction or injury.
  • Reconstruction after pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure) and drainage of pancreatic pseudocysts or chronic pancreatitis (Puestow-type variants).
  • Repair of congenital biliary atresia in the Kasai portoenterostomy.

Complications

Complications include anastomotic leak or stricture, internal hernia through the mesenteric defects created by the reconstruction (a cause of bowel obstruction after gastric bypass), marginal ulceration at the gastrojejunostomy, dumping syndrome, and "Roux stasis syndrome" — impaired motility of the Roux limb causing nausea and vomiting. In gastric bypass patients, the altered anatomy also predisposes to iron, vitamin B12, calcium, and vitamin D malabsorption, requiring lifelong supplementation and monitoring.
