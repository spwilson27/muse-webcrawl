Atmospheric circulation

Atmospheric circulation is the large-scale movement of air and together with ocean circulation is the means by which thermal energy is redistributed on the surface of the Earth. The Earth's atmospheric circulation varies from year to year, but the large-scale structure of its circulation remains fairly constant. The smaller-scale weather systems — mid-latitude depressions, or tropical convective cells — occur at random, and weather at a local scale cannot be predicted beyond about two weeks.

The three cells — Hadley, Ferrel and polar — in each hemisphere.

Latitudinal circulation

The large structure of the atmospheric circulation consists of three cells in each hemisphere. In the Hadley cell, air rises near the equator, flows poleward in the upper troposphere, descends in the subtropics around 30° latitude, and returns equatorward at the surface as the trade winds. The Ferrel cell operates in the mid-latitudes, and the polar cell carries air from the poles toward about 60° latitude, where it rises along the polar front. The pattern results from the unequal heating of the Earth combined with its rotation, which gives rise to the Coriolis effect.

Longitudinal circulation

Superimposed on the zonal (west–east) average circulation are longitudinal variations driven by the contrast between oceans and continents. The most important of these is the Walker circulation over the tropical Pacific, which is closely involved in the El Niño–Southern Oscillation. Monsoon circulations are another major longitudinal feature, reversing seasonally with the heating of large land masses.

Effects

The circulation cells explain the main global patterns of rainfall and surface wind: the wet equatorial belt of the Intertropical Convergence Zone, the dry subtropical high-pressure belts where most of the world's deserts lie, the prevailing westerlies of the mid-latitudes, and the polar easterlies.
