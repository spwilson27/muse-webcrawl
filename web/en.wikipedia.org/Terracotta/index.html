Terracotta

Terracotta, also known as terra cotta (Italian for "baked earth"), is a term used for relatively coarse, porous, unglazed or glazed earthenware made from fired clay. The word generally refers both to the material and to objects made from it, such as sculpture, pottery, roof tiles, bricks, and architectural ornament. Its colour varies from ochre through red-brown to buff, depending on the clay's iron content and the firing conditions.

History

Terracotta is among the oldest ceramic materials, used from prehistoric times for figurines and vessels. In ancient Greece, terracotta figurines — notably the Tanagra figurines of the Hellenistic period — were produced in moulds on a near-industrial scale. Terracotta was used extensively in ancient China, most famously for the Terracotta Army of the first Qin emperor (c. 210 BCE), thousands of life-size soldiers buried near Xi'an. In the Indus Valley and later Indian traditions, terracotta sculpture and ritual plaques were widespread, and Bengal temple architecture made extensive use of terracotta relief panels.

In Europe, terracotta was used for architectural sculpture in Etruscan temples, in Renaissance Italy (including the glazed work of the della Robbia family), and — as glazed architectural terra cotta — for the cladding of steel-frame buildings in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, where it provided fireproof, mouldable ornament.

Production

Terracotta is shaped from prepared clay by hand-building, throwing, moulding, or extrusion, then dried and fired in a kiln, typically at 1,000–1,100 °C. The fired body remains porous unless glazed; unglazed terracotta used outdoors, as in flowerpots and roof tiles, is frost-sensitive in severe climates. Colour derives mainly from iron oxide in the clay, which fires red in an oxidising atmosphere.

Uses

Current uses include flowerpots, roof tiles, bricks and pipes, tableware, and garden ornament. Artistically, terracotta remains a standard medium for sculpture models and finished works, valued for its warmth, low cost, and directness.
