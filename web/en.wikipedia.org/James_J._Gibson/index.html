James J. Gibson

Born27 January 1904, McConnelsville, Ohio, U.S.
Died11 December 1979, Ithaca, New York, U.S.
Known forEcological psychology; affordances

James Jerome Gibson (27 January 1904 – 11 December 1979) was an American psychologist and one of the most influential figures in the study of visual perception. He founded the field of ecological psychology and introduced the concept of affordances.

Career

Gibson taught at Smith College and later Cornell University. During World War II he directed research on visual perception for pilot training in the U.S. Army Air Forces, which shaped his interest in how observers perceive the world in motion, especially "optic flow".

Ecological approach

In The Perception of the Visual World (1950), The Senses Considered as Perceptual Systems (1966), and The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception (1979), Gibson argued that perception is direct: animals pick up information from the structured light of the environment without needing to construct internal representations. He proposed that the environment offers "affordances" — possibilities for action, such as a surface affording walking or an object affording grasping — which are perceived directly.

Influence

Gibson's ideas influenced psychology, human–computer interaction (notably through Donald Norman), design, robotics, and sports science, and continue to frame debates about embodied and enactive cognition.
