Natural rights and legal rights

Key thinkersThomas Hobbes, John Locke, Thomas Paine, Jeremy Bentham
Related conceptsNatural law, human rights, legal positivism

Natural rights are rights that, according to some philosophical and legal theories, individuals possess inherently by virtue of their nature, independent of any act of government or culture. Legal rights, by contrast, are rights bestowed upon a person by a legal system, and can be modified, repealed, or restrained by human laws. The distinction between the two has been central to political philosophy since the Enlightenment.

History

The idea of natural rights developed from the older concept of natural law in medieval theology and ancient philosophy. John Locke argued that individuals in the state of nature possess rights to life, liberty, and property, and that governments are instituted to protect them. These ideas were echoed in the United States Declaration of Independence, which proclaimed "unalienable Rights" including "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness," and in the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen.

Criticism

The utilitarian Jeremy Bentham famously dismissed natural rights as "nonsense upon stilts," arguing that rights are creatures of law alone. Modern debate continues over whether human rights — the contemporary descendants of natural rights theory — have foundations independent of legal enactment, and documents such as the Universal Declaration of Human Rights are often read through both frameworks.
