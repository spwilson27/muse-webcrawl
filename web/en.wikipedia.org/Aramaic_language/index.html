Aramaic is a Northwest Semitic language that originated among the Arameans of the ancient Near East. Over three thousand years, Aramaic served successively as the language of city-states, the administrative lingua franca of the Neo-Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian, and Achaemenid empires, and a major language of Judaism and early Christianity — it was the everyday language of Jesus and of parts of the books of Daniel and Ezra, as well as the language of the Talmuds and of Syriac Christian literature.

History

Old Aramaic inscriptions date from the tenth century BC. Imperial (Official) Aramaic spread with Assyrian and Persian administration. In the Hellenistic and Roman periods, written standards diverged into Western and Eastern branches, including Jewish Palestinian Aramaic, Samaritan Aramaic, Syriac, Babylonian Talmudic Aramaic, and Mandaic. Arabic gradually displaced Aramaic as a vernacular after the seventh century AD, but literary Syriac remained influential for centuries.

Modern Aramaic

Modern Aramaic languages (Neo-Aramaic) are still spoken by an estimated several hundred thousand people, chiefly Assyrian and Aramean Christians, Mandaeans, and some Jewish communities originating in northern Iraq, southeastern Turkey, northwestern Iran, and northeastern Syria, with large diasporas worldwide. Most varieties are endangered.
