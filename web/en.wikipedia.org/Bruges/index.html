Bruges

Bruges (Dutch: Brugge) is the capital and largest city of the province of West Flanders in the Flemish Region of Belgium, in the northwest of the country. Because of its many canals, it is sometimes called the "Venice of the North".

History

Bruges originated as a coastal settlement and grew into one of the most important commercial centres of medieval Europe. From the 12th to the 15th century, its port connected Hanseatic trade in the north with the cloth industry of Flanders and the markets of France and Italy. The city was a centre of Flemish primitive painting — artists such as Jan van Eyck and Hans Memling worked there — and the residence of the Dukes of Burgundy gave it a glittering court culture.

From about 1500, the silting of the Zwin inlet cut Bruges off from the sea, and trade shifted to Antwerp. The city declined into a quiet provincial backwater, which paradoxically preserved its medieval streetscape. In the 19th and 20th centuries it was revived as a tourist destination, assisted by the novel Bruges-la-Morte (1892) and the construction of the port of Zeebrugge.

Landmarks

  • The Belfry of Bruges, a 13th-century bell tower on the market square (Markt), rising 83 metres.
  • The Church of Our Lady, whose brick spire is among the tallest in the world and which houses Michelangelo's Madonna and Child.
  • The Basilica of the Holy Blood, the Groeningemuseum, and the canals and almshouses of the historic centre, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2000.

Economy and culture

Tourism dominates the modern economy; several million visitors arrive each year. Bruges is also known for lace-making, chocolate, and beer, and it hosts the College of Europe, a postgraduate institute of European studies.
