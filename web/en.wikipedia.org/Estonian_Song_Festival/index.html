The Estonian Song Festival (Estonian: Laulupidu) is one of the largest amateur choral events in the world, held every five years in Tallinn, in which joint choirs of tens of thousands of singers perform before audiences of more than a hundred thousand. Together with the song festivals of Latvia and Lithuania, it is inscribed on UNESCO's list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity as the "Baltic song and dance celebrations".

History

The first festival was organized in Tartu in 1869 by the journalist and national awakener Johann Voldemar Jannsen and the Vanemuine society, with 46 choirs and about 850 singers. The festivals became a central institution of the Estonian national awakening, sustaining Estonian language and identity through periods of Russification, German and Soviet occupation. Since 1928 the festival has been held at the Tallinn Song Festival Grounds; the present grounds, with their distinctive arched song stage designed by Alar Kotli, opened in 1960 and can hold some 15,000–25,000 singers at once, with later festivals fielding combined forces far larger.

The Singing Revolution

Mass singing became a vehicle of political resistance in the late 1980s: in 1988, huge crowds gathered at the festival grounds to sing forbidden patriotic songs in the movement known as the Singing Revolution, which helped lead to the restoration of Estonian independence in 1991. The festival's repertoire still mixes folk songs and new works, and beloved pieces such as "Mu isamaa on minu arm" ("My fatherland is my love") function almost as unofficial anthems, traditionally closing the festival.
