School segregation in the United States

School segregation in the United States refers to the separation of students in public schools by race. After the abolition of slavery, state laws in the South mandated racially separate schools, a system upheld by the Supreme Court's "separate but equal" doctrine in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896). In the North and West, segregation was more often de facto, arising from housing patterns, districting and private discrimination, though it affected Black, Mexican-American, Native American and Asian-American children as well.

Legal desegregation

The NAACP's litigation campaign, led by Thurgood Marshall, won a series of graduate-school cases before the landmark Brown v. Board of Education (1954), in which a unanimous Supreme Court held that "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal" and violated the Equal Protection Clause. Implementation met massive resistance: the Little Rock Nine required federal troops in 1957, and several states closed public schools rather than integrate.

Enforcement era

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 allowed federal funding cut-offs, and in Green v. County School Board (1968) and Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg (1971) the Court required affirmative dismantling of dual systems, approving busing as a remedy. Southern schools integrated substantially in the late 1960s and 1970s. But Milliken v. Bradley (1974) barred most cross-district remedies, insulating suburban systems, and later decisions (1990s–2007) released districts from desegregation orders and limited race-conscious assignment plans.

Contemporary patterns

Since the 1990s researchers have documented resegregation: the share of Black and Latino students attending intensely segregated schools has risen, driven by residential segregation, school-district boundaries, school choice and the end of court supervision. Studies link segregation to persistent gaps in funding, teacher experience and outcomes. Segregation by income has also increased, and debate continues over remedies within current constitutional limits.
