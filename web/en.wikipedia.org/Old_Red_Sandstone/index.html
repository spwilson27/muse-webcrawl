Old Red Sandstone

The Old Red Sandstone is an assemblage of sedimentary rocks in the North Atlantic region, largely of Devonian age. It is famously developed in the British Isles, where it gives its name both to a distinctive body of red-bed deposits and, historically, to the Devonian System itself. The term was brought into geological currency in the 19th century and is conventionally contrasted with the marine New Red Sandstone of Permo-Triassic age.

Distribution and setting

Old Red Sandstone deposits accumulated in the continental basins formed after the Caledonian Orogeny, within the landmass known as the Old Red Sandstone Continent or Euramerica (Laurussia). Outcrops occur across Scotland — notably in the Midland Valley, the Orcadian Basin of Caithness and Orkney, and the Anglo-Welsh Basin — as well as in Ireland, Norway, Greenland and the eastern seaboard of North America.

Lithology

The succession consists predominantly of red, brown and purple sandstones, conglomerates, siltstones and mudstones, deposited by rivers, in lakes and on alluvial plains under a seasonal, semi-arid climate. The red colour comes from haematite coatings on grains, formed by oxidation in the depositional environment.

Fossils and significance

The Old Red Sandstone is celebrated for its fossil fish faunas — jawless ostracoderms, acanthodians, placoderms and early lobe-finned fishes — which made it central to 19th-century debates on evolution; Hugh Miller's popular work The Old Red Sandstone (1841) brought the formation to wide public attention. The Rhynie chert of Aberdeenshire, an early Devonian hot-spring deposit within the sequence, preserves some of the earliest known land plants and terrestrial ecosystems in extraordinary detail.
