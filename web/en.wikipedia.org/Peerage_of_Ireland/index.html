Peerage of Ireland

The Peerage of Ireland consists of the nobility titles created by the English and later British monarchs in their capacity as Lord or King of Ireland. It comprises five ranks: duke, marquess, earl, viscount, and baron. Irish peerages were created from the Middle Ages until 1898, the last being the Barony of Curzon of Kedleston.

History

A small number of Irish titles date to the medieval Lordship of Ireland. Large numbers were created under the Tudors and Stuarts, and the sale of peerages under James I became a byword for corruption. Irish peers sat in the Irish House of Lords in Dublin until the Acts of Union 1800 abolished the Irish Parliament.

After the Union

Under the Act of Union, Ireland elected 32 representative peers—28 temporal and 4 spiritual—to sit in the United Kingdom House of Lords. Elections of temporal representative peers continued until 1919; the last surviving representative peer died in 1961. Uniquely, the Union settlement allowed one new Irish peerage to be created for every three that became extinct, a provision exercised until 1898.

Because Irish peers who were not representative peers were not automatically members of the House of Lords, many were able to sit in the House of Commons—a route famously used by Lord Palmerston, an Irish viscount who became British prime minister.

Present day

Following the establishment of the Irish Free State in 1922, no further Irish peerages were created and the remaining titles are purely hereditary honours. The premier Irish titles include the Duke of Leinster (premier duke, marquess, and earl) and the Earl of Waterford.
