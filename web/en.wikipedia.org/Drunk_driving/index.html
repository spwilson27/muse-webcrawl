Drunk driving

Drunk driving is the act of operating a motor vehicle while impaired by alcohol. It is a criminal offence in most jurisdictions, typically defined by a blood alcohol concentration (BAC) at or above a legal limit — commonly 0.08% in the United States and Canada, and lower limits such as 0.05% or 0.02% in many other countries. Related terms include driving under the influence (DUI), driving while intoxicated (DWI), and drink-driving in British English.

Effects and risk

Alcohol impairs judgement, reaction time, coordination, and vision. Crash risk rises measurably even at low BAC levels and increases steeply above the legal limit. Drunk driving is a leading factor in traffic deaths; in the United States, alcohol-impaired driving accounts for roughly one-third of traffic fatalities, killing more than 13,000 people in 2021 according to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration.

Detection and penalties

Police detect impairment through observation, field sobriety tests, and breath testing (breathalysers), with blood or urine tests used for confirmation. Penalties typically include fines, licence suspension, mandatory education or treatment, community service, and imprisonment, escalating for repeat offences or crashes causing injury or death. Many jurisdictions require ignition interlock devices for convicted drivers. Some countries, such as Sweden and Norway, enforce limits of 0.02%, and several apply zero tolerance to novice and professional drivers.

Prevention

Prevention campaigns by organisations such as Mothers Against Drunk Driving (MADD, founded 1980) helped raise the U.S. drinking age and lower legal limits. Other measures include sobriety checkpoints, server liability, designated-driver promotion, public transport, and ridesharing.
