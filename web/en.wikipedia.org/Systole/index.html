Systole

Systole is the phase of the cardiac cycle during which some chamber of the heart contracts. The term most often refers to ventricular systole, when the left and right ventricles contract and eject blood into the aorta and pulmonary artery. Its counterpart, when the heart muscle relaxes and the chambers refill, is diastole.

Phases

Ventricular systole begins when the ventricles start to contract, closing the atrioventricular (mitral and tricuspid) valves and producing the first heart sound ("lub"). During isovolumetric contraction, all valves are closed and ventricular pressure rises steeply. When ventricular pressure exceeds aortic and pulmonary artery pressure, the semilunar valves open and the ejection phase follows, expelling a stroke volume of roughly 70 mL from each ventricle in a resting adult.

Atrial systole occurs late in diastole, just before ventricular systole, and tops up ventricular filling; it contributes roughly 10–30% of ventricular volume, a fraction that becomes more important when ventricular compliance is reduced.

Electrical basis

Systole is initiated by the cardiac action potential spreading from the sinoatrial node through the atria, delayed at the atrioventricular node, and then conducted rapidly through the bundle of His and Purkinje fibres, producing coordinated ventricular contraction. On an electrocardiogram, ventricular systole follows the QRS complex.

Clinical significance

The peak arterial pressure during ventricular systole is the systolic blood pressure, normally about 120 mmHg in a healthy adult; sustained elevation defines hypertension. Impaired systolic contraction is the hallmark of systolic heart failure, assessed by ejection fraction on echocardiography.
