Eidolon (apparition)

In ancient Greek belief, an eidolon (Greek: εἴδωλον, "image, idol, phantom"; plural eidola) was a spirit-image of a living or dead person; a shade or phantom-lookalike of the human form. The concept of Helen of Troy's eidolon was used in Greek literature, including in Euripides' play Helen, which presents the idea that only an eidolon of Helen went to Troy, while the real Helen was spirited away to Egypt.

In Greek literature

In Homer's Iliad, the god Apollo creates an eidolon of Aeneas to mislead the combatants on the battlefield. In the Odyssey, Odysseus encounters the eidola of the dead in the underworld, insubstantial shades that retain the appearance of the living. Heracles, too, was said by some to have an eidolon in Hades while his true self feasted among the gods on Olympus. The concept allowed Greek poets to play with identity, appearance, and deception: an eidolon might be sent by the gods as a phantom double, bearing the form but not the substance of a person.

Helen's eidolon

The lyric poet Stesichorus, according to legend struck blind for slandering Helen, composed a palinode declaring that Helen never went to Troy at all. The philosopher Plato and the playwright Euripides adopted versions of the story: in Euripides' Helen (412 BC), the goddess Hera fashioned a phantom Helen out of air, which Paris carried off, while the real Helen waited chastely in Egypt throughout the Trojan War. The theme has fascinated later writers as an early exploration of illusion, reputation, and reality.

Later usage

The word passed into later literature in the sense of an ideal, phantom, or apparition. Walt Whitman titled a poem "Eidólons" (1876), surveying the soul's journey. In modern fantasy and gaming, eidolons appear as summoned spirits or phantom doubles, and the term is used in psychology and literary criticism for an idealised image of a person.
