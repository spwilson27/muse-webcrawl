Sumptuary law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sumptuary laws (from Latin sumptuariae leges) are laws that regulate consumption. Historically, they were an attempt to regulate and reinforce social hierarchies and morals through restrictions on clothing, food, and luxury expenditures, often depending on a person's social rank.

Communities in various cultures have used sumptuary laws for a variety of purposes. Historically, such laws were established as an attempt to control behaviour perceived as undesirable, discriminatory against a segment of the population, or designed to protect a protected industry's trade. They have existed in numerous cultures from ancient times to the present day.

History

Sumptuary laws were enacted in ancient Greece and Rome. In medieval Europe, such codes proliferated as growing merchant wealth threatened the visibility of aristocratic status: only certain classes were permitted to wear particular furs, silks, or colours, and violations could be punished by fines, confiscation, or public humiliation. England passed numerous statutes between the 14th and 16th centuries restricting cloth of gold, purple silk, and other luxuries to the nobility.

In Tokugawa Japan, detailed regulations governed the dress, housing, and consumption of each of the four recognised classes — samurai, peasants, artisans, and merchants — and were repeatedly reissued because wealthy merchants circumvented them. Colonial governments and religious authorities in many parts of the world imposed similar codes.

Purposes and criticism

Supporters justified sumptuary laws as preserving social order, discouraging extravagance and indebtedness, protecting domestic industries from foreign luxuries, and upholding religious morals. Critics, including Enlightenment thinkers such as Adam Smith, argued they were an improper interference with individual liberty and were in practice unenforceable. Most European sumptuary codes fell into disuse by the 18th and 19th centuries, though related regulations survive in modern rules on uniforms, official mourning, professional dress, and taxes on luxury goods.
