Total institution

Batch living in a total institution

A total institution is a place of work and residence where a great number of similarly situated people, cut off from the wider community for a considerable time, together lead an enclosed, formally administered life. The concept was popularized by the sociologist Erving Goffman in his 1957 paper "On the Characteristics of Total Institutions" and his 1961 book Asylums.

Characteristics

Goffman identified several defining features. All aspects of life are conducted in the same place under a single authority; daily activities are carried out in the immediate company of a large batch of others, all treated alike; activities are tightly scheduled and imposed from above; and the various enforced activities are integrated into a single rational plan. A sharp division exists between a large managed group ("inmates") and a small supervisory staff.

Examples

Goffman grouped total institutions into five rough categories: institutions caring for people felt to be incapable and harmless (such as homes for the elderly); places for people felt to be incapable yet an unintended threat (mental hospitals, tuberculosis sanitaria); institutions protecting the community against intentional dangers (prisons, concentration camps); institutions pursuing an instrumental task (army barracks, ships, boarding schools); and retreats from the world (monasteries, convents).

Mortification and adaptation

On admission, inmates undergo what Goffman called "mortification of the self": they are stripped of personal possessions, uniforms replace their own clothing, and institutional procedures reshape their identity. Inmates adapt through modes such as situational withdrawal, intransigence, colonization, and conversion, and they develop an "underlife" of unofficial practices that subvert the institution's formal order.
