Offender profiling

Offender profiling, also known as criminal profiling, is an investigative strategy used by law enforcement agencies to infer characteristics of an unknown offender from the analysis of a crime scene and the behaviour exhibited during the offence. It is most commonly applied to serial violent crimes such as homicide and sexual assault.

FieldForensic psychology / criminology
Key institutionsFBI Behavioral Analysis Unit
ApproachesCrime scene analysis; investigative psychology; geographic profiling

Profilers analyse victimology and crime-scene behaviour to narrow suspect pools.

History

Early profiling is associated with cases such as the Jack the Ripper murders, when police surgeon Thomas Bond offered an opinion on the offender's character. The modern discipline developed in the FBI's Behavioral Science Unit in the 1970s, where agents including John Douglas and Robert Ressler interviewed imprisoned serial offenders and developed the organised/disorganised typology of crime scenes.

Approaches

The FBI's crime scene analysis method proceeds through profiling inputs, decision-process models, crime assessment, and the production of a profile. In Britain, David Canter's investigative psychology emphasised statistical analysis of offender behaviour, and geographic profiling (developed by Kim Rossmo) analyses the spatial pattern of a crime series to predict the offender's home base.

Criticism

The scientific status of profiling is contested. Studies have found limited evidence that professional profilers outperform comparison groups, and the organised/disorganised dichotomy has been criticised as oversimplified. Profiling is generally regarded by courts as an investigative aid rather than evidence of guilt, and profiles are rarely admissible at trial.
