Konzo

Konzo
SpecialtyNeurology, public health
SymptomsSudden-onset spastic paraparesis; difficulty walking
CausesHigh dietary cyanide from insufficiently processed bitter cassava, with low protein intake

Konzo is an epidemic paralytic disease that occurs among poor rural communities in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Mozambique, Tanzania, Cameroon, the Central African Republic and Angola. The name, from the Yaka language, means "tied legs", describing the characteristic spastic gait of those affected.

Signs and symptoms

Konzo has a sudden onset, usually over less than a week, of symmetrical spastic weakness of the legs. In mild cases walking is possible without support but with a spastic gait; in severe cases the person cannot walk at all. The arms may also be affected in the most severe cases, and speech can be slurred. The disability is permanent but does not progress after the initial phase.

Cause

The disease is associated with chronic dietary exposure to cyanide from bitter cassava (manioc) that has not been adequately processed to remove cyanogenic glucosides such as linamarin, combined with a diet low in sulfur-containing amino acids needed to detoxify cyanide. Outbreaks typically occur during droughts or war, when food shortages force people to shorten cassava processing and eat a monotonous cassava diet.

Prevention

Konzo can be prevented by promoting effective cassava processing methods, such as the "wetting method" of flour treatment developed by Australian scientist J. Howard Bradbury, and by diversifying diets with protein sources. The World Health Organization recognises konzo as a disease of poverty linked to food insecurity.
