Quartz clock

A quartz clock is a clock that uses an electronic oscillator regulated by a quartz crystal to keep time. The crystal oscillator creates a signal with a precise frequency, which makes quartz clocks and watches much more accurate than good mechanical timepieces. The first quartz clock was built in 1927 by Warren Marrison and J. W. Horton at Bell Telephone Laboratories.

Mechanism

Quartz crystals are piezoelectric: they vibrate at a precise resonant frequency when a voltage is applied. In most watches the crystal is cut in the shape of a tiny tuning fork and oscillates at 32,768 Hz — a power of two, so that a simple chain of 15 binary dividers produces one pulse per second. This pulse drives a stepping motor or an electronic counter for a digital display. Quartz timepieces typically keep time to within about 15 seconds per month, and accuracy is limited mainly by temperature variation, which changes the crystal's resonant frequency slightly.

History

Through the mid-20th century quartz clocks were large laboratory instruments used as time standards. In 1969 Seiko introduced the Astron 35SQ, the first commercial quartz wristwatch, and in the 1970s cheap mass-produced quartz movements triggered the "quartz crisis" in the Swiss watch industry, which lost about two-thirds of its workforce as inexpensive and highly accurate Japanese and digital watches captured the market. By the 1980s quartz movements had become the dominant technology in clocks and watches worldwide.

Use

Quartz oscillators are found not only in clocks and watches but in virtually all digital electronics — computers, phones, radios — as clock signal generators.
