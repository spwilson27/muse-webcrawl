Epibatidine is a chlorinated alkaloid secreted by the Ecuadorian poison frog Epipedobates anthonyi (formerly placed in the genus Dendrobates). Isolated and characterised by John W. Daly and colleagues at the National Institutes of Health in the early 1990s, it proved to be an exceptionally potent non-opioid analgesic, acting on nicotinic acetylcholine receptors.

Pharmacology

Epibatidine is a powerful agonist of neuronal nicotinic receptors, notably the α4β2 subtype. In animal tests it showed antinociceptive potency estimated at roughly 200 times that of morphine, but its therapeutic window is very narrow: doses near the analgesic dose produce hypertension, paralysis, seizures, and death. Its toxicity prevented its own development as a drug.

Research significance

Because its analgesic action is independent of opioid receptors, epibatidine attracted intense interest as a lead compound. Structural analogues such as tebanicline (ABT-594) were developed to retain pain relief with fewer side effects, though clinical progress was limited by gastrointestinal toxicity. The compound remains an important pharmacological tool for studying nicotinic signalling.
