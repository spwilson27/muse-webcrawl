Ancient Greek sculpture

Ancient Greek sculpture is the sculpture of ancient Greece, produced from roughly the 8th century BC to the Roman conquest and beyond. Working mainly in marble and bronze, Greek sculptors developed an increasingly naturalistic representation of the human body that profoundly influenced Western art.

Archaic period (c. 800–480 BC)

Early large-scale sculpture drew on Egyptian and Near Eastern models. Its characteristic free-standing figures are the kouros (nude youth) and kore (draped maiden), frontal and stiff, often wearing the enigmatic "Archaic smile."

Classical period (c. 480–323 BC)

The Classical period brought contrapposto, idealised anatomy, and balanced proportions, codified in Polykleitos' Canon and embodied in his Doryphoros. Phidias supervised the Parthenon sculptures and created colossal cult statues of Athena and Zeus. Myron's Discobolus captured the body in motion; Praxiteles later introduced a more sensuous style and the first monumental female nude, the Aphrodite of Knidos.

Hellenistic period (323–31 BC)

After Alexander the Great, sculpture became more dramatic, emotional, and varied in subject — old age, suffering, and movement — as in the Laocoön group, the Winged Victory of Samothrace, and the Venus de Milo.

Most bronze originals survive only through Roman marble copies, and the bright paint that once covered marble statues has almost entirely worn away.
