History of Bolivia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of Bolivia spans the pre-Columbian civilisations of the Andes and Altiplano, three centuries of Spanish colonial rule centred on the silver mines of Potosí, independence in 1825, and a modern history marked by territorial losses, revolution, military rule, and, in the twenty-first century, the first Indigenous presidency of Evo Morales.

Pre-Columbian era

The Bolivian Altiplano was home to the Tiwanaku (Tiahuanaco) empire, centred near Lake Titicaca, which flourished from about AD 300 to 1000 and was one of the most important civilisations of the ancient Andes. After Tiwanaku's collapse, Aymara kingdoms dominated the highlands until the Inca Empire conquered the region in the 15th century, incorporating it as the province of Collasuyu.

Spanish colonisation

Spanish conquistadors under Francisco Pizarro and his successors overran the Inca world in the 1530s; the territory of modern Bolivia became "Upper Peru" (Alto Perú), administered by the Real Audiencia of Charcas. The discovery of silver at Potosí in 1545 made the Cerro Rico ("Rich Mountain") one of the world's greatest sources of silver and its city briefly one of the largest in the Americas, worked through the mita system of forced Indigenous labour, which caused enormous mortality. Indigenous resistance culminated in the great rebellions of 1780–81 led by Túpac Katari, who besieged La Paz, and Túpac Amaru II in Peru.

Independence

The independence struggle began with uprisings in Chuquisaca (Sucre) and La Paz in 1809. After fifteen years of guerrilla warfare by republiquetas and campaigns by the armies of Simón Bolívar and Antonio José de Sucre, Upper Peru declared independence on 6 August 1825 and named itself Bolivia in honour of Bolívar, its first president. Sucre, the second president, founded institutions before being driven out in 1828.

The nineteenth century

Early republican Bolivia was plagued by caudillo politics and coups. Andrés de Santa Cruz created the short-lived Peru–Bolivian Confederation (1836–39), dissolved after defeat by Chile and Argentine forces at Yungay. Bolivia lost territory in a series of wars: the War of the Pacific (1879–84) cost it its entire Pacific coastline to Chile, leaving it landlocked—a grievance still pressed today; the Acre territory was ceded to Brazil in 1903. The economy shifted from silver to tin at the end of the century, dominated by the "tin barons" Patiño, Hochschild, and Aramayo, while the Liberal and Republican parties alternated in power.

Chaco War and the 1952 Revolution

Defeat in the Chaco War (1932–35) against Paraguay, in which Bolivia lost most of the Gran Chaco and some 65,000 lives, discredited the old order. After years of military governments and reformist experiments, the Nationalist Revolutionary Movement (MNR) under Víctor Paz Estenssoro won the 1951 election; when the army annulled it, the revolution of April 1952 brought the MNR to power. The revolution enacted universal suffrage, nationalised the great tin mines (COMIBOL), and carried out a sweeping agrarian reform in 1953. Economic difficulties and factionalism followed, and in 1964 the military seized power, beginning nearly two decades of dictatorship. In 1967 the army, assisted by the CIA, captured and executed the Argentine-Cuban revolutionary Ernesto "Che" Guevara, who had launched a guerrilla campaign in the Bolivian countryside. The regime of Hugo Banzer (1971–78) was among the harshest of the era.

Return to democracy and neoliberalism

Democracy was restored in 1982 under Hernán Siles Zuazo amid economic collapse. In 1985 Paz Estenssoro, returned to the presidency, imposed a shock-therapy programme (Decree 21060) that tamed hyperinflation but closed mines and threw thousands out of work. Successive governments continued market reforms and coca-eradication campaigns under US pressure, provoking growing protest from miners, coca growers (cocaleros), and Indigenous movements—including the 2000 Cochabamba "Water War" and the 2003 "Gas War", which forced President Gonzalo Sánchez de Lozada from office.

The Morales era and after

In December 2005 Evo Morales, an Aymara cocalero leader heading the Movement for Socialism (MAS), won the presidency—the first Indigenous head of state in Bolivian history. His government nationalised hydrocarbons, redistributed gas revenues to social programmes, reduced poverty, and promulgated a new constitution in 2009 redefining Bolivia as a "Plurinational State" with extensive Indigenous rights. Morales won re-election in 2009 and 2014 but lost a 2016 referendum on term limits, then claimed victory in a disputed 2019 election; mass protests and an OAS audit alleging irregularities led to his resignation and exile in November 2019, an episode his supporters call a coup. After the interim government of Jeanine Áñez, MAS returned to power with the election of Luis Arce in 2020, and the party subsequently split into rival factions around Arce and Morales.
