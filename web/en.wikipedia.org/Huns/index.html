The Huns were a nomadic people who lived in Central Asia, the Caucasus, and Eastern Europe between the 4th and 6th centuries AD. According to European tradition, first reported by the Greek geographer Ptolemy, they originated east of the Caspian Sea. The Huns formed a confederation of Eurasian tribes, and by the 440s they had established a vast, if short-lived, dominion in Europe under their ruler Attila.

The Huns are thought to have stimulated the Great Migration, a major factor in the collapse of the Western Roman Empire. Their military success rested on highly skilled mounted archery, composite bows, and rapid mobility on the steppe, together with a reputation for ferocity that Roman writers such as Ammianus Marcellinus recorded with fear.

Origins

The origin of the Huns is debated. Many scholars connect them with the Xiongnu, a nomadic empire that dominated the steppe north of China from the 3rd century BC. Others treat the name "Hun" as a loose label applied by sedentary chroniclers to successive steppe groups. By the 370s the Huns had crossed the Volga and subjugated the Alans and many of the Goths north of the Black Sea.

History

Early incursions

Hunnic groups raided the Balkans in the 395–398 period and served as mercenaries for both Roman halves. Under Uldin (d. c. 412) the Huns reached the Danube frontier. In the 420s and 430s, under Rugila and his nephews Attila and Bleda, they extracted large annual tributes of gold from Constantinople.

Attila's empire

Attila became sole ruler in 445 after the death of Bleda. From his wooden palace on the Hungarian plain he campaigned through the Balkans (447) and into Gaul, where a coalition of Romans under Aetius and Visigoths under Theodoric I checked him at the Battle of the Catalaunian Plains in 451. In 452 he invaded Italy, sacking Aquileia, Padua and Milan, before withdrawing. Attila died in 453 on his wedding night, reportedly from a nosebleed.

Decline

Attila's sons divided the subject peoples among themselves, provoking revolt. At the Battle of Nedao (454) the Gepids and other subject tribes destroyed Hunnic supremacy. Dengizich, the last major Hunnic king, was killed in 469 and his head displayed in Constantinople. Surviving Hunnic groups merged into later steppe confederations such as the Bulgars.

Society and warfare

Hunnic society was a layered confederation: a Hunnic core ruled over Alans, Goths, Gepids, Heruls and others, plus Roman defectors and secretaries who handled correspondence and trade. Priscus, a Byzantine envoy to Attila's court in 449, left a valuable eyewitness account of its court life. Hunnic warriors fought primarily as horse archers with reflex composite bows, lasso and long swords, using feigned retreats and deep raids.

Legacy

The Huns left a lasting mark on European memory, from the Nibelungenlied to the epithet "Hun" applied to Germans in the First World War. In Hungary, Attila remains a popular given name and Hunnic descent is part of national tradition.
