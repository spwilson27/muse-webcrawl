Divisor

In mathematics, a divisor of an integer n, also called a factor of n, is an integer m that may be multiplied by some integer to produce n. In this case, one also says that n is a multiple of m. An integer n is divisible by a nonzero integer m if, and only if, there exists an integer k such that n = km. This is written m ∣ n, read "m divides n".

Examples and basic facts

The divisors of 10 are 1, 2, 5 and 10. Every positive integer has at least two divisors, 1 and itself, unless the number is 1, whose only divisor is 1. Integers with exactly two positive divisors are prime numbers; the others are composite. A divisor of n other than n itself is called a proper divisor or an aliquot part. An integer with more divisors than any smaller positive integer is called a highly composite number. Negative divisors are usually excluded in elementary contexts: strictly, divisors may be taken positive or negative, carrying the sign into the quotient.

Divisor functions

The number of positive divisors of n is denoted d(n) or σ0(n); the sum of the positive divisors is denoted σ(n) or σ1(n). Both are multiplicative functions: for n with prime factorisation n = ∏ pa, one has d(n) = ∏ (a + 1). A perfect number equals the sum of its proper divisors — for example 6 = 1 + 2 + 3 — while abundant and deficient numbers exceed or fall short of that sum.

Generalizations

The concept extends to any integral domain: in a ring, a divides b if there exists an element c with b = ac. In algebraic geometry, the term "divisor" denotes a formal sum of codimension-one subvarieties of an algebraic variety, a central object in the theory of line bundles and the Picard group. In complex analysis, divisors record the zeros and poles of meromorphic functions.

Computation

Finding all divisors of large integers is intimately connected with integer factorisation, which is believed to be computationally hard and underlies the security of the RSA cryptosystem. Divisibility rules — for 2, 3, 5, 9, 11 and others — simplify the search for small divisors by inspection of decimal digits.
