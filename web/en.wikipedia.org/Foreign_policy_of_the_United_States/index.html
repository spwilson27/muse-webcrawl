Foreign policy of the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The foreign policy of the United States is the way in which the United States interacts with foreign nations and sets standards of interaction for its organizations, corporations and individual citizens. The officially stated goals of the foreign policy of the United States, as mentioned in the Foreign Policy Agenda of the U.S. Department of State, are "to build and sustain a more democratic, secure, and prosperous world for the benefit of the American people and the international community."

History

Early republic

From its founding, the United States balanced idealism and realism in its dealings abroad. President George Washington's Farewell Address of 1796 warned against permanent alliances with foreign nations. The Monroe Doctrine of 1823 declared the Western Hemisphere closed to further European colonization. Through the nineteenth century the young republic concentrated on continental expansion under the banner of Manifest Destiny, acquiring territory from France, Spain, Mexico and Russia.

The world wars and the Cold War

The United States emerged from the two world wars as one of two global superpowers. After 1945, its foreign policy was dominated by containment of the Soviet Union, expressed in the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan for European recovery, and the creation of the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) in 1949. The policy produced interventions in Korea and Vietnam, a prolonged nuclear arms race, and support for anti-communist governments worldwide.

Post-Cold War era

After the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, the United States stood as the world's sole superpower. The attacks of September 11, 2001, reshaped policy around the "War on Terror", leading to wars in Afghanistan and Iraq. In the twenty-first century, American foreign policy has increasingly focused on strategic competition with a rising China, relations with Russia, climate change, and the defence of a rules-based international order.

Institutions

The President of the United States is constitutionally the chief diplomat and commander-in-chief, while Congress controls funding, declares war, and ratifies treaties through the Senate. The Department of State, led by the Secretary of State, conducts diplomacy; the Department of Defense and the intelligence community, coordinated since 2004 by the Director of National Intelligence, support policy with military and analytical capabilities.

Foreign aid and alliances

The United States is the world's largest provider of foreign aid in absolute terms, administered largely through the U.S. Agency for International Development (USAID). Its alliance network includes NATO in Europe, and bilateral treaty alliances with Japan, South Korea, Australia, the Philippines and Thailand in the Asia-Pacific region.
