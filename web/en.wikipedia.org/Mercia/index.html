Mercia

Mercia (Old English: Miercna rīċe, "kingdom of the border people") was one of the great kingdoms of Anglo-Saxon England, centred on the valley of the River Trent and its tributaries in what is now the Midlands. From the seventh to the ninth century it was the dominant power in England south of the Humber during much of the Heptarchy.

History

The kingdom was established by Angles around the settlement of Tamworth. Under Penda (died 655), a pagan king, Mercia became a major military power. His successors included Wulfhere and Æthelbald, and under Offa (reigned 757–796) Mercia reached its peak: Offa controlled most of England south of the Humber, issued a notable coinage, and is credited with Offa's Dyke along the Welsh border.

Decline

After the death of Coenwulf in 821 Mercian power declined, and the Viking Great Heathen Army conquered eastern Mercia in 874. The western remnant came under the overlordship of Alfred the Great of Wessex; his daughter Æthelflæd ruled as "Lady of the Mercians". Mercia was finally absorbed into the unified English kingdom in the tenth century, surviving as an earldom into the Norman period.
