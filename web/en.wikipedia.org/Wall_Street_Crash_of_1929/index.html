The Wall Street Crash of 1929 was a major stock market crash in the United States that began in late October 1929. It was the most devastating market collapse in US history and is conventionally taken as the start of the worldwide Great Depression, although its exact causal role remains debated by economists.

Background

The "Roaring Twenties" saw rapid economic growth and a speculative mania in shares. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose sixfold between 1921 and its peak of 381.17 on 3 September 1929. Much buying was done on margin — with borrowed money, often 90% of the purchase price — and investment trusts and utility holding companies added layers of leverage. Warning signs, including weakening production, were visible by mid-1929.

The crash

After several weeks of decline, panic erupted on Black Thursday (24 October), when a record 12.9 million shares changed hands; a bankers' pool briefly stabilised prices. The following Monday and Black Tuesday (29 October, 16.4 million shares) saw collapses of roughly 13% and 12% on the Dow. Shares of blue-chip companies such as U.S. Steel and General Electric lost more than half their value within weeks.

Aftermath

The market did not regain its 1929 peak until 1954. The crash shattered confidence, cut spending and investment, and interacted with bank failures, deflation, and the gold standard to produce mass unemployment. It led to major reforms: the Securities Act of 1933, the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 creating the SEC, and the Glass–Steagall separation of commercial and investment banking.
