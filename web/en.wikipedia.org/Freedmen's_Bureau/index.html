The Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, usually referred to as simply the Freedmen's Bureau, was a United States federal government agency established in 1865 to aid freedmen (freed slaves) and war refugees in the South during the Reconstruction era of the United States. Headed by Union Army General Oliver Otis Howard, the agency was created by Congress on 3 March 1865 and operated until 1872.

Work

The Bureau's tasks were enormous: supervising relief and educational activities for refugees and freedmen, including issuing rations to the destitute of both races, building hospitals, negotiating labour contracts between freedpeople and planters, reviewing those contracts in its own courts where local courts excluded Black testimony, legalising marriages entered into under slavery, locating family members separated by slavery and war, and helping Black soldiers and sailors obtain back pay and pensions owed them.

Its most enduring legacy was education. The Bureau spent over $5 million on schooling, helping to establish more than 1,000 schools for freedpeople, supporting teachers sent by Northern aid societies, and contributing to the founding of institutions including Howard University (named for the Commissioner), Fisk University, Hampton Institute, and Atlanta University. By 1870 more than 240,000 pupils were enrolled in Bureau-supported schools.

Land and politics

The Bureau initially controlled some 850,000 acres of abandoned and confiscated land, and freedpeople hoped it would be redistributed in "forty acres and a mule" allotments. President Andrew Johnson, however, ordered the land returned to its pardoned former owners, and Congress's attempt to override his veto of a renewed Bureau bill failed in 1866, though a second act was passed over his veto that July. Chronic underfunding, understaffing, white Southern hostility, and violence by groups such as the Ku Klux Klan limited the Bureau's effectiveness; most of its functions ended by 1869 and the agency was formally abolished in 1872.

Assessment

Historians credit the Bureau with significant achievements in relief, labour, legal aid, and especially education, despite limited resources and political opposition, while noting that it ultimately failed to secure land reform or lasting economic independence for the freedpeople.
