The Fens

LocationEastern England (Lincolnshire, Cambridgeshire, Norfolk, Suffolk)
AreaApprox. 3,900 km²
RiversGreat Ouse, Nene, Welland, Witham
Highest pointAbout 10 m above sea level

The Fens, also known as the Fenlands, is a low-lying marshy region in eastern England, covering parts of Lincolnshire, Cambridgeshire, Norfolk, and Suffolk around the Wash. Much of the land lies at or below sea level.

History

Before drainage, the Fens were a watery landscape of meres, reed beds, and islands — including the Isle of Ely — inhabited by "Fen Tigers" who resisted outside control. Drainage began in earnest in the 17th century under the Earl of Bedford, with Dutch engineer Cornelius Vermuyden cutting great channels such as the Old and New Bedford Rivers. Wind pumps, later steam and diesel pumps, and now electric pumps keep the land dry.

Geography and economy

The drained peat and silt soils make the Fens some of the most fertile farmland in Britain, producing a large share of the nation's vegetables, cereals, and sugar beet. As the peat shrinks and oxidises, however, the land surface continues to sink — the Holme Fen post famously records several metres of lowering since 1851 — posing long-term challenges for flood defence and carbon emissions.
