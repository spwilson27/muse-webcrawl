Psychedelic therapy

Psychedelic therapy is the use of psychedelic drugs — such as psilocybin, LSD, mescaline, DMT, and the entactogen MDMA — as part of a structured psychotherapeutic treatment for mental disorders. Modern protocols typically combine preparation sessions, a small number of supervised dosing sessions in a controlled setting with trained therapists present, and subsequent "integration" sessions in which the patient processes the experience.

History

Research began in earnest in the 1950s, when LSD and mescaline were investigated for alcoholism, anxiety, and other conditions, and psychiatrists including Humphry Osmond and Abram Hoffer reported promising results. The field was largely shut down in the late 1960s and 1970s as psychedelics were criminalized and placed in the most restrictive drug schedules in the United States and elsewhere. A research revival beginning in the 1990s and accelerating after 2006 — often called the "psychedelic renaissance" — has produced new clinical trials at institutions such as Johns Hopkins University and Imperial College London.

Conditions studied

Randomized trials have examined psilocybin for major depressive disorder and treatment-resistant depression, end-of-life anxiety in cancer patients, tobacco and alcohol addiction, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and cluster headache. MDMA-assisted therapy for post-traumatic stress disorder completed Phase 3 trials sponsored by the Multidisciplinary Association for Psychedelic Studies (MAPS); in 2024 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration declined approval and requested an additional trial. Psilocybin received FDA "breakthrough therapy" designations for depression, and Australia in 2023 became the first country to permit authorized psychiatrists to prescribe psilocybin and MDMA for specific conditions.

Risks and criticism

Reported risks include acute anxiety or panic during sessions, exacerbation of psychotic disorders in vulnerable individuals, and rare cases of hallucinogen persisting perception disorder. Methodological criticisms focus on functional unblinding — participants can usually tell whether they received an active drug — expectancy effects, and the difficulty of separating drug effects from the intensive psychotherapy that accompanies them.
