Black Hills

The Black Hills are a small, isolated mountain range rising from the Great Plains of North America in western South Dakota and northeastern Wyoming. The highest summit is Black Elk Peak (formerly Harney Peak), at 2,208 metres (7,244 ft), the highest point in the United States east of the Rocky Mountains. The hills take their name from their dark appearance from a distance, caused by dense ponderosa pine forest.

Geology

The Black Hills are a dome-shaped uplift formed around 62 million years ago during the Laramide orogeny. Erosion has exposed a core of Precambrian granite and schist — some rocks nearly 2.5 billion years old — ringed by younger sedimentary formations. Famous carved granite formations include Mount Rushmore and the Crazy Horse Memorial.

History

The hills are sacred to the Lakota Sioux, who call them Pahá Sápa, and the Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868) guaranteed them to the Sioux as part of the Great Sioux Reservation. After George Armstrong Custer's 1874 expedition confirmed gold, prospectors flooded in; the United States seized the hills in 1877 in violation of the treaty. The gold rush founded towns such as Deadwood and Lead, home of the Homestake Mine. In United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians (1980) the Supreme Court ruled the taking illegal and awarded compensation, which the Sioux have refused, insisting on the return of the land; the unclaimed fund has grown to well over a billion dollars.

Tourism and nature

The region includes Custer State Park, Wind Cave National Park, Jewel Cave National Monument, Badlands-adjacent scenery and the Sturgis Motorcycle Rally. The Black Hills National Forest covers much of the range.
