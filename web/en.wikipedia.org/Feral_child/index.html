Feral child

A feral child (sometimes called a "wild child") is a young human child who has lived isolated from human contact from a very young age, with little or no experience of human care, behavior, or language. Such children may have been raised by wild animals, confined by adults, or left to survive on their own. The study of feral children has been of enduring interest to psychologists and linguists because it bears on debates about nature versus nurture, the critical period hypothesis for language acquisition, and the social foundations of human development.

Characteristics

Documented feral children typically show severe impairments in language and social behavior. Many never acquire full spoken language if rescued after early childhood, though outcomes vary with the age of rescue and the nature of prior isolation. They may display animal-like behaviors such as walking on all fours, eating raw food, and insensitivity to temperature, depending on their upbringing.

Famous cases

Victor of Aveyron

Victor, captured in France around 1800 at roughly twelve years old, was studied by physician Jean Marc Gaspard Itard, who attempted to educate him and teach him speech. Victor learned to read some words and express simple needs but never acquired fluent language. His case became foundational in special education and child psychology.

Genie

Genie (born 1957) was discovered in Los Angeles in 1970 at age thirteen, having been kept in extreme isolation and restraint by her parents. She became the subject of a major linguistic research project; she acquired a large vocabulary but struggled with grammar, offering evidence relevant to the critical period hypothesis.

Kaspar Hauser

Kaspar Hauser appeared in Nuremberg in 1828 claiming to have been raised in a dark cell with almost no human contact. His mysterious origins and later assassination made him a famous and contested case in European history.

Oxana Malaya

Oxana Malaya, born in Ukraine in 1983, lived among dogs in a kennel from early childhood until age eight. After rescue she learned to speak and function in society to a significant degree, one of the better outcomes on record.

Hoaxes and legends

Many celebrated cases, including the legendary Roman founders Romulus and Remus said to have been suckled by a wolf, and the fictional Mowgli and Tarzan, belong to myth and literature rather than documented fact. Some reported cases, such as the "wolf children" of Midnapore (Kamala and Amala), have been challenged as hoaxes or misinterpretations of children with congenital conditions.

Scientific significance

Genuine cases are rare and ethically fraught to study, but they offer evidence that language and social cognition depend heavily on early environmental input, while also showing the resilience and partial recovery possible with intensive intervention.
