Frame analysis

Frame analysis examines how people organise experience and how messages are packaged.

Frame analysis (also called framing analysis) is a research method used in the social sciences and communication studies to analyse how people understand situations and activities, and how texts and messages present particular interpretations of reality. The concept derives from the notion of a "frame": a schema of interpretation that enables individuals to locate, perceive, identify and label events and occurrences.

Origins

The idea of frames in social interaction was developed by Gregory Bateson and taken up most influentially by the sociologist Erving Goffman in his 1974 book Frame Analysis: An Essay on the Organization of Experience. For Goffman, frames are principles of organisation that govern the subjective meaning people assign to social events, answering the question "What is it that's going on here?" Frames can be transformed through processes Goffman called keying (as when play re-keys fighting) and fabrication (the deliberate deception of others about what is going on).

In communication research

In media and political communication, framing analysis examines how issues are packaged in news coverage and political messaging. Robert Entman defined framing as selecting and highlighting "some aspects of a perceived reality" to promote "a particular problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation, and/or treatment recommendation." Framing analysis is a core method in the study of social movements, where scholars such as David Snow and Robert Benford analysed how activists construct diagnostic, prognostic and motivational frames to mobilise supporters.
