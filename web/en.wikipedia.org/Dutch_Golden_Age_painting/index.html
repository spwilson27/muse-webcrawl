Dutch Golden Age painting is the painting of the Dutch Golden Age, a period in Dutch history roughly spanning the 17th century, during and after the later part of the Eighty Years' War (1568–1648) for Dutch independence. The new Dutch Republic was the most prosperous nation in Europe and led European trade, science, and art. Painting flourished on an unprecedented scale: an estimated 1.3 to 5 million paintings were produced during the period, bought by an unusually broad public of merchants, artisans, and farmers rather than only by churches and courts.

Background

After the northern provinces broke from Spanish rule, the Calvinist Reformed Church discouraged religious painting, and the collapse of church and court patronage pushed artists toward secular subjects for the open market. Amsterdam, Haarlem, Leiden, Delft, and Utrecht became major centres, each with guilds of St Luke regulating the trade.

Genres

Dutch painters specialised in distinct genres to a degree unmatched elsewhere: landscapes and seascapes (Jacob van Ruisdael, Jan van Goyen, Aelbert Cuyp); still lifes, from lavish pronkstilleven to modest breakfast pieces (Willem Claesz. Heda, Pieter Claesz, Rachel Ruysch); genre scenes of peasant and bourgeois life (Jan Steen, Adriaen van Ostade, Gerard de Lairesse); domestic interiors and "merry companies" (Johannes Vermeer, Pieter de Hooch, Gerard ter Borch); portraiture, including group portraits of civic guards and regents (Frans Hals, Rembrandt's The Night Watch and The Anatomy Lesson of Dr Nicolaes Tulp); cityscapes (the Delft school, Emanuel de Witte's church interiors); and history painting, which retained the highest theoretical prestige.

Leading artists

Rembrandt van Rijn (1606–1669) dominated Amsterdam painting with portraits, biblical scenes, and etchings of extraordinary psychological depth. Frans Hals (c. 1582–1666) of Haarlem revolutionised portraiture with loose, animated brushwork. Johannes Vermeer (1632–1675) of Delft painted only about 37 known works—quiet interiors with luminous colour—yet is now among the most celebrated of all. Other prominent names include Judith Leyster, Jacob van Campen in architecture, Hendrick Avercamp (winter scenes), Meindert Hobbema, Willem van de Velde the Younger (marine painting), and Gerard Dou and the Leiden fijnschilders ("fine painters").

The art market

Paintings were sold at markets, fairs, auctions, and lotteries, and through dealers; many artists also worked as dealers or in other trades. Prices for ordinary works were modest, putting art within reach of middle-class households, while the economic crash of the Rampjaar ("disaster year") of 1672, when France invaded the Republic, sharply contracted the market and ended the Golden Age of painting. Rembrandt died in poverty in 1669 and Vermeer left his family in debt in 1675.
