Stereoisomerism

In stereochemistry, stereoisomerism, or spatial isomerism, is a form of isomerism in which molecules have the same molecular formula and sequence of bonded atoms (constitution), but differ in the three-dimensional orientations of their atoms in space. This contrasts with structural isomers, which share the same molecular formula but differ in bonding.

Enantiomers

Enantiomers are stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other. A molecule with one stereogenic carbon typically exists as a pair of enantiomers, which rotate plane-polarised light in opposite directions and can differ dramatically in biological activity — one enantiomer of thalidomide is a sedative while the other is teratogenic.

Diastereomers

Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images. They have different physical properties and can be separated by ordinary techniques. The maximum number of stereoisomers for a molecule with n stereocentres is 2n (Le Bel–van't Hoff rule), reduced when meso compounds introduce internal symmetry.

Cis–trans and E/Z isomerism

Cis–trans isomerism arises from restricted rotation, typically about double bonds or in rings. The more general E–Z notation assigns priorities by the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog rules.

Conformation

Conformers interconvert by rotation about single bonds (e.g. staggered and eclipsed ethane, chair cyclohexane) and are usually not isolable, unlike configurational stereoisomers.

Importance

Stereoisomerism is central to pharmacology, since receptors are chiral; regulators often require single-enantiomer drugs or justification of racemates. It also governs polymer properties (tacticity) and flavour/fragrance chemistry.
