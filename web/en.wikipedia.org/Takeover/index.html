Takeover

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In business, a takeover is the purchase of one company (the target) by another (the acquirer, or bidder). In the UK the term refers to the acquisition of a public company whose shares are listed on a stock exchange, in contrast to the acquisition of a private company.

Friendly and hostile takeovers

A friendly takeover is an acquisition that is approved by the management of the target company, usually negotiated between the boards of the two firms. A hostile takeover proceeds against the wishes of the target's board, typically by the bidder making a tender offer directly to shareholders or waging a proxy fight to replace the board. Targets may deploy defences such as poison pills, staggered boards, golden parachutes, or seeking a friendly "white knight" acquirer.

Financing and regulation

Takeovers may be financed with cash, shares, debt, or a combination; a buyout financed principally with borrowed money is a leveraged buyout (LBO). Public takeovers are tightly regulated: in the United Kingdom the Takeover Panel administers the City Code on Takeovers and Mergers, while in the United States the Williams Act of 1968 requires disclosure of large share accumulations and regulates tender offers. Antitrust authorities may block or condition takeovers that substantially reduce competition.

Rationale

Common motivations include synergy, market share, diversification, acquiring undervalued assets, and tax advantages. Critics argue that many takeovers fail to create value for the acquirer's shareholders, pointing to empire-building, hubris, and integration costs.
