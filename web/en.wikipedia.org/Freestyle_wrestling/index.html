Freestyle wrestling

Freestyle wrestling is a style of wrestling that originated in Great Britain and the United States, and is practised internationally at the collegiate, Olympic, and world championship levels. Along with Greco-Roman wrestling, it is one of the two styles of wrestling contested in the Olympic Games; women's freestyle wrestling was added to the Olympic programme in 2004.

In freestyle wrestling, competitors may use their legs in both offence and defence, and may hold opponents above or below the waist. The object is to pin the opponent's shoulders to the mat (a fall), or to win on points scored for takedowns, exposures, reversals, and penalties assessed against the opponent.

Scoring

A takedown, in which a wrestler gains control over the opponent from a neutral position, is worth two points. Exposing the opponent's back to the mat earns two or more points depending on the amplitude of the throw; a grand amplitude throw directly to a danger position can earn five points and immediately end the period. A match is contested over two periods of three minutes, and a wrestler who builds a lead of ten points wins by technical superiority.

History

The style developed from British "catch-as-catch-can" wrestling and was included in the first modern Olympic Games at which wrestling appeared, in 1904 at St. Louis. The international governing body, United World Wrestling (formerly FILA), standardises rules and organises the annual World Championships.
