Manual transmission

Type: Vehicle transmission
Also called: Stick shift, standard transmission
Typical gears: 5–6 forward, 1 reverse

A manual transmission (also known as a stick shift) is a transmission system for motor vehicles in which the driver selects the gear ratio by operating a gear stick and a clutch pedal. It contrasts with an automatic transmission, which changes gears without direct driver selection.

Operation

The driver presses the clutch pedal to disconnect the engine from the gearbox, moves the gear lever through a gate — commonly an H-pattern — to select a pair of gears, then releases the clutch to re-engage power. Most modern passenger-car gearboxes use synchromesh to equalise shaft speeds and allow smooth engagement without double-clutching. Typical units offer five or six forward ratios and one reverse.

Types

Sliding-mesh gearboxes, found on early cars, required skilled double-declutching. Constant-mesh designs with synchromesh became standard from the mid-20th century. Sequential manual gearboxes, common on motorcycles and racing cars, permit only adjacent-ratio changes. Automated manual transmissions use computer-controlled actuators to operate a conventional manual gearbox and clutch.

Usage

Manuals dominated world markets for most of the 20th century and remain common in much of Europe, though automatics, CVTs and electric drivetrains have steadily displaced them, particularly in North America and in new-car sales generally. Advocates value the control, engagement and typically lower purchase cost; critics cite convenience in traffic and the growing efficiency of modern automatics.
