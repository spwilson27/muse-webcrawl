The Great Fire of Rome (Latin: incendium Romae) began on the night of 18 to 19 July AD 64 in the merchant shops around Rome's chariot stadium, the Circus Maximus. After six days, the fire was brought under control, but before the damage could be assessed, it reignited and burned for another three days. In the aftermath, 71 percent of Rome had been destroyed: of the city's 14 districts, 3 were completely devastated, 7 more were reduced to a few scorched and mangled ruins, and only 4 were left completely undamaged.

Background

Rome in the 1st century AD was a densely built city of perhaps one million inhabitants, full of timber-framed tenements (insulae), narrow streets and warehouses of flammable goods. Fires were common; major conflagrations had occurred in 6 BC, AD 27 and AD 36. According to Tacitus, the fire of 64 started in shops selling inflammable goods at the eastern end of the Circus Maximus and was driven by wind through the city.

Nero's response and the "fiddling" legend

Emperor Nero was at Antium when the fire broke out and returned to Rome to organise relief: he opened public buildings and his own gardens to refugees, arranged emergency food supplies from Ostia and neighbouring towns, and reduced the price of grain. Nevertheless, rumours spread that Nero had sung the "Sack of Troy" from a stage while the city burned — the origin of the later expression "Nero fiddled while Rome burned" (the fiddle did not exist; later accounts mention a lyre). Tacitus reports the rumour without endorsing it, and modern historians generally doubt it. Rumours also accused Nero of ordering the fire to clear land for his planned palace, an allegation Tacitus treats with scepticism; the true cause remains unknown.

Rebuilding and the Domus Aurea

Nero used the devastation to build his extravagant Domus Aurea ("Golden House") on cleared land in the city centre, a vast palace complex with a lake, gardens and a colossal bronze statue of himself. At the same time, the rebuilding of the city followed new regulations: wider streets, height limits on tenements, fire-resistant materials and porticoes from which fires could be fought. Much of the cost was extracted from the provinces, contributing to discontent with Nero's rule.

Persecution of Christians

To scotch the rumours blaming him, Nero, according to Tacitus, blamed the Christians, a small and despised sect in Rome, and subjected them to spectacular punishments: some were sewn into animal skins and torn apart by dogs, others crucified, and others set on fire to serve as lamps at night. This episode is one of the earliest Roman persecutions of Christians and a key source for their presence in Rome, including the traditional association of the martyrdoms of Peter and Paul with Nero's reign.

Sources

The principal surviving accounts are those of Tacitus (Annals 15.38–44), Suetonius and Cassius Dio, each written decades after the event and differing in their treatment of Nero's culpability.
