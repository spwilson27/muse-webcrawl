Principle of faunal succession

The principle of faunal succession, also known as the law of faunal succession, is based on the observation that sedimentary rock strata contain fossilized flora and fauna, and that these fossils succeed each other vertically in a specific, reliable order that can be identified over wide horizontal distances. Because organisms evolve and go extinct over time, each interval of geologic time is characterised by a distinctive assemblage of fossils, allowing rocks of the same age in different places to be correlated by their fossil content.

History

The principle was formulated by the English engineer and surveyor William Smith in the 1790s and early 1800s, from observations made while surveying routes for canals across England. Smith realised that each stratum contained its own characteristic fossils and that he could identify and order strata anywhere by them; he used the insight to produce the first geological map of a whole country, published in 1815. In France, Georges Cuvier and Alexandre Brongniart independently developed similar ideas from the fossil sequences of the Paris Basin.

Application

Faunal succession underlies biostratigraphy: the use of index fossils — species that are widespread, abundant, and short-lived — to date and correlate strata. Together with the principle of superposition and later radiometric dating, it forms the basis of the geologic time scale. The ordered succession of fossils also became, in the nineteenth century, powerful evidence for the fact of evolution.
