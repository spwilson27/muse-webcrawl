The Rolls-Royce Merlin is a British liquid-cooled V-12 piston aero engine of 27 litres' (1,650 cu in) capacity, designed and built by Rolls-Royce. First run in 1933 as the private-venture PV-12, it became one of the most successful aircraft engines of the Second World War, powering the Hawker Hurricane and Supermarine Spitfire fighters central to the Battle of Britain, the Avro Lancaster and de Havilland Mosquito bombers, and — in the form of the Packard-built V-1650 — the North American P-51 Mustang. Nearly 150,000 Merlins were built in total.

Design and development

The Merlin grew out of Rolls-Royce's Kestrel engine and the racing "R" engine that powered the Schneider Trophy-winning Supermarine seaplanes. It was a 60-degree V-12 with a single overhead camshaft per bank, two intake and two exhaust valves per cylinder, and a single-stage, later two-stage, supercharger. Named following Rolls-Royce's convention of naming piston engines after birds of prey, the Merlin entered service in 1936 in the Fairey Battle, Hurricane, and Spitfire prototypes.

Early Merlins produced about 1,030 hp; wartime development — higher boost pressures enabled by 100-octane fuel, improved superchargers designed under Stanley Hooker, and eventually two-stage two-speed supercharging with intercooling — raised output to more than 2,000 hp in late marks. The float-controlled carburettor of early versions cut out under negative g, a flaw remedied first by "Miss Shilling's orifice" and later by injection carburettors.

Production

Rolls-Royce built Merlins at Derby, Crewe, and Glasgow, and Ford of Britain built some 30,000 at a purpose-built Manchester plant. In the United States, Packard produced the design under licence as the V-1650, adapting it to American mass-production methods; the Packard Merlin transformed the performance of the P-51 Mustang when it replaced the Allison engine. Total production reached nearly 150,000 engines, one of the largest aero-engine programmes in history.

Applications and postwar use

Besides the famous fighters and bombers, Merlins powered the Boulton Paul Defiant, Bristol Beaufighter variants, Halifax and Lancaster bombers, transport conversions after the war, and the CASA-built Spanish variants that flew into the 1960s. Civil Lancastrians and Yorks used Merlins in early postwar airliners. A de-rated derivative, the Meteor, powered British tanks including the Cromwell and Centurion. Surviving Merlins remain prized powerplants for warbird restorations, and their distinctive exhaust note — often called "the sound of freedom" — is a fixture of airshows.

Legacy

The Merlin was succeeded in Rolls-Royce's line by the larger Griffon. Its combination of continuous development, mass producibility, and high-altitude performance made it arguably the most important Allied piston engine of the war; the Spitfire–Merlin pairing has become an enduring symbol of Britain's wartime resistance.
