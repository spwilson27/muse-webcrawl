Teddy Boy

The Teddy Boys (also known as Teds) were a British youth subculture of the 1950s, characterised by distinctive dress inspired by Edwardian-era tailoring — hence "Teddy," from Edward — combined with American rock and roll culture. Teddy Girls (also called Judies) participated with their own adapted fashions. The movement is generally regarded as the first post-war British youth subculture.

Style

The classic Teddy Boy outfit comprised a long drape jacket with velvet collar and cuffs, narrow "drainpipe" trousers, a white or coloured shirt with a slim "bootlace" tie, a brocade waistcoat, and thick crepe-soled shoes known as "brothel creepers." Hair was worn in a quiff with a "duck's arse" (DA) at the back. The style began in London around 1950 among young working-class men adopting clothes Savile Row tailors had revived for wealthy customers after the war.

Music and reputation

Teds embraced American rock and roll — Bill Haley, Elvis Presley, Gene Vincent — and British acts such as Tommy Steele; later revivalists championed rockabilly. The subculture acquired a reputation for gang violence, amplified by press coverage of clashes and by the role of some Teds in the 1958 Notting Hill race riots. In the 1960s some Teds evolved into rockers.

Revival

A major Teddy Boy revival occurred in the 1970s, with bands such as Crazy Cavan and the Rhythm Rockers and a well-publicised rivalry with punks in the late 1970s. Enthusiasts maintain the style today.
