Orbital maneuver

In spaceflight, an orbital maneuver (otherwise known as a burn) is the use of propulsion systems to change the orbit of a spacecraft. The change in velocity required is called delta-v (Δv), and the total Δv a vehicle can deliver — its delta-v budget — is a fundamental constraint on mission design, set by the rocket equation.


A Hohmann transfer

Types of maneuver

  • Hohmann transfer: an efficient two-burn transfer between coplanar circular orbits via an elliptical arc.
  • Bi-elliptic transfer: a three-burn alternative that can be cheaper for very large orbit changes.
  • Plane change: alters orbital inclination; notoriously expensive in Δv, cheapest at low speed near apoapsis.
  • Phasing and rendezvous maneuvers: adjust position along the orbit to meet another spacecraft.
  • Station-keeping: small periodic burns that counteract perturbations such as drag and gravitational irregularities.
  • Deorbit burn: lowers perigee so the vehicle re-enters the atmosphere.

Impulsive versus low-thrust

Chemical rockets deliver short, nearly instantaneous ("impulsive") burns, well modeled as instant velocity changes. Low-thrust electric propulsion burns for weeks or months, producing spiraling trajectories and requiring different optimization. The Oberth effect — burns are more efficient at higher speed, deep in a gravity well — governs the timing of efficient maneuvers.
