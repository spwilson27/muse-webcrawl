Assassination

Assassination is the murder of a prominent or important person, generally for political, ideological or religious reasons, and usually carried out deliberately and often by surprise attack. Targets have included heads of state and government, monarchs, political leaders, activists and public figures. The word derives from the Arabic ḥashshāshīn, the name given to the medieval Nizari Ismaili order whose agents carried out targeted killings in Persia and Syria between the 11th and 13th centuries.

History

Assassination is among the oldest tools of politics: recorded examples include the killing of the Roman dictator Julius Caesar in 44 BC by senators fearing his power. Tyrannicide — the killing of a tyrant — was debated by Greek and Roman philosophers and later by medieval and early modern theorists. In the modern era, assassinations have repeatedly changed the course of history: the shooting of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in 1914 triggered the First World War, and in the United States four presidents (Lincoln, Garfield, McKinley and Kennedy) have been assassinated.

Methods and motives

Assassins have used firearms, explosives, poison, blades and vehicles; attacks may be carried out by lone actors, organized groups, or state agents ("targeted killing"). Motives include ideology, religion, personal grievance, mental illness, desire for fame, and state policy. Modern protective services, law enforcement investigation, and legal prohibitions — such as the United States' executive orders banning assassination — reflect attempts to curb the practice.

Consequences

Assassinations often fail to achieve their perpetrators' aims and can provoke repression, war, or sympathy for the victim's cause; they remain a recurring source of political instability and public trauma.
