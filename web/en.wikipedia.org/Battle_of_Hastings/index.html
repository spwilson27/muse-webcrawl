The Battle of Hastings was fought on 14 October 1066 between the Norman-French army of William, Duke of Normandy, and the English army under King Harold Godwinson, near the present-day town of Battle, East Sussex. It was the decisive victory of the Norman Conquest of England: Harold was killed in the fighting, and on Christmas Day 1066 William was crowned king in Westminster Abbey, replacing the Anglo-Saxon ruling class with a Norman aristocracy and permanently reshaping English language, law, and society.

Background

When Edward the Confessor died childless in January 1066, Harold Godwinson, the most powerful English earl, was chosen king. William of Normandy claimed Edward had promised him the throne in 1051 and that Harold had sworn an oath in 1064 to support his claim — an oath the Normans charged Harold with breaking, obtaining papal blessing for the invasion. Simultaneously, King Harald Hardrada of Norway invaded the north; Harold marched to Yorkshire and destroyed the Norwegian army at Stamford Bridge on 25 September, killing Hardrada. Three days later William landed unopposed at Pevensey. Harold force-marched his depleted army south and took position on Senlac Hill, astride the London road.

The battle

Harold's army — perhaps 7,000 men, overwhelmingly infantry including elite housecarls — formed a dense shield wall along the ridge. William's force combined infantry, archers, and heavy cavalry in roughly equal thirds, drawn from Normandy, Brittany, Flanders, and France. Fighting lasted all day, exceptionally long for a medieval battle. Norman attacks uphill repeatedly failed against the shield wall; at one point the Breton left wing broke and fled, and a rumour of William's death spread — he famously raised his helmet to show himself alive. Norman (and possibly feigned) retreats drew English troops downhill in pursuit, where cavalry turned and cut them down. As day waned, archery at high trajectory thinned the wall; Harold was killed late in the battle — the Bayeux Tapestry shows a figure with an arrow in the eye beneath the caption "Harold the king is killed", though whether that figure is Harold is disputed. Leaderless, the English army broke; the housecarls died around their king's body.

Aftermath

William marched on London, meeting submission after a show of force, and was crowned on 25 December 1066. English resistance and revolts (1067–71) were crushed, culminating in the devastation of the north ("the Harrying of the North", 1069–70). Within twenty years the great land survey of the Domesday Book recorded an England almost entirely in Norman hands. William founded Battle Abbey on the battlefield site, its high altar traditionally marking the spot where Harold fell; the town of Battle grew around it.

The battle's story is told visually in the Bayeux Tapestry, embroidered within a generation of the event, and in Norman and English chronicles whose partisan accounts historians still weigh against each other. In English memory "1066" became the most famous date in the national history — the last successful invasion of England.
