Millard Fillmore (7 January 1800 – 8 March 1874) was the 13th president of the United States, serving from 1850 to 1853. A former congressman from New York and comptroller of New York state, he was elected vice president in 1848 as Zachary Taylor's running mate on the Whig ticket, and succeeded to the presidency upon Taylor's sudden death in July 1850. He was the last president who was a member of the Whig Party while in office.

Fillmore rose from frontier poverty to the presidency.

Early life and career

Fillmore was born in a log cabin in the Finger Lakes region of New York to a poor farming family. Apprenticed as a clothmaker, he taught himself law while working, studied under local judges, and was admitted to the bar in 1823. He entered politics with the Anti-Masonic Party, served in the New York State Assembly, and won election to the US House of Representatives in 1832, later becoming a leading Whig and chairman of the Ways and Means Committee. He married his former teacher, Abigail Powers, in 1826.

Presidency

Fillmore inherited the crisis over slavery in the territories acquired from Mexico. Unlike Taylor, he supported Henry Clay's compromise proposals and signed the Compromise of 1850, including the controversial Fugitive Slave Act, which required the return of escaped slaves and severely damaged his standing in the North. His administration also opened relations with Japan: he sent Commodore Matthew Perry's expedition, which reached Japan in 1853–54 shortly after Fillmore left office. Domestically he supported internal improvements and signed measures promoting commerce and the transcontinental railroad surveys.

Later life

Denied the Whig nomination in 1852 — which went to Winfield Scott — Fillmore ran for president in 1856 as the candidate of the nativist Know Nothing (American) Party, carrying only Maryland. He retired to Buffalo, where he became the first chancellor of the University of Buffalo and a prominent civic leader. He died in 1874 after a stroke. Historians generally rank Fillmore below average, largely for his enforcement of the Fugitive Slave Act, though some note his role in preserving the Union settlement of 1850.
