Relativistic Doppler effect

The relativistic Doppler effect is the change in frequency of light (or other electromagnetic radiation) arising from the relative motion of the source and the observer, calculated using the principles of special relativity. Unlike the classical Doppler effect for sound, no medium is involved, and the effect depends only on the relative velocity between source and observer.

Longitudinal effect

For motion directly along the line of sight, the observed frequency f′ relates to the emitted frequency f by f′ = f √((c + v)/(c − v)) for an approaching source, where v is the relative speed and c the speed of light. In terms of redshift z, 1 + z = √((1 + β)/(1 − β)) with β = v/c. The relativistic formula can be understood as the combination of the classical Doppler shift with time dilation: a moving source's clock runs slow by the Lorentz factor γ, contributing an extra redshift beyond the classical value.

Transverse Doppler effect

A distinctive prediction of relativity is the transverse Doppler effect: light emitted by a source moving perpendicular to the line of sight is still redshifted by the factor γ, purely because of time dilation. This second-order effect was confirmed by the Ives–Stilwell experiment (1938) using canal rays, and is routinely verified in particle physics with Mössbauer rotor experiments and fast ion beams.

Applications

The effect underlies cosmological redshift measurements of distant galaxies, radar and satellite communications corrections, and precision tests of special relativity. At low speeds it reduces to the classical Doppler formula.
