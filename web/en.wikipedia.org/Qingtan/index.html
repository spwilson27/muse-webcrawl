Qingtan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Qingtan (Chinese: 清談; pinyin: qīngtán; Wade–Giles: ch'ing-t'an), literally "pure conversation" or "clear discourse," was a philosophical and social practice of the Wei–Jin period (3rd–4th centuries CE) in China, in which literati gathered to debate metaphysical questions in an elegant, witty and deliberately non-political style.

Origins and character

Qingtan grew out of the xuanxue ("dark learning," or Neo-Daoist) movement and the political dangers of the late Han and Three Kingdoms eras: with frank discussion of state affairs perilous under the Cao and Sima regimes, scholars turned to abstract topics drawn from the "Three Mysterious" texts — the Yijing, the Daodejing and the Zhuangzi. Conversationalists debated such problems as whether being arises from non-being, whether words can fully express meaning, and whether the sage has emotions.

Leading figures included He Yan and Wang Bi, who grounded xuanxue in commentaries on the classics, and the later circle celebrated as the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove, among them Ruan Ji and Ji Kang, whose gatherings mixed discourse with wine, music and conspicuous eccentricity. Debaters often wielded a fly-whisk (zhuwei) as a badge of the practice, and reputations could be made in a single brilliant exchange.

Records and legacy

The richest record of qingtan culture is the Shishuo Xinyu ("A New Account of the Tales of the World," 5th century), a collection of anecdotes about Wei–Jin gentlemen compiled under Liu Yiqing, which preserves the wit, repartee and character judgements typical of the conversations. Confucian critics later blamed qingtan for encouraging frivolity and neglecting statecraft — the collapse of the Western Jin was sometimes laid at its door in the phrase "pure talk ruins the state" — yet its emphasis on individual character appraisal and free metaphysical speculation left a lasting mark on Chinese intellectual culture and on the later development of Chan (Zen) dialogue.
