Traffic collision avoidance system

TCAS display in an airliner cockpit

A traffic alert and collision avoidance system (TCAS), also known as a traffic collision avoidance system and in ICAO terminology as an airborne collision avoidance system (ACAS), is an aircraft collision avoidance system designed to reduce the incidence of mid-air collisions between aircraft. It monitors the airspace around an aircraft for other aircraft equipped with a corresponding active transponder, independent of air traffic control, and warns pilots of the presence of other transponder-equipped aircraft that may present a threat of a mid-air collision.

Operation

TCAS interrogates the Mode C and Mode S transponders of nearby aircraft and, from the replies, builds a three-dimensional picture of traffic, predicting closest points of approach. If a conflict is predicted, the system first issues a traffic advisory (TA), alerting the crew to look for the intruder; if the threat develops, it issues a resolution advisory (RA), a direct instruction to climb or descend. When both aircraft are TCAS-equipped, the systems coordinate via the Mode S data link so that complementary manoeuvres are commanded. TCAS II, the widely mandated version, provides vertical-plane resolution advisories only; the newer ACAS X family introduces optimised logic and, in some variants, additional surveillance sources.

History and mandates

Development followed a series of mid-air collision disasters, including the 1956 Grand Canyon collision and the 1986 Cerritos collision, after which the United States mandated TCAS II on most commercial aircraft; ICAO standards and regional rules extended carriage requirements worldwide. TCAS is regarded as the last safety net against mid-air collision when separation services and see-and-avoid have failed. Pilots are trained to follow an RA immediately, even in preference to a contradictory air traffic control instruction — a lesson reinforced by the 2002 Überlingen mid-air collision.
