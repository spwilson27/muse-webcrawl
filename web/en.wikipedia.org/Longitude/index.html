Longitude

Longitude is a geographic coordinate that specifies the east–west position of a point on the surface of the Earth, or another celestial body. It is an angular measurement, usually expressed in degrees and denoted by the Greek letter lambda (λ), measured from the Prime Meridian, which passes through Greenwich, London.

Measurement

Positions east of the Prime Meridian run to +180° and west to −180°. Because the Earth rotates 15° per hour, longitude can be found from the difference between local time and Greenwich Mean Time, each hour of difference corresponding to 15° of longitude. Unlike latitude, there is no natural starting line, so a prime meridian had to be chosen by convention — formalised at the International Meridian Conference of 1884.

The longitude problem

Determining longitude at sea was one of the great scientific challenges of the 18th century. After the Scilly naval disaster of 1707, Britain offered the Longitude Prize (1714). Two solutions matured: the lunar distance method, supported by the Nautical Almanac, and John Harrison's marine chronometers H1–H4, which finally made accurate timekeeping at sea practical.
