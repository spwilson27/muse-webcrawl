Jet lag

Jet lag results when the body's circadian rhythm falls out of step with local time.
SpecialtySleep medicine
SymptomsInsomnia, daytime sleepiness, fatigue, poor concentration, gastrointestinal upset
CauseRapid travel across multiple time zones
ManagementLight exposure, sleep scheduling, melatonin

Jet lag, medically termed desynchronosis, is a temporary physiological condition that results from rapid long-distance travel across several time zones, as on a jet aircraft. The body's internal circadian clock, regulated largely by light and darkness, remains aligned to the departure time zone and must gradually readjust to the destination, during which sleep and alertness are disturbed.

Symptoms and course

Common symptoms include difficulty falling asleep or early awakening, excessive daytime sleepiness, reduced concentration and performance, irritability, headaches, and digestive problems. Severity increases with the number of time zones crossed; a rough rule is that recovery takes about one day per zone crossed. Eastward travel is usually harder than westward, because it requires advancing the body clock—falling asleep earlier than the body expects—whereas westward travel merely delays it.

Mechanism

The master circadian pacemaker in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus entrains to the light–dark cycle, chiefly through daylight exposure. After a sudden shift in local time, peripheral clocks in organs such as the liver adjust at different rates, producing internal desynchrony until the system realigns.

Management

Strategies include timing bright-light exposure to advance or delay the clock as needed, gradually shifting sleep before departure, staying hydrated, and short-term use of melatonin or sleep aids. Airlines and athletes increasingly use structured light and sleep protocols to reduce its effects.
