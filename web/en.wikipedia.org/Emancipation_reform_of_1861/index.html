The emancipation reform of 1861 in Russia was the abolition of serfdom, which by that time bound some 23 million peasants — more than a third of the empire's population — to private landowners, the state, and the imperial family. Proclaimed by Emperor Alexander II in his Emancipation Manifesto of 3 March 1861, it formed the first and most important of the liberal reforms undertaken during his reign.

Background

Serfdom had become entrenched over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, binding peasants to the land and giving landlords extensive powers over them, including the right to sell them, sometimes separately from the land. By the mid-nineteenth century the institution was widely blamed for Russia's economic backwardness and technological lag. The defeat in the Crimean War (1853–1856) exposed the weakness of a state whose army and industry rested on serf labour, and Alexander II told the Moscow nobility in 1856 that it was better to abolish serfdom from above than to wait until it began to abolish itself from below.

Terms of the settlement

The reform freed the serfs of private landowners in two stages. Peasants received personal freedom immediately, but land remained formally the landlords' property during a period of "temporary obligation", during which peasants continued to pay dues or render labour for their allotments. Redemption agreements then allowed peasant communes (the mir or obshchina) to purchase their allotments, with the state advancing 80 per cent of the price as a loan repayable over 49 years at 6 per cent interest. The landlord received government bonds for most of the value.

Allotments were often smaller than the land peasants had previously worked, particularly in the fertile black-earth provinces, where landlords retained valuable "cut-offs" (otrezki). State peasants received generally better terms in a separate settlement of 1866.

Consequences

The emancipation transformed Russian society without resolving the agrarian question. Redemption payments burdened peasant households for decades — they were reduced in 1881, halved in 1906, and abolished from 1907 — and communal tenure, with periodic repartition of land, was blamed for low agricultural productivity. Land hunger and peasant unrest remained central drivers of revolutionary politics through 1905 and 1917. Nevertheless, the reform freed labour for industry, ended the legal sale of human beings, and is generally regarded as the defining act of Alexander II's reign, earning him the name "the Liberator".
