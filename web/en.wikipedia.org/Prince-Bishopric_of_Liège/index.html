Prince-Bishopric of Liège

Existedc. 980 – 1795

StatusEcclesiastical principality of the Holy Roman Empire

The Prince-Bishopric of Liège (French: Principauté de Liège) was an ecclesiastical principality of the Holy Roman Empire in the Low Countries, ruled by the bishop of Liège as a temporal prince. For more than eight centuries it formed a substantial state in the Meuse valley, roughly corresponding to much of the modern Belgian province of Liège and parts of neighbouring provinces.

History

The principality originated in the late tenth century, when Bishop Notker (972–1008) acquired comital rights over Huy and other territories, uniting spiritual and secular authority. The prince-bishops governed a prosperous region of metalworking, coal mining, and trade, but their rule was repeatedly contested by the guilds and citizens of Liège, whose revolts — including the destruction of the city by Charles the Bold of Burgundy in 1468 — punctuated its medieval history. The Peace of Fexhe (1316) gave the principality an early constitutional charter.

Revolution and end

In 1789 the Liège Revolution expelled the prince-bishop and proclaimed a republic, in parallel with events in France. After restoration and renewed upheaval, French revolutionary forces annexed the territory in 1795, formally ending the prince-bishopric; its lands were incorporated into France and, after 1815, into the United Kingdom of the Netherlands and then Belgium.
