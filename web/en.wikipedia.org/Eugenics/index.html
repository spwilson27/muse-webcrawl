Eugenics

Eugenics (/juːˈdʒɛnɪks/ yoo-JEN-iks; from Greek εὐ- "good" and -γενής "born") is a set of beliefs and practices that aim to improve the genetic quality of a human population. Historically, eugenicists attempted to alter human gene pools by excluding people and groups judged to be inferior or promoting those judged to be superior. In recent years, the term has seen a revival in bioethical discussions on the ethics of human genetic engineering.

The concept predates Francis Galton's coinage of the term in 1883, but Galton, a cousin of Charles Darwin, systematised it after reading On the Origin of Species. Eugenics became an academic discipline at many colleges and universities and received funding from various sources. Organisations such as the Eugenics Record Office at Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory were established.

History

Eugenic ideas were widespread in the early 20th century. In the United States, compulsory sterilisation laws were enacted in more than 30 states, beginning with Indiana in 1907; the Supreme Court upheld such laws in Buck v. Bell (1927). Eugenic policies were also adopted in Canada, Sweden, the United Kingdom, and many other countries.

Nazi Germany's racial hygiene policies, including the T4 euthanasia programme and ultimately the Holocaust, brought eugenics into disrepute after the Second World War. The Nuremberg trials exposed the crimes committed in eugenics' name, and UNESCO statements on race in the 1950s repudiated its scientific basis.

Methods

Historically, eugenics was promoted through two broad approaches: positive eugenics, encouraging reproduction among those considered "fit", and negative eugenics, discouraging or preventing reproduction among those considered "unfit" — including through marriage laws, forced sterilisation, segregation, and immigration restrictions.

Modern debates

Advances in genetic testing, preimplantation genetic diagnosis, and gene editing (such as CRISPR) have revived ethical debate, sometimes described under terms such as "liberal eugenics" or "new eugenics", though most bioethicists emphasise the distinction between voluntary individual choices and state-coerced programmes.
