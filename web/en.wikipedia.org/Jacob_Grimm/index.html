Jacob Grimm

Born: 4 January 1785, Hanau, Hesse-Kassel
Died: 20 September 1863, Berlin, Prussia
Occupation: Philologist, folklorist, jurist
Known for: Grimms' Fairy Tales; Grimm's law; Deutsches Wörterbuch

Jacob Ludwig Carl Grimm (4 January 1785 – 20 September 1863) was a German philologist, jurist and folklorist, the elder of the Brothers Grimm. With his brother Wilhelm he collected and published Grimm's Fairy Tales and undertook the monumental Deutsches Wörterbuch, while his own linguistic scholarship — above all the formulation of Grimm's law — founded the scientific study of the Germanic languages.

Life

Jacob Grimm was born in Hanau and studied law at Marburg, where Friedrich Carl von Savigny awakened his interest in the historical study of language and law. He worked in Paris on Napoleon's library service, then as a librarian and civil servant in Kassel. In 1830 both brothers moved to the University of Göttingen, Jacob as professor and librarian. In 1837 they were among the "Göttingen Seven", professors dismissed for protesting King Ernest Augustus I's abrogation of Hanover's constitution. Invited to Berlin in 1841 by the King of Prussia, Jacob spent his later decades there, and in 1848 he sat in the Frankfurt Parliament during the revolutions. He outlived Wilhelm (d. 1859) by four years, dying in Berlin in 1863.

The fairy tales

The brothers' Kinder- und Hausmärchen (Children's and Household Tales), first published in two volumes (1812, 1815) and repeatedly expanded and revised through seven editions to 1857, collected tales from oral informants — notably the Hassenpflug and Wild families and the storyteller Dorothea Viehmann — alongside literary sources. Conceived as a scholarly monument to German folk poetry, the collection, containing "Cinderella", "Snow White", "Hansel and Gretel" and "Rapunzel", became one of the most widely read books in the world and a cornerstone of folklore studies.

Philology

Jacob's Deutsche Grammatik (1819–1837) was a comparative grammar of all the Germanic languages. In its 1822 revision he set out the systematic consonant correspondences between Germanic and the other Indo-European languages — the First Germanic Sound Shift, now called Grimm's law — a landmark in historical linguistics and a model of the regularity of sound change. His Deutsche Mythologie (1835) reconstructed pre-Christian Germanic religion from language, tale and custom, and Deutsche Rechtsalterthümer (1828) applied the same historical method to Germanic law. The Deutsches Wörterbuch, begun by the brothers in 1838 and published from 1854, was completed by successor scholars only in 1961, a century after Jacob's death.
