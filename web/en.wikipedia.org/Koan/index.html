Koan

A kōan (Japanese: 公案; Chinese: gōng'àn) is a story, dialogue, question, or statement used in Zen Buddhist practice to provoke the "great doubt" and to test a student's progress. Koans are often paradoxical or nonsensical on their surface — famously, "What is the sound of one hand clapping?" — and are not meant to be solved by rational analysis alone.

Origins

The term originally meant a legal case or public record in Chinese bureaucracy. In Chan Buddhism it came to denote recorded encounters between masters and students, treated as precedents of awakened conduct. Koan study was systematised during the Song dynasty, especially by Dahui Zonggao, who promoted kanhua ("observing the phrase") meditation centred on a koan's critical phrase (huatou).

Famous koans

Well-known examples include: Zhaozhou's "Mu" (whether a dog has Buddha-nature, answered with "Mu" — "no/not"); "What was your face before your parents were born?"; Yunmen's "Every day is a good day"; and Hakuin's "one hand" question. Classic collections annotate cases with verses and commentary: the Blue Cliff Record (100 cases) and The Gateless Barrier (48 cases) are the most influential.

Practice

In the Japanese Rinzai school, students work through a structured curriculum of koans under a teacher, presenting their understanding in private interviews (dokusan). The Soto school has historically been more sceptical of formal koan curricula, favouring shikantaza ("just sitting"), though Soto teachers also comment on koans.

In Western culture

Koans entered Western awareness through the writings of D. T. Suzuki, Alan Watts, and the Beats. The word is now loosely used for any paradoxical riddle, and "programming koans" are used as exercises in software education.
