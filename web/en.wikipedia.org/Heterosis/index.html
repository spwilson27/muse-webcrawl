Heterosis


Hybrid vigour in maize

Heterosis, also called hybrid vigour, is the improved or increased function of any biological quality in a hybrid offspring. An offspring is heterotic if its traits are enhanced as a result of mixing the genetic contributions of its parents. These effects can be due to Mendelian or non-Mendelian inheritance. Heterosis is the opposite of inbreeding depression, the reduced fitness of offspring of related parents.

Genetic explanations

Three classical hypotheses account for heterosis:

  • Dominance — deleterious recessive alleles from one parent are masked by dominant alleles from the other, so the hybrid is fitter than either inbred parent.
  • Overdominance — the heterozygous state at particular loci is itself superior to either homozygote.
  • Epistasis — favourable interactions among genes from the two parents produce the enhanced phenotype.

Modern genomic work suggests dominance effects explain most heterosis, with contributions from overdominance and epistasis at some loci.

Agricultural importance

Heterosis underpins much of modern agriculture. George Harrison Shull and Edward M. East independently described the phenomenon in maize in 1908; hybrid seed corn, commercialised from the 1920s–30s, raised U.S. corn yields dramatically and must be repurchased each season because vigour declines in the second generation. Hybrid rice, developed in China under Yuan Longping in the 1970s, similarly boosted yields and food security. Hybrid vigour is also exploited in sorghum, sunflower, vegetables, and livestock crossbreeding.
