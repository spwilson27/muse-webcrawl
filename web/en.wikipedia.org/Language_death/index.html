Language death

In linguistics, language death occurs when a language loses its last native speaker; by extension, language extinction is when the language is no longer known by anyone, including second-language speakers. Language death may occur suddenly, when a community is dispersed or destroyed, or gradually, as speakers shift over generations to a dominant language.

Scale and causes

Of the approximately 7,000 living languages, a large share are spoken by very small communities; linguists estimate that a language dies roughly every few weeks, and that 40–90% of languages may vanish by 2100. Causes include colonisation, forced assimilation and boarding schools, urbanisation, mass media, economic pressure toward lingua francas such as English, Spanish, Mandarin, and Russian, and the death of speakers in epidemics or disasters. Hotspots of loss include northern Australia, the Amazon, Siberia, and the Pacific Northwest.

Stages

UNESCO's framework grades endangerment from "vulnerable" (children learn the language but it is restricted in use) through "definitely", "severely", and "critically endangered" to "extinct". Before death, languages typically shrink in domains and structure, and their last fluent speakers are often elderly "terminal speakers", sometimes alongside "rememberers" with partial knowledge.

Consequences and revitalisation

Each language encodes unique knowledge — of local ecology, kinship, oral history, and cognition — that is lost with it. Revitalisation movements have achieved notable successes: Hebrew was revived as a spoken vernacular; Welsh, Māori, and Hawaiian have been strengthened through immersion schools ("language nests"), broadcasting, and official status; Cornish and Manx have been revived from written records after losing their last native speakers. Documentation by linguists creates records from which future generations may reclaim dormant languages.
