Kulturkampf

The Kulturkampf (German for "culture struggle") was a conflict between the government of the German Empire, led by Chancellor Otto von Bismarck, and the Roman Catholic Church under Pope Pius IX, from about 1871 to 1878. Bismarck sought to subject the Church — and Germany's Catholic minority and its Centre Party — to state control; the campaign largely backfired and was gradually abandoned.

Background

The unification of Germany in 1871 created a Protestant-majority empire with a large Catholic minority. The proclamation of papal infallibility (1870) and the founding of the Catholic Centre Party alarmed Bismarck and his National Liberal allies, who saw "ultramontane" loyalty to Rome as a threat to the new state. The liberal pathologist Rudolf Virchow coined the term Kulturkampf.

Measures

Prussia and the Reich enacted a series of laws: the Pulpit Law forbade clergy from discussing politics in sermons; the Jesuit Law expelled the Jesuits; the May Laws of 1873 placed the training and appointment of clergy under state supervision and removed church discipline from ecclesiastical courts; civil marriage became compulsory; and the Bread Basket Law cut state payments to non-compliant dioceses. Bishops who resisted were fined, imprisoned, or exiled — by 1876 all Prussian dioceses were vacant or their bishops in custody or exile — and some 1,800 priests were jailed or expelled.

Failure and retreat

Far from weakening political Catholicism, persecution unified it: the Centre Party under Ludwig Windthorst doubled its vote in 1874. Bismarck, needing Catholic support against the Socialists and shifting alliances after 1878, negotiated with the new Pope Leo XIII. Most anti-Catholic laws were repealed during the 1880s, though the Jesuit Law lasted until 1917 and civil marriage remained.

Legacy

The Kulturkampf deepened the confessional divide in German politics, strengthened the Catholic milieu, and stands as a cautionary example of state overreach against a religious minority; similar church-state struggles occurred in Switzerland and Austria in the same period.
