Cumbric

Cumbric was a variety of the Common Brittonic language spoken during the Early Middle Ages in the Yr Hen Ogledd ("The Old North") in what is now the counties of Westmorland, Cumberland, and the southern Scottish Lowlands of Great Britain. It was closely related to Old Welsh and likely also to Pictish and the Brittonic spoken in other parts of sub-Roman Britain. Little survives of the language beyond place names, some personal names, and a small number of terms preserved in legal documents.

Evidence

No connected texts in Cumbric survive. Evidence comes chiefly from place names across Cumbria and southern Scotland — such as Carlisle, Penrith, and Glasgow — from the names of kings and saints in Welsh and Latin sources, and from a handful of legal terms in the medieval Scottish Leges inter Brettos et Scottos. Shepherds' counting scores recorded in northern England (such as "yan, tan, tethera") preserve numerals strikingly similar to Welsh and are often cited as possible survivals of Cumbric or of a related Brittonic counting tradition.

History

After the Roman withdrawal, Brittonic-speaking kingdoms such as Rheged, Strathclyde, and Gododdin flourished in the Old North and were celebrated by the poets Taliesin and Aneirin. From the seventh century, the expansion of the Anglian kingdom of Northumbria, Norse settlement, and the growing dominance of Gaelic and later Scots eroded Cumbric speech. The Kingdom of Strathclyde survived into the eleventh century; Cumbric is generally thought to have died out by the 12th or 13th century, though traces may have persisted longer in remote uplands.

Classification

Cumbric is classified as a P-Celtic Brittonic language alongside Welsh, Cornish, and Breton. Scholars debate whether it was a distinct language or a dialect of Old Welsh; the surviving evidence shows some distinctive developments, but the fragmentary record makes firm conclusions difficult.
