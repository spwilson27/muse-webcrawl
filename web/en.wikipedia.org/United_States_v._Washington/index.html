United States v. Washington

United States v. Washington, 384 F. Supp. 312 (W.D. Wash. 1974), is the landmark federal court case known for the Boldt Decision, which affirmed the fishing rights of treaty tribes in Washington State. It takes its name from Judge George Hugo Boldt of the United States District Court for the Western District of Washington.

Holding

Judge Boldt ruled that the treaties of the 1850s, in which tribes had reserved the right of taking fish "in common with" citizens of the territory, entitled the treaty tribes to harvest up to fifty percent of the harvestable salmon and steelhead passing through their usual and accustomed fishing grounds, and recognised the tribes as co-managers of the resource alongside the state. The decision followed years of conflict, including the "fish-in" protests of the 1960s in which figures such as Billy Frank Jr. had challenged state enforcement.

Aftermath

The Ninth Circuit affirmed, and the US Supreme Court substantially upheld the ruling in Washington v. Washington State Commercial Passenger Fishing Vessel Association (1979). Later phases of the case addressed hatchery fish, environmental protections, and shellfish rights.
