The Kingdom of Sardinia was a state in Southern Europe created in 1324 as a possession of the Crown of Aragon, encompassing the island of Sardinia. Formally granted by Pope Boniface VIII to James II of Aragon in 1297 (as the Regnum Sardiniae et Corsicae), it was realised by conquest in 1323–1324 and remained under Aragonese and then Spanish rule for nearly four centuries, until it was ceded to the House of Savoy in 1720.

Creation and the Arborean wars

The infante Alfonso (later Alfonso IV) landed in 1323 and defeated the Pisans, who had held much of the island, taking Cagliari in 1324. Aragonese rule, however, was resisted for decades by the Judicate of Arborea, the surviving native Sardinian state, led by judges such as Marianus IV and his daughter Eleanor of Arborea, the celebrated lawgiver of the Carta de Logu. Only with the Battle of Sanluri (1409) and the sale of Arborea's rights in 1420 did Aragon control the whole island.

Spanish period

Under the Catholic Monarchs Sardinia entered the Spanish composite monarchy, governed by a viceroy from Cagliari and represented in parliament by the three stamenti. It shared Spain's wars and fiscal burdens, suffered Barbary corsair raids on its coasts, and remained a largely feudal, agrarian society exporting grain, cheese, and tuna. During the War of the Spanish Succession the island was seized by Habsburg forces in 1708, assigned to Austria by the Treaty of Utrecht (1713), reconquered briefly by Spain in 1717, and finally exchanged in 1720: Victor Amadeus II of Savoy gave up Sicily and received Sardinia, taking the royal title "King of Sardinia" that his house would carry into the unification of Italy.

Legacy

The kingdom gave the Savoyard state its royal name: the later "Kingdom of Sardinia" (1720–1861), centred on Turin, is a distinct continuation treated in its own article. The Catalan-Aragonese centuries left deep marks on Sardinia, from the Catalan-speaking town of Alghero to the island's legal and architectural heritage.
