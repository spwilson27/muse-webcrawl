William Pitt, 1st Earl of Chatham (15 November 1708 – 11 May 1778) was a British statesman of the Whig group who served as Prime Minister of Great Britain from 1766 to 1768. He is best remembered for his wartime leadership during the Seven Years' War, in which Britain achieved a string of decisive victories that made it the world's dominant colonial power.

Early life

Pitt was born in London, the grandson of Thomas Pitt, governor of Madras, who had amassed a fortune in India including the famous Pitt Diamond. He was educated at Eton College and Trinity College, Oxford, and entered Parliament in 1735 as MP for Old Sarum, a rotten borough. He quickly became a leading voice among the "Patriot Whigs" opposed to the government of Robert Walpole.

Seven Years' War

Appointed Secretary of State in 1756, Pitt directed British strategy in the Seven Years' War, declaring "I know that I can save this country and that no one else can." His strategy of subsidising Prussia under Frederick the Great while concentrating British naval and colonial power abroad produced the annus mirabilis of 1759, with victories at Quebec, Minden, Lagos and Quiberon Bay. The conquest of Canada and gains in India laid the foundations of the British Empire.

Prime Minister and later life

Pitt returned to office as Prime Minister in 1766, accepting the earldom of Chatham, but ill health (including severe gout and mental illness) undermined his ministry, which effectively ended by 1768. In the 1770s he spoke in defence of the American colonists, urging conciliation. On 7 April 1778 he collapsed while speaking in the House of Lords against a motion to surrender America, and died a month later at Hayes in Kent. He was given a state funeral and buried in Westminster Abbey.

Legacy

Pitt is widely regarded as one of Britain's greatest prime ministers. His son, William Pitt the Younger, also became Prime Minister. The city of Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, is named in his honour.
