Ba'athism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ba'athism, also spelled Baathism (from Arabic baʿth, "renaissance" or "resurrection"), is an Arab nationalist ideology that promotes the creation and development of a unified Arab state through the leadership of a vanguard party over a socialist state. The ideology is officially based on the theories of the Syrian intellectuals Michel Aflaq (a Christian), Salah al-Din al-Bitar (a Sunni Muslim) and Zaki al-Arsuzi (an Alawite).

Core principles

Ba'athist ideology rests on three pillars summarised in the slogan "Unity, Freedom, Socialism": the unity of the Arab nation across existing state borders; freedom from colonial and foreign domination; and an Arab form of socialism distinct from Marxist-Leninist internationalism. Aflaq argued the Arab nation had an "eternal mission" and that the Ba'ath ("resurrection") party would lead its renaissance. In practice, Ba'athist governments in Syria and Iraq emphasised secularism, state-led economic development, and the armed forces as the guardian of the revolution.

History

The Arab Ba'ath Party was founded in Damascus in 1947 and, after merging with Akram al-Hawrani's Arab Socialist Party in 1952, became the Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party. A military coup brought the party to power in Syria in 1963, and a second branch seized power in Iraq in 1968, ruling under Ahmed Hassan al-Bakr and then Saddam Hussein until the 2003 invasion of Iraq. A bitter schism in 1966 split the movement into rival Syrian- and Iraqi-dominated factions, each claiming to be the legitimate "National Command." The Syrian branch under Hafez al-Assad and his son Bashar al-Assad ruled Syria until the fall of the Assad regime in December 2024.

Criticism

Critics charge that Ba'athist regimes, despite socialist and pan-Arab rhetoric, developed into authoritarian one-party states dominated by security services and, in both Syria and Iraq, by networks associated with minority communities and ruling families. The ideology's commitment to secular Arab nationalism also brought it into conflict with Islamist movements and with non-Arab minorities, notably the Kurds.
