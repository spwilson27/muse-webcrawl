Panthéon, Paris

The Panthéon on the Montagne Sainte-Geneviève, in the Latin Quarter of Paris.

LocationPlace du Panthéon, 5th arrondissement, Paris, France
ArchitectJacques-Germain Soufflot
Construction1758–1790
StyleNeoclassicism
FunctionMausoleum of distinguished French citizens

The Panthéon is a monument in the 5th arrondissement of Paris, France, standing in the Latin Quarter atop the Montagne Sainte-Geneviève. Built between 1758 and 1790 to designs by Jacques-Germain Soufflot, it was originally intended as a church dedicated to Saint Genevieve, the patron saint of Paris, but the French Revolution converted it into a mausoleum for distinguished French citizens — an enduring role symbolised by the inscription on its pediment: Aux grands hommes la patrie reconnaissante ("To the great men, the grateful fatherland").

History

King Louis XV vowed in 1744 to rebuild the ruined abbey church of Sainte-Geneviève after recovering from illness. Soufflot's design combined a Greek-cross plan, a triple dome and a classical portico, aiming to unite Gothic lightness with classical grandeur. Completed in 1790, the building was secularised by the Revolutionary government in 1791, when Mirabeau and Voltaire were interred there. Its function alternated between church and mausoleum under successive regimes before becoming definitively a civic temple under the Third Republic, on the occasion of Victor Hugo's funeral in 1885.

Architecture

The Panthéon is 110 m long and 84 m wide, crowned by a dome rising 83 m, modelled in part on St Paul's Cathedral in London and the original Pantheon in Rome. Its crypt occupies the entire lower level and houses the tombs of France's national heroes.

Burials

Those honoured include Voltaire, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Victor Hugo, Alexandre Dumas, Émile Zola, Marie Curie (the first woman interred on her own merits, in 1995), Louis Braille, Jean Monnet, Simone Veil and Josephine Baker, who was inducted in 2021. Admission ("pantheonisation") is decided by the President of the Republic.

Foucault's pendulum

In 1851 the physicist Léon Foucault publicly demonstrated the rotation of the Earth with a 67-metre pendulum suspended from the Panthéon's dome; a working pendulum is again displayed in the building today.
