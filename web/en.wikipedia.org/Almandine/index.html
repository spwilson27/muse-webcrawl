Almandine

Almandine, also known as almandite, is a species of mineral belonging to the garnet group. The name is a corruption of alabandicus, the term used by Pliny the Elder for a stone cut at Alabanda in Asia Minor. Its chemical formula is Fe2+3Al2(SiO4)3, an iron aluminium silicate; it is the iron end-member of the pyralspite garnet series.

Properties

Almandine is typically deep red to reddish brown or purplish, with a hardness of about 7 to 7.5 on the Mohs scale and a specific gravity around 4.3. It forms a solid solution series with pyrope (the magnesium garnet) and with spessartine (the manganese garnet), so most natural crystals contain proportions of all three components.

Occurrence and use

Almandine is the most common garnet and is widespread in metamorphic rocks such as mica schists and gneisses, where its growth records the pressure and temperature history of the rock; geologists use it as an index mineral of medium-grade metamorphism. It also occurs in some granites and pegmatites and in alluvial deposits as a heavy mineral. Transparent stones are cut as gemstones — the dark red "carbuncle" of older literature was usually almandine — and crushed garnet is widely used as an industrial abrasive for sandblasting and water-jet cutting. Notable localities include India, Sri Lanka, Brazil, and the Adirondack region of New York.
