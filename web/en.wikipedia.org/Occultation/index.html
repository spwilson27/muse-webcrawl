Occultation

Occultation
FieldAstronomy
DefinitionOne celestial body passing in front of another, hiding it from view
Common examplesLunar occultations of stars and planets; asteroid occultations
Related eventsTransit, eclipse

An occultation is an event that occurs when one object is hidden from the view of an observer by another object that passes between them. The term is used in astronomy to describe events such as the passage of the Moon in front of a star or planet, or of an asteroid in front of a distant star.

Types

A lunar occultation occurs when the Moon moves in front of a star, planet or other body; because the Moon has no substantial atmosphere, stars disappear and reappear almost instantaneously at its limb. A solar eclipse is a special case of occultation in which the Moon hides the Sun. When the nearer body appears much smaller than the farther one, the event is usually called a transit, as with the transits of Venus and Mercury across the Sun.

Scientific uses

Timing occultations has historically refined knowledge of the Moon's orbit and profile. Asteroid occultations — in which an asteroid briefly dims a star — allow astronomers, including coordinated networks of amateur observers, to measure asteroid sizes and shapes with great precision. Stellar occultations by planets and their rings revealed the rings of Uranus in 1977 and remain a key tool for studying the atmospheres of distant bodies such as Pluto, by watching how starlight fades gradually through an atmosphere. Occultations by Kuiper Belt objects are used to detect small, faint bodies that are otherwise hard to observe.

Observation

Predicted occultations are published in advance, and observations are coordinated by organisations such as the International Occultation Timing Association. Grazing occultations, in which a star skims the Moon's mountainous limb and blinks in and out, are especially valuable for mapping lunar terrain.
