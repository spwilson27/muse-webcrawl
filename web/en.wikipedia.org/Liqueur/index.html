Liqueur

A liqueur (US: /lɪˈkɜːr/; UK: /lɪˈkjʊər/) is an alcoholic drink composed of spirits (such as brandy, vodka or neutral grain spirit) and additional flavorings such as sugar, fruits, herbs, and spices. They are often served with or after dessert, and are typically heavily sweetened, though not necessarily unmixed with other ingredients.

History

Liqueurs trace their origins to the herbal cordials and elixirs prepared by monks and apothecaries in medieval Europe, who steeped herbs and spices in distilled spirits for medicinal purposes. Notable early products include Chartreuse, made by Carthusian monks from a 1605 recipe, and Bénédictine. Improvements in distillation and the availability of sugar and exotic spices in the early modern period spread liqueur-making across Europe.

Production and categories

Liqueurs may be produced by infusion, maceration, percolation or redistillation of flavouring materials in spirit, followed by sweetening. Under European Union law a liqueur must have a minimum alcohol content of 15% by volume and a minimum sugar content of 70–100 grams per litre depending on the type. Categories include fruit liqueurs (such as Cointreau and Grand Marnier from oranges, and limoncello), cream liqueurs (such as Baileys), coffee liqueurs (Kahlúa, Tia Maria), herbal liqueurs, and anise-flavoured liqueurs such as sambuca. A liqueur differs from a flavoured spirit or "schnapps" mainly by its higher sugar content and lower typical alcohol strength.
