Superior highly composite number

In number theory, a superior highly composite number is a natural number which, roughly speaking, has many divisors relative to its size. Formally, a natural number n is superior highly composite if there exists a positive real number ε such that for all natural numbers k, d(n)/nε ≥ d(k)/kε, where d(n) denotes the number of divisors of n.

History

The concept was introduced by Srinivasa Ramanujan in his 1915 paper on highly composite numbers, written while he was in England. Ramanujan studied numbers maximising the ratio d(n)/nε for various values of ε and showed that such "superior" numbers have a rigid structure: their prime factorisations use the first primes with non-increasing exponents.

Properties

Every superior highly composite number is highly composite, but not conversely. Their prime factorisation has the form 2a3b5c… with exponents a ≥ b ≥ c ≥ … decreasing by at most limited amounts as ε varies. As ε decreases, the sequence of such numbers changes only at critical values; the numbers are closely related to the primorials and to record values of the divisor function. Superior highly composite numbers have been used to test the limits of the Riemann hypothesis, since violations would imply the existence of numbers with exceptionally many divisors relative to their size.

Examples

The first superior highly composite numbers are 2, 6, 12, 60, 120, 360, 2520, 5040, 55440, 720720, 1441440, 4324320, 21621600, 367567200, 6983776800 and 13967553600. Many of them, such as 5040 and 55440, were also singled out historically — Plato mentioned 5040 as a convenient number for a city's population because of its many divisors.
