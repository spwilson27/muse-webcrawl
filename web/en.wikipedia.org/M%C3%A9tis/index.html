The Métis are an Indigenous people of Canada and parts of the northern United States whose ethnogenesis arose from unions between First Nations women and European (chiefly French and Scottish) fur traders beginning in the 17th and 18th centuries. They developed a distinct culture, language and political identity, particularly in the Red River region of present-day Manitoba.

History

Métis communities grew around the fur trade, combining European and Indigenous technologies, languages and kinship systems. Their language, Michif, blends French nouns with Cree verbs and grammar. In 1869–70, under Louis Riel, the Métis of Red River formed a provisional government that negotiated Manitoba's entry into Confederation. A second resistance followed in 1885 in Saskatchewan, after which Riel was executed and many Métis were dispersed westward.

Legal status

The Constitution Act, 1982 recognises the Métis as one of the three Aboriginal peoples of Canada. The Supreme Court's 2003 decision in R v Powley set out a test for Métis rights, and in Daniels v Canada (2016) the Court held that Métis fall within federal jurisdiction under section 91(24) of the Constitution Act, 1867.

Culture

Métis culture is symbolised by the infinity flag, the sash (ceinture fléchée), fiddle music and jigging, and distinctive floral beadwork. The Métis Nation is represented politically by the Métis National Council and provincial organisations.
