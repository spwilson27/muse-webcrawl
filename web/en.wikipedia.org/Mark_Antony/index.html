Mark Antony (Latin: Marcus Antonius; 14 January 83 BC – 1 August 30 BC), commonly known in English as Mark Antony, was a Roman politician and general who played a critical role in the transformation of the Roman Republic into the autocratic Roman Empire.

Early career

Antony was a supporter of Julius Caesar and served with him during the Gallic Wars and the civil war. Appointed administrator of Italy while Caesar eliminated his opponents in Greece and Africa, Antony mishandled his office and was removed. After Caesar's assassination in 44 BC, Antony formed an official political alliance, the Second Triumvirate, with Octavian (later Augustus) and Marcus Aemilius Lepidus.

The Triumvirate

The triumvirs carried out bloody proscriptions, in which the orator Cicero was killed, and defeated Caesar's assassins Brutus and Cassius at the Battle of Philippi (42 BC). Antony then took control of Rome's eastern provinces, summoned Cleopatra, queen of Egypt, to Tarsus, and began a political and romantic alliance with her. He married Octavian's sister Octavia in 40 BC to cement the alliance, but later repudiated her.

War with Octavian and death

Relations with Octavian collapsed over Antony's grants of Roman territory to Cleopatra and their children (the Donations of Alexandria) and his will, which Octavian seized and publicised in Rome. The Senate declared war on Cleopatra, and Antony was defeated at the naval Battle of Actium in 31 BC. He fled to Egypt and, when Octavian invaded, took his own life at Alexandria in 30 BC; Cleopatra died shortly afterwards, and Egypt was annexed by Rome.

Legacy

Antony was subjected to a damnatio memoriae under Augustus, but later emperors of his line through Octavia rehabilitated his memory. His dramatic life — dramatised by Plutarch and by Shakespeare in Antony and Cleopatra and Julius Caesar — made him one of the most famous figures of the late Republic.
