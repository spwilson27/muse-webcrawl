History of the Democratic Party (United States)

The Democratic Party of the United States is the world's oldest active political party. It traces its origins to the Democratic-Republican Party founded by Thomas Jefferson and James Madison in the 1790s, and was organised in its modern form in 1828 under Martin Van Buren to support Andrew Jackson. For much of the 19th century it was the party of the agrarian South and of limited government; in the 20th century it was transformed by Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal into the party of economic regulation and social welfare.

Origins and Jacksonian democracy

The party that emerged in 1828 championed "Jacksonian democracy": expanded white male suffrage, opposition to a national bank, and hostility to concentrated economic privilege. Jackson's presidency (1829–1837) destroyed the Second Bank of the United States and survived the Nullification Crisis. Under Jackson and his successors the party dominated national politics until 1860.

Slavery, Civil War and the Gilded Age

Disputes over the expansion of slavery split the party in 1860, enabling Abraham Lincoln's election. During the Civil War, Democrats divided between War Democrats and anti-war "Copperheads". After the war, the party of the white South opposed Reconstruction and relied on the "Solid South". Nationally it remained competitive — Grover Cleveland won the presidency twice (1884, 1892) — campaigning on low tariffs and conservative finance, while the bulk of its support came from Southern whites, immigrants and urban machines.

Progressivism and Wilson

In 1896 William Jennings Bryan's populist campaign for free silver redirected the party toward reform. Woodrow Wilson (1913–1921) enacted the Federal Reserve, the income tax under the Sixteenth Amendment, tariff reductions and antitrust legislation, and led the United States into the First World War. The 1920s, however, were a decade of Republican dominance.

The New Deal coalition

Franklin D. Roosevelt's victory in 1932, amid the Great Depression, built the New Deal coalition — labour unions, farmers, urban ethnics, intellectuals, the Solid South and increasingly African Americans — that dominated American politics for a generation. The New Deal created Social Security, banking regulation, labour rights and vast public works. Roosevelt won four elections; Harry S. Truman succeeded him and desegregated the armed forces in 1948, prompting a segregationist "Dixiecrat" walkout.

Civil rights and realignment

John F. Kennedy (1961–1963) and Lyndon B. Johnson enacted the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, along with Medicare, Medicaid and the War on Poverty. The civil rights revolution ended the Solid South: white Southern voters gradually moved to the Republican Party, while Black voters became a core Democratic constituency. The Vietnam War split the party in 1968, and the 1970s brought Watergate-era reform and the presidency of Jimmy Carter (1977–1981).

Late 20th century

After losses to Ronald Reagan, the party's centrist wing — organised in the Democratic Leadership Council — promoted a "Third Way". Bill Clinton (1993–2001) won two terms on that platform, balancing the budget while signing welfare reform and trade agreements such as NAFTA, amid strong economic growth.

21st century

George W. Bush's disputed 2000 victory and the Iraq War galvanised the party. Barack Obama, elected in 2008 as the first African American president, passed the Affordable Care Act and the Dodd-Frank financial reforms. The party lost the presidency in 2016 to Donald Trump, regained it with Joe Biden in 2020 amid the COVID-19 pandemic, and lost again in 2024. In the 21st century the party's base has become concentrated among urban, educated and minority voters, championing expanding healthcare access, climate policy and social liberalism.
