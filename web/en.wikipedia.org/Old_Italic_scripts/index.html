Old Italic scripts

The Old Italic scripts are a family of ancient alphabets used in Italy from about the eighth century BC for writing Etruscan, Oscan, Umbrian, Faliscan, early Latin, and other Italic languages, as well as several languages of the Alpine region. All derive from a western form of the Greek alphabet transmitted through the Greek colonies of southern Italy, most likely via Cumae.

Characteristics

The scripts were typically written right to left, though early Latin and some transitional texts run left to right or boustrophedon. Letter inventories varied by language — Etruscan, lacking voiced stops, dropped or repurposed several Greek letters, while Latin retained others. The Etruscan alphabet of 26 letters, known from ivory writing tablets such as the Marsiliana d'Albegna abecedarium, served as the main conduit through which the alphabet passed to Rome.

Legacy

Of the family, only the Latin alphabet achieved lasting use, becoming — through Rome — the ancestor of the modern Latin script used worldwide. The Old Italic scripts are encoded as a single block in Unicode, and inscriptions in them, such as the Pyrgi tablets in Etruscan, remain central to the study of pre-Roman Italy.
