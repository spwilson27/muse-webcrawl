Gegenschein

The gegenschein appears as a faint oval glow at the antisolar point.

The gegenschein (German for "counter-glow") is a faintly bright spot in the night sky centred at the antisolar point — the point directly opposite the Sun. It is caused by backscatter of sunlight from interplanetary dust in the zodiacal cloud: dust particles near the antisolar point are in full phase, and their combined light produces a slight brightening compared with the surrounding zodiacal light.

Observation

The gegenschein appears as a dim oval patch, some 10–20 degrees across, best seen under very dark skies away from the Milky Way, on moonless nights around midnight when the antisolar point is highest. It was first described scientifically by the Danish astronomer Theodor Brorsen in 1854. Because the zodiacal light is faint, the gegenschein is one of the most difficult naked-eye astronomical phenomena to observe, and light pollution makes it invisible from most populated areas.

Explanation

Several mechanisms contribute to the brightening, including the opposition surge (shadow-hiding) familiar from the full Moon, coherent backscatter, and the geometry of sunlit dust along the Earth–Sun line. The glow confirms that interplanetary dust is distributed throughout the inner Solar System, concentrated in the ecliptic plane.
