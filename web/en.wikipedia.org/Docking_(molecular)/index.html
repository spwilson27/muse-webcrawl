Docking (molecular)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the field of molecular modeling, docking is a method which predicts the preferred orientation of one molecule to a second when a ligand and a target are bound to each other to form a stable complex. Knowledge of the preferred orientation may be used to predict the strength of association or binding affinity between two molecules using, for example, scoring functions.

Approaches

Docking approaches differ in how they treat molecular flexibility. In rigid-body docking, both ligand and target are treated as rigid. Most modern programs allow the ligand's rotatable bonds to be flexible while keeping the target largely fixed, and flexible-target (induced fit) methods model movement of selected side chains or backbone regions. Search algorithms include systematic searches, genetic algorithms, Monte Carlo methods, and incremental construction.

Scoring functions

A docking program typically generates many candidate poses and ranks them with a scoring function estimating binding affinity. Scoring functions are commonly classified as force-field-based, empirical, or knowledge-based; more recently, machine-learning-based scorers have been developed. Accurate ranking remains a central challenge because solvation, entropy, and protein motion are difficult to capture cheaply.

Applications

Docking is widely used in drug discovery, particularly in virtual screening of large compound libraries against a target protein, in lead optimization, and in predicting off-target binding relevant to side effects. Well-known software packages include AutoDock, DOCK, GOLD, and Glide.
