Deuterium

Deuterium (hydrogen-2)
Symbol: 2H or D
Protons: 1
Neutrons: 1
Natural abundance: approx. 0.0156% of hydrogen
Discovered: 1931, Harold Urey

Deuterium (symbol D or 2H) is one of two stable isotopes of hydrogen; the other is the far more common protium (1H). The nucleus of a deuterium atom, called a deuteron, contains one proton and one neutron, whereas the protium nucleus has no neutrons. Deuterium comprises about 0.0156 per cent of all hydrogen in the Earth's oceans — roughly one atom in 6,400.

Discovery

Deuterium was discovered and named by Harold Urey in 1931, an achievement for which he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1934. Its existence had been suspected from discrepancies in hydrogen's measured atomic weight, and Urey confirmed it spectroscopically after concentrating the heavy fraction from liquid hydrogen.

Properties

Because a deuterium atom is about twice as massive as protium, deuterated compounds differ measurably in reaction rates — the kinetic isotope effect exploited in chemistry and pharmacology. Deuterium oxide, D2O, is known as heavy water: it is about 10 per cent denser than ordinary water, freezes at 3.8 °C, and is used as a neutron moderator in CANDU nuclear reactors. Unlike tritium, deuterium is not radioactive.

Applications

Deuterium is a fuel for nuclear fusion: deuterium–tritium fusion powers experimental reactors and thermonuclear weapons, and deuterium–deuterium fusion is a longer-term goal. Seawater is an effectively inexhaustible source — about 33 milligrams of deuterium per litre. Deuterium is also used as a tracer in metabolic studies, in deuterated drugs designed to slow metabolism, in NMR spectroscopy solvents, and in fibre optics and lighting.
