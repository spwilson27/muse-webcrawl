Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679)

Thomas Hobbes (5 April 1588 – 4 December 1679) was an English philosopher, best known for his 1651 book Leviathan, in which he expounded an influential formulation of social contract theory. He is considered one of the founders of modern political philosophy.

Life

Hobbes was born in Westport, now part of Malmesbury in Wiltshire, England. His father, a vicar, abandoned the family after a fight outside his church. Hobbes was raised by his uncle and educated at Magdalen Hall, Oxford. He became tutor and secretary to the Cavendish family, a connection that shaped much of his life. Fearing persecution during the English Civil War, he fled to Paris in 1640, where he remained for eleven years and tutored the future King Charles II in mathematics.

Leviathan

Published in 1651, Leviathan argued that in the "state of nature" human life would be "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short". To escape this condition, individuals surrender some freedoms to an absolute sovereign in exchange for security. The work remains one of the most influential texts in political philosophy, foundational for later thinkers such as John Locke and Jean-Jacques Rousseau.

Philosophy

Hobbes was a materialist who held that everything, including thought, is matter in motion. He wrote on optics, geometry, ethics and history, and engaged in famous disputes with Bishop John Bramhall on free will and with Robert Boyle on the nature of science. His translation of Thucydides' History of the Peloponnesian War was among the earliest of his published works.

Legacy

Hobbes's political theory profoundly influenced the development of Western political thought, from liberalism to theories of sovereignty and the modern state. His works were controversial in his lifetime and were publicly burned at Oxford after his death.
