Belfort

Belfort (French pronunciation: [bɛl.fɔʁ]) is a commune in north-eastern France and the prefecture (capital) of the Territoire de Belfort department in the Bourgogne-Franche-Comté region. Situated in the Belfort Gap — a natural corridor between the Vosges and Jura mountains, known in French as the Trouée de Belfort — the city has long been a strategic fortress town. It is famous for the Lion of Belfort, a monumental sculpture by Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi commemorating the city's resistance during the Siege of Belfort in the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71), after which Belfort remained French while the rest of Alsace was annexed by Germany.

History

First mentioned in 1226, Belfort guarded the pass between Alsace and Franche-Comté. It passed from the counts of Montbéliard to Austria, and to France by the Treaty of Wesel/Westphalia settlement era, then was fortified by Vauban under Louis XIV. In the Franco-Prussian War, Colonel Denfert-Rochereau held the citadel for 103 days against Prussian siege (November 1870 – February 1871), surrendering only on government orders after the armistice. The Treaty of Frankfurt ceded Alsace-Lorraine to Germany, but Belfort and its surroundings stayed French — the origin of the Territoire de Belfort department, unique in France for its small size and history.

Economy and culture

Historically an engineering and railway town — the Alstom works in Belfort built TGV power cars and locomotives — the city now also hosts energy and digital industries. It is known for the Eurockéennes music festival, held each summer on the Malsaucy peninsula and one of France's largest rock festivals, and for the FIMU university music festival. The citadel and its museum overlook the old town of squares and colourful façades.
