Henoch–Schönlein purpura

SpecialtyRheumatology, paediatrics, nephrology
SymptomsPalpable purpura, arthritis, abdominal pain, kidney involvement
Usual onsetChildren aged 3–15 years
PrognosisUsually self-limiting; small risk of chronic kidney disease

Henoch–Schönlein purpura (HSP), now formally known as IgA vasculitis, is a disease of the skin, joints, intestines, and kidneys. It is the most common form of vasculitis in children. It is a small-vessel vasculitis in which complexes of immunoglobulin A (IgA) and complement component 3 (C3) are deposited on arterioles, capillaries, and venules, often following an upper respiratory tract infection. It is named after the German physicians Johann Lukas Schönlein and his student Eduard Heinrich Henoch.

Signs and symptoms

The classic tetrad consists of:

  • Palpable purpura — a non-blanching, raised rash, typically over the buttocks and legs, present in virtually all cases and required for diagnosis.
  • Arthritis or arthralgia — transient joint pain and swelling, especially of the knees and ankles.
  • Abdominal pain — which may be complicated by gastrointestinal bleeding, intussusception, or bowel ischaemia.
  • Renal involvement — haematuria and proteinuria, occurring in a substantial minority of patients and determining the long-term prognosis.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, based on the characteristic rash plus at least one other feature. Platelet counts are normal (distinguishing HSP from thrombocytopenic purpuras). Skin or kidney biopsy shows leukocytoclastic vasculitis with predominant IgA deposition. The 1990 American College of Rheumatology criteria and later EULAR/PRINTO/PRES criteria formalise the classification.

Treatment and prognosis

Most cases resolve spontaneously within a few weeks with supportive care, rest, and analgesia. Corticosteroids can relieve severe abdominal and joint symptoms and are used for significant renal disease, sometimes with immunosuppressants. Most children make a full recovery, though a small percentage develop chronic kidney disease; long-term follow-up of urine and blood pressure is recommended, particularly for those with nephritis.
