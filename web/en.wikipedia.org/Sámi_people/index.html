Sámi people

Sámi
Total population: estimated 80,000–100,000
Regions: Norway, Sweden, Finland, Russia (Kola Peninsula)
Languages: Sámi languages, Norwegian, Swedish, Finnish, Russian
Traditional livelihood: reindeer herding, fishing, hunting

The Sámi people are an Indigenous Finno-Ugric people inhabiting Sápmi, which today encompasses large northern parts of Norway, Sweden and Finland, and the Kola Peninsula in Russia. The Sámi are the only Indigenous people recognised and protected under international conventions through the parliaments of Norway, Sweden and Finland.

Traditionally, the Sámi have pursued a variety of livelihoods, including coastal fishing, fur trapping and sheep herding, but are best known for semi-nomadic reindeer herding, in which a minority of Sámi are still engaged full-time. Their languages belong to the Uralic family and include Northern Sámi, the most widely spoken.

Culture

Sámi culture is expressed in the colourful traditional clothing called gákti, in handicrafts (duodji), and in the joik, a form of song with a deeply personal, evocative character. The Sámi flag, adopted in 1986, bears a circle representing the sun and moon on fields of red, green, yellow and blue.

History

From the eighteenth and especially the nineteenth century, the Nordic states and Russia pursued policies of assimilation — "Norwegianisation" and its counterparts — that suppressed Sámi languages and culture. Since the late twentieth century there has been a strong cultural and political revival, with elected Sámi parliaments established in Norway (1989), Finland and Sweden (1993).
