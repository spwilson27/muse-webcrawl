Race and genetics


Placeholder image: Race and genetics

Researchers have investigated the relationship between race and genetics since the sequencing of the human genome. The scientific consensus is that human genetic variation is continuous and mostly clinal, and that conventional racial categories do not correspond to discrete genetic divisions.

Genetic variation

Studies since Richard Lewontin's 1972 analysis have consistently found that most genetic variation (roughly 85–95%) occurs within populations, with a small fraction between continental groups. Allele frequencies nevertheless vary geographically, and statistical clustering can recover broad continental ancestry, which is used in ancestry inference.

Medicine and ancestry

Some disease risks and drug responses correlate with ancestry — for example sickle-cell trait with African and Mediterranean ancestry, or Tay–Sachs disease with Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry. Researchers debate whether self-identified race is a useful proxy in medicine or whether direct genetic ancestry measures are preferable. Mainstream scientific bodies reject the idea that genetics supports a biological hierarchy of races.
