Misch metal

Misch metal (from German: Mischmetall, "mixed metal") is an alloy of rare-earth elements. It typically consists of about 55 per cent cerium, 25 per cent lanthanum, 15–18 per cent neodymium and smaller amounts of other rare-earth metals; the most common commercial form also contains around 5 per cent iron. It is used mainly as the sparking material in lighter flints and in making ferrocerium.

Properties and production

Misch metal is a soft, silvery, brittle alloy. It is pyrophoric: scraped or struck, it produces hot sparks, which is why it is alloyed with iron and magnesium to form ferrocerium, the "flint" of cigarette lighters and gas strikers. It is produced by electrolysis of the fused mixed rare-earth chlorides obtained from ores such as monazite, without the expense of separating the individual lanthanides — historically the metal was in effect a by-product of extracting thorium for gas mantles.

Uses

Besides lighter flints, misch metal is added to steel to improve its mechanical properties and workability, to vacuum tubes as a getter to absorb residual gases, and as the starting material from which individual rare-earth metals are separated for magnets, phosphors and other applications.

History

The alloy was developed by the Austrian chemist Carl Auer von Welsbach, who found that the mixed rare-earth residues from his incandescent gas-mantle business, combined with iron, gave an effective sparking metal, which he patented in 1903.
