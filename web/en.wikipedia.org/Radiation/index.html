Radiation

Ionizing radiation types and their penetrating power: alpha (stopped by paper), beta (by aluminium), gamma (attenuated by lead).

In physics, radiation is the emission or transmission of energy in the form of waves or particles through space or through a material medium. This includes electromagnetic radiation (radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays), particle radiation (alpha, beta and neutron radiation), acoustic radiation (ultrasound, sound and seismic waves), and gravitational radiation.

Ionizing and non-ionizing radiation

Radiation is usually classified as ionizing or non-ionizing depending on the energy of the radiated particles. Ionizing radiation carries enough energy (typically more than about 10 eV per particle) to ionize atoms and break chemical bonds, and includes alpha and beta particles, neutrons, gamma rays and X-rays. It can damage living tissue and DNA, and its effects on health are the subject of radiation protection; it is also deliberately used in medicine (radiotherapy, diagnostic imaging) and industry. Non-ionizing radiation — most of the electromagnetic spectrum below ultraviolet, including visible light and radio waves — lacks this energy, though intense exposures can still cause heating or burns.

Sources

Natural sources include cosmic rays, terrestrial radioactivity in rocks and soil, and radon gas; average natural background exposure is about 2.4 mSv per year worldwide. Artificial sources include nuclear power, medical procedures and consumer products. The spreading of energy away from a source gives rise to the inverse-square law for intensity in free space.
