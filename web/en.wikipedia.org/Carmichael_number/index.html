In number theory, a Carmichael number is a composite number n that satisfies the modular arithmetic congruence relation bn−1 ≡ 1 (mod n) for all integers b coprime to n. They are named after the American mathematician Robert Carmichael, who studied them in the early 20th century. The smallest Carmichael number is 561 = 3 × 11 × 17.

Significance

By Fermat's little theorem, every prime number satisfies this congruence. Carmichael numbers are therefore "Fermat pseudoprimes" in the strongest possible sense: they fool the Fermat primality test for every base, even though they are composite. This matters in practice, because probabilistic primality tests used in cryptography must be designed to detect them; the Miller–Rabin test avoids the problem because Carmichael numbers are not strong pseudoprimes to all bases.

Korselt's criterion

Korselt's criterion (1899) characterises Carmichael numbers: a composite n is a Carmichael number if and only if n is square-free and, for every prime divisor p of n, p − 1 divides n − 1. It follows that every Carmichael number is odd, is a product of at least three distinct primes, and has all prime factors congruent in a constrained way. In 1994 Alford, Granville, and Pomerance proved that there are infinitely many Carmichael numbers, resolving a long-standing question.
