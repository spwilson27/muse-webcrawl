Destroyer

In naval terminology, a destroyer is a fast, manoeuvrable, long-endurance warship intended to escort larger vessels in a fleet, convoy or carrier battle group and defend them against a wide range of general threats. The name derives from the original "torpedo boat destroyer" of the 1890s, built to counter the new threat of fast torpedo boats. Modern destroyers are among the most powerful surface combatants, equipped with guided missiles, guns, torpedoes and helicopters for anti-air, anti-surface and anti-submarine warfare.

Origins

The self-propelled torpedo created the torpedo boat, and by the 1880s every major navy feared swarms of them. In 1893 the Royal Navy launched HMS Havock and HMS Hornet, the first torpedo boat destroyers: larger, faster, ocean-going vessels armed with quick-firing guns and torpedoes of their own. Other navies followed, and by the First World War destroyers screened the battle fleet, laid smoke, scouted and launched mass torpedo attacks.

World Wars

Between the wars destroyers grew into general-purpose fleet escorts. In the Second World War they were the workhorses of the Atlantic convoys, hunting U-boats with sonar and depth charges, screening carrier task forces in the Pacific against aircraft, kamikazes and submarines, and performing shore bombardment, radar picket and rescue duties. Destroyers suffered some of the heaviest losses of any ship type.

Modern destroyers

The guided-missile era transformed the type. Ships such as the American Arleigh Burke class — built around the Aegis combat system and vertical launching cells — defend fleets against aircraft and ballistic missiles, strike land targets with cruise missiles, and hunt submarines. Large modern destroyers such as China's Type 055 and the U.S. Zumwalt class rival Second World War cruisers in size, blurring the distinction between destroyer and cruiser. Navies operating destroyers today include those of the United States, China, Japan, the United Kingdom (Type 45), France, India, Russia and South Korea.
