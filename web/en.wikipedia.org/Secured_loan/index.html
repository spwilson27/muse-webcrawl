Secured loan

A secured loan is a loan in which the borrower pledges an asset as collateral for the loan. If the borrower defaults, the lender may seize and sell the collateral to recover the amount owed. Because the lender's risk is reduced, secured loans generally carry lower interest rates, larger principal amounts and longer repayment terms than unsecured loans.

Common types

The most familiar secured loan is the mortgage, in which real property secures the debt and the lender can foreclose on default. Other common forms include auto loans secured by the vehicle purchased, home equity loans and lines of credit, pawnbroker loans secured by deposited goods, securities-based lending secured by investment portfolios, and business loans secured by inventory, receivables or equipment.

Legal mechanics

The borrower's grant of a security interest gives the lender a lien on the collateral. In the United States, Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code governs security interests in personal property, including how interests are "perfected" — typically by filing a financing statement — so that they bind third parties. In real property, the instrument may be a mortgage or a deed of trust, and enforcement proceeds through foreclosure, judicial or non-judicial depending on the jurisdiction. Where the collateral sells for less than the debt, the borrower may remain liable for a deficiency; where it sells for more, the surplus is generally returned.

Secured versus unsecured credit

Unsecured loans, such as most credit cards and personal loans, rest only on the borrower's promise to pay and are priced accordingly, with higher rates and lower limits. Secured loans shift risk to the borrower's asset: the borrower gains cheaper credit but risks losing the pledged property. In bankruptcy, secured creditors are generally paid from their collateral ahead of unsecured creditors, reflecting the priority of their lien.
