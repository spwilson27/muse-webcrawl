The Satsuma Rebellion (1877), also known as the Seinan War, was a revolt of disaffected samurai against the new imperial government of Meiji Japan. It was the last and most serious of a series of armed uprisings against the reforms that abolished the samurai class's privileges.

Background

The Meiji Restoration of 1868 had been driven in large part by samurai from Satsuma Domain, including Saigō Takamori. But reforms — the abolition of domains, a conscript army, and the 1876 ban on wearing swords — alienated many samurai. Saigō withdrew to Kagoshima, where his private schools became a focus of discontent.

Course of the war

Fighting began in February 1877 when Saigō's forces marched north and laid siege to Kumamoto Castle, which held out for nearly two months. The rebels were progressively driven south by the larger and better-supplied Imperial Army, whose conscript troops proved they could defeat samurai in battle. The war ended at the Battle of Shiroyama on 24 September 1877, where Saigō died — according to legend by ritual suicide performed with a follower's assistance, an image later romanticised in popular culture.

Aftermath

The rebellion's defeat confirmed the end of the samurai as a military class and validated conscription. Saigō was posthumously pardoned in 1889 and is still revered in Japan as a symbol of loyalty and tradition.
