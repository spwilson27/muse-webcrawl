Long-arm jurisdiction

Long-arm statutes extend a court's reach to non-resident defendants

Long-arm jurisdiction is the ability of a court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a non-resident defendant on the basis of the defendant's activities within the jurisdiction, as authorised by a long-arm statute. Such statutes are a feature of United States law, where state courts historically could not reach defendants who were neither present nor domiciled in the state; the growth of interstate commerce in the 20th century prompted states to enact statutes allowing jurisdiction over outsiders who, for example, transact business, commit a tort, or own property within the state.

Constitutional limits

Long-arm statutes are constrained by the Due Process Clauses of the U.S. Constitution. In International Shoe Co. v. Washington (1945), the Supreme Court held that a defendant must have "minimum contacts" with the forum such that maintaining the suit does not offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." Later decisions refined the doctrine:

  • Hanson v. Denckla (1958) — the defendant must purposefully avail itself of the forum.
  • World-Wide Volkswagen v. Woodson (1980) — foreseeability alone is insufficient.
  • Burger King v. Rudzewicz (1985) — burden on the defendant and other fairness factors.
  • J. McIntyre Machinery v. Nicastro (2011) and Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court (2021) — on jurisdiction based on goods sold in a state.

Specific and general jurisdiction

Jurisdiction under a long-arm statute is typically specific: the claim must arise out of or relate to the defendant's contacts with the forum. Some statutes, such as New York's CPLR 302 and the Illinois long-arm statute, extend to the limits of due process; others enumerate narrower categories of conduct. General (all-purpose) jurisdiction over corporations requires affiliations so continuous and systematic as to render the defendant "at home," per Daimler AG v. Bauman (2014).
