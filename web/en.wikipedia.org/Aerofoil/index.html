Aerofoil

Cross-section of a typical aerofoil showing the chord line, camber and thickness.

An aerofoil (British English) or airfoil (American English) is the cross-sectional shape of an object whose motion through a gas is capable of generating significant lift, such as the wing of an aircraft, the blade of a propeller, rotor or turbine, or a sail. The shape is designed so that air flowing over the upper surface moves faster and at lower pressure than air below, producing an upward force.

Terminology

The leading edge is the frontmost point of the aerofoil and the trailing edge the rearmost. The straight line joining them is the chord line, and its length is the chord. The camber line runs midway between the upper and lower surfaces; its curvature describes the camber of the section. Thickness is usually expressed as a percentage of chord; most aerofoils are between 6 and 18 percent thick.

Lift generation

Lift arises from the pressure difference between the surfaces combined with the downward deflection of the airflow (downwash). Explanations invoking equal transit time on the two surfaces are incorrect; the accepted account involves circulation and the Kutta condition, which fixes the flow to leave the sharp trailing edge smoothly. The lift coefficient increases roughly linearly with angle of attack until the stall, where flow separation causes a rapid loss of lift.

Families of sections

Systematic families of aerofoils were developed in the twentieth century, notably the NACA four-digit and five-digit series and the later NACA 6-series laminar-flow sections, each defined by mathematical formulas. Modern sections are often designed computationally for specific aircraft, gliders, wind turbines or propellers, balancing lift, drag, pitching moment and stall behaviour. Supercritical aerofoils, with flattened upper surfaces, delay shock formation on transonic airliner wings and reduce drag at cruise.
