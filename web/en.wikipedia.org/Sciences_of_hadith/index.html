Sciences of hadith

The sciences of hadith (Arabic: علم الحديث, ʿilm al-ḥadīth) are a set of disciplines in Islamic scholarship used to evaluate and authenticate hadith — the reports of the sayings, actions, and tacit approvals of the Prophet Muhammad. Because hadith form a principal source of Islamic law and guidance second only to the Quran, Muslim scholars developed elaborate methods for assessing the reliability of transmitters and the soundness of transmission chains.

Components

The sciences of hadith include the study of isnad (the chain of narrators), the biographical evaluation of transmitters (ʿilm al-rijāl, including criticism known as al-jarḥ wa-l-taʿdīl, "wounding and accrediting"), analysis of the text (matn), and the terminology of classification (muṣṭalaḥ al-ḥadīth). A related discipline studies the differing and reconciling of apparently contradictory reports.

Classification of hadith

Classical scholars graded individual hadith as ṣaḥīḥ (sound), ḥasan (good), or ḍaʿīf (weak), depending on the continuity of the chain, the uprightness and precision of its narrators, and the absence of anomalies or hidden defects. Reports transmitted by so many independent chains that forgery was considered impossible were termed mutawātir. Famous compendia of the terminology include Ibn al-Salah's Introduction (Muqaddimah) and Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani's Nukhbat al-Fikar.

History

Oral and written scrutiny of transmission began in the first Islamic century, in part to combat fabrication of reports for political and theological ends. The great canonical collections — such as Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim in the 9th century CE — were compiled by applying these criteria. Modern academic scholars have debated the extent to which the classical methods can establish the historical authenticity of hadith.
