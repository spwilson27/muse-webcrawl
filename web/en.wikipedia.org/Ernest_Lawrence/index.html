Ernest Orlando Lawrence (8 August 1901 – 27 August 1958) was an American nuclear physicist who invented the cyclotron, the first practical circular particle accelerator. He won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1939 for this invention and founded the Radiation Laboratory at the University of California, Berkeley — the prototype of the large national laboratories and of "big science".

The cyclotron

In 1929, inspired by a diagram in a paper by Rolf Widerøe, Lawrence conceived of accelerating charged particles in a spiral path between two hollow D-shaped electrodes inside a magnetic field, so that a modest alternating voltage applied many times could bring particles to high energies. With his student M. Stanley Livingston he built the first working cyclotron in 1930–31; it was only inches across and made of brass and sealing wax, yet it demonstrated the principle. Successively larger cyclotrons followed, culminating in the 184-inch machine, used to produce mesons and new isotopes.

War work and the national laboratories

During the Second World War, Lawrence adapted the cyclotron principle into the calutron, an electromagnetic separator used at Oak Ridge to enrich uranium for the Manhattan Project. After the war he championed the expansion of the Berkeley laboratory (later Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory) and helped establish the laboratory at Livermore (Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory). He also supported the development of the hydrogen bomb and served as a scientific adviser to the US government.

Legacy

The chemical element lawrencium (Lr, atomic number 103) is named in his honour, as are the two national laboratories he founded. Lawrence's organizational model — large teams, big machines, and government funding — permanently changed the practice of physics.
