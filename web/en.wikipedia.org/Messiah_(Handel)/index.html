Messiah (Handel)

Messiah, HWV 56
Composer: George Frideric Handel
Libretto: Charles Jennens (from the King James Bible and Prayer Book Psalms)
Composed: 1741
Premiere: 13 April 1742, Dublin

Messiah (HWV 56) is an English-language oratorio composed in 1741 by George Frideric Handel. With a scriptural text compiled by Charles Jennens from the King James Bible and the Coverdale Psalter, it has become one of the best-known and most frequently performed choral works in Western music.

Structure

Unlike Handel's other oratorios, Messiah is a meditation rather than a drama: no named characters speak, and the story is told through commentary. Its three parts trace prophecy and the Nativity (Part I), Christ's Passion, Resurrection and Ascension (Part II), and thanksgiving for the final victory over death (Part III). Part II closes with the famous "Hallelujah" chorus; other celebrated numbers include "Ev'ry valley shall be exalted", "For unto us a child is born", "I know that my Redeemer liveth" and the concluding "Worthy is the Lamb" with its great "Amen".

Composition and performance history

Handel composed the score in about 24 days in the summer of 1741. The premiere in Dublin in April 1742 was a charity triumph; the London premiere the following year was cooler, but performances for the Foundling Hospital from 1750 established it in Britain. Handel repeatedly revised the work for particular singers. After his death it was performed in ever-larger forces — the 1784 Westminster Abbey commemoration used hundreds of performers — and Mozart re-orchestrated it in 1789. The tradition of standing for the "Hallelujah" chorus is legendarily, though unreliably, traced to King George II.

Legacy

Annual Christmas (and Easter) performances by professional and amateur choirs worldwide, sing-along Messiahs, and countless recordings keep the work central to concert life; Handel's autograph score is held by the British Library.
