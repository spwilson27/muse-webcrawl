Alfred Marshall (26 July 1842 – 13 July 1924) was an English economist and one of the most influential thinkers of his time. His book Principles of Economics (1890) dominated economics teaching for decades and established the neoclassical synthesis of classical cost-of-production theory with the marginal utility analysis of the 1870s.

Life

Marshall was born in Bermondsey, London, and studied mathematics at St John's College, Cambridge, taking second place in the Mathematical Tripos. He turned to economics, taught at Cambridge and Bristol, and married his former student Mary Paley, with whom he wrote The Economics of Industry (1879). From 1885 to 1908 he was Professor of Political Economy at Cambridge, where he founded the Economics Tripos and taught a generation that included John Maynard Keynes and Arthur Pigou.

Contributions

Marshall systematised the analysis of supply and demand — famously comparing their role in determining price to the two blades of a pair of scissors — and introduced the concepts of price elasticity of demand, consumer surplus, the representative firm, external economies, and the distinction between the short and long run. His partial-equilibrium method and the maxim natura non facit saltum ("nature does not make leaps") expressed his view of economics as an evolutionary, gradual science. Later works include Industry and Trade (1919) and Money, Credit and Commerce (1923).
