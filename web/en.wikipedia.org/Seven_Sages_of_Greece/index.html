Seven Sages of Greece

The Seven Sages were famed for practical wisdom expressed in short maxims.

The Seven Sages of Greece (also called the Seven Wise Men) were a canonical group of early Greek thinkers, statesmen and lawgivers of the seventh and sixth centuries BC, famed for their practical wisdom. Ancient lists vary, but the most common roster, given by Plato in the Protagoras, comprises Thales of Miletus, Pittacus of Mytilene, Bias of Priene, Solon of Athens, Cleobulus of Lindos, Myson of Chenae and Chilon of Sparta.

Maxims

Each sage was associated with a concise aphorism, many of which were inscribed at the temple of Apollo at Delphi. "Know thyself" was attributed variously to Thales, Chilon or Solon; "Nothing in excess" to Cleobulus or Chilon; Solon was remembered for "Keep an eye on the end of everything" and for observing that no man can be counted happy until he is dead; Bias for "Most men are bad"; Pittacus for "Know your opportunity"; and Thales for "Suretyship brings ruin".

Traditions

Stories about the sages include the tale of the tripod, a prize for wisdom passed from sage to sage until it was dedicated to Apollo, and the fictional account of their banquet in Plutarch's Banquet of the Seven Sages. Diogenes Laërtius devoted the first book of his Lives of the Eminent Philosophers to them. The sages represent the transition in Greek thought from mythic to practical and political wisdom, preceding the systematic philosophy of the Presocratics — a succession complicated by Thales appearing in both roles.
