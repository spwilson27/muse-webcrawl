Optic ataxia

Optic ataxia is a neurological condition characterized by an inability to accurately reach for and grasp objects under visual guidance, despite normal limb strength, sensation, and primary visual perception. Patients can typically reach accurately to parts of their own body and perform movements guided by touch or proprioception, but misreach toward objects seen in the peripheral visual field.


Misreaching in optic ataxia

Causes

Optic ataxia results from damage to the posterior parietal cortex, particularly the superior parietal lobule and the intraparietal sulcus — regions of the dorsal visual stream involved in transforming visual information into motor coordinates. It was first described in detail by the Austrian neurologist Rudolph Bálint in 1909 and forms one of the three components of Bálint's syndrome, alongside simultanagnosia and ocular apraxia, although it also occurs in isolation after unilateral lesions.

Features

The misreaching is often worse for targets in the visual field opposite the lesion and when using the contralateral hand, a pattern known as field and hand effects. Grasp formation may also be impaired: patients fail to scale their grip aperture appropriately to object size during a reach, though they can describe the object's size verbally. Unlike cerebellar ataxia, movements guided without vision are relatively preserved.

Significance

Optic ataxia has been influential in the two-visual-systems hypothesis of perception and action: its dissociation from visual agnosia suggests that the dorsal "vision-for-action" stream can be damaged independently of the ventral "vision-for-perception" stream. Patients often improve by moving their hand slowly or by looking directly at the target, strategies exploited in rehabilitation.
