Subject–verb–object word order

In linguistic typology, subject–verb–object (SVO) is a sentence structure where the subject comes first, the verb second, and the object third. Languages may be classified according to the dominant sequence of these constituents in unmarked sentences; the typology was pioneered by Joseph Greenberg in the 1960s.

Distribution

SVO is one of the two most common word orders among the world's languages, alongside subject–object–verb (SOV); together they account for the large majority of languages. Roughly 42% of languages are predominantly SVO. Examples include English, French, Italian, Spanish, Mandarin Chinese, Malay/Indonesian, Swahili, Yoruba, Thai, Vietnamese, and Bulgarian.

An English example: "Sam ate oranges" — Sam is the subject, ate the verb, and oranges the object. By contrast, Japanese and Hindi follow SOV order ("Sam oranges ate"), while Welsh and Classical Arabic are typically verb–subject–object (VSO).

Properties

SVO order correlates statistically with other typological features: SVO languages tend to use prepositions rather than postpositions, place genitives after nouns (though many European SVO languages vary), and put relative clauses after the nouns they modify. In many SVO languages, questions are formed by partial inversion of subject and auxiliary verb or by question words moving to the front of the clause.
