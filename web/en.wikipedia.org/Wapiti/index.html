Wapiti

The wapiti, also known as the elk (Cervus canadensis), is one of the largest species within the deer family, Cervidae, and one of the largest terrestrial mammals in its native range of North America and eastern Asia. The word “wapiti” derives from the Shawnee and Cree word waapiti, meaning “white rump”.

Taxonomy and naming

The wapiti was long considered a subspecies of the European red deer, but genetic studies since the early 2000s have established it as a distinct species. In North America the animal is called “elk”, a name that in British English refers to the moose (Alces alces), which North Americans call by the Algonquian name. Several subspecies are recognised, including the Rocky Mountain elk, Roosevelt elk, tule elk and the extinct eastern elk and Merriam’s elk.

Description

Bulls stand about 1.5 metres at the shoulder and weigh 320 kilograms or more; mature bulls carry large branching antlers that are shed each winter and regrown in spring. During the autumn rut, bulls produce a loud, high-pitched vocalisation known as bugling, used to attract cows and challenge rivals.

Distribution and habitat

The species ranged across much of North America and central and eastern Asia. Overhunting and habitat loss eliminated it from most of its eastern North American range by the late 19th century; reintroductions have since restored herds in states such as Kentucky, Tennessee and North Carolina. In Asia it survives in parts of Mongolia, China, Siberia and Central Asia.

Behaviour and ecology

Wapiti are social animals that live in herds, migrating seasonally between summer alpine pastures and lower winter ranges. They graze on grasses, sedges and forbs and browse shrubs and bark. Predators include wolves, bears, cougars and coyotes.

Relationship with humans

The wapiti is a major game animal, valued for its meat and antlers; antler velvet is harvested in North America and Asia for traditional medicine. The species is classified as Least Concern by the IUCN, though some Asian populations are declining.
