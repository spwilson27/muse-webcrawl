Panel data

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Panel data, also known as longitudinal data, are multi-dimensional data involving measurements over time. A panel data set contains observations of multiple individuals (such as people, firms, countries or regions), each observed at two or more time periods. This contrasts with cross-sectional data (many individuals at one point in time) and time series data (one individual at many points in time). A panel is called balanced if every individual is observed in every period, and unbalanced otherwise.

Analysis

Panel data are widely used in econometrics and the social sciences because observing the same units over time allows researchers to control for unobserved, time-invariant characteristics of individuals that may bias estimates. Common methods include:

  • Pooled OLS – combining all observations without accounting for the panel structure.
  • Fixed effects models – allowing each individual its own intercept, thereby removing all time-invariant differences between individuals.
  • Random effects models – treating individual effects as random draws, which is more efficient if the effects are uncorrelated with the explanatory variables; the Hausman test is commonly used to choose between fixed and random effects.
  • Dynamic panel models – including lagged values of the dependent variable, for which estimators such as Arellano–Bond were developed.

Examples

Well-known panel data sets include the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID) and the National Longitudinal Surveys in the United States, the German Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP), the British Household Panel Survey and its successor Understanding Society, and cross-country data sets assembled by the World Bank and the Penn World Table.
