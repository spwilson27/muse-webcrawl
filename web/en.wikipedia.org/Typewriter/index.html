A typewriter is a mechanical or electromechanical machine for typing characters. Typically, a typewriter has an array of keys, and each one causes a different single character to be produced on paper by striking an inked ribbon selectively against the paper with a type element. At the end of the nineteenth century, the term typewriter was also applied to a person who used such a device.

History

The first typewriter to be commercially successful was invented in 1868 by Americans Christopher Latham Sholes, Frank Haven Hall, Carlos Glidden and Samuel W. Soulé. Sholes filed a patent application on 23 June 1868, and the machine entered production in 1874 as the Sholes and Glidden typewriter, manufactured by E. Remington and Sons. The QWERTY keyboard layout, designed to prevent the jamming of neighboring typebars, became standard.

How it works

In a manual typewriter, pressing a key lifts a typebar, which strikes an inked ribbon against paper wrapped around a cylindrical platen. A spring advances the carriage after each character; at the end of a line the typist returns the carriage, which also advances the paper to the next line. Electric typewriters replace finger power with an electric motor (IBM's Selectric, introduced 1961, used a rotating "golf ball" type element).

Cultural role and decline

Typewriters enabled modern office work, created the profession of the typist (and with it, large-scale female employment in offices), and shaped keyboard layouts, fonts, and document conventions still used in computing. Personal computers and word processors replaced typewriters in most offices in the 1980s and 1990s, though typewriters remain in use where mechanical reliability and carbon copies are valued, and among enthusiasts.
