Goodnight–Loving Trail

Established1866
FoundersCharles Goodnight and Oliver Loving
RouteYoung County, Texas, via the Pecos River to Fort Sumner, New Mexico, and on to Colorado

The Goodnight–Loving Trail was a cattle trail in the southwestern United States used in the late 19th century to drive herds of Texas longhorn cattle north to markets, army posts, and reservations in New Mexico and Colorado. It was blazed in 1866 by cattlemen Charles Goodnight and Oliver Loving.

Route

The trail began in Young County, Texas, ran southwest to the Pecos River — avoiding the dangerous Llano Estacado and Comanche territory — then followed the Pecos north to Fort Sumner, New Mexico, where the partners sold beef to the army and to the interned Navajo and Apache at Bosque Redondo. In 1868 the trail was extended north through New Mexico to Colorado, reaching markets at Denver and the railheads of Wyoming, eventually connecting with routes to Cheyenne.

History

On a later drive in 1867, Oliver Loving was wounded by Comanches in an ambush and died of gangrene in Fort Sumner; Goodnight carried out his partner's wish to be buried in Texas. The partnership's success made Goodnight one of the legendary figures of the cattle frontier, and the trail story partly inspired Larry McMurtry's novel Lonesome Dove. Heavy traffic on the trail lasted into the 1880s, until railways and fenced ranching transformed the cattle industry.
