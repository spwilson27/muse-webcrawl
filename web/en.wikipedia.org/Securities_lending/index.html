Securities lending

Securities lending describes the practice of loaning shares of stock, bonds, or other securities, by their owner to a borrower, usually so that the borrower can settle a short sale or cover a failed delivery. In exchange for the loan, the borrower provides collateral — typically cash or other securities — and pays the lender a fee.

Mechanics

Large institutional investors such as pension funds, mutual funds, and insurance companies are the main lenders. Loans are commonly arranged by agent lenders such as custodian banks. Where cash collateral is taken, the lender typically reinvests it and shares the earnings with the borrower in the form of a rebate rate; the difference between the reinvestment return and the rebate is the lender's revenue.

Risks

The principal risks include borrower default, collateral reinvestment losses (which became prominent during the 2008 financial crisis when cash collateral pools invested in illiquid assets), and operational risk. Because legal title passes to the borrower, the lender temporarily loses voting rights in loaned shares.

Market role

Securities lending supports market liquidity, price discovery, and the settlement of short sales, and generates incremental income for long-term investors.
