Harvey Cushing

Harvey Williams Cushing (April 8, 1869 – October 7, 1939) was an American neurosurgeon, pathologist, writer, and medical illustrator. A pioneer of brain surgery, he is often called the "father of modern neurosurgery". His name is associated with Cushing's syndrome and Cushing's disease, disorders of excess cortisol that he described.

Career

Cushing was born in Cleveland, Ohio, graduated from Yale University in 1891 and Harvard Medical School in 1895, and trained under William Stewart Halsted at Johns Hopkins Hospital, where he became associate professor of surgery. There he introduced routine measurement of blood pressure in the operating room (adopting the Riva-Rocci cuff in the United States), the anaesthesia record, and the use of local anaesthesia techniques. In 1912 he became surgeon-in-chief of the Peter Bent Brigham Hospital and professor of surgery at Harvard Medical School, where he built a world-leading neurosurgical service. During the First World War he served with the American Expeditionary Forces in France, treating head wounds. In 1933 he moved to Yale as Sterling Professor of Neurology.

Contributions

Through meticulous operative technique, use of electrocautery (developed with physicist William T. Bovie), silver clips for vessels, and careful tumour classification with Percival Bailey, Cushing reduced operative mortality for intracranial tumours from over 50% to about 10%. In 1912 he described the pituitary disorder that became known as Cushing's syndrome; his 1932 paper on pituitary basophilism defined Cushing's disease. He trained a generation of neurosurgeons and founded the discipline as a distinct specialty.

Writing and legacy

Cushing was also a celebrated medical biographer: his two-volume Life of Sir William Osler (1925) won the Pulitzer Prize for Biography. He amassed a great medical library and brain-tumour specimen collection, now held at Yale's Harvey Cushing/John Hay Whitney Medical Library. He died in New Haven, Connecticut, in 1939.
