Space opera


A pulp-era space opera scene

Space opera is a subgenre of science fiction that emphasizes space warfare, melodramatic adventure, interplanetary battles, chivalric romance, and risk-taking. Set mainly or entirely in outer space, it usually involves conflict between opponents possessing advanced abilities, futuristic weapons, and other sophisticated technology. The term has no relation to music, as in a traditional opera, but is instead a play on the terms "soap opera", "horse opera", and "blood-and-thunder opera", coined in 1941 by fan writer Wilson Tucker as a pejorative for hackneyed space adventure fiction.

History

Precursors include the interplanetary romances of Edgar Rice Burroughs (the Barsoom series, from 1912) and the spectacular adventures of E. E. "Doc" Smith, whose Skylark and Lensman series set the pattern for the genre in the pulp magazines of the 1920s–40s. Edmond Hamilton and Jack Williamson were other prominent early practitioners. Works of the era typically featured vast space fleets, ray guns, and galactic empires modelled on the Roman Empire.

Later developments

From the 1960s the "new space opera" of writers such as Samuel R. Delany, and later Iain M. Banks (the Culture series), Alastair Reynolds, Peter F. Hamilton, and Ann Leckie, combined the scale of classic space opera with harder scientific rigour, literary ambition, and more complex characterization. In film and television, the genre is exemplified by the Star Wars and Star Trek franchises, Battlestar Galactica, and The Expanse.

Characteristics

Common elements include faster-than-light travel, galactic politics, ancient alien artefacts, space battles on a grand scale, and clearly defined heroes and villains. Critics have long debated the dividing line between space opera and military science fiction or planetary romance.
