Gregg Wesley Toland, A.S.C. (May 29, 1904 – September 28, 1948) was an American cinematographer known for his innovative use of techniques such as deep focus, examples of which can be found in his work on Orson Welles's Citizen Kane (1941), William Wyler's The Best Years of Our Lives (1946), and John Ford's The Long Voyage Home (1940) and The Grapes of Wrath (1940).

Career

Toland began working in the film industry as a teenager, as an office boy and assistant cameraman, and became a director of photography while still in his twenties at the Samuel Goldwyn studio. During the 1930s he was among the most sought-after cameramen in Hollywood, earning a reputation for experimentation with lighting, lenses and composition. He won the Academy Award for Best Cinematography (black-and-white) for Wyler's Wuthering Heights (1939).

Deep focus and Citizen Kane

Toland actively sought the assignment on Citizen Kane, saying he wanted to work with the inexperienced Welles because he expected to be able to experiment. Using fast film stocks, coated lenses, powerful arc lights and small apertures, he achieved compositions in which foreground, middle ground and background remained simultaneously sharp — the celebrated "deep focus" style. Combined with low-angle shots revealing ceilings, long takes and elaborate staging in depth, the work profoundly influenced subsequent cinematography.

Later work and death

During the Second World War Toland served in the Navy camera department and co-directed, with John Ford, the documentary December 7th (1943). His later credits included The Best Years of Our Lives and The Bishop's Wife (1947). He died in his sleep in 1948, aged 44, of coronary thrombosis. In polls of cinematographers he is regularly ranked among the most influential in film history.
