Casimir effect

Predicted byHendrik Casimir (1948)
FieldQuantum field theory
CauseChanges in vacuum fluctuation energy from boundary conditions

In quantum field theory, the Casimir effect is a physical force acting between close uncharged conducting plates, arising from the quantization of the electromagnetic field. It is a manifestation of the zero-point energy of the vacuum: the plates restrict the wavelengths of virtual photons that can exist between them, so the radiation pressure outside exceeds that inside, pushing the plates together.

Parallel conducting plates attract because fewer vacuum modes fit between them.

Theory

Hendrik Casimir predicted the effect in 1948 while working at Philips Research Laboratories, following discussions with Niels Bohr. For two perfectly conducting parallel plates of area A separated by distance d, the attractive force is F = −(π2ħc/240) · A/d4. At a separation of 10 nm, the pressure is roughly one atmosphere; the effect is negligible at macroscopic distances but crucial in micro- and nanoelectromechanical systems, where it can cause moving parts to stick ("stiction").

Measurement

Marcus Sparnaay attempted the first measurement in 1958 with inconclusive precision. Accurate confirmations came in the 1990s, notably by Steve Lamoreaux (1997) using a torsion pendulum, and by Umar Mohideen's group using an atomic force microscope, agreeing with theory to within a few percent after corrections for finite conductivity and surface roughness.

Related phenomena

The effect can be repulsive for certain materials and geometries. The dynamical Casimir effect — production of real photons from a rapidly moving mirror or modulated cavity — was observed in 2011. The Casimir effect is frequently cited in discussions of vacuum energy, though its derivation does not require zero-point energy as an input and can be framed in terms of van der Waals forces between the plates' constituents.
