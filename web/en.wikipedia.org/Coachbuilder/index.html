A coachbuilder is a company or craftsman that manufactures bodies for passenger-carrying vehicles. The term derives from the makers of horse-drawn coaches and carriages and was carried over to the early motor industry, in which a customer bought a bare rolling chassis from a manufacturer and commissioned a coachbuilder to design and build bespoke bodywork to order.

History

Fine carriage building was an established luxury trade in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, centred in London, Paris and Vienna. When motor cars arrived, chassis makers such as Rolls-Royce, Bentley, Hispano-Suiza and Delahaye supplied only mechanicals, and coachbuilders — Hooper, Mulliner, Barker, Park Ward in Britain; Chapron, Saoutchik and Figoni et Falaschi in France; Touring, Pininfarina and Zagato in Italy; Brewster and LeBaron in the United States — clothed them in individual bodies. Wealthy clients ordered several bodies for one chassis, to be changed with the seasons.

Decline

The coachbuilding model collapsed in the 1930s–1950s as manufacturers adopted steel unitary construction, in which body and chassis are a single structure, and mass production made separate coachwork uneconomic. Most independent coachbuilders closed or were absorbed by the manufacturers they had served — Mulliner by Rolls-Royce, for example.

Modern coachbuilding

Some Italian carrozzerie reinvented themselves as design houses and contract manufacturers: Pininfarina styled production cars for Ferrari and others and built niche models under contract. A small revival of bespoke coachbuilding emerged in the twenty-first century, with one-off and limited-run bodies created for wealthy collectors on modern supercar mechanicals by firms such as Zagato and Touring Superleggera, and by manufacturers' own special divisions. The word also survives for builders of bus and coach bodies, and in "coachbuilt" motorhomes.
