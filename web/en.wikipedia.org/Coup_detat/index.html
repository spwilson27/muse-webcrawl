Coup detat

A coup d'etat — commonly written coup detat without diacritics, or simply a coup — is an illegal and overt attempt by the military or other state elites to unseat the incumbent leadership of a state. A self-coup (autogolpe) occurs when a leader, having come to power through legal means, dissolves or renders powerless the legislature and other checks in order to remain in office illegally. Unlike a revolution, a coup seeks to change the government at the top rather than transform society, and unlike a civil war it is typically swift and limited in scope.

Characteristics

Successful coups rely on seizing the nodes of state power — communications, broadcasting, transport hubs, and the capital — and on presenting the takeover as a fait accompli to which other actors acquiesce. Political scientist Edward Luttwak's Coup d'État: A Practical Handbook (1968) analyzed the coup as a technique available where state bureaucracies are developed but political participation is limited. Scholars such as Naunihal Singh argue that coups succeed largely through coordination: plotters win by convincing soldiers and officials that the coup has already succeeded.

History

The term derives from the French coup d'État, "a blow of state." Famous examples include Napoleon Bonaparte's coup of 18 Brumaire (1799), the 1973 Chilean coup led by Augusto Pinochet against Salvador Allende, and the failed 1991 Soviet coup attempt. Waves of coups characterized postcolonial Africa and Latin America in the 1960s and 1970s, and periods of democratic backsliding have brought renewed waves, as in the Sahel in the early 2020s.

Outcomes

Research finds most coups fail, but those that succeed frequently lead to authoritarian rule; a minority, especially after the Cold War, have been followed by elections and democratization. International bodies such as the African Union now formally condemn and sanction unconstitutional changes of government.
