Crane (bird)

Scientific classification
Kingdom: Animalia
Phylum: Chordata
Class: Aves
Order: Gruiformes
Family: Gruidae

Cranes are a family, the Gruidae, of large, long-legged, and long-necked birds in the group Gruiformes. The 15 species of cranes are placed in three genera: Antigone, Balearica, and Grus. Unlike the similar-looking but unrelated herons, cranes fly with necks outstretched, not pulled back. Cranes live on most continents, with the exception of Antarctica and South America.

Common crane (Grus grus)

Description

Cranes are large to very large birds, including the world's tallest flying bird, the sarus crane. They range in length from the demoiselle crane, which measures 90 cm (35 in) in length, to the sarus crane, which can be up to 176 cm (69 in). Their plumage varies by habitat: species inhabiting vast, open wetlands tend to have more white in their plumage than do smaller species and forest-dwelling species, which tend toward grey plumage.

Behaviour and ecology

Cranes are highly social birds that generally live in pairs or family groups. They are opportunistic feeders that change their diets according to the season and their own nutrient requirements. They eat a range of items from suitably sized small rodents, fish, amphibians, and insects to grain and berries. Cranes construct platform nests in shallow water and typically lay two eggs at a time.

Distribution and habitat

Cranes have a cosmopolitan distribution that spans most of the world's continents. They are absent from Antarctica and, mysteriously, South America. East Asia has the highest crane diversity, with eight species, followed by Africa with five resident species. Most species are at least partly migratory, flying long distances between breeding and wintering grounds.

In mythology and symbolism

The beauty of cranes and their spectacular mating dances have made them highly symbolic birds in many cultures, with records dating back to ancient times. Crane motifs are found in many cultures, representing longevity, good fortune, and fidelity; the crane is a national symbol in several countries, and origami cranes are a famous motif in Japanese culture.

Conservation

Many crane species are threatened: the whooping crane of North America was reduced to a handful of individuals in the 20th century before conservation efforts began its recovery, and the Siberian crane is critically endangered. Habitat loss, especially the drainage of wetlands, is the principal threat facing most species.
