Livonia

Livonia (Latvian: Vidzeme in part; Estonian: Liivimaa) is a historic region on the eastern Baltic coast, named after the Finnic Livonians, roughly corresponding to present-day northern Latvia and southern Estonia.

History

German crusaders of the Livonian Brothers of the Sword conquered the region in the early thirteenth century, founding Riga in 1201. The resulting Livonian Confederation joined the prince-bishoprics and the Livonian Order's lands with Hanseatic towns. After the Livonian War (1558–1583) the territory was partitioned among Poland–Lithuania, Sweden and Denmark; Swedish Livonia, centred on Riga, became the dominant Baltic power's granary in the seventeenth century. Russia took the region in the Great Northern War, confirmed by the Treaty of Nystad (1721), and Livonia became a governorate of the Russian Empire governed largely by Baltic German nobles until the First World War, when its lands were divided between independent Latvia and Estonia.

The Livonian language, once spoken along the coast, is now being revived after the death of its last native speaker in 2013.
