Inverse matrix

In linear algebra, an n-by-n square matrix A is called invertible (or non-singular) if there exists another matrix B such that AB = BA = I, where I is the identity matrix. The matrix B, unique if it exists, is called the inverse of A and is denoted A−1.

Conditions for invertibility

A square matrix over a field is invertible if and only if its determinant is non-zero; equivalently, its columns are linearly independent, its rank is n, zero is not an eigenvalue, and the only solution of Ax = 0 is the trivial solution. A matrix that is not invertible is called singular.

Computation

For a 2×2 matrix, the inverse is given by (1/(ad − bc)) [[d, −b], [−c, a]]. In general, the inverse can be computed by Gauss–Jordan elimination on the augmented matrix [A | I], or via the adjugate formula A−1 = adj(A) / det(A). In numerical practice, systems Ax = b are usually solved by factorisation (such as LU decomposition) rather than by forming the inverse explicitly.

Properties and applications

The inverse of a product reverses order: (AB)−1 = B−1A−1. Invertible matrices represent invertible linear transformations and form the general linear group. Inverses are used to solve systems of linear equations, in computer graphics transformations, cryptography, and statistics (for example in least-squares estimation, where the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse generalises the inverse to non-square matrices).
