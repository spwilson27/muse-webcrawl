Francisco I. Madero

Francisco Ignacio Madero González (30 October 1873 – 22 February 1913) was a Mexican businessman, revolutionary, writer and statesman, who served as the 37th president of Mexico from 1911 until he was deposed in a military coup in February 1913 and assassinated along with his vice president, José María Pino Suárez.

Born into one of Mexico's wealthiest families, Madero studied in France and the United States and managed family estates in Coahuila. A follower of the spiritualist movement, he entered politics in opposition to the long dictatorship of Porfirio Díaz. His 1908 book La sucesión presidencial en 1910 ("The Presidential Succession of 1910") called for fair elections and made him the leading figure of the anti-reelectionist movement.

Revolution and presidency

After Díaz imprisoned him during the fraudulent 1910 election, Madero escaped to Texas and issued the Plan of San Luis Potosí, calling for armed revolt on 20 November 1910 — the opening act of the Mexican Revolution. Díaz resigned in May 1911, and Madero was elected president, taking office on 6 November 1911.

His presidency was turbulent: he preserved many Porfirian officials and institutions, disappointed revolutionary followers such as Emiliano Zapata, who proclaimed the Plan of Ayala against him, and faced rebellions from both conservatives and radicals. In February 1913, during the Decena Trágica ("Ten Tragic Days"), General Victoriano Huerta overthrew him; Madero and Pino Suárez were murdered days later, an act that reignited civil war.

Legacy

Madero is remembered as the "Apostle of Democracy" in Mexico. His brief presidency is generally seen as a sincere but ineffective attempt to establish democratic government, and his overthrow and murder are regarded as one of the pivotal tragedies of the revolutionary decade.
