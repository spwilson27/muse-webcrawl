The Group Theatre was an American theatre collective based in New York City and formed in 1931 by Harold Clurman, Cheryl Crawford, and Lee Strasberg. It was intended to be a base for a new kind of theatre: a permanent company of actors, directors, and playwrights working together, inspired by the methods of Konstantin Stanislavski and committed to plays that confronted contemporary social issues. Although it lasted only a decade, the Group is regarded as one of the most influential institutions in American theatre history.

Work and method

The company trained together intensively, spending summers in the country developing an ensemble approach and an American adaptation of Stanislavski's system that Strasberg codified as "the Method". Directors in the company — including Strasberg, Clurman, and Elia Kazan — differed over emphasis, a tension chronicled in Clurman's memoir The Fervent Years (1945).

Productions

The Group's signature successes included Clifford Odets's plays Awake and Sing! and Waiting for Lefty (both 1935), the latter an agitprop one-act whose premiere famously ended with the audience chanting "Strike!" Other notable productions included Paul Green's The House of Connelly (the company's first production, 1931), Sidney Kingsley's Men in White (1933, Pulitzer Prize), Irwin Shaw's The Gentle People, and Odets's Golden Boy (1937).

Legacy

Members and alumni — among them Elia Kazan, Stella Adler, Sanford Meisner, Robert Lewis, John Garfield, Franchot Tone, and Morris Carnovsky — went on to reshape American acting teaching. The Actors Studio (founded 1947) and the various Method-derived schools carried the Group's influence into postwar film and theatre, while its model of a socially engaged ensemble remained an ideal for later companies.
