Bartholomeus Jan "Bart" Bok (April 28, 1906 – August 5, 1983) was a Dutch-American astronomer, teacher, and lecturer. He is best known for his work on the structure and evolution of the Milky Way galaxy, and for the discovery of Bok globules — small, dense dark nebulae that are sites of star formation.

Early life and education

Bok was born in Hoorn, the Netherlands, and studied at Leiden and Groningen universities, completing his PhD in 1932. At the International Astronomical Union meeting in 1928 he met his future wife, astronomer Priscilla Fairfield; they married in 1929 and collaborated throughout their careers. In 1933 they moved to Harvard University.

Career

Bok spent nearly three decades at Harvard, where he became Robert Wheeler Willson Professor of Applied Astronomy and directed Harvard College Observatory from 1946 to 1952. An advocate of radio astronomy, he promoted the use of the 21-cm hydrogen line to map the spiral structure of the Galaxy.

In the 1940s Bok drew attention to small, round, nearly black nebulae visible against bright backgrounds, arguing they were collapsing clouds where stars were forming — the objects now known as Bok globules.

From 1957 to 1966 Bok and his wife worked in Australia, where he directed the Mount Stromlo Observatory and helped build up astronomy at the Australian National University. They then moved to the University of Arizona in Tucson, where Bok helped develop the Steward Observatory until his retirement in 1974.

Outreach and legacy

Bok was a celebrated popularizer; his book The Milky Way (with Priscilla Bok, first edition 1941) ran through five editions. He served as president of the American Astronomical Society (1970–1971) and vice-president of the International Astronomical Union. He was also a prominent skeptic of UFO claims and astrology. The asteroid 1983 Bok and the lunar crater Bok are named for him and his wife.
