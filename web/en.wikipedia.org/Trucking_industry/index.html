The trucking industry (also referred to as the transportation or logistics industry) comprises the business of transporting goods by road using trucks. In the United States, trucks move the large majority of all freight by value and tonnage on short and medium hauls, and the industry is a central component of the national economy, linking manufacturers, warehouses, ports, rail terminals and retailers.

Structure

The industry is divided broadly into truckload (TL) carriers, which dedicate an entire trailer to a single shipper, and less-than-truckload (LTL) carriers, which consolidate shipments from many customers. Alongside large national fleets, a great many small companies and independent owner-operators haul freight. Freight brokers and third-party logistics providers match loads with available capacity.

History

Motor freight grew rapidly after the First World War and with the construction of paved highways. Federal regulation of interstate trucking began with the Motor Carrier Act of 1935, which placed the industry under the Interstate Commerce Commission. The Motor Carrier Act of 1980 largely deregulated the industry in the United States, easing entry and rate controls and greatly increasing competition.

Regulation and safety

In the United States the industry is regulated by the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA), which sets hours-of-service rules limiting driving time, requires commercial driver's licences, and administers safety ratings. Electronic logging devices became mandatory for most carriers in the late 2010s to record drivers' hours.

Drivers

Truck drivers may work local, regional or long-haul ("over-the-road") routes. The occupation is among the largest in the United States, employing millions of drivers, and the industry frequently reports difficulties recruiting and retaining drivers for long-haul work.
