Pyrrhus (Greek: Πύρρος, Pýrrhos; 319/318–272 BC) was a Greek king and statesman of the Hellenistic period. He was king of the Greek tribe of the Molossians, of the royal Aeacid house, and later king of Epirus. One of the most celebrated generals of antiquity — Hannibal reportedly ranked him among the very greatest commanders — he is remembered above all for his war against Rome, in which his costly victories gave rise to the term "Pyrrhic victory": a success so ruinous it amounts to defeat.

Early life

Pyrrhus was the son of Aeacides of Epirus and Phthia, a daughter of the Thessalian leader Menon. A descendant, in the Molossian royal line, of Neoptolemus — son of Achilles — he was made king as an infant in 319 BC but was deposed in a coup while still a child. Restored by Illyrian intervention in 306 BC, he was deposed again in 302 BC and joined the entourage of Demetrius I of Macedon, fighting at the Battle of Ipsus (301 BC). He later spent time as a hostage-courtier at the court of Ptolemy I in Alexandria, married into the Ptolemaic house, and with Ptolemaic support recovered his throne in 297 BC. He briefly seized Macedon itself (288–284 BC) before being driven out by Lysimachus.

The Pyrrhic War

In 280 BC Pyrrhus crossed to Italy with some 25,000 men and 20 war elephants, answering an appeal from the Greek city of Tarentum against the expanding Roman Republic. He defeated the Romans at Heraclea (280 BC), his elephants panicking the Roman cavalry, and again at Asculum (279 BC). Both victories cost him irreplaceable veterans and officers; after Asculum he is said by Plutarch to have remarked, "If we are victorious in one more battle with the Romans, we shall be utterly ruined." He then campaigned in Sicily against Carthage (278–276 BC), liberating the Greek cities but alienating them with his harsh rule, before returning to Italy. At Beneventum (275 BC) the Romans avoided defeat, and Pyrrhus withdrew to Epirus, leaving Italy — and ultimately Sicily and southern Italy — to Rome and Carthage.

Death and legacy

Back in Greece, Pyrrhus seized Macedon again (274 BC) and invaded the Peloponnese. In 272 BC, during night street fighting inside Argos, he was killed — according to tradition, stunned by a roof tile thrown by an old woman and then slain. Pyrrhus's memoir and tactical writings were admired in antiquity, and his career became the classic illustration of brilliant generalship squandered on unsustainable ambition. His name survives in the phrase "Pyrrhic victory", coined from the battles of Heraclea and Asculum.
