Scarp retreat

Scarp retreat is a geological process in which an escarpment (scarp) — a steep slope or cliff separating two relatively level areas — migrates backward over time through weathering and erosion at its base and face, while roughly maintaining its steep profile. It is a central concept in models of landscape evolution in arid and semi-arid regions.

Mechanism

Retreat is driven by weathering of the rock face, undermining of resistant caprock by erosion of softer underlying strata, rockfall, and the removal of debris by streams and slope processes. Because the scarp maintains its angle as it recedes, erosion proceeds by parallel retreat rather than by general lowering of the slope. Famous examples include the retreating cliffs of the Grand Canyon and the Drakensberg escarpment.

Theoretical models

Scarp retreat is a key element of Lester Charles King's pediplanation model of landscape evolution, in which broad pediments form at the foot of retreating scarps and eventually coalesce into pediplains. It contrasts with W. M. Davis's model of slope decline and Walther Penck's slope replacement model. Measured retreat rates are typically a few millimetres per year or less, but over geological time they can shift escarpments tens of kilometres.
