Control of fire by early humans

The control of fire by early humans was a critical technological innovation in human evolution. Fire provided warmth, protection from predators, a way to create more advanced hunting tools, and a means of cooking food. These cultural advances allowed human geographic dispersal, cultural innovations, and changes to diet and behaviour.

Evidence

The oldest generally accepted evidence of controlled fire comes from sites such as Gesher Benot Ya'aqov in Israel, dated to roughly 790,000 years ago, though some researchers argue for earlier use at sites in Africa such as Koobi Fora and Chesowanja in Kenya. At Qesem Cave near Tel Aviv, evidence indicates habitual fire use from around 400,000 years ago. Claims for the million-year-old ashes at Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa remain debated.

Effects on evolution

Cooking made food easier to digest, reduced exposure to pathogens and parasites, and increased the caloric yield of meals. Richard Wrangham's "cooking hypothesis" argues that cooking drove the evolution of smaller guts and larger brains in Homo erectus. Fire also extended activity into the night, provided protection at campsites, and later enabled technologies such as pottery hardening, heat treatment of stone tools, and metallurgy.
