Whale oil is oil obtained from the blubber of whales. It varied chemically according to its source and the way it was processed; oil from baleen whales was used chiefly for lighting, soap and margarine, while the liquid wax known as sperm oil, taken from the head cavity of the sperm whale, was prized as a fine lubricant and lamp oil that burned with a bright, smokeless flame. Whale oil was the chief product of the whaling industry for centuries and a major commodity of the 18th and 19th centuries.

Production

After a whale was killed, its blubber was stripped ("flensed") in long sheets and cut into pieces. The oil was extracted by boiling the blubber in large iron try-pots — a process called "trying out" — either at shore stations or, aboard pelagic whaleships, on a brick furnace on deck. A large right or bowhead whale could yield well over one hundred barrels of oil. The most valuable product, spermaceti, is a waxy substance from the sperm whale's head used in candles and cosmetics.

Uses

Whale oil lit the lamps of Europe and North America before the petroleum era; it burned in Argand lamps and in lighthouses, where sperm oil's steady bright flame was preferred. It lubricated the machinery of the Industrial Revolution, dressed leather, softened wool in textile mills, and went into soap and paint. In the 20th century, hydrogenation made whale oil edible on an industrial scale, and it became a principal ingredient of margarine and shortening, as well as a source of glycerine for explosives during both world wars.

Decline

The industry declined from the 1850s as petroleum-derived kerosene undercut oil prices and whale stocks fell. Industrial whaling with factory ships revived production in the early 20th century, reaching a peak around the 1930s–1960s, but collapsing whale populations, substitute vegetable oils and petroleum products, conservation law (notably the International Whaling Commission's 1982 moratorium, effective 1986) and national bans — the United States prohibited whale-oil imports under the Marine Mammal Protection Act of 1972 — ended the trade in most countries. Jojoba oil, chemically similar to sperm oil, became its standard replacement in cosmetics and lubricants.
