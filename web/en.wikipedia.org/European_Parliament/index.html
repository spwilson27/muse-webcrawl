The European Parliament (EP) is one of the three legislative branches of the European Union and one of its seven institutions. Together with the Council of the European Union (known as the Council), it adopts European legislation, normally on a proposal by the European Commission. The Parliament is composed of 720 members (MEPs), elected directly by EU citizens every five years, and represents the second-largest democratic electorate in the world.

Powers and functions

The Parliament shares legislative power with the Council under the ordinary legislative procedure, holds budgetary authority, and exercises democratic oversight of the other institutions: it elects the President of the European Commission, approves the Commission as a body, and can force its resignation by a motion of censure. Its powers have grown with successive treaties, most significantly the Treaty of Lisbon.

History

The Parliament grew out of the Common Assembly of the European Coal and Steel Community (1952), whose members were initially appointed by national parliaments. It adopted the name European Parliament in 1962, and the first direct elections were held in 1979. Since then, a new Parliament has been elected every five years.

Political groups

MEPs sit in transnational political groups organised by ideology rather than nationality, including the European People's Party (EPP), Socialists and Democrats (S&D), Patriots for Europe, European Conservatives and Reformists (ECR), Renew Europe, the Greens/EFA, and The Left.
