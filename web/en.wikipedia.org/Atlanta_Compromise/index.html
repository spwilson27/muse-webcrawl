Atlanta Compromise


Booker T. Washington

The Atlanta Compromise is the name given to the understanding associated with a speech delivered by Booker T. Washington at the Cotton States and International Exposition in Atlanta, Georgia, on September 18, 1895. In the address — often called the "Atlanta Exposition Speech" — Washington, the founder of Tuskegee Institute and the most influential African American leader of his era, proposed that Black people in the South should concentrate on economic self-improvement through industrial and agricultural education, and for the moment accept segregation and political disenfranchisement, in exchange for white support for Black economic progress and a measure of security.

The speech

Washington urged his Black audience to "cast down your bucket where you are" — to make friends with the white people around them and to prove themselves indispensable through agriculture, mechanics, and commerce. In the speech's most famous passage, he declared: "In all things that are purely social we can be as separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress." White listeners, including former Georgia governor Rufus Bullock, applauded enthusiastically, and the speech made Washington a national figure, consulted by presidents and philanthropists.

Criticism

The compromise was sharply criticised by W. E. B. Du Bois, who in The Souls of Black Folk (1903) coined the term "Atlanta Compromise" and argued that Washington's programme amounted to a surrender of political power, civil rights, and higher education. The dispute between Washington's accommodationist strategy and Du Bois's demand for full civil and political equality became the defining debate of early African American political thought. Later historians have noted that Washington, while preaching accommodation publicly, secretly funded court challenges to segregation and disenfranchisement.
