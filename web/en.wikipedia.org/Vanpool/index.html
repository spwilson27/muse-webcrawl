Vanpool

A vanpool is a form of shared-ride commuting in which a group of people travel together in a van, typically between home and a common workplace or employment area. Vanpools are part of the broader field of ridesharing and transportation demand management, and are often supported by transit agencies, employers, or third-party operators who provide the vehicle, maintenance and insurance.

How vanpools operate

A vanpool usually consists of 5 to 15 commuters. One participant typically serves as the primary driver — sometimes receiving a free or discounted fare in exchange — and backup drivers are designated for days when the primary driver is unavailable. Riders generally pay a monthly fare based on the round-trip distance and the number of participants, which covers fuel, maintenance, insurance and depreciation. The group sets its own schedule, pick-up points and route.

History

In the United States, vanpooling expanded rapidly during the energy crises of the 1970s. One of the earliest large formal programs was begun in 1973 by the 3M Company in St. Paul, Minnesota, which drew national attention. Federal and state policies later encouraged vanpooling through commuter benefit programs, including pre-tax transportation benefits under Section 132(f) of the Internal Revenue Code, which treats qualified vanpooling in a vehicle seating at least six adults (excluding the driver) similarly to transit passes.

Benefits and limitations

Vanpools reduce the number of vehicles on the road, lower per-person commuting costs, and can serve longer-distance commutes poorly suited to conventional fixed-route transit. They are most effective for commuters with similar schedules and common destinations. Limitations include the coordination burden on participants and reduced flexibility compared with driving alone.
