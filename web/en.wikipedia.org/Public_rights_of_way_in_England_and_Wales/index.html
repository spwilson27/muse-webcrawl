Public rights of way in England and Wales

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Public rights of way
Countries: England and Wales
Total length: approx. 140,000 miles (225,000 km)
Categories: footpath, bridleway, byway, restricted byway
Governing law: Highways Act 1980; Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000

Public rights of way in England and Wales are routes over land, including roads, paths and tracks, along which the public have a legal right to travel. The network extends to roughly 140,000 miles (225,000 km) of paths, of which the great majority are public footpaths. Rights of way are a form of highway and are recorded on the definitive map maintained by each local surveying authority.

Categories

Rights of way are classified according to the rights they carry. A public footpath is open only to walkers (and users of mobility aids). A bridleway may also be used by horse riders and cyclists. A restricted byway carries rights on foot, on horseback and for non-motorised vehicles. A byway open to all traffic (BOAT) may be used by all classes of traffic, including motor vehicles, though many are unsurfaced green lanes.

History

Many paths are ancient in origin, following routes used for centuries between villages, churches, mills and markets. The modern legal framework developed through the Rights of Way Act 1932, the National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949, which required the creation of definitive maps, and the Countryside Act 1968.

The Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000

The Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 (CRoW Act) introduced a statutory right of access on foot to mapped areas of mountain, moor, heath, down and registered common land — often called the "right to roam". The Act also originally set a deadline, later extended, after which unrecorded historic rights of way might be extinguished.

Maintenance and obstruction

Local highway authorities are responsible for signposting and for asserting and protecting the public's rights. Obstructing a right of way is an offence under the Highways Act 1980, and landowners must keep routes crossing their land free from obstruction, including keeping vegetation cut back and stiles and gates in repair.
