The capybara (also called the greater capybara, Hydrochoerus hydrochaeris) is a giant cavy rodent native to South America and the largest living rodent in the world. Its name derives from the Tupi language and means "master of the grasses." A smaller relative, the lesser capybara, is found from Panama into northern South America.

Description and habitat

Capybaras have heavy, barrel-shaped bodies, blunt muzzles, and partially webbed toes adapted for swimming. Their eyes, ears, and nostrils sit high on the head, allowing them to remain almost fully submerged while watching for predators such as jaguars, caimans, and anacondas; they can stay underwater for up to five minutes. They inhabit savannas, flooded grasslands, and forest edges near rivers, lakes, and swamps throughout most of South America east of the Andes.

Behaviour

Highly social, capybaras live in groups typically of 10–20 individuals, sometimes aggregating to a hundred around shrinking waterholes in the dry season. They are herbivores grazing mainly on grasses and aquatic plants, and practise coprophagy to extract maximum nutrition. Females give birth to litters of about four pups after a gestation of roughly 130–150 days.

Relationship with humans

Capybaras are hunted for meat and hides in parts of their range and are ranched in Venezuela; conversely, escaped or released animals have established populations in Florida. Known for their placid temperament, they have become popular zoo animals and internet favourites worldwide.
