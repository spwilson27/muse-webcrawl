Constructivist architecture

Constructivist buildings favoured bold geometry and exposed structure.

Constructivist architecture was a form of modern architecture that flourished in the Soviet Union in the 1920s and early 1930s. It combined advanced technology and engineering with an avowedly Communist social purpose. The movement grew out of Russian constructivism in the visual arts and was organised through groups such as OSA (the Union of Contemporary Architects), led by Alexander Vesnin and Moisei Ginzburg, and ASNOVA, associated with Nikolai Ladovsky.

Characteristics

Constructivist buildings are marked by geometric abstraction, functional planning, strip windows, flat roofs and the rejection of ornament. New building types embodied the hoped-for socialist society: workers' clubs, communal houses (such as Ginzburg's Narkomfin building in Moscow, 1930), communal kitchens and factories. Vladimir Tatlin's unbuilt Monument to the Third International (1920) became an emblem of the movement.

Decline and legacy

By the early 1930s the movement was suppressed in favour of Stalinist architecture, though its ideas influenced the International Style and later modernism in the West. Many surviving constructivist buildings in Moscow, Yekaterinburg (Sverdlovsk) and Kharkiv have been the subject of preservation campaigns.
