Thoracic duct

In human anatomy, the thoracic duct is the larger of the two lymph ducts of the lymphatic system, the other being the right lymphatic duct. The thoracic duct usually begins from the cisterna chyli, an elongated lymph sac located in the abdomen, and ascends through the thorax to empty into the systemic venous circulation at the junction of the left subclavian vein and the left internal jugular vein. It is about 40 centimetres long in the adult human and transports up to four litres of lymph per day, including chyle, the fat-rich lymph absorbed from the intestines.

Structure

The duct originates at the level of the twelfth thoracic vertebra from the cisterna chyli, which receives lymph from the intestinal trunk and the right and left lumbar lymphatic trunks. It enters the thorax through the aortic hiatus of the diaphragm, ascends on the vertebral column between the aorta and the azygos vein, and crosses from right to left at about the level of the fifth thoracic vertebra. It then continues upward behind the oesophagus and arches laterally at the level of the seventh cervical vertebra before descending to its termination in the venous system.

Function

The thoracic duct drains lymph from both lower limbs, the pelvis, the abdomen, the left half of the thorax, the left upper limb and the left half of the head and neck — roughly three quarters of the body. Only the right upper quadrant is drained by the right lymphatic duct. Along with lymph, the duct returns dietary lipids absorbed in the small intestine to the bloodstream in the form of chylomicrons.

Clinical significance

Injury to the thoracic duct, for example during oesophageal or neck surgery, can cause a chylothorax, an accumulation of chyle in the pleural cavity. Variations in the duct's course and termination are common and are of practical importance to surgeons operating in the posterior mediastinum and neck.
