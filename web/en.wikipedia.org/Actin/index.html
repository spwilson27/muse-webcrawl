Actin is a family of globular multi-functional proteins that form microfilaments in the cytoskeleton and the thin filaments in muscle cells. It is one of the most abundant and highly conserved proteins in eukaryotic cells, typically making up 1–5% of total cellular protein, and up to around 10% in muscle cells.

Structure

An actin monomer, called G-actin (globular actin), is a protein of about 375 amino acids with a molecular mass of roughly 42 kDa. It binds ATP or ADP and a divalent cation, usually Mg2+. Monomers polymerise head-to-tail into helical filaments called F-actin (filamentous actin), which appear as two intertwined strands with a diameter of about 7 nm and a helical repeat of roughly 37 nm. Filaments are polar: a fast-growing "barbed" (plus) end and a slower-growing "pointed" (minus) end.

Polymerisation and dynamics

Actin filaments assemble by nucleation and elongation. Because ATP-bound monomers are added mainly at the barbed end and ADP-actin dissociates mainly from the pointed end, filaments can undergo treadmilling, in which subunits flux through the filament while its length stays roughly constant. A large family of actin-binding proteins — including profilin, cofilin, the Arp2/3 complex, formins, gelsolin and capping proteins — regulates assembly and disassembly, allowing cells to remodel their cytoskeleton rapidly.

Functions

In muscle, actin thin filaments interact with myosin thick filaments; ATP-driven myosin cross-bridge cycling slides the filaments past one another and produces contraction, the basis of the sliding-filament theory. In non-muscle cells, actin networks drive cell migration (lamellipodia and filopodia), cytokinesis (the contractile ring), endocytosis, vesicle trafficking and the maintenance of cell shape and junctions. The cortical actin network beneath the plasma membrane also contributes to membrane tension and mechanical resilience.

Isoforms and disease

Mammals have six actin isoforms encoded by separate genes, including α-skeletal, α-cardiac, α-smooth and γ-smooth actins in muscle, and β- and γ-cytoplasmic actins in non-muscle cells. Mutations in actin genes cause diseases such as nemaline myopathy, hypertrophic and dilated cardiomyopathy, and certain forms of deafness. Several natural toxins target actin: phalloidin from the death-cap mushroom stabilises filaments, while cytochalasins cap barbed ends and latrunculins sequester monomers.
