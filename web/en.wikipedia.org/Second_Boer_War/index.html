The Second Boer War (11 October 1899 – 31 May 1902), also known as the Boer War, the Anglo-Boer War, or the South African War, was a conflict fought between the British Empire and the two Boer republics — the South African Republic (Transvaal) and the Orange Free State — over the Empire's influence in Southern Africa.

Causes

The discovery of gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886 flooded the Transvaal with foreign "uitlander" miners, whose demands for political rights strained relations with Paul Kruger's government. The failed Jameson Raid of 1895–96 deepened mistrust, and disputes over suzerainty and the franchise culminated in a Boer ultimatum and war in October 1899.

Conventional phase

Boer commandos invaded Natal and the Cape Colony, besieging Ladysmith, Mafeking and Kimberley. British forces suffered a series of defeats in "Black Week" (December 1899), including Stormberg, Magersfontein and Colenso. Reinforced under Lord Roberts and Lord Kitchener, the British relieved the sieges in 1900, captured Bloemfontein and Pretoria, and annexed both republics.

Guerrilla phase

Refusing defeat, Boer "bitter-enders" waged a guerrilla war from horseback commandos. Kitchener responded with blockhouse lines, drives across the veld, scorched-earth farm burnings and the internment of Boer women and children — and many Black Africans — in concentration camps, where some 26,000 Boer civilians, mostly children, died of disease, along with at least 14,000 Black inmates. The camps provoked outrage in Britain, notably from Emily Hobhouse.

Peace and consequences

The Treaty of Vereeniging (31 May 1902) ended the war: the republics became British colonies with a promise of eventual self-government, leading to the Union of South Africa in 1910. The war cost Britain some 22,000 dead and £200 million, drew troops from across the Empire, and exposed weaknesses that prompted major military reforms.
