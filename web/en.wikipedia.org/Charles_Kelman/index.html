Charles David Kelman (May 23, 1930 – June 1, 2004) was an American ophthalmologist, surgeon, inventor, jazz musician, and entertainer who is best known for inventing phacoemulsification, the modern technique of cataract surgery in which the eye's clouded lens is emulsified with an ultrasonic handpiece and aspirated through a small incision. His innovation transformed cataract extraction from a major inpatient operation requiring large incisions and long recovery into an outpatient procedure performed through an incision of about 3 millimeters.

Invention of phacoemulsification

Kelman drew his inspiration in 1962 while sitting in a dentist's chair, listening to the ultrasonic scaler used to clean teeth, and wondering whether similar vibrations could break up a cataract. Working with engineer Anton Banko and supported by a grant, he developed the first phacoemulsifier over several years of animal experiments, enduring many failures before the first human procedures in 1967. Initially resisted by much of the ophthalmological establishment, the technique — taught personally by Kelman to thousands of surgeons in the 1970s — became the standard of care worldwide, with tens of millions of procedures performed annually.

Other work and honors

Kelman held more than 100 patents, developed intraocular lens designs and cryoprobes, and taught at the New York Eye and Ear Infirmary and New York Medical College. He received the National Medal of Technology in 1992, the Albert Lasker Award for Clinical Medical Research in 2004, and was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame. A lifelong jazz saxophonist and clarinetist who had played professionally as a youth, he performed on television and recorded several albums, and he authored a memoir, Through My Eyes.
