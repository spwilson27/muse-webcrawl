Chinese constellations

Chinese constellations are the traditional groupings of stars in Chinese astronomy. Unlike the constellations inherited from Greek astronomy and now standardized by the International Astronomical Union, the Chinese system divides the sky into smaller asterisms organized within three great enclosures around the north celestial pole and twenty-eight mansions along the ecliptic. The system reached a mature form by the Han dynasty and remained in official use for over two thousand years.

Structure

The Three Enclosures

The sky near the north celestial pole is divided into three enclosures (sān yuán): the Purple Forbidden Enclosure at the center, symbolizing the imperial palace; the Supreme Palace Enclosure; and the Heavenly Market Enclosure. Stars within them bear names drawn from the imperial court — officials, princes, guards, and market goods — so that the heavens mirrored the structure of the earthly empire.

The Twenty-eight Mansions

The region along the ecliptic and equator is divided into twenty-eight lunar mansions (èrshíbā xiù), the resting places of the Moon in its monthly circuit. They are grouped by fours into the Four Palaces, each associated with a directional animal: the Azure Dragon of the East, the Black Tortoise of the North, the White Tiger of the West, and the Vermilion Bird of the South.

History

Star names and asterisms were recorded by the astrologers Shi Shen, Gan De, and Wu Xian during the Warring States period (4th century BCE). The astronomer Chen Zhuo in the 3rd century CE combined their schools into a single catalogue. Chinese guest-star records — of comets, novae, and supernovae such as the Crab supernova of 1054 — remain valuable to modern astronomy.
