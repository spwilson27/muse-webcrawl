Biological species concept

The biological species concept is a way of defining a species in terms of interbreeding. It was formulated by the evolutionary biologist Ernst Mayr in 1942 and became the most widely used species definition of the modern evolutionary synthesis.

Definition

"Species are groups of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations, which are reproductively isolated from other such groups." — Ernst Mayr

Under this concept, populations belong to the same species if their members can breed with one another in nature and produce viable, fertile offspring, and they are a different species if reproductive isolating mechanisms — geographic separation, behavioural differences, incompatible gametes, or sterile hybrids such as the mule — prevent gene flow between them.

Limitations

The concept cannot be applied to asexual organisms such as bacteria and many plants, nor directly to fossils, whose breeding behaviour is unobservable. It is also challenged by ring species, by natural hybridization between otherwise distinct species (common in plants, fish, and canids), and by populations that are geographically separated and thus never have the opportunity to interbreed. For these reasons, biologists also use morphological, ecological, phylogenetic, and genetic species concepts, and hundreds of competing definitions exist; the choice among them depends on the organisms and questions involved.
