Gondi people

Total populationApprox. 13–14 million (2011 census, India)
RegionsMadhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Odisha
LanguagesGondi (Dravidian), Hindi, Marathi, Telugu
StatusScheduled Tribe (India)

The Gondi (Gōṇḍī) people, also known as the Gond tribe, are a large Indigenous (Adivasi) ethnic group of central India, recognised as a Scheduled Tribe. They are one of the largest tribal groups in India and in the world, spread mainly across Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra, and neighbouring states, in the region historically known as Gondwana.

History

From roughly the 14th to the 18th centuries, Gond kingdoms such as Garha-Mandla, Deogarh, and Chanda ruled large parts of central India, building forts, palaces, and temples. The Gond queen Rani Durgavati, who resisted Mughal expansion in 1564, remains a celebrated figure. Gond polities declined under Maratha and then British rule, and the Gond heartland was progressively incorporated into colonial forest and revenue administration.

Culture

The Gondi language belongs to the Dravidian family, though many Gonds today speak regional Indo-Aryan languages. Traditional religion centres on clan and village deities such as Persa Pen, with festivals, dances, and the bardic tradition of the Pardhan musicians. Gond art, including the modern "Gond painting" style popularised by artists such as Jangarh Singh Shyam, is now widely exhibited.

Society and economy

Traditionally shifting cultivators and forest gatherers, most Gonds today practise settled agriculture. The Gond homeland overlaps mineral-rich and forested districts, and issues of land rights, displacement, and political representation — including demands associated with a proposed Gondwana state — feature in contemporary politics.
