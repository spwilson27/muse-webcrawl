Moore's law is the observation that the number of transistors in an integrated circuit doubles about every two years. It is named after Gordon Moore, co-founder of Fairchild Semiconductor and Intel, who described the trend in a 1965 paper and revised the doubling period to two years in 1975.

Nature of the "law"

Moore's law is not a law of physics but an empirical observation about technological and economic progress. For roughly five decades it held remarkably well and became a self-fulfilling target for the semiconductor industry's roadmaps, driving exponential improvements in computing power, memory capacity, and cost per transistor.

Slowdown

Since the end of Dennard scaling in the mid-2000s, clock speeds plateaued and further gains have come increasingly from multi-core designs, specialized accelerators, and three-dimensional chip packaging rather than straightforward transistor shrinkage. Industry leaders have periodically declared Moore's law dead or slowing, while transistor counts have continued to rise through advanced nodes and new architectures.
