Albumins are a family of globular proteins, the most common of which are the serum albumins. Human serum albumin is the most abundant protein in human blood plasma, accounting for about half of serum protein. It is produced in the liver, is water-soluble, and becomes insoluble (denatures) on heating — as when egg white, rich in the related protein ovalbumin, cooks.

Functions

Serum albumin maintains oncotic pressure, the force that keeps fluid within blood vessels, and transports a wide range of substances: fatty acids, bilirubin, hormones such as thyroxine, metal ions, and many drugs, which bind to albumin and are carried in the circulation. It also acts as a buffer and a source of amino acids.

Clinical significance

Low serum albumin (hypoalbuminemia) occurs in liver disease, kidney disease (nephrotic syndrome), malnutrition and chronic inflammation, and is a strong marker of poor prognosis in hospital patients. Albumin lost in urine (albuminuria) is an early sign of kidney damage, particularly in diabetes. Intravenous albumin solutions are used clinically in selected settings such as large-volume paracentesis, hepatorenal syndrome and plasma exchange, though their benefits in general resuscitation are debated.

Other albumins

Ovalbumin is the main protein of egg white; lactalbumin occurs in milk; and 2S albumins are storage proteins in seeds. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) is a standard laboratory reagent used as a protein concentration standard and blocking agent.
