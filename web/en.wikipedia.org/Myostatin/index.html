Myostatin, also known as growth differentiation factor 8 (GDF-8), is a protein encoded by the MSTN gene that inhibits muscle growth. A member of the transforming growth factor beta (TGF-beta) superfamily, myostatin is produced mainly in skeletal muscle and circulates in the blood, limiting the size and number of muscle fibres.

Discovery and effects of loss

Myostatin was discovered in 1997 by Alexandra McPherron, Ann Lawler and Se-Jin Lee at Johns Hopkins University; mice lacking the gene grew to roughly twice the muscle mass of normal mice. Naturally occurring inactivating mutations explain the "double-muscled" phenotype of Belgian Blue and Piedmontese cattle, and a similar mutation affects whippet dogs, in which heterozygous animals are faster racers while homozygotes are heavily muscled "bully whippets". A human case was reported in 2004: a child with myostatin mutations was exceptionally muscular from birth.

Clinical significance

Because blocking myostatin increases muscle mass, inhibitors — including follistatin, engineered receptors and monoclonal antibodies — have been explored for treating muscle-wasting conditions such as muscular dystrophy, cachexia and sarcopenia. Results in clinical trials have been mixed, and myostatin manipulation is also a concern in sport, where it is prohibited by anti-doping authorities.
