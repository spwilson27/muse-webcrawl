Astroid

An astroid is a particular mathematical curve: a hypocycloid with four cusps. Specifically, it is the locus of a point on a circle as it rolls around the inside of a fixed circle with four times the radius. Its modern name comes from the Greek word for "star" (ἀστήρ); the curve was known to Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in 1674 and studied by Johann Bernoulli and d'Alembert among others. It is also a special case of a superellipse.

Equations

The astroid can be described parametrically by x = a cos³t, y = a sin³t, where a is the radius of the fixed circle (divided such that the curve reaches ±a on the axes). Eliminating the parameter gives the Cartesian equation (x² + y² − a²)³ + 27a²x²y² = 0, and in the common normalised form with a = 1, it is the set of points satisfying x^(2/3) + y^(2/3) = 1. The astroid has cusps at (±a, 0) and (0, ±a).

Properties

The area enclosed by an astroid of radius a is 3πa²/8, and its perimeter (arc length) is 6a. The astroid is the evolute of an ellipse: the envelope of the normals of an ellipse traces an astroid-like scaled curve. A notable tangent property is that every tangent segment intercepted between the coordinate axes has constant length equal to a, which underlies the classic "sliding ladder" problem — a ladder sliding down a wall has as its envelope an astroid. The curve also appears in physics as a caustic pattern, and Stoner astroid models describe magnetisation reversal in single-domain magnetic particles.
