Megathrust earthquake

Megathrust earthquakes occur at subduction zones, where one tectonic plate is forced beneath another, along the huge thrust fault (the megathrust) that forms the plate boundary. They are the most powerful earthquakes on Earth: all known earthquakes of magnitude 9.0 or greater have been megathrust events, capable of rupturing fault areas hundreds of kilometres long and producing destructive tsunamis.

Mechanism

As the plates converge, friction locks the shallow part of the megathrust while strain accumulates over centuries. When the fault finally slips, the overriding plate rebounds, displacing the seafloor vertically and often generating a tsunami. The deeper parts of the interface typically slip aseismically.

Notable examples

The largest instrumentally recorded earthquake was the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile (magnitude 9.5). Other great megathrust earthquakes include the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake off Sumatra (9.1–9.3) and the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake in Japan (9.0–9.1), both followed by devastating tsunamis, and the 1964 Alaska earthquake (9.2). The Cascadia subduction zone off North America's Pacific Northwest last ruptured in 1700 and is considered overdue for another great event.

Terminology

The term "megathrust" refers to the extremely large scale of the fault, which can extend over 1,000 km. Megathrust earthquakes are distinguished from ordinary thrust earthquakes on smaller faults within the crust.
