Synchrotron light source

A synchrotron light source is a facility that produces electromagnetic radiation — mainly bright X-rays — by circulating electrons at nearly the speed of light in a storage ring, a large synchrotron whose magnets keep the electrons on a closed orbit. The emitted synchrotron radiation is channelled down beamlines to experimental stations, where it is used to probe the structure and properties of matter.

How it works

Electrons from a linear accelerator are boosted to full energy in a booster synchrotron and injected into the storage ring, where they circulate for hours. Bending magnets, wigglers, and undulators force the beam to radiate; the light is extremely intense, tightly collimated, pulsed, and tunable in wavelength. Facilities are conventionally grouped into generations, with the newest “fourth-generation” diffraction-limited storage rings, such as MAX IV in Sweden and the ESRF-EBS in Grenoble, achieving dramatic gains in brilliance.

Applications and facilities

Beamline techniques include X-ray crystallography of proteins, X-ray absorption spectroscopy, small-angle scattering, and imaging. Famous structures determined with synchrotron data include the ribosome and many drug targets. Major sources include the Advanced Photon Source (Argonne, USA), SPring-8 (Japan), the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (France), Diamond Light Source (UK), PETRA III (Germany), and the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility; roughly fifty such facilities operate worldwide, serving tens of thousands of visiting scientists each year.
