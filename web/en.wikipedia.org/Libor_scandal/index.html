Libor scandal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Libor scandal was a series of revelations, beginning in 2012, that banks had deliberately misreported the interest rates they submitted for the calculation of the London Interbank Offered Rate (Libor), a benchmark used to price trillions of dollars of loans, mortgages, and derivatives worldwide. The manipulation was undertaken both to profit from derivatives positions and, during the 2008 financial crisis, to make banks appear more creditworthy than they were.

Background

Libor was calculated daily for several currencies and maturities from submissions by a panel of major banks, each estimating the rate at which it could borrow unsecured funds from another bank. Because submissions were estimates rather than records of actual transactions, the system depended on honesty and was vulnerable to abuse. Studies and press reports — notably a 2008 Wall Street Journal analysis — had raised suspicions that submissions during the financial crisis understated banks' true borrowing costs.

Revelations and settlements

On 27 June 2012, Barclays agreed to pay fines totalling about £290 million (US$450 million) to the UK Financial Services Authority and US regulators, admitting that its submitters had made false submissions influenced by derivatives traders and, in 2007–2009, by management concern about the bank's perceived financial health. The ensuing scandal forced the resignation of Barclays chairman Marcus Agius, chief executive Bob Diamond, and chief operating officer Jerry del Missier.

Subsequent settlements involved many major banks, including UBS (fined about US$1.5 billion in December 2012), Royal Bank of Scotland, Rabobank, Lloyds, Citigroup, JPMorgan, and Deutsche Bank (fined US$2.5 billion in 2015). Total fines exceeded US$9 billion. Individual prosecutions followed: former UBS and Citigroup trader Tom Hayes was convicted in London in 2015 of conspiring to rig Libor and sentenced to 14 years (reduced to 11 on appeal); several other traders were convicted or acquitted in the UK and US, and some US convictions were later overturned on appeal, including in 2022, when a federal appeals court reversed the convictions of two Deutsche Bank traders on the ground that the challenged submissions were not necessarily false under the governing definitions. In 2025, the UK Supreme Court quashed the convictions of Tom Hayes and Carlo Palombo, holding that the juries had been misdirected on the proper interpretation of the Libor definition.

Reform and transition

The scandal led to criminal sanctions for benchmark manipulation in the UK, transfer of Libor administration from the British Bankers' Association to ICE Benchmark Administration in 2014, and ultimately to the decision to phase out Libor altogether. Regulators promoted transaction-based "risk-free" alternatives such as SONIA (sterling), SOFR (US dollar), and €STR (euro). Publication of most Libor settings ceased at the end of 2021, and the remaining US dollar settings ended in June 2023.
