Duty to rescue

A duty to rescue is a concept in tort and criminal law according to which a person may be held liable for failing to help another person who is in peril. Legal systems differ sharply: most civil law countries impose a general duty to render reasonable assistance, while common law systems generally impose no such duty except in special circumstances.

Common law position

Under the traditional common law rule, a stranger has no legal duty to rescue another in danger, however easy the rescue might be — a reflection of the general reluctance to penalise omissions. Recognised exceptions include cases where the defendant created the peril, where a special relationship exists (such as parent and child, employer and employee, or carrier and passenger), where a statute imposes a duty, or where the defendant has voluntarily begun a rescue and must then act with reasonable care. A number of US states, including Vermont and Minnesota, have enacted statutes creating a limited duty to render reasonable assistance at the scene of an emergency; so-called "Good Samaritan laws" typically protect rescuers from liability rather than compel rescue.

Civil law position

Many civil law jurisdictions criminalise the failure to assist. Article 223-6 of the French Penal Code punishes anyone who voluntarily abstains from assisting a person in danger when this could be done without risk to themselves, with penalties of up to five years' imprisonment and a substantial fine. Germany's §323c of the Strafgesetzbuch ("unterlassene Hilfeleistung") similarly makes failure to render assistance at accidents or in emergencies an offence. Quebec's Charter of Human Rights and Freedoms also recognises a right to assistance and a corresponding obligation.

Ethical debate

The question of whether there is a moral duty to rescue is a staple of moral philosophy. Peter Singer's 1972 essay "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" used the example of a drowning child in a shallow pond to argue that we are obliged to prevent bad things happening whenever we can do so without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance. Critics of legal duties to rescue raise concerns about liberty, enforceability and the competence of bystanders to intervene.
