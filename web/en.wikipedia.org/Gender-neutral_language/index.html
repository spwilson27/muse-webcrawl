Gender-neutral language

Gender-neutral language or gender-inclusive language is language that avoids bias towards a particular sex or social gender. In English, this includes use of nouns that are not gender-specific, such as firefighter or police officer instead of fireman or policeman, and avoidance of the pronouns he, him or his to refer to people of unknown or unspecified gender.

History

Prescriptive grammarians of the 18th and 19th centuries promoted the generic he as the correct pronoun for a person of unspecified sex, and the 1850 Interpretation Act in Britain formally declared that masculine words in legislation included women. During the second-wave feminist movement of the 1970s, writers and style guides increasingly questioned these conventions, arguing that generic masculine forms rendered women invisible. Major publishers and institutions, including the Associated Press and many universities, gradually adopted guidelines favouring neutral alternatives.

Strategies

Common strategies include replacing gendered occupational nouns with neutral ones (chairperson, flight attendant), recasting sentences into the plural, using the singular they, and alternating or avoiding pronouns. The singular they has a long history in English literature and was endorsed by the American Psychological Association's style guide in 2019.

In other languages

Languages with grammatical gender pose different challenges. Swedish officially adopted the gender-neutral pronoun hen alongside han (he) and hon (she). In Spanish and French, proposed inclusive forms such as the -e ending or the midpoint (·) have been debated and, in some jurisdictions, restricted in official documents. Chinese distinguishes the spoken pronoun tā, which is gender-neutral in speech, while written characters differentiate masculine and feminine forms.

Criticism and debate

Debate continues over whether neutral forms are necessary, natural, or effective. Supporters argue language shapes perception and inclusion; critics argue some reforms are awkward, ideologically driven, or unnecessary because readers understand generic forms. Public opinion and institutional practice vary widely by language and country.
