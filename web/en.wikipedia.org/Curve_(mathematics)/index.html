Curve

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A parabola

In mathematics, a curve (also called a curved line in older texts) is an object similar to a line, but that does not have to be straight. Intuitively, a curve may be thought of as the trace left by a moving point.

Since the 19th century the concept has been made precise in several ways: a curve in a topological space is a continuous map from an interval of the real line into that space; in differential geometry a (plane or space) curve is usually given parametrically, with properties such as curvature and torsion derived from its derivatives. A curve is closed if its endpoints coincide, and simple if it does not intersect itself (Jordan curve theorem). Curves whose points satisfy a polynomial equation are algebraic curves — including lines, circles, conics and elliptic curves — while others, such as the logarithmic spiral, are transcendental. Space-filling curves (Peano, Hilbert) show that a curve can have positive area, refining naive intuition.

History

Interest in curves began with the geometry of the ancient Greeks — conic sections in Apollonius's Conics, spirals and cissoids devised for the classical problems. Analytic geometry (Descartes, Fermat) recast curves as equations, and calculus made their tangents, lengths and areas computable.
