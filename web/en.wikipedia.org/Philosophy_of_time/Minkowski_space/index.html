Minkowski space

Minkowski space (or Minkowski spacetime) is the four-dimensional mathematical setting in which Einstein's special relativity is most conveniently formulated. Proposed by Hermann Minkowski in 1908, it unites three-dimensional Euclidean space and time into a single manifold whose geometry is pseudo-Euclidean, with an indefinite metric that assigns to each pair of events an invariant spacetime interval.

Light cones and causal structure

For any event, Minkowski space divides all other events into three regions: the timelike future inside the forward light cone, the timelike past inside the backward cone, and the spacelike "elsewhere" outside the cones, whose events can neither cause nor be caused by the given event. Because all observers agree on this cone structure even while disagreeing about simultaneity, Minkowski geometry captures what is objective in relativistic physics.

Philosophical significance

In the philosophy of time, Minkowski space is the standard model of the block universe: since there is no privileged foliation of the manifold into "nows", many philosophers — following arguments by C. W. Rietdijk and Hilary Putnam — conclude that past and future events must be as real as present ones, a doctrine called eternalism. The world of a persisting object is represented in this geometry by its world line, a curve threading through the manifold. Einstein initially dismissed Minkowski's reformulation as "superfluous learnedness", but it became indispensable to general relativity, where spacetime is locally Minkowskian but globally curved.
