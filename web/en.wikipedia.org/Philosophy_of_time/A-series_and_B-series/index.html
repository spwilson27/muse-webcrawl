A-series and B-series

A-series and B-series
Introduced byJ. M. E. McTaggart (1908)
A-seriesEvents ordered as past, present, future (tensed)
B-seriesEvents ordered as earlier than / later than (tenseless)
Associated theoriesA-theory and B-theory of time

The A-series and B-series are two ways of ordering events in time, distinguished by the philosopher J. M. E. McTaggart in his 1908 paper "The Unreality of Time." The distinction became the standard vocabulary of the philosophy of time.

The two orderings

The A-series orders events by their possession of the properties of being past, present, or future. These A-determinations are constantly changing: a future event becomes present, then past. The B-series orders events by the fixed, permanent relations of being earlier than, simultaneous with, or later than one another; if one event is earlier than another, it always is. McTaggart also identified a C-series—an unordered set of events—which he took to be what remains if time is removed.

McTaggart's argument

McTaggart argued that time requires change, that the B-series alone contains no change (since B-relations never alter), and that the A-series is contradictory, because every event must be past, present, and future, which are incompatible properties. The reply that an event is these at different times launches a regress McTaggart deemed vicious. He concluded that time is unreal—a conclusion few accept, though his framework still organizes the debate.

A-theory and B-theory

A-theorists hold that the A-series is metaphysically fundamental: temporal becoming is real, and typically combine this with presentism or a growing-block view. B-theorists hold that the B-series suffices, tensed language reflects our perspective, and past and future events exist tenselessly (eternalism). The debate interacts with special relativity, whose relativity of simultaneity fits awkwardly with a unique objective present.
