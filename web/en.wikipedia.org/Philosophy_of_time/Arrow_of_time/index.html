Arrow of time

Arrow of time
Term coined byArthur Eddington (1927)
Arrows identifiedThermodynamic, cosmological, radiative, causal, psychological, quantum
Key lawSecond law of thermodynamics (entropy increase)

The arrow of time is the concept that time flows in one direction only—from past to future—although the fundamental laws of physics are mostly symmetric under time reversal. The expression was coined by the British astronomer Arthur Stanley Eddington in 1927, who argued that the direction of time could be determined by studying the organization of matter and energy.

The thermodynamic arrow

The most prominent arrow is grounded in the second law of thermodynamics: the entropy of an isolated system never decreases. We see eggs break but never unbreak, and heat flow from hot to cold but never the reverse, because disordered states vastly outnumber ordered ones. The puzzle is why the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state (the "Past Hypothesis"), given which the thermodynamic arrow, and plausibly the others, follow. Loschmidt's paradox and Poincaré recurrence raise difficulties that statistical mechanics answers probabilistically.

Other arrows

The cosmological arrow points in the direction of the universe's expansion. The radiative arrow describes waves spreading outward from sources rather than converging. The causal and psychological arrows reflect that causes precede effects and that we remember the past but not the future. Weak nuclear interactions violate time-reversal symmetry slightly (via CP violation and the CPT theorem), though this is generally considered too small an effect to explain the everyday arrow.

Philosophical significance

Whether the arrows reflect an objective asymmetry of the world or a perspectival feature of our situation within it is a live question connecting physics with debates between the A-theory and B-theory of time.
