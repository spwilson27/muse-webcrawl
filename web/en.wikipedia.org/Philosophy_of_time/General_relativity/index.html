General relativity

General relativity, published by Albert Einstein in 1915, is the geometric theory of gravitation in which gravity is not a force but the curvature of a four-dimensional spacetime caused by mass and energy. Within the philosophy of time, general relativity raises deep questions about the nature of time itself: whether time is fundamental, whether the universe has a global "now", and how temporal structure depends on the distribution of matter.

Relativity of simultaneity and curved time

Einstein's earlier special relativity had already undermined absolute simultaneity. General relativity goes further: the rate at which clocks tick depends on their path through curved spacetime and on the strength of the gravitational field they inhabit, a phenomenon confirmed by experiments with atomic clocks and by the daily corrections applied to GPS satellites. There is, in general, no unique way to slice spacetime into a stack of universal presents, which many philosophers take to support the block-universe picture associated with four-dimensional space.

Philosophical issues

The theory's field equations permit exotic solutions, including rotating universes described by Kurt Gödel in 1949 that contain closed timelike curves — paths along which an object could return to its own past. Gödel took these models to argue that time, as an objective lapse, is an illusion even in worlds physically similar to ours. Other debates concern the "hole argument", which Einstein himself used to explore whether spacetime points have identity independent of the metric field, and the problem of time in attempts to quantise gravity, where the fundamental equation of quantum gravity appears to contain no time variable at all.
