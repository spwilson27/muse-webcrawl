Philosophy of time

Philosophy of space and time

The philosophy of space and time is the branch of philosophy concerned with the nature of space and time: whether they exist independently of the objects and events within them, whether time flows, and whether past and future are as real as the present. It stands at the intersection of metaphysics, epistemology and the philosophy of physics.

Substantivalism and relationism

The classical debate opposes substantivalism, defended by Isaac Newton, on which space and time are entities in their own right — a container within which events occur — and relationism, defended by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, on which spatial and temporal facts reduce to relations among material objects and events. The Newton–Leibniz correspondence, conducted through Samuel Clarke, remains the founding document of the debate, which has been revived in modern form by arguments over the interpretation of general relativity.

The reality of temporal passage

A second debate concerns tense. The A-theory holds that events are objectively past, present or future and that time genuinely passes; the B-theory holds that temporal discourse reduces to tenseless relations of earlier and later, as articulated in J. M. E. McTaggart's 1908 argument for the unreality of time. Closely allied is the choice between presentism, eternalism and the growing-block theory. Modern physics, especially special relativity and its formulation in Minkowski space, is widely taken to favour the B-theory and eternalism, though the inference remains contested.

Other topics

The field also includes the direction of time and its relation to entropy, the possibility of time travel and the paradoxes it raises, the nature of persistence through time (see four-dimensional space), Zeno's paradoxes of motion, and the experience of temporal passage studied by phenomenologists and psychologists.
