Non-Euclidean geometry

Non-Euclidean geometry is any geometry that relaxes or replaces Euclid's parallel postulate. Developed in the nineteenth century by Nikolai Lobachevsky, János Bolyai and Bernhard Riemann, hyperbolic and elliptic geometries showed that Euclid's system was not the unique possible description of space — a result that transformed the philosophy of space and time.

From space to spacetime

Riemann's 1854 lecture "On the Hypotheses Which Lie at the Bases of Geometry" introduced the idea of a manifold of variable curvature and suggested that the geometry of physical space is an empirical question. Half a century later, Einstein used Riemannian geometry to build general relativity, in which spacetime itself is a curved, non-Euclidean (more precisely, pseudo-Riemannian) manifold, and Minkowski space appears as its flat special case. The geometry of time thereby became a branch of physics rather than of pure mathematics or a priori philosophy.

Philosophical consequences

The consistency of non-Euclidean geometries refuted Kant's claim that Euclidean geometry is a synthetic a priori truth about the form of outer experience. It also fuelled conventionalism, associated with Henri Poincaré, who argued that experiments test geometry and physics only in combination, so the geometry of space — and by extension the structure of time — cannot be settled by measurement alone. Debates continue over whether the curved spacetime of general relativity should be interpreted realistically, as a physical substance, or instrumentally, as a mathematical device.
