Philosophy of time

Time's nature, passage and direction are enduring philosophical questions.

The philosophy of time is the branch of philosophy concerned with the nature of time: whether time exists independently of events, whether the past and future are as real as the present, why time seems to flow in one direction, and how time relates to change, causality and human experience. Together with the philosophy of space, it forms the philosophy of space and time.

A-theory and B-theory

Following J. M. E. McTaggart's 1908 argument for the unreality of time, philosophers distinguish the A-series, which orders events as past, present and future, from the B-series, which orders them as earlier than, simultaneous with, and later than. Presentists hold that only present things exist; eternalists hold that past and future events exist just as present ones do, in a four-dimensional "block universe"; the growing block theory holds that the past and present exist but the future does not.

Substantivalism and relationalism

Isaac Newton defended absolute time, flowing uniformly "without relation to anything external." Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz countered that time is merely an order of events — a relational view. The debate, dramatized in the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence, was reframed by Einstein's theory of relativity, which unified space and time into spacetime and made simultaneity relative to the observer.

The arrow of time

Physical laws are mostly time-symmetric, yet time has a conspicuous direction: we remember the past and not the future, causes precede effects, and entropy increases (the thermodynamic arrow). Explaining the alignment of these arrows — and whether temporal passage is a real feature of the world or a feature of human experience — remains a central problem. Augustine of Hippo famously confessed in his Confessions: "What then is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I wish to explain it, I do not know."
