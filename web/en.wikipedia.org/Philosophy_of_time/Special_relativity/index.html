Special relativity

Special relativity is the physical theory of space and time proposed by Albert Einstein in 1905. Built on two postulates — that the laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames, and that the speed of light in vacuum is constant for all observers — it predicts length contraction, time dilation and the relativity of simultaneity. Einstein's paper grew out of dissatisfaction with the asymmetries in the electrodynamics of moving bodies and rendered the luminiferous aether superfluous.

Consequences for the philosophy of time

The relativity of simultaneity is the theory's most philosophically explosive result: observers in relative motion slice spacetime into different sets of simultaneous events, so there is no observer-independent "now" extending across the universe. Formulated geometrically in Minkowski space, this led C. W. Rietdijk (1966) and Hilary Putnam (1967) to argue that the distinction between past, present and future cannot be ontologically fundamental — an argument for eternalism in the philosophy of time. Defenders of presentism have replied by invoking privileged frames, Lorentzian interpretations of the formalism, or a distinction between what exists and what is real relative to a frame.

Time dilation and persistence

Time dilation — confirmed by experiments with unstable particles and transported atomic clocks — shows that elapsed time is path-dependent: twins following different world lines age differently, as in the famous twin paradox. Philosophers debate whether this proves that time is a dimension like space, as the picture of four-dimensional space suggests, or merely that clocks measure different quantities along different trajectories. Einstein generalised the theory a decade later into general relativity, which incorporates gravitation and acceleration.
