Financial contagion

Financial contagion refers to the spread of economic or financial shocks from one market, institution or country to others. The term gained prominence during the Asian financial crisis of 1997–98, when currency and banking crises spread across economies with limited direct trade links, and again during the global financial crisis of 2007–08.

Channels

Contagion can travel through trade links, common lenders, correlated investor portfolios, information effects ("wake-up call" contagion) and balance-sheet interconnections among banks. Herding and liquidity spirals can amplify transmission beyond what fundamentals alone would predict.

Debate

Economists distinguish "true" contagion — transmission in excess of normal interdependence — from mere co-movement. The distinction matters for policy: capital-flow management, macroprudential regulation and international lender-of-last-resort arrangements are all partly motivated by contagion risk.

Examples

Notable episodes include the 1994 Tequila crisis spillovers, the 1998 Russian default and LTCM collapse, the euro-area sovereign debt crisis, and emerging-market sell-offs during the 2013 "taper tantrum".
