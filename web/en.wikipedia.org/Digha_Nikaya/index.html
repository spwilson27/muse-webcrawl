The Dīgha Nikāya (Pāli for "Collection of Long Discourses") is the first of the five collections (nikāyas) of the Sutta Piṭaka, the basket of discourses in the Pāli Canon of Theravāda Buddhism. It contains 34 long discourses attributed to the Buddha and his chief disciples, addressing audiences ranging from kings and brahmins to wandering ascetics, and covering ethics, meditation, doctrine and Buddhist cosmology. Its suttas have close parallels in the Dīrgha Āgama preserved in Chinese translation, indicating a shared early heritage.

Structure and contents

The 34 suttas are arranged in three divisions (vaggas) of roughly equal size:

  • Sīlakkhandha Vagga (Division on Morality, DN 1–13): opens with the Brahmajāla Sutta, a survey of 62 speculative views, and the Sāmaññaphala Sutta, the Buddha's discourse to King Ajātasattu on the fruits of the contemplative life.
  • Mahā Vagga (Great Division, DN 14–23): includes the Mahāpadāna Sutta on past Buddhas, the Mahānidāna Sutta on dependent origination, and the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (DN 16), the longest sutta in the canon, narrating the Buddha's last days, death and the distribution of his relics.
  • Pāṭika Vagga (DN 24–34): includes the Sigālovāda Sutta (DN 31), the Buddha's code of ethics for laypeople, the Saṅgīti Sutta (DN 33), a communal recitation of doctrinal lists, and the Dasuttara Sutta (DN 34).

Notable teachings

Several of Buddhism's most quoted discourses are found here: the parable of the poisoned arrow is echoed in related texts; the Aggañña Sutta (DN 27) offers a satirical origin story of social classes; the Kūṭadanta Sutta redefines sacrifice in ethical terms; and the Mahāsatipaṭṭhāna Sutta (DN 22) is a foundational meditation manual on the four foundations of mindfulness. The collection is especially rich in narrative detail about ancient Indian society, court life and religious debate.

Translations

The Pali Text Society published the collection in three volumes as Dialogues of the Buddha (1899–1921), translated by T. W. Rhys Davids and C. A. F. Rhys Davids. Maurice Walshe's complete modern translation, The Long Discourses of the Buddha (Wisdom Publications, 1987), is now the standard single-volume English edition.
