Marshal of the Empire

Marshal of the Empire (French: Maréchal d'Empire) was a military distinction created by Napoleon Bonaparte during the First French Empire. It was not strictly a military rank, but a title granted to exceptional generals, carrying the highest honours and privileges at court. The role held some resemblance to the Marshal of France under the Ancien Régime, but differed in both political and military functions.

Creation

On 18 May 1804, the day the Empire was proclaimed, Napoleon created the title and named the first eighteen marshals, a mixture of celebrated generals of the Revolutionary Wars and senior officers of the Senate. Eight more were created later, bringing the total to twenty-six over the course of the Empire.

The twenty-six marshals

The marshals included some of the most famous commanders of the Napoleonic Wars: Joachim Murat, Michel Ney, Louis-Nicolas Davout (regarded by many as the most capable), Jean Lannes, André Masséna, Nicolas Soult, Jean Bernadotte (later King Charles XIV John of Sweden), Louis-Alexandre Berthier (Napoleon's chief of staff), and Auguste de Marmont. Davout, Masséna, Soult and Lannes were among those Napoleon considered his best.

Privileges and status

Marshals received enormous incomes, landed estates, and dukedoms and principalities. They addressed Napoleon as "Sire" and were addressed as "Mon cousin" by the Emperor. The marshal's baton, covered in blue velvet with golden eagles, became an enduring symbol of French military glory; the phrase "every French soldier carries a marshal's baton in his knapsack" expressed the meritocratic promise of the revolutionary armies.
