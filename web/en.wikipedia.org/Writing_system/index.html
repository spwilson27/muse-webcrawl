Writing system

A writing system is a method of visually representing verbal communication — a set of symbols (a script) together with rules for their use (an orthography) for a language. Writing systems are conventionally classified by the linguistic unit their basic signs represent: a concept (logography), a syllable (syllabary), a consonant (abjad), a segment or phoneme (alphabet), or combinations of consonants with inherent vowels (abugida).

Types

Logographic systems use signs for morphemes or words; Chinese characters are the outstanding modern example, and historical systems include Egyptian hieroglyphs and cuneiform. Syllabaries, in which each sign denotes a syllable, include Japanese kana and Cherokee. Alphabets represent consonants and vowels with separate letters (Latin, Greek, Cyrillic), while in an abjad (Arabic, Hebrew) vowels are optional or secondary. In an abugida (Devanagari, Ethiopic), consonant letters carry an inherent vowel modified by diacritics. Korean hangul is a featural alphabet whose letter shapes encode articulatory features.

History

Writing was invented independently in Mesopotamia (cuneiform, by c. 3400–3300 BC), Egypt (hieroglyphs), China (oracle bone script) and Mesoamerica (Maya script). The alphabet derives from Proto-Sinaitic signs adapted by Phoenician, from which Greek, and thence Latin and Cyrillic, descend. Direction of writing — left-to-right, right-to-left, or vertical — differs among traditions.

Properties

Orthographies vary in "depth": shallow systems like Finnish or Italian spelling map letters to sounds consistently, while deep systems like English and Chinese require more memorised word-specific knowledge. Writing systems underpin literacy, administration and literature; roughly 300 scripts have existed, of which around 150 remain in use, encoded and standardised today largely through Unicode.
