Goharshad Mosque rebellion

The Goharshad Mosque rebellion was an uprising that took place in August 1935 in Mashhad, Iran, when worshippers and local residents gathered in protest inside the Goharshad Mosque, part of the shrine complex of Imam Reza. The protest, directed against the secularising and Westernising policies of Reza Shah Pahlavi — in particular the new dress laws requiring Western clothing and hats — was violently suppressed by the army, with many killed.

Background

Reza Shah's modernisation programme sought to remake Iranian society along secular, Western lines. Measures in the mid-1930s included a law prescribing Western dress and the European-style (Pahlavi) hat for men, enforced by the police. In Mashhad, a strongly religious city and a centre of pilgrimage, resentment at the dress code, at conscription and at the rising cost of living boiled over in the summer of 1935. A local cleric, Sheikh Mohammad Taqi Bohlol, became the voice of the crowds who occupied the Goharshad Mosque adjoining the Imam Reza shrine.

Suppression

After local police failed or refused to disperse the crowd, regular troops were ordered in. Soldiers opened fire on the mosque courtyard, killing large numbers of protesters; estimates of the dead vary widely, from dozens to several hundred or more, and the event remained a forbidden topic under the Pahlavi monarchy. Bohlol fled to Afghanistan, and several clergy were arrested or exiled.

Legacy

The massacre became one of the emblematic grievances against Reza Shah's rule and is officially commemorated in the Islamic Republic of Iran as an early episode of resistance to imposed Westernisation, foreshadowing the unveiling decree (Kashf-e hijab) of 1936 and the clerical opposition that culminated in the revolution of 1979.
