Detroit techno

Detroit techno is a type of techno music that emerged in Detroit, Michigan, in the United States during the mid-to-late 1980s. It was created by young African-American producers — principally Juan Atkins, Derrick May and Kevin Saunderson, school friends from Belleville sometimes called the "Belleville Three" — who fused the machine funk of Parliament-Funkadelic and Prince with the European electronic sounds of Kraftwerk, Yellow Magic Orchestra and Italo disco, filtered through the futuristic writings of Alvin Toffler, whose term "techno rebels" inspired the genre's name.

Origins

Atkins, recording as Cybotron ("Clear", 1983; "Techno City", 1984) and then Model 500 ("No UFOs", 1985), laid the groundwork on his Metroplex label. May's Rhythim Is Rhythim productions ("Strings of Life", 1987) and Saunderson's work as Inner City with singer Paris Grey ("Big Fun", "Good Life", 1988) carried Detroit techno to Britain, where it fuelled the acid house explosion and the Second Summer of Love. The 1988 UK compilation Techno! The New Dance Sound of Detroit gave the genre its marketing name.

Second wave and beyond

In the early 1990s a second wave — Carl Craig, Jeff Mills, Robert Hood and the Underground Resistance collective formed by Mills, Hood and "Mad" Mike Banks — pushed the music toward harder, minimal and politically charged territory, alongside Richie Hawtin and John Acquaviva's Windsor-based Plus 8 label. Detroit's Movement festival, held at Hart Plaza each Memorial Day weekend since 2000, celebrates the city's electronic heritage. Detroit techno's melancholy machine soul and militant independence deeply influenced European rave, IDM and virtually all subsequent techno.
