Morris worm
Released: 2 November 1988
Author: Robert Tappan Morris
Platform: VAX and Sun systems running BSD Unix
Impact: ~10% of the early Internet

The Morris worm (also called the Internet worm of November 2, 1988) was one of the first computer worms distributed via the Internet, and the first to gain significant mainstream media attention. It was written by Robert Tappan Morris, a graduate student at Cornell University, and released — apparently to disguise its origin — from a computer at MIT.

How it spread

The worm exploited several vulnerabilities: a debugging feature in the sendmail program, a buffer overflow in the finger daemon, trusted hosts via rsh/rexec, and a built-in password-guessing attack using a small dictionary. A programming error in its self-limitation logic — designed to stop multiple copies accumulating — caused the worm to reinfect machines repeatedly, overloading roughly 6,000 computers, an estimated tenth of the Internet at the time, with damages estimated between $100,000 and $10 million.

Aftermath

Morris was the first person tried and convicted under the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act of 1986; he received probation, community service and a fine. The incident led DARPA to fund the creation of the CERT Coordination Center at Carnegie Mellon University, the first computer emergency response team. Morris later co-founded Viaweb with Paul Graham, became a professor at MIT, and was elected to the National Academy of Engineering.
