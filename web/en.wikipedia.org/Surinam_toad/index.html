Surinam toad

The Surinam toad (Pipa pipa) is a species of frog in the family Pipidae, native to northern South America. It is almost entirely aquatic and is famous for its extraordinary reproductive biology: the female carries the eggs embedded in the skin of her back, from which fully formed froglets emerge.

Description

The Surinam toad has an exceptionally flattened, leaf-like body, a triangular head, tiny eyes set high on the head, and no tongue. Its mottled brown-grey colouration camouflages it among leaf litter on the beds of slow-moving streams and flooded forests. The fingertips bear distinctive star-shaped sensory organs, and the hind feet are broadly webbed for swimming.

Reproduction

During mating, the pair perform a series of underwater somersaults in amplexus. As the female releases eggs, the male fertilises them and presses them onto her back, where the skin swells to enclose each egg in its own chamber protected by a horny lid. The young develop entirely within these pockets — bypassing a free-swimming tadpole stage — and after roughly three to four months tiny fully formed toads push out of the mother's back.

Behaviour and diet

The toad is an ambush predator and scavenger, taking invertebrates, small fish and carrion, which it sweeps into its mouth with its long fingers. Like other pipids, it lacks a tongue and relies on suction and forelimb-assisted feeding. It rarely leaves the water.
