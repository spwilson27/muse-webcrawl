Sonar

Sonar (sound navigation and ranging or sonic navigation and ranging) is a technique that uses sound propagation (usually underwater, as in submarine navigation) to navigate, measure distances (ranging), communicate with or detect objects on or under the surface of the water, such as other vessels.

Two kinds of technology share the name "sonar": passive sonar is essentially listening for the sound made by vessels; active sonar is emitting pulses of sounds and listening for echoes. Sonar may be used as a means of acoustic location and of measurement of the echo characteristics of "targets" in the water. The study of underwater sound is known as underwater acoustics or hydroacoustics.

History

The first recorded use of the technique was by Leonardo da Vinci in 1490, who inserted a tube into water to detect vessels by listening. Modern sonar developed rapidly during World War I in response to the submarine threat; the British ASDIC system and American systems were deployed for anti-submarine warfare. The term SONAR was coined in the United States during World War II as an analogue to radar.

Applications

Beyond naval uses — submarine detection, mine hunting, and navigation — sonar is used in commercial fishing (fish finders), bathymetric mapping of the seabed, medical ultrasonography (in air-tissue analogues), and in nature by animals such as bats and dolphins for echolocation. Concerns exist over the impact of powerful naval sonar on marine mammals, including mass strandings of beaked whales.
