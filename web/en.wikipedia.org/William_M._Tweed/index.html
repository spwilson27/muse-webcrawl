William Magear Tweed (April 3, 1823 – April 12, 1878), widely known as "Boss Tweed", was an American politician most notable for being the political boss of Tammany Hall, the Democratic Party political machine that played a major role in the politics of 19th-century New York City and State.

Rise to power

Tweed, the son of a chair-maker, worked as a brush-maker and bookkeeper and was a volunteer fireman before entering politics. He was elected to the U.S. House of Representatives in 1852 and later served on the New York City Board of Supervisors and in the New York State Senate. As head of Tammany Hall and through control of patronage, city contracts and the courts, he came to dominate New York politics by the late 1860s.

The Tweed Ring

At the peak of his influence, Tweed and his associates — the "Tweed Ring" — systematically looted the city treasury, chiefly by inflating the cost of public works. The New York County Courthouse, budgeted at $250,000, ultimately cost more than $13 million. Estimates of the total stolen range from $25 million to $200 million. Exposure by The New York Times and the savage cartoons of Thomas Nast in Harper's Weekly turned public opinion against him.

Downfall

Tweed was arrested in 1871, convicted of forgery and larceny in 1873, and after an escape to Spain was extradited back to New York in 1876. He died in the Ludlow Street Jail in 1878. His name became a byword for municipal corruption and the political "boss".
