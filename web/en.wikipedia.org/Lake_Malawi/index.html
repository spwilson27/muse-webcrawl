Lake Malawi, also known as Lake Nyasa in Tanzania and Lago Niassa in Mozambique, is an African Great Lake and the southernmost lake in the East African Rift system. It is the world's ninth-largest lake by area and third-largest and second-deepest in Africa. It is home to more species of fish than any other lake in the world.

Geography

The lake is about 560 to 580 km long and up to 75 km wide, bordered by Malawi, Mozambique, and Tanzania. Its outlet is the Shire River, which drains south to the Zambezi. The lake is meromictic: its water layers do not mix, with a permanent anoxic layer below about 200 metres.

Biodiversity

Lake Malawi hosts an estimated 850–1,000 species of cichlid fish, nearly all endemic, evolved through one of the most spectacular adaptive radiations among vertebrates. The colourful mbuna (rock-dwelling cichlids) are famous in the aquarium trade. Lake Malawi National Park, at the southern end, was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984.

History

Scottish explorer David Livingstone reached the lake in 1859 during his Zambezi expedition, calling it the "Lake of Stars" for the lantern lights of fishermen seen at night. The boundary between Malawi and Tanzania along the lake has been disputed since colonial times; Tanzania claims the border runs along the middle of the lake, while Malawi claims the eastern shoreline.
