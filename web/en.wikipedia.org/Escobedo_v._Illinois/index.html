Escobedo v. Illinois

Escobedo v. Illinois, 378 U.S. 478 (1964), was a United States Supreme Court case that held that criminal suspects have a right to counsel during police interrogations under the Sixth Amendment. The case was a major precursor to the Court's landmark decision in Miranda v. Arizona (1966), which built on Escobedo's reasoning.

Background

Danny Escobedo was arrested in Chicago in January 1960 in connection with the fatal shooting of his brother-in-law, Manuel Valtierra. During interrogation at police headquarters, Escobedo repeatedly asked to see his retained lawyer, who was present in the station house but was turned away by officers; the lawyer in turn obtained court orders but was still refused access. Escobedo, who had not been advised of his right to remain silent, made incriminating statements after an officer falsely told him that an accomplice had named him as the gunman. He was convicted of murder, and the Illinois Supreme Court affirmed, holding that no constitutional violation had occurred.

Decision

Writing for a 5–4 majority, Justice Arthur Goldberg reversed the conviction. The Court held that where an investigation has begun to focus on a particular suspect in police custody who has requested and been denied his lawyer, and who has not been effectively warned of his absolute right to remain silent, no statement elicited by police during the interrogation may be used against him at trial. Justice Byron White's dissent warned that the decision would seriously impair law enforcement; the case's focus test proved difficult to administer and was effectively superseded two years later by the clearer warning regime of Miranda. The Escobedo rule, with that of Miranda, was later narrowed in its application to pre-indictment settings by Kirby v. Illinois (1972).
