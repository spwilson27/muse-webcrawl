Tuyere

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tuyere (from French tuyère, "pipe, nozzle") is a tube or nozzle through which air is blown into a furnace, forge or smelter. Tuyeres are essential to high-temperature metallurgy because natural draught alone cannot supply enough oxygen to reach the temperatures needed to melt and reduce ores.

In the blast furnace

In a modern iron blast furnace, a ring of water-cooled copper tuyeres — typically twenty or more — is set into the wall of the hearth just above the level of molten iron. Through them the hot blast, air preheated to over 1,000 °C and often enriched with oxygen, pulverised coal or natural gas, is injected at high velocity. Combustion of coke in front of each tuyere creates a cavity called the raceway, the hottest part of the furnace, where temperatures approach 2,000 °C.

History

The simplest tuyeres were clay or stone nozzles attached to bellows in ancient bloomeries and forges; archaeological finds date to the early Iron Age. Metallurgical advances — the water-cooled "tuyere iron" of the nineteenth century and the hot blast patented by James Beaumont Neilson in 1828 — made larger and more efficient furnaces possible. Related devices serve glass-melting furnaces, cupolas in foundries and the bottom-blown converters of steelmaking.
