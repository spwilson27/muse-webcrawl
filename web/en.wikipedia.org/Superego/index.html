Superego

In psychoanalytic theory, the superego is one of three agencies of the psyche in Sigmund Freud's structural model, alongside the id and the ego. The superego is the component of personality that holds internalised moral standards, ideals and values acquired from parents and society; it acts as a kind of conscience that inhibits the impulses of the id and persuades the ego to pursue moralistic rather than merely realistic goals.

Freud's account

Freud introduced the structural model in The Ego and the Id (1923). The superego develops during childhood through the resolution of the Oedipus complex, by identification with the parents and internalisation of their prohibitions. It continues to be shaped by teachers, authority figures and cultural norms.

Functions

Freud assigned the superego two main aspects. The conscience punishes misdeeds with feelings of guilt and anxiety, while the ego ideal rewards good behaviour with pride and self-esteem. The superego strives for perfection, judging thoughts as well as actions, and much of its operation is unconscious.

Relation to id and ego

The id demands immediate gratification of instinctual drives; the superego demands moral perfection; the ego mediates between them and the demands of reality. An overly harsh superego was linked by Freud to neurotic guilt, melancholia and obsessive symptoms, while a weak superego was associated with delinquency and lack of inhibition.

Criticism

Later psychologists and philosophers have criticised the superego concept as unfalsifiable and culturally specific, though it remains influential in psychodynamic thought and in everyday conceptions of conscience.
