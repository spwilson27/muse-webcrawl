The Omnivore's Dilemma: A Natural History of Four Meals is a nonfiction book by American author Michael Pollan, published in 2006. Taking its title from the concept coined by psychologist Paul Rozin — the omnivore's dilemma of choosing what to eat when almost anything is edible — the book traces four meals back through the food chains that produced them, and examines how Americans decide what to eat amid abundance, industry marketing, and competing ethical claims.

Contents

The book is organized around four meals: an industrial meal (a McDonald's lunch eaten in a car, built on corn and fossil fuels, including a steer raised in a feedlot); an industrial organic meal (from large-scale organic producers such as Earthbound Farm and Whole Foods); a local, sustainable meal at Joel Salatin's Polyface Farm in Virginia, where rotational grazing links grass, cattle, chickens, and soil fertility; and a self-foraged meal of hunted wild pig and gathered mushrooms in California. Corn — subsidized, overproduced, and present in most processed foods — threads through the first sections as a central character.

Themes

Pollan argues that the industrial food system externalizes its ecological and health costs, and he contrasts it with alternative systems that mimic natural ecosystems. The book popularized terms and ideas such as "nutritional science's reductionism" and helped inspire Pollan's later maxim: "Eat food. Not too much. Mostly plants."

Reception and influence

The Omnivore's Dilemma was named one of the ten best books of 2006 by The New York Times and The Washington Post and received the James Beard Award. It is widely credited with galvanizing the modern local-food and food-reform movements. A young readers' edition appeared in 2009, and the book became a common college text. Agricultural industry groups criticized some of its claims about industrial farming and organic agriculture.
