A pine is any conifer in the genus Pinus of the family Pinaceae, the sole genus in the subfamily Pinoideae. With roughly 115 to 120 species, pines are among the most widespread and ecologically important trees of the Northern Hemisphere, ranging from the boreal forests of Siberia and Canada to mountain and Mediterranean habitats, and widely planted in the Southern Hemisphere for timber.

Description

Pines are evergreen, resinous trees (rarely shrubs) whose needle-like leaves grow in clusters called fascicles of two, three or five, a key identifying feature. They are monoecious, producing separate male and female cones; the female cones are woody and often armed, maturing over two or three years. Some species have serotinous cones that open only after fire. Pines include some of the oldest and tallest known trees: bristlecone pines in the White Mountains of California approach 5,000 years of age, and sugar pines exceed 80 metres.

Ecology and uses

Pines dominate vast forests, provide habitat and food—seeds eaten by birds and mammals—and are foundational to many fire-adapted ecosystems. Economically they are among the world's most important timber trees, yielding softwood lumber, pulp, plywood, resin, turpentine and tar, as well as edible pine nuts. Many species are grown as ornamentals and Christmas trees, and pines are prominent in art and symbolism, notably in East Asian painting, where the pine signifies longevity and endurance.
