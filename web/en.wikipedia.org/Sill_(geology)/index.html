Sill (geology)

In geology, a sill is a tabular sheet intrusion that has intruded between older layers of sedimentary rock, beds of volcanic lava, or tuff, or along foliation in metamorphic rock. The term means a layer of rock that is concordant — parallel to the bedding planes of the surrounding (country) rock — in contrast to a dike, which cuts discordantly across bedding.

Formation and features

Sills form when magma, often basaltic and fluid, exploits planes of weakness between strata at shallow depth; the pressure of the overlying rock keeps the intrusion horizontal. Thickness ranges from centimetres to hundreds of metres, and sills may extend for tens or hundreds of kilometres. Both upper and lower contacts are baked (contact-metamorphosed). Widely known examples include the Palisades Sill along the Hudson River in New York and New Jersey, the Great Whin Sill of northern England (underlying Hadrian's Wall in places), and the great sill complexes of the Karoo Basin in South Africa and the Siberian Traps.

Distinguishing features

Because sills lie parallel to strata, they can be mistaken for lava flows. The key tests are that a sill bakes the rock both above and below it (a lava flow bakes only what lies beneath), may include xenoliths of the overlying beds, and lacks the vesicular (bubbly) upper surface typical of flows.

Economic importance

Some of the world's richest ore deposits are associated with sill-like layered intrusions: the nickel–copper–platinum-group deposits of the Bushveld Complex (lopolith) in South Africa, Norilsk in Russia, and Sudbury in Canada were fed by vast magma systems of which sills form an integral part.
