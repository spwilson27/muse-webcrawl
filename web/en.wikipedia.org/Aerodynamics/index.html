Aerodynamics (from Greek ἀήρ aero, "air", and δυναμική, "dynamics") is the study of the motion of air, particularly when affected by a solid object, such as an aeroplane wing. It involves topics covered in the fields of fluid dynamics and gas dynamics, and is important in the design of aircraft, vehicles, wind turbines, buildings and sports equipment.

Forces and flow

A body moving through air experiences aerodynamic force resolved into lift (perpendicular to the flow) and drag (parallel to it). Lift on a wing is explained by the circulation theory of Kutta and Zhukovsky and, equivalently, by pressure differences between surfaces; the lift coefficient depends strongly on angle of attack until stall, when flow separation causes lift to collapse. Drag comprises skin-friction drag, pressure (form) drag, induced drag from trailing vortices, and, at high speed, wave drag from shock waves.

Regimes

Flow is classified by Mach number: incompressible (below Mach 0.3), subsonic, transonic (Mach 0.8–1.2, with mixed subsonic and supersonic flow), supersonic, and hypersonic (Mach 5+), where heating and real-gas effects dominate. The Reynolds number characterises the balance of inertial and viscous forces and determines whether boundary layers are laminar or turbulent.

History and methods

Newton developed an early (inaccurate) theory of air resistance; the modern discipline grew with the work of d'Alembert, Euler, Navier and Stokes on fluid equations, and with 19th-century experimenters such as Otto Lilienthal. The Wright brothers' wind-tunnel tests enabled powered flight in 1903; Prandtl's boundary-layer theory (1904) and thin-aerofoil and lifting-line theories followed. Today computational fluid dynamics — solving the Navier–Stokes equations numerically — complements wind tunnels and flight testing in nearly every aerospace programme.
