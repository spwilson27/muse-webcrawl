Listeriosis is a bacterial infection caused by Listeria monocytogenes, a Gram-positive rod that is widespread in soil, water and animal faeces. Human infection is almost always foodborne, acquired from foods such as unpasteurised milk and soft cheeses, deli meats, hot dogs, refrigerated pâtés and smoked seafood, and raw sprouts. Unusually among foodborne pathogens, the organism grows at refrigerator temperatures, and it can cross the intestinal barrier, the blood–brain barrier and the placenta.

Signs and symptoms

In healthy adults, infection usually causes a mild, self-limited febrile gastroenteritis — or no symptoms — after a short incubation. Invasive listeriosis, by contrast, is a severe disease of pregnant women, neonates, the elderly and immunocompromised people, with a long incubation (up to 70 days). It presents most often as bacteraemia or meningoencephalitis; the case-fatality rate of invasive disease is high, commonly cited at 20–30%. In pregnancy the mother may have only flu-like illness while the fetus suffers catastrophic infection, causing miscarriage, stillbirth or neonatal sepsis (granulomatosis infantiseptica).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by culture of the organism from normally sterile sites such as blood or cerebrospinal fluid; it may be mistaken for diphtheroids and dismissed as a contaminant. Treatment is high-dose intravenous ampicillin, often combined with gentamicin; trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole is the alternative for penicillin-allergic patients. Cephalosporins are ineffective, a well-known pitfall in empirical meningitis regimens for at-risk patients.

Epidemiology and prevention

Listeriosis is relatively uncommon (around 0.1–10 cases per million people per year in various countries) but causes a disproportionate share of foodborne deaths. Notable outbreaks have been traced to cantaloupes (the 2011 Jensen Farms outbreak in the United States, 33 deaths), caramel apples, ice cream, frozen vegetables, and processed meats in South Africa in 2017–18 — the largest recorded outbreak, with over 1,000 cases. Prevention advice for high-risk groups is to avoid unpasteurised dairy and soft cheeses, heat deli meats until steaming, and observe use-by dates and fridge hygiene.
