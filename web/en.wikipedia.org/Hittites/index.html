The Hittites were an Anatolian people who established one of the great powers of the Late Bronze Age, an empire centred on Hattusa in north-central Anatolia (modern Turkey) from about 1650 BC until its collapse around 1180 BC. Their language, Hittite, is the earliest historically attested member of the Indo-European language family, written in cuneiform on thousands of clay tablets recovered at Hattusa.

History

The Hittite Old Kingdom (c. 1650–1500 BC) was founded by Hattusili I; his successor Mursili I famously sacked Babylon in 1595 BC. After a weaker Middle Kingdom period, Suppiluliuma I (c. 1344–1322 BC) built the New Kingdom (Empire period) into a superpower rivalling Egypt and Assyria, dominating Syria and the Levant. His successors fought Egypt at the Battle of Kadesh (1274 BC), one of the largest chariot battles ever fought, after which Hattusili III concluded the earliest surviving international peace treaty with Ramesses II (1259 BC) — a copy of which hangs at the United Nations headquarters. Around 1180 BC the empire collapsed amid the Late Bronze Age collapse, famine and the Sea Peoples; smaller "Neo-Hittite" states in south-eastern Anatolia and northern Syria survived into the 8th century BC.

Society and culture

Hittite law codes, notably milder than their Near Eastern contemporaries in favouring compensation over corporal punishment, royal annals, myths and state treaties survive in the Hattusa archives (some 30,000 tablets). Religion was polytheistic and absorptive — "the thousand gods of Hatti" — headed by the storm god Tarhun and the sun goddess of Arinna. The Hittites were early masters of ironworking, though the extent of a supposed iron monopoly is debated. Knowledge of the empire was lost after antiquity until its rediscovery in the 19th–20th centuries through Egyptian and Assyrian records, the Amarna letters, and Bedřich Hrozný's decipherment of Hittite in 1915–17.
