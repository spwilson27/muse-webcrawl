Electronic band structure

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In solid-state physics, the electronic band structure (or simply band structure) of a solid describes the ranges of energy that an electron in the material may have (the energy bands) and the ranges it may not (the band gaps). Band structure arises because the discrete energy levels of isolated atoms broaden into quasi-continuous bands when atoms are assembled into a crystal, and its details — determined by solving the Schrödinger equation for electrons in the periodic potential of the lattice — explain most electrical and optical properties of materials.

Key concepts

Bloch's theorem shows that electron wavefunctions in a periodic lattice are plane waves modulated by a lattice-periodic function, labelled by a crystal momentum (wave vector k) within the Brillouin zone. Plotting energy versus k along high-symmetry directions gives the familiar band diagrams. The highest occupied band at absolute zero is the valence band, the lowest unoccupied band is the conduction band, and the energy of the highest occupied state defines the Fermi level.

Metals, insulators, and semiconductors

If the Fermi level falls within a partially filled band, the material is a metal: electrons can respond to fields with arbitrarily small energy changes. If it falls in a large band gap, the material is an insulator. A small gap (roughly below ~3 eV) makes a semiconductor, in which thermal energy or doping can move carriers into the conduction band or leave holes in the valence band. Whether the valence-band maximum and conduction-band minimum occur at the same k (a direct gap, as in GaAs) or at different k (an indirect gap, as in silicon) determines the strength of optical absorption and emission, hence uses in LEDs and lasers versus electronics.

Calculation and measurement

Band structures are computed with methods ranging from the nearly free electron and tight-binding approximations to first-principles density functional theory. Experimentally they are measured by angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES), optical spectroscopy, and transport experiments.
