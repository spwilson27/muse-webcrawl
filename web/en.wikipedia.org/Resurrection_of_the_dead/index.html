Resurrection of the dead

The resurrection of the dead is the belief that deceased people will be restored to life, commonly in connection with a final judgment and transformed world. It is a major doctrine in Christianity and Islam and appears in forms of Judaism and Zoroastrianism. It differs from reincarnation because the same person is ordinarily understood to receive renewed embodied life rather than begin an unrelated life.

Judaism

Ancient Israelite religion often portrayed the dead as dwelling in Sheol, but later texts express hope for divine restoration. Daniel 12 speaks of many who sleep in the dust awakening to everlasting life or contempt. Rabbinic Judaism made resurrection one of the principles associated with the world to come, although interpretations differed over its timing, scope, and physical character.

Christianity

Christian belief centres on the resurrection of Jesus and expects a general resurrection. In 1 Corinthians 15, Paul contrasts the perishable present body with an imperishable “spiritual body.” Historic creeds affirm “the resurrection of the body” and life everlasting. Traditions debate whether resurrection occurs at death, at Christ's return, before a millennium, or through stages, and how personal identity can persist through bodily transformation.

Islam

The Qur'an teaches that God will raise the dead for judgment on the Day of Resurrection, Yawm al-Qiyāmah. Bodily resurrection demonstrates divine power and precedes accountability for earthly deeds. Islamic theology has discussed whether resurrection involves the original body, a re-created body, or both.

Philosophical questions

Debates concern material continuity, duplication, decomposition, and whether identity depends on body, soul, memory, or divine reconstitution. Some modern theologians interpret resurrection chiefly as bodily renewal, while others emphasise symbolic or existential meanings, but the traditional doctrine is an eschatological act of God rather than a naturally achievable resuscitation.
