Julia Domna

Julia Domna (c. 160 – 217 CE) was a Syrian-born Roman empress, wife of the emperor Septimius Severus and mother of the emperors Caracalla and Geta. One of the most powerful women in Roman history, she held the titles Augusta and "mother of the camp" (mater castrorum), accompanied her husband and son on military campaigns, administered the empire's correspondence, and patronised a celebrated circle of Greek writers and philosophers.

Early life and marriage

Julia Domna was born in Emesa, Syria, into an Arab family of priest-kings of the sun god Elagabal. Her father, Julius Bassianus, was high priest of the temple of Elagabal. Around 187 she married Septimius Severus, then governor of Gallia Lugdunensis; later tradition held that Severus sought her out because her horoscope promised she would marry a king. She bore two sons: Caracalla (born 188) and Geta (born 189).

Empress

When Severus seized the throne in 193, Julia Domna became Augusta and an unusually public partner in power. She accompanied him on campaigns in Britain (208–211) and the East, received an expanding string of honorific titles — including mater castrorum et senatus et patriae, "mother of the camp, the senate, and the fatherland" — and was depicted with the emperor on coins and monuments throughout the empire. After Severus's death at York in 211, she mediated between her co-emperor sons, but Caracalla murdered Geta — according to tradition, in her presence — in December 211.

Later years and death

Under Caracalla, Julia Domna's role grew still further: she managed the imperial correspondence and received petitions while the emperor campaigned. She gathered around her a circle of sophists and philosophers, and it was at her request, according to tradition, that Philostratus wrote the Life of Apollonius of Tyana. When Caracalla was assassinated in Mesopotamia in April 217 and Macrinus seized power, she died at Antioch — some sources say she starved herself upon hearing the news. She was deified, and her remains were eventually placed in the Mausoleum of Hadrian. Her sister Julia Maesa would restore the family to power in 218.
