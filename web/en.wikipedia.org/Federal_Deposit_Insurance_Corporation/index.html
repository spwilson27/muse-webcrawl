Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) is a United States government corporation that supplies deposit insurance to depositors in American commercial banks and savings banks. It was created by the Banking Act of 1933 (the Glass–Steagall Act) in response to the thousands of bank failures that occurred during the Great Depression. The FDIC insures deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category.

History

Between 1930 and 1933 roughly 9,000 American banks failed, wiping out the savings of millions of depositors. Deposit insurance had been opposed by the American Bankers Association and by President Franklin D. Roosevelt himself, but Congress included it in the Banking Act of 1933, championed by Representative Henry B. Steagall. Insurance began on January 1, 1934, initially covering $2,500 per depositor. Bank failures fell dramatically thereafter. The coverage limit was raised in stages, reaching $100,000 in 1980 and $250,000 in 2008, a figure made permanent by the Dodd–Frank Act of 2010.

Functions

The FDIC is funded by insurance premiums paid by banks, not by congressional appropriations. It examines and supervises state-chartered banks that are not members of the Federal Reserve System, acts as receiver for failed banks, and resolves failures in the manner least costly to the Deposit Insurance Fund. During the 2008 financial crisis and the regional banking failures of 2023, the FDIC arranged purchase-and-assumption transactions and invoked the systemic risk exception to protect uninsured depositors at failed institutions.
