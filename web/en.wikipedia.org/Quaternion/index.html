In mathematics, the quaternions are a number system that extends the complex numbers. They were first described by the Irish mathematician William Rowan Hamilton in 1843 and applied to mechanics in three-dimensional space. A quaternion is written as a + bi + cj + dk, where a, b, c, d are real numbers and i, j, k are the basic quaternion units.

The defining feature of quaternions is that multiplication is not commutative: in general pq ≠ qp. The units obey Hamilton's rules:

History

Hamilton had long sought a three-dimensional analogue of the complex numbers. On 16 October 1843, walking along the Royal Canal in Dublin with his wife, he realized that the answer required four dimensions and that commutativity had to be abandoned. He famously carved the fundamental formula into the stone of Brougham Bridge with his penknife. Quaternions became hugely influential in 19th-century physics and geometry — Maxwell's equations were first written in quaternion form — before being largely displaced by the vector analysis of Gibbs and Heaviside. Hamilton founded a school of "quaternionists," and Peter Tait championed the system in Britain.

Properties

The quaternions form a four-dimensional associative normed division algebra over the real numbers. Every nonzero quaternion has a multiplicative inverse, so division is always possible, but the order of factors matters. Each quaternion has a conjugate, a + bi + cj + dk → a − bi − cj − dk, and a norm equal to a2 + b2 + c2 + d2. By the Frobenius theorem, the quaternions are, together with the real and complex numbers, one of only three finite-dimensional associative division algebras over the reals.

Quaternions and spatial rotation

Unit quaternions provide a compact, elegant way to represent rotations in three-dimensional space. A rotation through an angle θ about a unit axis u is encoded by cos(θ/2) + sin(θ/2)(uxi + uyj + uzk), and combining rotations corresponds to multiplying quaternions. Unlike Euler angles, quaternion interpolation avoids gimbal lock, which makes unit quaternions the standard tool for orientation in 3D computer graphics, robotics, spacecraft attitude control, and computer vision.
