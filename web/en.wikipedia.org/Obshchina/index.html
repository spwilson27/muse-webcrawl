Obshchina

Obshchina (Russian: общи́на, IPA: [ɐpˈɕːinə], literally "commune") or mir (мир, literally "world" or "peace") was a peasant village community in the Russian Empire. The term describes the communal organisation of the peasantry, in which arable land was held in common and periodically redistributed among households according to family size or the number of workers.

Organisation

The commune was governed by an assembly (skhod) of household heads, which allocated strips of land among the village's three open fields, organised crop rotation, apportioned taxes and recruits for military service, and exercised considerable control over its members, including issuing internal passports. After the emancipation of the serfs in 1861, the commune became the state's instrument for collecting redemption payments and taxes under collective responsibility (krugovaya poruka).

Historical debate

The obshchina was celebrated by Slavophiles and Russian populists (narodniki) as an indigenous, egalitarian institution that might allow Russia to bypass capitalism on a path to socialism, an idea Karl Marx discussed in his correspondence with Vera Zasulich. Critics, including later liberal historians, regarded periodic repartition as an obstacle to agricultural improvement and investment.

Decline

The Stolypin reforms of 1906–1911 sought to dissolve the commune by allowing peasants to claim their shares as private property. Although many households withdrew, the commune survived into the Soviet era, when it was superseded by the collective farm (kolkhoz).
