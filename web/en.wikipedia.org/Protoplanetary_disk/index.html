Protoplanetary disk

A protoplanetary disk is a rotating circumstellar disc of dense gas and dust surrounding a young newly formed star, a T Tauri star, or a Herbig Ae/Be star. The protoplanetary disk may also be considered an accretion disk for the star itself, because gases or other material may be falling from the inner edge of the disk onto the surface of the star. It is within these disks that planets form.

Protoplanetary disk
Composition~99% gas (H, He), ~1% dust
Typical radiusTens to hundreds of AU
Lifetime~1–10 million years
Notable exampleHL Tauri disk (ALMA image)

Formation and evolution

Disks form as a natural consequence of angular momentum conservation when a molecular cloud core collapses to form a star. Initially large and massive, the disk evolves through viscosity-driven accretion onto the star, photoevaporation by stellar radiation, and the growth of dust grains into pebbles, planetesimals, and eventually planets, which carve rings and gaps like those imaged by ALMA around HL Tauri.

Dispersal

Most disks disperse within about 10 million years. The disappearance of the gas disk sets the deadline for giant-planet formation, which must accrete their gaseous envelopes before the gas is gone.

Relation to the Solar System

The Solar System is believed to have formed from such a disk — the solar nebula — around the young Sun about 4.6 billion years ago, a picture developed from Kant's and Laplace's nebular hypothesis.
