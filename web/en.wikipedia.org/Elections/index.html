An election is a formal group decision-making process by which a population chooses individuals to hold public office, or to represent them in a legislature or assembly. Elections are the standard mechanism by which modern representative democracies have operated since the 17th century, and they are also used by many private organisations, from clubs and unions to corporations choosing directors.

Process

An election normally involves a defined electorate, the registration of voters and candidates, a campaign period, voting (by secret ballot in democratic systems) and the counting and certification of results. Ballots may be cast in person at polling stations, by post, or electronically, and the count is governed by an electoral system that translates votes into outcomes: majoritarian rules such as first-past-the-post, proportional representation by party list, or preferential systems such as the single transferable vote and ranked-choice voting.

Integrity

For an election to be considered "free and fair" it generally requires universal suffrage, an impartial electoral administration, freedom of speech and association, access to plural media, and remedies against fraud, vote buying and intimidation. International and domestic observer missions commonly monitor elections, and election science (psephology) studies results, turnout and voting behaviour.

History

Voting elected magistrates in classical Athens and Rome, and in the Indian republics of the first millennium BCE. Modern electoral democracy spread with the extension of the franchise — to working-class men, then to women in the early 20th century, and to younger voters — and remains the cornerstone of democratic legitimacy, though authoritarian states also stage elections without genuine competition.
