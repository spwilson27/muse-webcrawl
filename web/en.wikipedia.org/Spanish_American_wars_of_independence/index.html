Spanish American wars of independence

LocationSpanish America
Key leadersSimón Bolívar, José de San Martín, Miguel Hidalgo, Agustín de Iturbide
ResultIndependence of most of Spanish America; Spain retains Cuba and Puerto Rico

The Spanish American wars of independence (1808–1833) were numerous wars and revolutions in Spanish America against Spain, against the background of the Napoleonic invasion of the Iberian Peninsula. They destroyed Spain's American empire and created the independent states of Latin America, from Mexico to Argentina and Chile.

Causes

Criollo (American-born Spanish) elites resented peninsular privilege, trade restrictions and Bourbon reform taxation. Enlightenment ideas, the example of the United States and the Haitian Revolution, and resentment of colonial monopolies built pressure through the eighteenth century. The crisis came in 1808 when Napoleon deposed the Spanish Bourbons; colonial juntas claiming to govern for Ferdinand VII became the seedbeds of outright independence movements from 1810.

Course of the wars

In Mexico, Miguel Hidalgo's 1810 uprising began a decade of insurgency ended by Agustín de Iturbide's Plan of Iguala (1821). In South America, Simón Bolívar liberated Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru and Bolivia in a series of campaigns including the 1819 crossing of the Andes and the battles of Boyacá and Carabobo. From the south, José de San Martín crossed the Andes in 1817 to free Chile, then sailed to Peru, declaring its independence in 1821. The patriot victory at Ayacucho (1824), under Antonio José de Sucre, destroyed the last great royalist army in South America; the royalist holdout at Callao fell in 1826, though Spain did not recognise the new states for years.

Consequences

The wars were devastating civil conflicts as much as wars of liberation: economies were wrecked, populations fell, and the liberated territories fragmented into separate republics rather than the federations Bolívar dreamed of. Caudillo rule, militarisation and political instability marked the new states, while Britain and the United States — through trade and the Monroe Doctrine (1823) — became the region's dominant external influences. Cuba and Puerto Rico remained Spanish until 1898.
