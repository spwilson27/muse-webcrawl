Tampon

A tampon is a menstrual product designed to absorb blood and vaginal secretions during menstruation by insertion into the vagina. Unlike a menstrual pad, it is worn internally. Modern tampons are typically made of rayon, cotton, or a blend, compressed into a cylindrical shape, often with a plastic or cardboard applicator and a withdrawal string.

History

Absorbent vaginal plugs have been used since antiquity — ancient Egyptian sources describe softened papyrus tampons. The modern applicator tampon was patented by Dr. Earle Haas in 1931 and marketed as Tampax from 1936. Digital (non-applicator) tampons were developed in Germany in the 1940s by Dr. Judith Esser-Mittag for the o.b. brand. In the United States tampons are regulated as medical devices by the FDA, with standardized absorbency ratings.

Health

Tampon use is associated with a small risk of toxic shock syndrome (TSS), a rare but serious bacterial illness linked to Staphylococcus aureus toxins. Risk rose sharply around 1980 with ultra-absorbent synthetic tampons (notably Rely), which were withdrawn; standardized absorbency labelling and guidance to use the lowest effective absorbency and change tampons every 4–8 hours followed, and TSS incidence fell substantially.

Environmental impact

Conventional tampons with plastic applicators contribute to plastic waste and are sometimes flushed, entering waterways. Reusable alternatives include menstrual cups and period underwear; organic cotton and applicator-free products are marketed as lower-impact options. Efforts to reduce "period poverty" have led several jurisdictions to remove sales taxes on menstrual products or provide them free in schools.
