Candle

A candle is an ignitable wick embedded in wax, or another flammable solid substance such as tallow, that provides light, and in some cases, a fragrance. A candle can also provide heat or a method of keeping time. A person who makes candles is traditionally known as a chandler.

History

Candles have been used as a source of light and to illuminate celebrations for more than 5,000 years. Early candles were made from tallow, beeswax, spermaceti, and plant waxes such as bayberry and candelilla; the ancient Romans made dipped candles from rendered animal fat, and candles of whale fat were used in China during the Qin dynasty. In the 19th century, stearin and then paraffin wax — a by-product of petroleum refining — made candles cheap enough for everyday use. The introduction of gas and electric lighting in the late 19th and early 20th centuries ended the candle's role as a primary light source in the developed world.

Construction and burning

Modern candles typically consist of paraffin or soy wax around a braided cotton wick. When lit, the flame melts wax near the wick; capillary action draws the liquid fuel upward, where it vaporises and burns. The bright yellow part of the flame results from incandescent soot particles, and its teardrop shape is caused by convection — in microgravity, candle flames are spherical and blue.

Uses

Candles remain common for religious ceremonies, commemorations and vigils, birthday cakes, emergency lighting during power failures, and decorative and scented home products. Candles are also a significant cause of household fires when left unattended.
