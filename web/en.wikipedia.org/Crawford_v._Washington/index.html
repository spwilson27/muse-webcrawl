Crawford v. Washington

CourtSupreme Court of the United States
DecidedMarch 8, 2004
Citation541 U.S. 36
AuthorJustice Antonin Scalia
IssueConfrontation Clause; testimonial hearsay

Crawford v. Washington, 541 U.S. 36 (2004), is a landmark United States Supreme Court decision on the Confrontation Clause of the Sixth Amendment. The Court held that testimonial out-of-court statements by a witness who does not appear at trial are inadmissible unless the witness is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine the witness.

Background

Michael Crawford stabbed a man who had allegedly attempted to rape his wife, Sylvia. At trial, the prosecution played Sylvia's tape-recorded statement to police describing the stabbing, although she did not testify because of the state's marital privilege. Crawford objected that admitting the statement violated his right to confront witnesses against him. The Washington courts admitted it under the reliability framework of Ohio v. Roberts (1980).

Decision

Writing for the Court, Justice Scalia undertook an historical analysis — including the 1554 treason trial of Sir Walter Raleigh, conducted partly by affidavit — and concluded that the Confrontation Clause is directed at "testimonial" statements. Reliability alone, he held, is not a substitute for cross-examination; the decision overruled the Roberts framework for testimonial hearsay and reversed Crawford's conviction.

Legacy

Crawford reshaped hearsay practice in criminal cases and generated a line of successor decisions, including Davis v. Washington (2006), Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts (2009), and Michigan v. Bryant (2011), refining when statements are "testimonial".
