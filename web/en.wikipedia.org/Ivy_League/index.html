Ivy League

The Ivy League is an American collegiate athletic conference comprising eight private research universities in the Northeastern United States. The term also denotes the member schools as a group, connoting academic excellence, selectivity, and social prestige. The members are Brown University, Columbia University, Cornell University, Dartmouth College, Harvard University, Princeton University, the University of Pennsylvania, and Yale University.

History

The eight schools — seven of them founded in the colonial era — have long athletic rivalries, most famously the Harvard–Yale football game ("The Game", first played 1875). The name "Ivy League" was used by sportswriters from the 1930s; the presidents formalized an "Ivy Group" for football in 1945 and extended it to all sports in 1954. The league notably does not award athletic scholarships, competing instead on academic admissions standards.

Members

Harvard (1636) and Yale (1701) are the oldest members; Penn (1740), Brown (1764), Columbia (1754), Princeton (1746), and Dartmouth (1769) are also colonial foundations, while Cornell (1865) is the youngest and the only member with public (land-grant) colleges. Together they are among the wealthiest universities in the world, Harvard's endowment being the largest of any academic institution.

Perception and influence

"Ivy League" has become a byword for elite education; the schools regularly top national rankings, admit only a small fraction of applicants, and have educated numerous U.S. presidents, Supreme Court justices, and Nobel laureates, alongside ongoing debate about their role in social stratification.
