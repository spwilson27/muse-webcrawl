A Barr body (named after discoverer Murray Barr) is the inactive X chromosome in a cell with more than one X chromosome, rendered inactive in a process called lyonization (after Mary Lyon), in species with XY sex-determination (including humans). The Lyon hypothesis states that in cells with multiple X chromosomes, all but one are inactivated during mammalian embryogenesis.

The Barr body appears as a small, dense, dark-staining mass at the periphery of the nucleus in interphase cells. In humans and other placental mammals, the number of Barr bodies in a cell is one less than the total number of X chromosomes (the "n − 1 rule").

Discovery

Murray Barr and Ewart Bertram first identified the structure in 1949 while studying neurons in cats, noticing a sex difference in nuclear morphology: the dense body was present in females but absent in males. In 1959 Susumu Ohno showed that the Barr body was a single X chromosome, and in 1961 Mary Lyon proposed that this chromosome was genetically inactivated.

Mechanism

X-inactivation is initiated by the long non-coding RNA Xist, which coats the chromosome from which it is transcribed and recruits silencing complexes that condense it into heterochromatin. The choice of which X is inactivated is random in the early embryo of placental mammals, so females are mosaics of cells expressing one or the other X chromosome. Once inactivated, the same X remains inactive in all descendants of that cell.

A small number of genes, mainly in the pseudoautosomal regions, escape inactivation and remain expressed from the Barr body.

Applications

Barr body counts were historically used in sex testing, including in buccal smears and hair-root cells. The test counts the maximum number of Barr bodies in a sample: for example, an individual with Klinefelter syndrome (XXY) has one Barr body, while a normal female (XX) also has one and a normal male (XY) has none.
