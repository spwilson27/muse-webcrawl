Mean curvature

In differential geometry, the mean curvature H of a surface is an extrinsic measure of curvature: at each point it is the average of the two principal curvatures, κ1 and κ2.

H = (κ1 + κ2) / 2

The concept was introduced by Sophie Germain in her work on elasticity (1821 memoir), and it generalises to hypersurfaces in higher dimensions as the trace of the shape operator (second fundamental form).

Examples

A sphere of radius r has constant mean curvature 1/r (or −1/r, depending on orientation). A plane has H = 0 everywhere. A cylinder of radius r has principal curvatures 1/r and 0, hence H = 1/(2r).

Minimal surfaces

A surface with identically zero mean curvature is a minimal surface: soap films spanning wire frames minimise area and therefore take this form, as in the catenoid and helicoid. Mean curvature is related to the variation of area: a surface is a critical point of area under volume constraint exactly when its mean curvature is constant, which is why soap bubbles are spherical. The mean curvature flow, in which a surface evolves in the direction of its curvature, is a major topic in geometric analysis.
