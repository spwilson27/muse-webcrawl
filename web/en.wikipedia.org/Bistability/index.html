Bistability

In a dynamical system, bistability means the system has two stable equilibrium states. A bistable structure can rest in either of two states, and transitions between them occur when the system receives sufficient input energy. Bistability is a key concept in many fields, including electronics (where a bistable circuit, or flip-flop, stores one bit of information), optics, mechanics and cell biology, where bistable switches govern processes such as cell differentiation and the cell cycle.

A double-well potential: the ball can rest in either of two stable minima.

Mathematical description

Bistability arises in one-dimensional systems described by a potential with two minima separated by a maximum, and in systems of equations whose phase portrait contains two stable fixed points separated by an unstable one, often a saddle. As a control parameter varies, the two stable states can be created or destroyed in a saddle-node bifurcation. Bistable systems commonly display hysteresis: the state depends not only on the current parameter values but on the system's history.

Examples

  • The flip-flop circuit in digital electronics, with two stable output states.
  • The lac operon and other gene-regulatory networks that behave as toggle switches in synthetic biology.
  • Bistable perception in psychology, as in the Necker cube, where perception alternates between two interpretations.
  • Ferromagnetic materials, which retain one of two magnetization states.
