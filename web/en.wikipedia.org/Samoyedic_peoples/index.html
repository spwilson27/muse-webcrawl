Samoyedic peoples

RegionsNorthern Siberia, Russia
LanguagesSamoyedic languages (Uralic family)
GroupsNenets, Enets, Nganasan, Selkup, Kamasins (historical), Mators (extinct)
PopulationRoughly 50,000–60,000 in total
Traditional livelihoodReindeer herding, hunting, fishing

The Samoyedic peoples are a group of closely related Indigenous peoples of northern Siberia who speak the Samoyedic branch of the Uralic language family. They include the Nenets (the largest group), the Enets, the Nganasan and the Selkup; several southern Samoyedic groups, such as the Kamasins and Mators of the Sayan Mountains, were assimilated into Turkic and Russian populations by the early twentieth century.

Distribution and languages

Samoyedic peoples inhabit a vast arc of tundra and taiga from the White Sea and Kanin Peninsula in the west, across the Yamal and Taymyr peninsulas, to the Khatanga River in the east, with the Selkups living further south in the Ob and Yenisei basins. Their languages — Nenets, Enets, Nganasan and Selkup — are related to the Finno-Ugric languages but form a distinct primary branch of Uralic. All are endangered to varying degrees as Russian dominates schooling and media, although Nenets, with tens of thousands of speakers, is comparatively robust.

Culture and livelihood

Traditionally the northern Samoyedic groups are nomadic reindeer herders, migrating seasonally with large herds across the tundra and living in conical tents called chum. Reindeer provide transport, meat, hides for clothing and tent covers, and trade goods. Hunting of wild reindeer, waterfowl and marine mammals, and fishing, supplement herding; the Selkups of the forest zone historically relied more on hunting and fishing than on large-scale herding.

Traditional religion is animistic and shamanic, centred on spirits of nature and a layered cosmos; the Nenets sacred site on Vaygach Island was long known as the "island of idols". Soviet collectivisation, boarding schools and sedentarisation policies profoundly disrupted Samoyedic life in the twentieth century, and today resource extraction on the Yamal and Taymyr peninsulas presents new challenges, alongside efforts at cultural and linguistic revival.
