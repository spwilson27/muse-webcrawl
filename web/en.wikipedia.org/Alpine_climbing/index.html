Alpine climbing

Type: Mountaineering discipline
Terrain: Rock, snow, ice
Style: Light-and-fast, self-sufficient
Origin: European Alps, 19th century

Alpine climbing is a branch of mountaineering in which climbers ascend routes that combine rock, snow, and ice, typically in a single push and carrying all their equipment. It takes its name from the Alps, where the discipline developed, and is defined by self-sufficiency: unlike expedition-style "siege" climbing with fixed ropes and stocked camps, alpine style ascents are fast, light, and committed.

History

The golden age of alpinism in the mid-nineteenth century saw first ascents of the great Alpine peaks, organised partly through institutions such as the Alpine Club (founded 1857). In the twentieth century, climbers applied lightweight Alpine techniques to the greater ranges, a watershed being the 1975 ascent of Gasherbrum I by Reinhold Messner and Peter Habeler without supplementary oxygen or siege tactics.

Techniques and hazards

Alpine climbers must be competent in rock climbing, ice climbing, glacier travel, and crevasse rescue, and must manage objective hazards including avalanches, rockfall, altitude sickness, and sudden weather changes. Grading systems for alpine routes combine overall commitment (Roman numerals or French adjectival grades) with technical rock and ice grades.
