Golden Age of Science Fiction

Periodc. 1938 – 1950 (extended to c. 1960 by some accounts)
Key magazineAstounding Science Fiction
Key figuresJohn W. Campbell, Isaac Asimov, Robert A. Heinlein, Arthur C. Clarke, Theodore Sturgeon

The Golden Age of Science Fiction is the period in the history of the genre, roughly from the late 1930s through the 1940s and into the early 1950s, during which science fiction gained wide popularity and many classic works were produced. The era is closely associated with editor John W. Campbell Jr. of Astounding Science Fiction, who demanded greater scientific rigour and literary quality from his writers.

Characteristics

Golden Age stories typically emphasised scientific accuracy, problem-solving protagonists, and a generally optimistic view of technological progress. Campbell's stable of young writers — including Isaac Asimov, Robert A. Heinlein, A. E. van Vogt, and Theodore Sturgeon — introduced enduring concepts such as Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics and the Foundation series, and Heinlein's future history.

Beginnings and end

The start of the Golden Age is often dated to the July 1939 issue of Astounding, which included early stories by Asimov and van Vogt. World War II and its technological marvels — radar, rockets, and the atomic bomb — heightened public interest in the genre. By the early 1950s, new magazines such as The Magazine of Fantasy and Science Fiction and Galaxy Science Fiction, together with a growing paperback market, broadened the field beyond Campbell's mould, and the Golden Age gave way to the more experimental styles that culminated in the New Wave of the 1960s.

Legacy

The label itself was partly a product of nostalgia; a wry saying of the field holds that "the Golden Age of science fiction is twelve" — the age at which many readers first encountered it. Nevertheless, the works of the period remain foundational to the genre's themes and readership.
