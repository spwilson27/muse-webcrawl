Industrial espionage


Industrial espionage targets trade secrets such as designs, formulas and customer data.

Industrial espionage, also called economic espionage or corporate espionage, is a form of espionage conducted for commercial purposes rather than for national security. It involves the clandestine acquisition of a competitor's trade secrets — such as product designs, manufacturing processes, chemical formulas, customer lists and marketing plans — by or on behalf of a rival company or a foreign government. Industrial espionage is illegal in most jurisdictions, although it is distinguished from legitimate competitive intelligence, which gathers information from public sources.

Methods

Techniques range from recruiting or planting insiders within a target firm, to bribery, blackmail, theft of documents and prototypes, dumpster diving, and electronic eavesdropping. In the computer era, much industrial espionage takes the form of cyber intrusion: spear-phishing, malware and exploitation of network vulnerabilities used to exfiltrate research data and source code.

History

State-sponsored theft of industrial know-how is centuries old: European powers famously sought to break China's monopoly on silk production by smuggling silkworm eggs and mulberry seeds, and in the eighteenth century Britain tried to prevent the emigration of skilled textile workers and machinery designs to its rivals. During the Cold War, Soviet intelligence services systematically collected Western industrial and technological secrets through directorates devoted to scientific and technical intelligence. Since the end of the Cold War, Western governments have accused several states of conducting large-scale economic espionage against private companies, a charge those states generally deny.

Legal responses

In the United States, the Economic Espionage Act of 1996 criminalised the theft of trade secrets, with enhanced penalties where the theft benefits a foreign government, and the Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016 created a federal civil cause of action. Many other countries protect trade secrets through unfair-competition law, employment law and dedicated statutes. Companies respond with compartmentalisation of information, employee agreements, access controls and counterintelligence programmes.
