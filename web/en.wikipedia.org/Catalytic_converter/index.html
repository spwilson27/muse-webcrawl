A catalytic converter is an exhaust emission control device which converts toxic gases and pollutants in exhaust gas from an internal combustion engine into less-toxic pollutants by catalyzing a redox reaction. Catalytic converters are usually used with internal combustion engines fueled by gasoline or diesel, including lean-burn engines, and sometimes on kerosene heaters and stoves.

History

The first widespread introduction of catalytic converters was in the United States automobile market. To comply with the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's stricter regulation of exhaust emissions, most gasoline-powered vehicles starting with the 1975 model year were equipped with catalytic converters. The new devices required unleaded fuel, because the lead in leaded gasoline acts as a catalyst poison, coating the catalyst surface and destroying its effectiveness; this requirement drove the global phase-out of leaded petrol.

The French-American engineer Eugene Houdry had experimented with catalytic exhaust purification in the 1950s, and the modern three-way converter was developed in the early 1970s by engineers including John J. Mooney and Carl D. Keith at the Engelhard Corporation.

How it works

A modern gasoline converter is a three-way converter, so called because it performs three reactions simultaneously: the reduction of nitrogen oxides (NOx) to nitrogen and oxygen; the oxidation of carbon monoxide to carbon dioxide; and the oxidation of unburned hydrocarbons to carbon dioxide and water. The catalyst — a washcoat containing platinum, palladium, and rhodium — is applied to a ceramic or metallic honeycomb substrate with a very large surface area. Three-way converters work efficiently only near the stoichiometric air–fuel ratio, which engine management maintains using oxygen sensors in a closed loop.

Diesel engines, which run lean, use two-way oxidation catalysts (diesel oxidation catalysts) together with technologies such as selective catalytic reduction (SCR), using urea solution to reduce NOx, and particulate filters.

Theft and environmental issues

Because converters contain valuable platinum-group metals, catalytic converter theft became a major crime problem in many countries during the 2010s and 2020s; vehicles with high ground clearance, such as trucks and hybrid cars, are frequent targets. Converters also require the engine to warm before reaching operating temperature ("light-off"), so a large share of a short trip's emissions occur in the first minutes of driving.
