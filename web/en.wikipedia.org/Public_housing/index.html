Public housing

Public housing is a form of housing tenure in which the property is usually owned by a government authority, which may be central or local. Although the common goal is to maintain affordable housing, the details of the arrangements vary between countries, and so does the terminology. Public housing, sometimes called social housing or council housing in some jurisdictions, is provided in many countries in various forms, ranging from scattered single-family houses to large high-rise apartment estates.

History

Modern public housing emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries as industrial cities responded to overcrowding and poor sanitation among working-class households. Early examples included municipal housing built by local authorities in Britain and continental Europe. After the First World War, governments expanded construction substantially, most famously the municipal housing programme of "Red Vienna" in Austria during the 1920s and 1930s.

In the United States, the federal public housing programme was established by the Housing Act of 1937, which created the United States Housing Authority. In the United Kingdom, the Housing, Town Planning, &c. Act 1919 ("Homes fit for heroes") prompted large-scale council house building by local authorities. Large post-war programmes across Europe followed the Second World War to replace bombed housing stock.

By region

United States

The US programme is administered locally by public housing agencies (PHAs) under the supervision of the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD). Rent is generally set at about 30 percent of a household's adjusted monthly income. Large mid-century projects such as Pruitt–Igoe in St. Louis and Cabrini–Green in Chicago became symbols of the failures of high-rise, high-poverty public housing design and were demolished; later programmes such as HOPE VI replaced them with mixed-income developments. The Section 8 Housing Choice Voucher programme, created in 1974, shifted policy toward subsidising rents in the private market.

United Kingdom

Council housing was built and managed by local authorities. At its peak in the late 1970s, roughly a third of British households lived in council housing. The "Right to Buy" policy introduced by the Housing Act 1980 allowed tenants to purchase their homes at a discount, greatly reducing the stock, and much remaining stock was later transferred to housing associations.

Singapore

In Singapore, the Housing and Development Board (HDB), established in 1960, has built flats housing the large majority of the resident population; most are sold on 99-year leases rather than rented, making the system a form of publicly developed home ownership.

Vienna

Vienna's municipal housing (Gemeindebauten) accommodates a large share of the city's population across income groups, including famous estates such as Karl-Marx-Hof. The city's model, funded partly by a dedicated housing tax, is frequently cited as an example of broad-based social housing.

Debates

Supporters argue public housing guarantees housing for low-income households that the market underserves and stabilises communities. Critics point to maintenance backlogs, concentrated poverty where eligibility is narrowly means-tested, and cases of poor design or management. Many systems have moved toward mixed-income models, public–private partnerships, and renovation rather than demolition.
