In mathematics, a Catalan solid, or Archimedean dual, is a polyhedron whose faces are all congruent but which is not vertex-transitive; equivalently, it is the dual polyhedron of an Archimedean solid. The thirteen Catalan solids were first described by the Belgian mathematician Eugène Charles Catalan in 1865. Unlike the Archimedean solids, whose vertices are all identical and whose faces are regular polygons of more than one type, the Catalan solids have identical faces that are not regular polygons, while their vertices are of more than one type.

Properties

Catalan solids are face-transitive (isohedral), so they are often used as fair dice. All their dihedral angles are equal, and they have the same symmetry groups — tetrahedral, octahedral, or icosahedral — as their Archimedean duals. Because their faces are not regular, their face angles vary, though all faces of a given solid are congruent.

The thirteen Catalan solids

  • Triakis tetrahedron (dual of the truncated tetrahedron)
  • Rhombic dodecahedron (dual of the cuboctahedron)
  • Triakis octahedron, tetrakis hexahedron
  • Deltoidal icositetrahedron, pentagonal icositetrahedron
  • Rhombic triacontahedron (dual of the icosidodecahedron)
  • Triakis icosahedron, pentakis dodecahedron
  • Deltoidal hexecontahedron, pentagonal hexecontahedron
  • Disdyakis dodecahedron and disdyakis triacontahedron (the duals of the great rhombic solids)

Two of the Catalan solids — the pentagonal icositetrahedron and the pentagonal hexecontahedron — are chiral, existing in mirror-image forms.
