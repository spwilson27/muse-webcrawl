The history of geodesy is the history of measuring the Earth: its size, shape, orientation and gravity field. Geodesy underlies map-making, navigation, land registration and the modern satellite positioning systems, and its findings settled fundamental questions — above all, that the Earth is a sphere, and later that it is an oblate spheroid flattened at the poles.

Antiquity

Greek thinkers proved the Earth's sphericity from lunar eclipses and shifting constellations. Around 240 BCE Eratosthenes, comparing noon shadows at Alexandria and Syene, calculated the circumference to within a few per cent of the true value — one of antiquity's great scientific feats. Posidonius and Ptolemy refined (and in Ptolemy's case shrank) the estimate, an error that later encouraged Columbus.

Triangulation and the shape of the Earth

In 1615–17 Willebrord Snellius measured an arc in the Netherlands by triangulation, the technique that dominated land surveying for three centuries. The French Academy's expeditions to Peru (1735) and Lapland (1736) confirmed Newton's prediction that the Earth bulges at the equator. France's meridian survey by Delambre and Méchain (1792–99) defined the metre as one ten-millionth of the pole-to-equator quadrant — and produced the first rigorous national arc measurements.

Modern geodesy

The 19th century added the concepts of the geoid (Listing, 1873) and great national surveys such as the Struve Geodetic Arc (1816–55), now a World Heritage Site. The 20th century united geodesy with gravimetry and astronomy; satellites from the 1960s on — TRANSIT, then GPS with the WGS 84 datum — made global centimetre-level positioning routine. VLBI, satellite laser ranging and GRACE-style gravity missions now track continental drift, sea-level change and the Earth's changing shape in real time. See geodesy.
