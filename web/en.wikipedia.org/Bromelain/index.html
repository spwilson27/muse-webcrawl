Bromelain

Bromelain is a mixture of proteolytic enzymes extracted from the pineapple plant (Ananas comosus), a member of the Bromeliaceae family. Two principal forms are distinguished: stem bromelain, obtained from the stem and the main commercial product, and fruit bromelain, found in the juice. Like the related papain from papaya, these cysteine proteases digest proteins and are used in food processing, medicine, and industry.

History

The enzyme was first isolated in 1891 by Venezuelan chemist Vicente Marcano from pineapple juice, and the term "bromelain" was later applied to the related proteases of the whole bromeliad family. Commercial production developed in the mid-twentieth century, centred in Hawaii, Thailand, and other pineapple-growing regions.

Uses

Bromelain is used to tenderise meat, chill-proof beer, soften leather, and stabilise latex paints. In medicine, a bromelain-based concentrate (anacaulase, marketed as NexoBrid) is approved in the European Union and the United States for enzymatic debridement of severe burns. As a dietary supplement it is marketed for inflammation, sinusitis, and osteoarthritis, but regulators consider the clinical evidence for most such uses preliminary. It can interfere with certain antibiotics and blood thinners and may cause allergic reactions in people sensitive to pineapple, celery, or latex. In cooking, fresh pineapple prevents gelatin from setting, since bromelain digests the collagen — a property destroyed by the canning process's heat.
