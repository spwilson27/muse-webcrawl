Quality management

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The Plan–Do–Check–Act cycle

Quality management ensures that an organisation, product or service consistently functions well. It has four main components: quality planning, quality assurance, quality control and quality improvement. Quality management is focused not only on product and service quality, but also on the means to achieve it. It therefore uses quality assurance and control of processes as well as products to achieve more consistent quality.

History

Quality management evolved from inspection practices in early manufacturing, where finished goods were checked against specifications. In the 1920s, Walter A. Shewhart introduced statistical process control at Bell Telephone Laboratories, and W. Edwards Deming and Joseph M. Juran later developed these ideas further. After the Second World War, Deming and Juran helped Japanese industry adopt quality methods, contributing to the rise of Japanese manufacturing quality in the post-war decades.

In the 1980s Western firms responded with total quality management (TQM), a company-wide philosophy emphasising customer focus and continuous improvement. The ISO 9000 family of standards, first published in 1987, provided an international framework for quality management systems, and approaches such as Six Sigma and lean manufacturing extended the discipline further.

Principles

Modern quality management, as codified in ISO 9000, rests on principles including customer focus, leadership, engagement of people, a process approach, improvement, evidence-based decision making and relationship management. A quality management system documents processes, responsibilities and procedures for achieving quality policies and objectives.

Continuous improvement is commonly driven by the Plan–Do–Check–Act (PDCA) cycle: plan a change, implement it, check the results and act on what is learned. Quality assurance builds quality into processes prospectively, whereas quality control detects defects in outputs; the two are complementary.

Methods and tools

Standard tools include the seven basic tools of quality — such as cause-and-effect (Ishikawa) diagrams, control charts, histograms and Pareto charts — along with failure mode and effects analysis, benchmarking, and quality audits. Quality awards such as the Deming Prize and the Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award recognise organisational excellence in the field.
