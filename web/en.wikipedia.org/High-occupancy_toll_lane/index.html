High-occupancy toll lane

A high-occupancy toll lane (HOT lane) is a type of traffic lane or a whole road that is available to high-occupancy vehicles (HOVs) free of charge; other vehicles are required to pay a variable toll to use it. Unlike toll roads where all vehicles pay, and HOV lanes where only high-occupancy vehicles are allowed, HOT lanes allow single-occupant vehicles to buy access to the typically freer-flowing HOV lanes. The toll is often dynamically priced, rising with traffic density to maintain a minimum speed in the lane.

History and examples

The concept was pioneered in California: the State Route 91 Express Lanes in Orange County opened in 1995, and Interstate 15 in San Diego converted HOV lanes to HOT operation in the late 1990s. Other examples include the I-495 and I-95 Express Lanes in Northern Virginia, Katy Freeway managed lanes in Houston, and facilities in Minneapolis–Saint Paul (MnPASS), Atlanta, Miami, Denver, and the San Francisco Bay Area. Tolls are collected electronically, typically by transponder, and some systems let drivers declare their carpool status with a switchable tag.

Criticism

Critics deride HOT lanes as "Lexus lanes", arguing they let wealthier drivers buy their way out of congestion. Supporters counter that they make fuller use of underused HOV capacity, provide a reliable travel-time option, and can fund transit improvements.
