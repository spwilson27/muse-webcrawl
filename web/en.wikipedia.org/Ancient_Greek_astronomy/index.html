Ancient Greek astronomy

Ancient Greek astronomy is the astronomy written in the Greek language during classical antiquity, encompassing the Greek, Hellenistic, Greco-Roman, and late antique periods. Building on Babylonian observations, Greek astronomers developed geometrical models of the heavens that aimed to “save the phenomena” with combinations of uniform circular motions.

Early cosmology

The pre-Socratic philosophers proposed the first naturalistic cosmologies: Thales and Anaximander pictured the Earth floating in space, the Pythagoreans assigned the planets spherical orbits and harmonious intervals, and Plato demanded that celestial motions be explained by uniform circles. Eudoxus of Cnidus answered with a system of concentric spheres, later elaborated by Callippus and Aristotle.

Hellenistic advances

Aristarchus of Samos proposed a heliocentric arrangement and estimated the sizes and distances of the Sun and Moon. Eratosthenes measured the Earth's circumference with remarkable accuracy, Hipparchus compiled a star catalogue, discovered the precession of the equinoxes, and developed trigonometry for astronomical calculation. The geared Antikythera mechanism (c. 150–100 BC) modelled lunar and planetary motions mechanically.

Ptolemy and the Almagest

In the 2nd century AD, Claudius Ptolemy synthesised Greek astronomy in the Almagest, perfecting the geocentric model with deferents, epicycles, eccentrics, and the equant. His system predicted planetary positions well enough to dominate astronomy in Europe and the Islamic world for some 1,400 years, until Copernicus.
