Autoharp

The autoharp is a stringed musical instrument in the zither family. It has a series of chord bars; when a bar is pressed, felt dampers mute all strings except those forming the desired chord, allowing the player to strum a harmonious accompaniment with little training.

History

The instrument was developed in the late nineteenth century. A key patent was granted to Charles F. Zimmermann in 1882, and the name "Autoharp" was registered as a trademark; the Oscar Schmidt company became its best-known maker. Trademark disputes over the generic use of the name continued into the twentieth century.

Construction and playing

A typical autoharp has 36 or 37 strings across a wooden body and 15 or 21 chord bars. The player holds the instrument upright or lays it on the lap, pressing chord buttons with one hand and strumming or picking the strings with the other. Skilled players can play melodies by damping and releasing bars in sequence.

Use in music

The autoharp is closely associated with American folk, country, and Appalachian music. Maybelle Carter of the Carter Family popularized it, and later performers such as Bryan Bowers developed virtuoso melodic techniques. It is also widely used in elementary music education.
