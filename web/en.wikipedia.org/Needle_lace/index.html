Needle lace

Needle lace is a type of lace created using a needle and thread to stitch up to hundreds of tiny stitches to form the fabric itself. Unlike bobbin lace, which is woven, needle lace is essentially a form of embroidery: designs are built from variations of the buttonhole stitch worked over a framework of laid threads that follow a pattern drawn on a backing.

Technique

The lacemaker couches an outline thread along the lines of a pattern pricked on parchment or card, then fills the shapes with detached buttonhole stitches, connecting areas with narrow bars called brides. When the work is complete, the backing is cut away, leaving a self-supporting fabric of thread. Pure needle lace can take thousands of hours for a single garment.

History

Needle lace developed in late 15th- and early 16th-century Italy out of cutwork and drawn-thread embroidery. Venetian punto in aria ("stitches in the air") was the first true needle lace, and Venice dominated the luxury trade until France, under Colbert, established royal manufactories in the 1660s. The French point de France and the later laces of Alençon and Argentan rivalled the Venetian product. Famous regional types include Burano lace from Italy, Brussels and Mechlin-style needlepoints, Armenian needle lace (bebilla), and Turkish oya.

Decline and survival

Machine-made nets in the 19th century undercut handmade lace, and the craft collapsed as a commercial industry. It survives as an art form, taught by lace guilds and preserved in museum collections such as those of the Victoria and Albert Museum.
