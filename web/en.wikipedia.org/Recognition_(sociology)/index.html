Recognition (sociology)

Recognition in sociology and political philosophy is the public acknowledgement of a person's or group's social status, identity, merits or rights. Theories of recognition hold that individuals form their identities through being recognised by others, and that misrecognition or non-recognition can constitute a form of injustice and oppression.

Philosophical origins

The concept descends from Hegel's account of the struggle for recognition (Anerkennung) in the Phenomenology of Spirit (1807), including the master–slave dialectic, in which self-consciousness depends on being recognised by another self-consciousness. George Herbert Mead's social psychology similarly held that the self arises through taking the perspective of others.

Contemporary theory

Axel Honneth developed the most influential modern theory, distinguishing three spheres of recognition: love (primary relationships, grounding self-confidence), rights (legal recognition, grounding self-respect) and solidarity/esteem (valuation of one's traits and contributions, grounding self-esteem). Charles Taylor's essay "The Politics of Recognition" (1992) connected recognition to multiculturalism and the identity claims of minority groups. Nancy Fraser has argued that recognition must be complemented by redistribution, framing misrecognition as status subordination that blocks participatory parity. Recognition theory informs debates over multicultural policy, identity politics, restorative justice and workplace dignity.
