In mathematics, the Wallis product for π, published in 1656 by John Wallis, states that:

π/2 = ∏n=1∞ (2n/(2n−1)) · (2n/(2n+1)) = (2/1)(2/3)(4/3)(4/5)(6/5)(6/7)···

It was one of the first infinite products in mathematics, and the first published expression of π as a product of rational numbers.

Derivation

Wallis derived the product by interpolation of the integrals ∫0π/2 sinn x dx for integer powers n, a celebrated (if not fully rigorous by modern standards) feat of 17th-century analysis. Today the product follows from Euler's infinite product for the sine function, sin x = x ∏ (1 − x²/(n²π²)), by setting x = π/2. The integrals used in modern proofs are now called Wallis integrals, and satisfy a two-step recurrence that makes their ratio tend to π/2.

Convergence and consequences

The product converges very slowly: after n factors the error is of the order of 1/n, so hundreds of terms are needed for a few correct digits. Combinations of the product with correction factors yield much faster formulae. Wallis's result is connected with Stirling's approximation for factorials, with the value Γ(1/2) = √π of the gamma function, and with the central binomial coefficients.
