Monasticism

A monastery cloister, heart of communal monastic life

Monasticism (from Greek monachos, "solitary") is a religious way of life in which adherents — monks and nuns — renounce worldly pursuits, including property and marriage, to devote themselves to spiritual work, typically living apart from society in a monastery under a rule and vows of poverty, chastity, and obedience. It is a central institution in Buddhism, Jainism, Hinduism, and Christianity, and appears in other traditions.

Buddhist and Jain monasticism

The oldest surviving monastic order is the Buddhist sangha, founded by the Buddha in the 5th century BCE; monks (bhikkhus) and nuns (bhikkhunis) live by the Vinaya code, begging their food and meditating toward enlightenment. Jain monasticism, of similar antiquity, is renowned for extreme asceticism in pursuit of liberation (moksha), with monks and nuns of the Shvetambara and Digambara traditions taking five great vows headed by absolute non-violence.

Christian monasticism

Christian monasticism began in the Egyptian desert in the 3rd–4th centuries CE with hermits such as Anthony the Great and communal pioneers such as Pachomius. Basil of Caesarea shaped Eastern practice, while in the West the Rule of St. Benedict (c. 530) — balancing prayer, work, and reading (ora et labora) — became the template for medieval monasteries, which preserved learning, ran schools and hospitals, and cleared farmland. New orders followed: Cistercians, then the mendicant Franciscans and Dominicans of the 13th century. The Protestant Reformation dissolved monasteries in much of northern Europe and England, but monastic life continues in Catholic, Orthodox, and some Anglican communities.
