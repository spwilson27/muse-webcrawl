Discrimination

Discrimination is the act of making unjustified distinctions between human beings based on the groups, classes, or other categories to which they are perceived to belong. People may discriminate on the basis of age, caste, criminal record, height, disability, family status, gender identity, gender expression, generation, genetic features, language, nationality, race or ethnicity, religion, sex and sex characteristics, sexual orientation, social class, wealth, political opinion, colour and other personal characteristics.

Definitions

Philosophers and legal theorists distinguish discrimination that is morally objectionable from mere differential treatment. Moralized definitions hold that discrimination is disadvantageous treatment of a person or group based on membership in a socially salient group, where the treatment is wrongful — either because of the agent's contemptuous or hostile attitudes, or because it undermines the victim's status as an equal.

Direct discrimination occurs when a person is treated less favourably than another in a comparable situation because of a protected characteristic. Indirect discrimination occurs when a rule or practice that appears neutral puts people of a protected group at a particular disadvantage, unless it can be objectively justified.

Types

  • Racial and ethnic discrimination — differential treatment based on race or ethnicity, historically expressed in systems such as slavery, apartheid and segregation.
  • Gender and sex discrimination — including unequal pay, exclusion from occupations and under-representation in leadership.
  • Age discrimination — prejudice against people who are considered too old or too young, particularly in employment.
  • Religious discrimination — treating people differently because of their religious beliefs, attire or practices.
  • Disability discrimination — failing to make reasonable accommodations or excluding disabled people from goods, services and employment.
  • Linguistic discrimination — prejudice based on a person's language, dialect or accent.

Legal frameworks

Many countries prohibit discrimination through constitutional provisions and statutes. International instruments include the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (1965), and the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (1979). In the United States, the Civil Rights Act of 1964 outlawed discrimination in employment and public accommodations; the European Union addresses discrimination through directives on racial equality and employment equality.

Positive discrimination

Some policies, such as affirmative action in the United States or reservation systems in India, deliberately favour historically disadvantaged groups in education and employment. Such measures are intended to remedy past discrimination, though they remain politically and legally contested.

Effects

Research in psychology and public health links experiences of discrimination with poorer mental and physical health outcomes, reduced economic opportunity, and social exclusion. Structural or institutional discrimination can perpetuate inequalities even in the absence of individual prejudice.
