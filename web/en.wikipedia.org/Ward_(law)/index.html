Ward (law)

In law, a ward is a person who has been placed under the protection of a legal guardian, typically by a court. Historically the term most often refers to a minor child whose parent or parents have died or are unable to care for them, but it may also apply to an incapacitated adult. The corresponding relationship is guardianship; a person under the guardianship of a court is sometimes called a "ward of the court".

Guardianship of minors

A court may appoint a guardian to manage the ward's person, property, or both. The guardian owes the ward a fiduciary duty of care and loyalty, and major decisions — such as the sale of the ward's property — generally require court approval and accounting. Guardianship of a minor ordinarily ends when the ward reaches the age of majority, marries, or is emancipated.

Historical wardship

In feudal England, wardship was a profitable incident of tenure: when a tenant-in-chief died leaving a minor heir, the Crown or lord was entitled to custody of the heir's lands and person, and to arrange (effectively sell) the heir's marriage. The abuses of the royal Court of Wards and Liveries (1540–1646) were a grievance in the conflicts leading to the English Civil War, and feudal wardship was abolished by the Tenures Abolition Act 1660.

Modern usage

Modern statutes in common-law jurisdictions provide for court-supervised guardianship and conservatorship of minors and incapacitated persons, with periodic review intended to protect the ward's rights and estate. In some jurisdictions, children in state foster care are described as wards of the state.
