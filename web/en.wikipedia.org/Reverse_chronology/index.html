Reverse chronology

Reverse chronology is a narrative structure in which the plot is revealed in reverse order, from the end of the story toward its beginning. In reverse-chronology storytelling, each succeeding scene takes place earlier than the one preceding it. The technique is distinct from simply using flashbacks: in a full reverse-chronology work, the entire sequence of events is systematically inverted, and dramatic interest arises from the audience's gradually discovering the causes of effects they have already seen.

Literature

Reverse narration has early antecedents, but it became a recognized literary device in the 20th century. Harold Pinter's play Betrayal (1978) proceeds largely backwards through a love affair, and Martin Amis's novel Time's Arrow (1991) narrates the life of a Nazi doctor in literal reverse, with dialogue, actions, and even causality running backwards through a reversed world. Alejo Carpentier's short story "Viaje a la semilla" (Journey Back to the Source, 1944) follows its protagonist's life in reverse until he returns to the womb. Other examples include F. Scott Fitzgerald's short story "The Curious Case of Benjamin Button" (1922), whose hero ages in reverse, and Brian Aldiss's and Kurt Vonnegut's experiments with backward-moving narration, including the reversed bombing sequence in Slaughterhouse-Five.

Film

Cinema adopted the device prominently with Christopher Nolan's Memento (2000), in which the color sequences run in reverse order, mirroring the anterograde amnesia of its protagonist. Earlier examples include the Polish film Blind Chance variants and adaptations of Pinter's Betrayal (1983). Gaspar Noé's Irréversible (2002) unfolds backwards from an act of violence to its antecedents, and Peppermint Candy (1999) and the French film 5x2 (2004) use backward structures for melodrama. The 1945 film The Killers-style investigative flashback structures are more limited relatives of the device.

Theatre and television

On stage, reverse chronology appears in Kaufman and Hart's Merrily We Roll Along (1934), later adapted by Stephen Sondheim and George Furth into the 1981 musical of the same name, which tracks three friends backward from embittered middle age to youthful optimism. Television episodes have used the device for single installments, including episodes of Seinfeld ("The Betrayal," set in India), ER, and Star Trek: Voyager, as well as entire series arcs told in reverse.

Related uses

Outside fiction, "reverse chronological order" is an ordering convention: lists arranged from newest to oldest, as in résumés that list the most recent job first, bibliographies, and social-media feeds. In historiography and archaeology, working from the known present backward into the past is also sometimes described with the term.
