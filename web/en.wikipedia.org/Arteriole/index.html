Arteriole

An arteriole branches from an artery and leads to capillaries.

An arteriole is a small-diameter blood vessel in the microcirculation that extends and branches out from an artery and leads to capillaries. Arterioles have muscular walls (usually only one to two layers of smooth muscle cells) and are the primary site of vascular resistance. Changes in arteriole diameter — vasoconstriction and vasodilation — are the principal mechanism by which the body regulates blood flow and blood pressure.

Structure

Arterioles typically range from about 10 to 100 micrometres in diameter. Their walls consist of an inner endothelium, a layer of smooth muscle, and a thin outer connective tissue. Metarterioles are short vessels that link arterioles and capillaries, with precapillary sphincters controlling flow into capillary beds.

Function and clinical significance

Total peripheral resistance is largely determined by arteriolar tone, regulated by the sympathetic nervous system, local metabolites, and hormones such as angiotensin II. Chronic constriction or structural narrowing of arterioles contributes to hypertension, and damage to arterioles in the retina, kidney, and brain is a hallmark of long-standing high blood pressure and diabetes.
