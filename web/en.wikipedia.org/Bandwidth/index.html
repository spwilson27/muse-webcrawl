Bandwidth (computing)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In computing, bandwidth is the maximum rate of data transfer across a given path, commonly measured in bits per second (bit/s) and its multiples — kilobits, megabits, and gigabits per second. It is a central measure of the capacity of computer networks, internet connections, and interfaces between system components such as memory and processors. The term is borrowed from radio engineering, where bandwidth is the width of a band of frequencies used by a signal; by Shannon's theory, the capacity of a channel grows with that frequency bandwidth and the signal-to-noise ratio.

Measurement and usage

Bandwidth may describe a link's theoretical maximum rate, while throughput describes the rate actually achieved, which is reduced by protocol overhead, congestion, latency, and retransmissions. Network providers typically advertise download and (usually lower) upload bandwidths; consumers' effective experience also depends on latency and jitter. In web hosting, "bandwidth" is often used loosely to mean the total volume of data transferred per month, properly called data transfer. Memory bandwidth — the rate at which a processor can read and write RAM — is a key performance limit in computing, expressed in bytes per second.

Typical magnitudes

Historical access technologies ranged from dial-up modems at up to 56 kbit/s, through DSL and cable broadband (tens to hundreds of Mbit/s), to fiber-to-the-home services offering symmetric gigabit rates. Local interfaces are far faster: USB generations span 12 Mbit/s to tens of Gbit/s, and modern memory subsystems sustain hundreds of gigabytes per second. Shannon's law sets a theoretical ceiling for a channel of given analog bandwidth and noise.
