Truth and reconciliation commission


Victims testifying at a commission hearing

A truth commission, or truth and reconciliation commission, is an official body tasked with investigating and reporting on human rights abuses committed during a period of conflict or authoritarian rule. Unlike criminal courts, such commissions focus on establishing an authoritative historical record, giving victims a public voice, and recommending reforms and reparations, often as part of a transition to democracy.

Notable commissions

Early examples include Argentina's CONADEP (1983), whose report Nunca Más documented disappearances under the military junta, and Chile's Rettig Commission. The best known is South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1995–2002), chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu, which could grant amnesty to perpetrators who fully disclosed politically motivated crimes. Canada's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2008–2015) examined the residential school system for Indigenous children and issued 94 Calls to Action.

Debates

Supporters argue commissions promote healing and prevent denial; critics question trading prosecution for truth, the adequacy of reparations, and whether reconciliation can be achieved without justice.
