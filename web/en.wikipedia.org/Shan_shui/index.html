Shan shui (Chinese: 山水, literally "mountain-water") is the classical Chinese tradition of landscape painting, in which mountains, rivers, waterfalls, and mist are composed with brush and ink on silk or paper. The most prestigious genre in Chinese art for over a thousand years, shan shui does not aim at the faithful depiction of a particular view; instead the artist, having absorbed the forms of nature, constructs an idealised landscape that expresses a philosophy of harmony between humanity and the cosmos, deeply informed by Daoism and Confucian thought.

Principles and technique

Early theory, such as Zong Bing's 5th-century Preface to Painting Landscape and Xie He's Six Principles, held that landscape painting lets the spirit wander through nature. Composition typically uses a shifting, multi-level perspective — Guo Xi's "three distances" (high, deep, and level) — with vast voids of mist separating solid forms. Tiny figures, pavilions, and boats emphasise the immensity of nature. Brushwork is built from texture strokes (cun) that model cliffs and trees, washes that render water and sky, and calligraphic line, binding painting to calligraphy as expressions of the artist's character.

History

Landscape elements appear in Chinese art from the Han dynasty, but shan shui became an independent genre in the Tang dynasty (618–907) with artists such as Wang Wei and the blue-green landscape style. It reached monumental maturity in the Northern Song (960–1127): Fan Kuan's Travelers Among Mountains and Streams and Guo Xi's Early Spring are defining masterpieces. Under the Southern Song, Ma Yuan and Xia Gui developed lyrical, one-corner compositions. In the Yuan dynasty the scholar-amateur ideal prevailed — Zhao Mengfu and the Four Masters, above all Huang Gongwang, turned landscape into personal self-expression, the model for the later literati tradition.

Schools

Dong Qichang's influential Ming-dynasty theory divided the tradition into a Northern School of professional, academic painters and a Southern School of expressive literati, a framework that shaped collecting and practice for centuries. Regional schools — Wu, Zhe, Songjiang — and later individualists such as Shitao carried the genre into the Qing dynasty, and shan shui continues in contemporary ink painting.
