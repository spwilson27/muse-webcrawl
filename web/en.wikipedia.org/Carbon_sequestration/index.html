Carbon sequestration

Carbon is sequestered naturally by forests, soils and oceans, and artificially by geological storage.

Carbon sequestration is the process of storing carbon in a carbon pool. It plays a crucial role in limiting climate change by reducing the amount of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. There are two main types: geologic sequestration — storing CO2 underground in rock formations — and biologic (terrestrial) sequestration — storing carbon in plants, soils, and oceans. The concept of carbon sequestration gained prominence after growing recognition of the role of CO2 in the greenhouse effect.

Natural processes

Photosynthesis transfers roughly 120 gigatonnes of carbon per year from the atmosphere into terrestrial ecosystems, about half of which returns via respiration and decomposition. Forests store carbon in biomass and soils; soil organic carbon globally exceeds the carbon in the atmosphere and all vegetation combined. Oceans absorb CO2 through solubility and the biological pump — phytoplankton fix carbon that sinks as marine snow — while silicate weathering locks carbon into carbonate rocks on geological timescales. Land management systems such as agroforestry deliberately augment these sinks.

Artificial methods

  • Carbon capture and storage (CCS) — CO2 captured from power plants or industry and injected into depleted oil and gas fields or deep saline aquifers (e.g. Norway's Sleipner project, operating since 1996).
  • Bioenergy with CCS (BECCS) and direct air capture (DAC) — engineered negative-emissions approaches.
  • Enhanced weathering and ocean fertilisation — proposed geoengineering methods with contested efficacy and ecology.
  • Biochar — charcoal produced by pyrolysis and buried in soils.

Sequestration complements emissions reductions aimed at causes of air pollution and warming; its permanence, cost and verification remain active policy questions.
