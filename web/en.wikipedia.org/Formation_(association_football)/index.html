Formation (association football)

In association football, the formation of a team refers to the position players take in relation to each other on the pitch. It has been worked on since the prime days of football, and as different managers use different formations the result of the work of the players is affected. A formation is usually described by three or four numbers, which denote how many players are in each row of the formation, from the most defensive to the most attacking.

For example, the "4–5–1" formation has four defenders, five midfielders, and a single forward. The choice of formation is normally made by the team manager. By changing forwards play more players in attack, formation is often the first thing discussed when a match lineup is announced, and it may be adjusted during a game in response to the score or to injuries and dismissals.

Common formations

The 4–4–2 was for decades the standard formation in English football, pairing two central strikers ahead of a flat midfield four. The 4–3–3, associated with Dutch total football and later with Barcelona and the Netherlands, uses three forwards and emphasises width and pressing. The 3–5–2 employs three central defenders and wing-backs who supply width. The 4–2–3–1 became dominant in the 2000s, with two holding midfielders shielding the defence and a lone striker supported by three attacking midfielders.

History

Early football formations were extremely attacking by modern standards; the 2–3–5 "pyramid" was standard from the 1880s until the change in the offside law in 1925 prompted Herbert Chapman to develop the W–M formation (3–2–2–3). The Hungarian team of the 1950s experimented with a deep-lying centre forward, and Brazil's 4–2–4 at the 1958 World Cup helped establish the back four as the norm.

Modern usage

Modern coaching treats formations as fluid: a team's shape in possession often differs from its shape out of possession, and analysts distinguish the nominal formation named in the team sheet from the structures a side actually adopts during play. Formations are now routinely described with four or five numbers, such as 4–1–4–1, to capture split midfield lines.
