The Pacific Crest Trail (PCT), officially the Pacific Crest National Scenic Trail, is a long-distance hiking and equestrian trail closely aligned with the highest portion of the Cascade and Sierra Nevada mountain ranges in the western United States. It stretches 2,650 miles (4,265 km) from the Mexican border near Campo, California, to Manning Provincial Park in British Columbia, Canada.

Route

From Southern California the trail crosses the Laguna Mountains, the San Bernardino and San Gabriel ranges, and the Mojave Desert, before entering the Sierra Nevada. It passes through or near Yosemite, Kings Canyon and Sequoia national parks and over Forester Pass, its highest point at 13,153 feet (4,009 m). In Northern California it traverses the Klamath Mountains and the volcanic Cascade Range, continuing through Oregon past Crater Lake and the Three Sisters, then across the Columbia River into Washington, where it passes Mount Rainier and crosses the North Cascades to the Canadian border.

History

The trail was first proposed in the 1930s, notably by Catherine M. Montgomery and Clinton C. Clarke, and was surveyed by relay teams organised with the YMCA between 1935 and 1938. It was designated a National Scenic Trail under the National Trails System Act of 1968 and was officially completed in 1993. Eric Ryback is credited with the first end-to-end thru-hike in 1970, and Cheryl Strayed's 2012 memoir Wild (adapted into a 2014 film) greatly increased the trail's popularity.

Thru-hiking

Each year thousands attempt to "thru-hike" the entire trail in a single season, typically taking four to six months heading northbound starting in April or May to cross the Sierra before autumn snow and finish in Canada before winter. Hikers must contend with desert water scarcity, Sierra snowpack, river crossings, wildfires and permit quotas administered by the Pacific Crest Trail Association and federal land agencies. In recent years, roughly 700–800 northbound long-distance permits have been issued annually.
