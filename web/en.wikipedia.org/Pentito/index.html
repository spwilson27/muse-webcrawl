Pentito

A pentito (plural pentiti; Italian for "one who has repented") is a former member of a criminal or terrorist organisation who, after arrest or in exchange for reduced punishment, cooperates with the state by giving evidence against the organisation. The term is most associated with former members of the Sicilian Mafia who broke omertà to testify, under laws introduced in Italy in the 1980s and 1990s.

History

Legislation rewarding collaborators of justice was first developed against the Red Brigades terrorism of the 1970s. It was applied decisively against Cosa Nostra by the anti-Mafia pool of judges including Giovanni Falcone and Paolo Borsellino. The testimony of Tommaso Buscetta, beginning in 1984, was foundational to the Maxi Trial of 1986–87, in which hundreds of mafiosi were convicted. Other famous pentiti include Salvatore Contorno and Gaspare Mutolo.

Criticism

The system is controversial: critics argue that pentiti may fabricate evidence for reward, and some high-profile prosecutions (such as one strand involving Giulio Andreotti) relied on disputed turncoat testimony. Nevertheless, the pentiti system is widely credited with transforming the state's knowledge of the Mafia's structure and weakening Cosa Nostra.
