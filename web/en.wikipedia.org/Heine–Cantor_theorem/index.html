Heine–Cantor theorem

FieldReal analysis, topology
StatementA continuous function on a compact metric space is uniformly continuous
Named afterEduard Heine, Georg Cantor

In mathematics, the Heine–Cantor theorem, named after Eduard Heine and Georg Cantor, states that if f is a continuous function between two metric spaces, and its domain is compact, then f is uniformly continuous.

Statement and significance

Ordinary continuity is a pointwise property: the closeness required between inputs to guarantee closeness of outputs may depend on the point. Uniform continuity requires a single δ that works across the whole domain. The theorem says that compactness of the domain converts the former into the latter. A standard example of its use is the consequence that every continuous function on a closed bounded interval [a, b] is uniformly continuous there, which is a key step in proving that such functions are Riemann integrable.

Outline of proof

The usual proof proceeds by contradiction. If f were not uniformly continuous, one could construct two sequences of points, pairwise closer and closer together, whose images stay a fixed distance apart. Compactness provides convergent subsequences whose limits coincide, and continuity at that limit point then contradicts the separation of the images. A more general version holds for continuous functions from a compact uniform space to a uniform space.
