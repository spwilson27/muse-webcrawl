Libretto

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A libretto is the text of an opera or other extended musical work.

A libretto (from Italian libretto, "booklet") is the text used in, or intended for, an extended musical work such as an opera, operetta, masque, oratorio, cantata or musical. The term is also used for the printed booklet containing this text and sometimes a synopsis, traditionally sold at performances and now often replaced by projected surtitles.

History and role

The librettist writes the words—the dialogue, arias (often in verse) and stage directions—in collaboration with the composer, though the balance of influence varied by era. In the 17th and 18th centuries, librettists such as Pietro Metastasio were celebrities whose texts were set dozens of times; his opera seria libretti were used by composers across Europe. Lorenzo Da Ponte wrote the libretti for Mozart's The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni and Così fan tutte. Famous partnerships include W. S. Gilbert with Arthur Sullivan, and Richard Strauss with Hugo von Hofmannsthal; Wagner wrote his own libretti. In the 20th century, librettists such as Igor Stravinsky's collaborator W. H. Auden helped shape modern opera.

Characteristics

A good libretto must serve the music: it provides dramatic structure and singable text, often with repetitions, simpler diction at moments of musical intensity, and ensembles in which several characters express different feelings simultaneously—something spoken drama cannot easily do. Libretti are frequently adapted from existing plays, novels, or myths.
