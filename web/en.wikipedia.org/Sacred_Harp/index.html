Sacred Harp

Sacred Harp singing is a tradition of sacred choral music that originated in New England and was later carried to the American South, where it survived as a living folk tradition. It is named after The Sacred Harp, a tunebook first published in 1844 by B. F. White and E. J. King of Georgia. Singers use shape notes and the fa-sol-la solmisation system, singing a cappella in four harmony parts while seated in a hollow square facing inward.

History

The tradition descends from colonial American singing schools and the shape-note tunebooks produced to teach them. The Sacred Harp (1844) collected hymns, fuging tunes, and anthems from New England composers such as William Billings and from folk and camp-meeting sources. As "modern" seven-shape and gospel styles displaced it elsewhere, the four-shape Sacred Harp tradition persisted in rural communities of Georgia, Alabama, Texas, and Mississippi, sustained by all-day singings and conventions.

Practice

At a singing, participants sit in a hollow square arranged by part — treble, alto, tenor (which carries the melody), and bass — with a rotating leader standing in the centre, beating time. Each song is first sung through using the shape-note syllables (fa, sol, la, mi) and then with its words. Singings are participatory rather than performances: there is no audience and typically no rehearsal. A midday break features "dinner on the grounds", a communal potluck meal.

Revival and spread

From the 1970s, folk-music enthusiasts carried Sacred Harp singing far beyond the South; there are now active singing communities across the United States and in the United Kingdom, Ireland, Germany, Poland, Australia, Japan, and elsewhere. The 1991 edition of The Sacred Harp remains the most widely used version, while related books such as the Cooper Book and Christian Harmony serve other regional traditions. The music attracted wide attention through its use in the film Cold Mountain (2003).
