Randstad

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Randstad is a roughly crescent- or ring-shaped conurbation in the west of the Netherlands, consisting of the four largest Dutch cities — Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague and Utrecht — together with their surrounding suburbs and smaller cities including Almere, Haarlem, Leiden, Delft, Dordrecht, Gouda, Zoetermeer and Hilversum. With a population of over 8 million, it is one of the largest metropolitan regions in Europe and contains roughly half of the Netherlands' population on about a fifth of its territory.

Geography

The name Randstad means "rim city" or "border city": the urban ring surrounds a relatively open, green rural interior known as the Groene Hart ("Green Heart"), an area of peat meadows, lakes and small towns whose openness Dutch planning policy has long sought to preserve. The term is credited to KLM founder Albert Plesman, who is said to have coined it in the late 1930s while flying over the region and seeing a rim of cities around a green centre. Much of the region lies below sea level in polders protected by dikes and drainage systems.

Economy and transport

The Randstad is the economic engine of the Netherlands. It contains the Port of Rotterdam, the largest seaport in Europe, and Amsterdam Airport Schiphol, one of Europe's busiest airports. Amsterdam is a financial and creative-industries centre; The Hague is the seat of the Dutch government, parliament and supreme court, as well as the International Court of Justice; Rotterdam specialises in logistics, maritime business and petrochemicals; Utrecht is a central transport hub and university city. The region is linked by a dense motorway and railway network, and a dedicated light-rail/metro system, RandstadRail, connects The Hague, Rotterdam and Zoetermeer.

Governance

The Randstad has no single administrative authority; it spans four provinces and dozens of municipalities. Cooperation takes place through bodies such as the Metropoolregio Rotterdam Den Haag and the Metropoolregio Amsterdam, and Dutch national spatial planning has treated the Randstad as a single polycentric urban system since the mid-twentieth century.
