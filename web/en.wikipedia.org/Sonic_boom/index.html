Sonic boom

A sonic boom is a sound associated with shock waves created when an object travels through the air faster than the speed of sound. Sonic booms generate enormous amounts of sound energy, sounding similar to an explosion or a thunderclap to the human ear. A decibel level of 200, larger objects can have booms powerful enough to crack windows and cause minor structural damage.

The sound is not a one-time event as the object "breaks the sound barrier"; rather, the boom is a continuous effect that occurs along the entire length of a cone-shaped region trailing the aircraft, sweeping the ground in a "boom carpet". As the aircraft flies at supersonic speed, shock waves emanate from its nose and tail, and a ground observer hears a sharp double bang (the N-wave) when the pressure jumps arrive.

Causes and characteristics

When an aircraft passes through the air, it creates a series of pressure waves. At supersonic speeds these waves merge into a single shock wave travelling at the speed of sound. The boom's intensity depends on the aircraft's size, shape, altitude, and flight path; higher altitude spreads and weakens the boom. Whip cracks, bullwhips, and the crack of a bullet are small-scale examples of the same phenomenon.

Regulation

Public reaction to testing over cities, notably the 1964 Oklahoma City sonic boom tests, led to restrictions on supersonic flight over land, such as the FAA ban in the United States; consequently supersonic airliners such as Concorde restricted supersonic operation to over-ocean routes. Modern research into low-boom design (e.g., NASA's X-59) aims to soften the boom to a "thump", potentially allowing overland supersonic flight again.
