In fluid dynamics, a Kármán vortex street (or a von Kármán vortex street) is a repeating pattern of swirling vortices, caused by a process known as vortex shedding, that is responsible for the unsteady separation of flow of a fluid around bluff bodies.

It is named after the engineer and fluid dynamicist Theodore von Kármán, and is responsible for such phenomena as the "singing" of suspended telephone or power lines and the vibration of a car antenna at certain speeds.

Definition

A vortex street forms when a fluid flows past a bluff (non-streamlined) body at moderate to high Reynolds numbers. Vortices are shed alternately from either side of the body, forming two staggered rows of counter-rotating vortices in the wake. The pattern persists downstream over long distances before viscous diffusion dissipates it.

History

Although the phenomenon had been observed earlier — Strouhal studied the tones produced by wires in wind in 1878 — von Kármán gave the first theoretical analysis of the stability of the staggered vortex configuration in 1911–1912, showing that only one ratio of vortex spacing to row separation (approximately 0.281) is stable.

Examples in nature and engineering

Satellite photographs frequently show vortex streets in clouds downwind of islands, such as the Canary Islands and Guadalupe Island. In engineering, vortex streets can cause destructive oscillations: the collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in 1940 is popularly associated with vortex-induced resonance, and tall chimneys are fitted with helical strakes to disrupt coherent shedding.

Mathematics

The frequency of shedding is characterised by the Strouhal number, St = fD/U, where f is the shedding frequency, D the body's characteristic width and U the flow speed. For a circular cylinder over a wide range of Reynolds numbers, St is approximately 0.2.
