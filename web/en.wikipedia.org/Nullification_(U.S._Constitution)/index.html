Nullification (U.S. Constitution)

Nullification, in United States constitutional history, is a legal theory that a US state has the right to nullify, or invalidate, any federal laws that it deems to have exceeded the powers reserved to the states or the people. The theory is based on the compact theory of the Union, under which the Constitution was a compact among sovereign states.

Origins

The theory was articulated in the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions of 1798–99, written secretly by Thomas Jefferson and James Madison, which opposed the Alien and Sedition Acts. It was developed further by John C. Calhoun in the South Carolina Exposition and Protest (1828) and invoked during the Nullification Crisis of 1832–33, when South Carolina declared federal tariffs void within the state; President Andrew Jackson rejected the doctrine and Congress authorized force, though a compromise tariff defused the crisis.

Rejection

The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected nullification, from Ableman v. Booth (1859) to Cooper v. Aaron (1958), which held that states cannot nullify federal law or Supreme Court interpretations of the Constitution. The outcome of the Civil War also settled the question of secession in practice. Nullification arguments have nonetheless recurred in debates over federal authority, and related concepts such as jury nullification remain distinct legal topics.
