The Russian Civil War (Russian: Гражданская война в России, Grazhdanskaya voyna v Rossii) was a multi-party civil war in the former Russian Empire sparked by the overthrowing of the Provisional Government in the October Revolution, as many factions vied to determine Russia's political future. It lasted from late 1917 (or 1918) until 1922, with the main fighting ending in 1920.

The two largest combatants were the Red Army, fighting for the Bolshevik form of socialism, and the loosely allied forces known as the White Army, which included monarchists, conservatives, and liberal anti-socialists. In addition, rival militant socialists, national independence movements, and peasant "Green" armies fought against both, while foreign powers — including the Allied expeditionary forces in North Russia and Siberia, and Japanese forces in the Far East — intervened.

Course of the war

After seizing power in Petrograd in November 1917 and signing the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk with Germany (March 1918), the Bolsheviks faced armed opposition on multiple fronts. The Czechoslovak Legion's revolt in May 1918 ignited full-scale war. White armies advanced under Kolchak from Siberia, Denikin and later Wrangel from the south, and Yudenich from the northwest, nearly capturing Moscow and Petrograd in 1919. Leon Trotsky built the Red Army into a mass conscript force of some five million, directed from the interior lines of central Russia. The Red Terror and the White Terror accompanied the fighting, alongside pogroms and mass executions.

By late 1920 mainland fighting had largely ended: Wrangel's army was evacuated from Crimea, Kolchak was captured and shot, and the Polish–Soviet War closed with the Treaty of Riga (1921). Vladivostok fell in October 1922, generally taken as the war's end; Basmachi resistance in Central Asia continued into the 1930s.

Consequences

The war, together with the famine of 1921–22, killed an estimated 7–12 million people, devastated the economy, and drove roughly two million into emigration. The Bolsheviks emerged in control of most of the former empire, forming the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics in December 1922, but Poland, Finland, and the Baltic states secured independence. War Communism's grain requisitioning provoked peasant revolts such as the Tambov Rebellion, prompting Lenin's retreat to the New Economic Policy in 1921.
