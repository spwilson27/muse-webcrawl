Playing possum

A Virginia opossum feigning death

Playing possum is an idiomatic phrase meaning to pretend to be dead or asleep, derived from the involuntary defensive behaviour of the Virginia opossum (Didelphis virginiana). The technical term for the behaviour is thanatosis, or apparent death: a state of tonic immobility that an animal enters in response to an extreme threat, which in the opossum can last from several minutes to several hours.

The behaviour

When a Virginia opossum is confronted by a predator and escape or intimidation (hissing, baring its fifty teeth, drooling) fails, it may collapse onto its side, mouth open, tongue lolling, eyes staring and unresponsive. The opossum also emits a foul-smelling greenish fluid from its anal glands that mimics the odour of decay. Its body temperature and heart rate drop, and it does not respond even to being carried or prodded. The behaviour is not conscious deception: it is an involuntary, coma-like stress response governed by the autonomic nervous system, more akin to fainting than to acting.

The strategy works because many predators are stimulated to attack moving prey and avoid carcasses that might be diseased. A predator that loses interest and leaves gives the opossum a chance to recover and escape.

Thanatosis in other animals

Death-feigning occurs across the animal kingdom. Hognose snakes roll onto their backs, mouths agape, and musk. Many beetles, grasshoppers and other insects drop and lie motionless when disturbed. Some cichlid fish lie on their sides on lake bottoms — though in that case as an aggressive mimicry lure for scavengers rather than a defence. Certain sharks and rays enter tonic immobility when inverted, a state exploited by orcas and by researchers handling the animals.

In language and culture

The phrase entered American English in the 19th century from the opossum's reputation. "Playing possum" now describes any person who feigns ignorance, sleep or incapacity to avoid attention or obligation. The behaviour also features in folklore and cartoons, where the opossum is a stock trickster-survivor figure, notably in the Uncle Remus stories of the American South.
