The Kurgan hypothesis (also known as the Kurgan theory, Kurgan model, or steppe theory) is the most widely accepted proposal to identify the Proto-Indo-European homeland, from which the Indo-European languages spread out across Europe and parts of Asia. It postulates that the people of a "Kurgan culture" in the Pontic steppe, north of the Black Sea, were the speakers of the Proto-Indo-European language.

The term derives from the Russian word kurgan (курган), meaning a tumulus or burial mound. The hypothesis was introduced and developed in the 1950s by the Lithuanian-American archaeologist Marija Gimbutas, and was later refined extensively by scholars including David Anthony.

The Kurgan cultures

Gimbutas grouped a succession of Eneolithic and Early Bronze Age cultures of the steppe under the label "Kurgan culture", characterised by burial beneath mounds, a pastoral economy based on cattle and sheep, the domestication of the horse, wheeled vehicles, and a patriarchal, mobile social structure. The principal horizons include the Sredny Stog culture (c. 4500–3500 BCE) and especially the Yamnaya culture (c. 3300–2600 BCE), whose pit-grave burials under kurgans, accompanied by wagons and animal sacrifices, mark the mature steppe tradition.

Expansion

Under the hypothesis, steppe pastoralists expanded in several waves between roughly 4000 and 1000 BCE. Westward migrations are linked with the spread of Corded Ware culture across northern and central Europe (c. 2900–2350 BCE) and later the Bell Beaker phenomenon; eastward, the Sintashta and Andronovo cultures are associated with the spread of Indo-Iranian languages, eventually reaching the Indian subcontinent as Indo-Aryan. Southeastward movements carried early forms of Greek, Armenian and other languages into the Balkans and Anatolia, though the route and timing of the Anatolian branch (Hittite and relatives) remain debated, since Anatolian appears to have diverged before the classic Yamnaya horizon.

Evidence and support

The hypothesis draws on linguistic palaeontology — reconstructed Proto-Indo-European vocabulary for wheels, axles, yokes, horses and sheep implies a homeland after about 4000 BCE in a region where those technologies and animals coexisted — combined with archaeology. Since 2015, ancient DNA studies have provided powerful corroboration: large-scale studies showed that the Corded Ware populations of Europe carried substantial ancestry derived from Yamnaya steppe herders, indicating a major migration from the steppe into Europe in the early 3rd millennium BCE. Steppe-related ancestry has also been detected in Bronze Age South Asia, consistent with the arrival of Indo-Aryan languages.

Rival hypotheses

The main alternatives are the Anatolian hypothesis, championed by Colin Renfrew, which places the homeland in Neolithic Anatolia and ties the spread of the languages to the spread of farming from around 7000 BCE, and the Armenian plateau hypothesis of Gamkrelidze and Ivanov. The Anatolian model has struggled to accommodate the wheel vocabulary and, more recently, the genetic evidence of a steppe migration; however, a refined "hybrid" view — in which the earliest Indo-European divergences occurred south of the Caucasus, with the steppe as a secondary homeland — has been proposed in recent years to account for the Anatolian branch.
