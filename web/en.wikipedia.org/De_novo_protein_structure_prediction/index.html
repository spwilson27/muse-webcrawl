De novo protein structure prediction

In computational biology, de novo protein structure prediction refers to an algorithmic process in which protein tertiary structure is predicted from its primary amino acid sequence alone, without using a solved structure of a homologous protein as a template. It is contrasted with template-based modelling (homology modelling and threading), and is also called ab initio prediction.

Approach

De novo methods typically combine two elements: an energy function that scores candidate conformations, approximating the physical or statistical likelihood of a fold, and a search strategy that explores conformational space. Many successful strategies use fragment assembly: short fragments (typically 3 and 9 residues) are drawn from known structures whose sequences resemble segments of the target, and are assembled by Monte Carlo simulation, followed by refinement.

The theoretical basis of the approach is Anfinsen's dogma — the native structure of a protein is determined by its amino acid sequence and corresponds to a global minimum of free energy — together with Levinthal's paradox, which shows that an exhaustive search of conformations is impossible and sampling must be biased or heuristic.

Notable methods and progress

  • Rosetta — developed by David Baker's laboratory; fragment assembly plus an all-atom energy function; also deployed at scale through Rosetta@home.
  • QUARK, I-TASSER (in part), and other fragment-assembly servers.
  • CASP — the biennial Critical Assessment of Structure Prediction experiment, whose "free modelling" category benchmarks de novo methods.

Classical de novo prediction worked best for small proteins. From 2018 onwards, deep-learning methods that exploit co-evolutionary information in multiple sequence alignments — culminating in AlphaFold and AlphaFold2, which performed exceptionally at CASP13 and CASP14 — transformed the field, largely superseding classical de novo approaches for most targets.
