Omertà

OriginSouthern Italy (Sicily, Calabria, Campania)
MeaningCode of silence and non-cooperation with authorities
Associated withMafia organizations (Cosa Nostra, 'Ndrangheta, Camorra)

Omertà is a code of honour and silence originating in southern Italy that forbids cooperation with authorities or outsiders, particularly regarding criminal activities, and obliges individuals to settle grievances without recourse to the law. It is a cornerstone of the cultures of the Mafia and similar organizations.

Origins

The word's etymology is uncertain; it is often linked to Sicilian omu ("man") and the virtue of manliness, or to a corruption of umiltà ("humility/submission"). The code developed in a society where state authority was distant, distrusted, or absent, and where self-help and clan loyalty filled the gap. Refusing to inform — even about one's enemies — was a matter of honour, while informers (infami) faced ostracism or death.

Breakdown and pentiti

From the 1980s, Italian anti-Mafia prosecutions led by judges such as Giovanni Falcone and Paolo Borsellino induced a number of mafiosi — the pentiti, most famously Tommaso Buscetta — to break omertà and testify, enabling the Maxi Trial convictions. The Mafia's retaliatory assassinations of Falcone and Borsellino in 1992 provoked a national backlash and stronger state action. Scholars debate how far omertà reflected peasant society at large versus the Mafia's own imposed discipline.
