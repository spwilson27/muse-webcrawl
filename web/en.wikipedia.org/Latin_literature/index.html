Latin literature comprises the body of writing in the Latin language produced by the Romans and their successors, from the 3rd century BC through antiquity and the Middle Ages, and continuing as Neo-Latin into the Renaissance and early modern era. It began under strong Greek influence — its conventional starting point is the first Roman play, a translation from Greek by Livius Andronicus in 240 BC — but developed distinctive Roman voices in epic, lyric, satire (a genre the Romans claimed as wholly their own), historiography, oratory, philosophy, and the novel.

Periods

Early Latin (to c. 70 BC) includes the comedies of Plautus and Terence, the epic of Ennius, and the prose of Cato. The Golden Age (Ciceronian and Augustan periods) produced Cicero's speeches and philosophy, Caesar's commentaries, the poetry of Virgil (the Aeneid), Horace, Ovid, Tibullus, and Propertius, Sallust's and Livy's histories. The Silver Age (1st–2nd centuries AD) brought Seneca, Lucan, Statius, Martial, Quintilian, Tacitus, Juvenal, Suetonius, Pliny, and Apuleius's novel The Golden Ass.

Later Latin

In late antiquity Latin literature turned increasingly Christian (Augustine, Jerome, Boethius), while remaining the language of learning, law, and liturgy throughout the medieval West. Medieval Latin encompassed chronicles, scholastic philosophy, hymns, and the Goliardic songs of the Carmina Burana; Renaissance humanists revived classical style, and Latin remained the medium of science and scholarship into the age of Newton and Linnaeus. Because nearly all surviving texts passed through monastic copying, much ancient Latin literature — including most of Livy and most Republican drama — has been lost.
