Vitamin A deficiency

Vitamin A deficiency (VAD) or hypovitaminosis A is a lack of vitamin A in blood and tissues. It is common in poorer countries, especially among children and women of reproductive age, but is rarely seen in more developed countries. Vitamin A deficiency is the leading cause of preventable childhood blindness.

Signs and symptoms

Nyctalopia (night blindness) is one of the first signs. Prolonged deficiency causes xerophthalmia: the conjunctiva and cornea become dry, Bitot's spots (foamy patches on the conjunctiva) appear, and the cornea may ulcerate and soften (keratomalacia), leading to irreversible blindness. Vitamin A is also essential for immune function and growth, so deficiency increases the severity of infections such as measles and diarrhoea.

Causes

Primary deficiency results from prolonged dietary deprivation — diets dominated by polished rice or other staples low in vitamin A and provitamin-A carotenoids, with little animal-source food. Secondary deficiency arises from malabsorption of fats, liver disorders, or increased excretion during illness.

Epidemiology and prevention

The World Health Organization estimates that some 250 million preschool children are vitamin A deficient, and that deficiency causes blindness in 250,000–500,000 children each year, half of whom die within a year of losing their sight. Periodic high-dose vitamin A supplementation for children under five, fortification of foods such as sugar and oil, promotion of vitamin-A-rich foods, and biofortified crops such as orange-fleshed sweet potato (and the controversial Golden Rice) are the main preventive strategies.
