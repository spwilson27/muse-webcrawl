Shechita

Shechita (Hebrew: שחיטה, [ʃχiˈta]) is the ritual slaughter of animals for food as prescribed in Jewish law (halakha). A trained ritual slaughterer, the shochet, kills a permitted mammal or bird with a single, swift, uninterrupted cut across the throat using an exceptionally sharp knife (a chalaf) checked to be free of nicks, severing the trachea, oesophagus and the major blood vessels, so that the animal bleeds out rapidly. Meat from animals not slaughtered by shechita is not kosher (it is classed as nevelah).

Procedure and laws

The laws of shechita derive from the oral tradition codified in the Talmudic tractate Hullin and later codes. Five actions invalidate the slaughter: pausing during the cut (shehiyyah), pressing the knife down rather than drawing it (derasah), burying the blade under tissue (haladah), cutting outside the prescribed region of the neck (hagramah), and tearing rather than cutting the vessels (ikkur). Before slaughter a blessing is recited. Afterward the shochet inspects the internal organs — especially the lungs of cattle — for defects that would render the animal terefah (unfit); stringent "glatt kosher" standards require lungs free of adhesions. Because the sciatic nerve and certain fats are forbidden and difficult to remove, in most communities the hindquarters of cattle are not sold as kosher (the process of removing them is called nikkur, or porging). Residual blood is removed by soaking and salting (melichah) or by broiling.

Training and supervision

A shochet must be an observant, learned Jew trained and examined in the laws of shechita and knife preparation, licensed by rabbinic authority, and subject to periodic re-examination. Commercial shechita is carried out under the supervision of kashrut agencies whose inspectors (mashgichim) oversee the slaughter, inspection and labelling.

Debate and regulation

Traditional shechita does not include pre-stunning, and its animal-welfare status is debated: supporters cite the speed of the cut and rapid loss of consciousness from blood loss, while critics and some veterinary bodies argue stunning before cutting is more humane. Several European countries and regions have at various times restricted or banned slaughter without stunning, generating litigation involving religious-freedom claims by Jewish and Muslim communities (Islamic dhabihah raises parallel issues). Some halakhic authorities permit post-cut stunning.
