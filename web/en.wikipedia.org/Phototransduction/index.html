Phototransduction

Phototransduction (visual phototransduction) is the process by which photoreceptor cells of the retina — rods and cones — convert light into electrical signals. It is a classic G-protein-coupled receptor cascade and one of the best-understood sensory transduction pathways.

The cascade

In darkness, cyclic GMP (cGMP) keeps sodium channels in the photoreceptor outer segment open, maintaining a "dark current." When a photon is absorbed by the visual pigment (rhodopsin in rods), the activated pigment stimulates the G protein transducin, which activates a phosphodiesterase that hydrolyzes cGMP. Falling cGMP closes the channels, hyperpolarizing the cell and reducing its release of the neurotransmitter glutamate — the signal that light has arrived.

Amplification and adaptation

The cascade provides enormous amplification: one photoactivated rhodopsin can lead to the hydrolysis of many cGMP molecules, letting rods signal the absorption of a single photon. Calcium feedback mechanisms restore cGMP levels and terminate the response, enabling adaptation over a wide range of light intensities. Defects in phototransduction genes cause diseases such as retinitis pigmentosa.
