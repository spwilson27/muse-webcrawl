Derrick Albert Bell Jr. (November 6, 1930 – October 5, 2011) was an American lawyer, professor and civil rights activist. A pioneering scholar of race and law, Bell is regarded as one of the founders of critical race theory. He was the first tenured African American professor of law at Harvard Law School, and later served as dean of the University of Oregon School of Law.

Early life and career

Bell was born in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. He graduated from Duquesne University in 1952 and served as an officer in the United States Air Force during the Korean War. He earned his law degree from the University of Pittsburgh School of Law in 1957. After graduation, Bell joined the Civil Rights Division of the U.S. Department of Justice; he resigned in 1959 after being told his membership in the NAACP was a conflict of interest. He then worked for the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund in Mississippi, where he handled hundreds of civil rights cases, including school desegregation matters arising in the wake of Brown v. Board of Education.

Academic career

In 1969 Bell joined the Harvard Law School faculty; in 1971 he became its first Black tenured professor. His 1973 casebook Race, Racism and American Law was a landmark work that placed race at the centre of American legal analysis. In 1980 he left Harvard to become dean of the University of Oregon School of Law, returning to Harvard in 1986.

In 1990 Bell took an unpaid leave of absence to protest Harvard Law School's failure to grant tenure to any Black women faculty members, a protest that led to his departure from the school. He later taught at New York University School of Law as a visiting professor. Among his students at Harvard were Barack Obama, who as a law student participated in demonstrations supporting Bell, and the legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw, who helped develop the theory of intersectionality.

Ideas

Interest convergence

In his celebrated 1980 Harvard Law Review article, "Brown v. Board of Education and the Interest-Convergence Dilemma," Bell argued that the Supreme Court's desegregation ruling succeeded not chiefly because of a moral awakening, but because the interests of white elites — in Cold War credibility, economic modernisation of the South, and placating Black veterans — converged with the interests of Black Americans seeking relief from segregation. Interest convergence became a foundational tenet of critical race theory.

Racial realism and storytelling

Bell argued for "racial realism": the view that racism is a permanent feature of American society, which should be confronted honestly rather than wished away. His allegorical fiction — including the "Space Traders" story in Faces at the Bottom of the Well (1992), in which America's leaders consider trading Black citizens to aliens in exchange for material benefits — used counter-storytelling to probe the depth of racial subordination. Other works include And We Are Not Saved (1987), featuring the fictional civil rights lawyer Geneva Crenshaw, and Silent Covenants (2004).

Death and legacy

Bell died of carcinoid cancer on October 5, 2011, aged 80. His critique of liberal legalism and his scholarship on the permanence of racism remain central reference points in legal academia, and critical race theory has become both an influential field of study and a subject of political controversy in the United States.
