Six Sigma

Six Sigma Origin: Motorola, 1986
Key figures: Bill Smith; Mikel Harry
Methodologies: DMAIC; DMADV
Target: 3.4 defects per million opportunities

Six Sigma (6σ) is a set of techniques and tools for process improvement. It was developed by Motorola engineer Bill Smith in 1986. Six Sigma strategies seek to improve manufacturing quality by identifying and removing the causes of defects and minimising variability in manufacturing and business processes. This is done by using a set of quality management methods, mainly empirical and statistical, and by creating a special infrastructure of people within the organisation — "Champions", "Black Belts", "Green Belts" and so on — who are experts in these methods.

Methodology

Six Sigma projects follow one of two five-phase methodologies. DMAIC (Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control) is used to improve existing processes, while DMADV (Define, Measure, Analyse, Design, Verify) is used to create new product or process designs. A process that operates at six sigma quality produces no more than 3.4 defects per million opportunities, a figure that incorporates a conventional 1.5-sigma shift of the process mean.

History and adoption

Motorola registered Six Sigma as a service mark in 1991 and credited it with saving the company billions of dollars. The approach was famously adopted by Jack Welch at General Electric in 1995 and spread through manufacturing and later into services, healthcare and finance. Lean Six Sigma combines the method with lean manufacturing principles aimed at eliminating waste.

Criticism

Critics have argued that Six Sigma's reported savings are difficult to verify, that its belt certification lacks a standardising body or agreed curriculum, and that an excessive focus on incremental improvement can stifle innovation. Some studies of adopting firms have found mixed effects on financial performance.
