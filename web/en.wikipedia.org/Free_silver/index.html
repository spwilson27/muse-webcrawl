Free silver

Free silver was a major political and economic movement in the United States during the late 19th century, advocating that the federal government accept silver bullion on demand at the U.S. Mint and convert it into legal tender money at a fixed ratio to gold — "free and unlimited coinage of silver at 16 to 1." Supporters believed this bimetallic standard would expand the money supply, raise crop prices, ease the burden of farm debts in an era of deflation, and counter the effects of the gold standard.

Background

The Coinage Act of 1873 ended the minting of standard silver dollars, a step silver supporters later denounced as the "Crime of 1873." As prices fell through the 1870s–1890s, indebted farmers — especially in the South and West — blamed the tight gold-based money supply. Congress compromised with the Bland–Allison Act (1878) and the Sherman Silver Purchase Act (1890), requiring limited federal silver purchases, but these stopped short of free coinage. The Panic of 1893 led President Grover Cleveland to secure the repeal of the Sherman Act, splitting the Democratic Party.

The election of 1896

Free silver reached its climax in the presidential election of 1896. William Jennings Bryan, the Democratic and Populist nominee, electrified the Democratic convention with his "Cross of Gold" speech, declaring that mankind should not be crucified "upon a cross of gold." Bryan lost to Republican William McKinley, and again in 1900. The Gold Standard Act of 1900 formally placed the United States on gold, and new gold discoveries plus rising prices removed the grievances that had fueled the movement.
