Charon

Charon (Ancient Greek: Χάρων) is a figure of Greek mythology, the ferryman of the dead who carried the souls of the newly deceased across the rivers Styx and Acheron into Hades, the underworld. In payment he required the coin — an obol or danake — placed in or on the mouth of the dead; those who could not pay were left to wander the shores for a hundred years.

Mythology

Charon is described in ancient sources as a morose, filthy old man with a fiery gaze, poling his skiff across the waters of the underworld. He appears in Aristophanes' Frogs, in Book 6 of Virgil's Aeneid, where the Sibyl must show him the golden bough to secure passage for Aeneas, and throughout Dante's Inferno, where he ferries Dante and Virgil across the Acheron. In some traditions he is the son of Erebus and Nyx.

In art and culture

Charon's boat is a frequent subject of classical and Renaissance art, including works by Joachim Patinir and Gustave Doré's engravings for Dante. The largest moon of the dwarf planet Pluto, discovered in 1978, is named Charon after the ferryman — a naming influenced both by the myth and by the discoverer James Christy's wife, Charlene.
