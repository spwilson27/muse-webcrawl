Fujiwara Clan

The Fujiwara clan was a powerful family of imperial regents in Japan. The clan, founded by Fujiwara no Kamatari in the 7th century, dominated Japanese politics during the Heian period (794–1185) by marrying its daughters into the imperial family and ruling as regents (sesshō and kampaku) for child and adult emperors.

Rise to power

Fujiwara no Kamatari received the Fujiwara surname for his role in the Taika Reform of 645. His descendants, particularly the Hokke (Northern) branch, gradually monopolised high office. Fujiwara no Yoshifusa became the first regent for a child emperor in 858, and Fujiwara no Mototsune established the office of kampaku for adult emperors. The clan's power peaked under Fujiwara no Michinaga (966–1027), whose daughters married emperors and whose sons governed the court; he famously wrote that the world was his to command.

Decline and legacy

Fujiwara dominance eroded in the late 11th century when Emperor Go-Sanjō, whose mother was not a Fujiwara, introduced rule by retired emperors (insei). After the Hōgen and Heiji disturbances and the rise of the warrior class, real power shifted to the shogunate, but the Fujiwara retained prestige and continued to supply regents and court officials for centuries. The five regent houses (go-sekke) descended from the Fujiwara provided sesshō and kampaku into the 19th century.
