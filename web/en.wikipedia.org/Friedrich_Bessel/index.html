Friedrich Bessel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel (22 July 1784 – 17 March 1846) was a German astronomer, mathematician, physicist, and geodesist. He was the first person to measure the distance to a star other than the Sun by the method of stellar parallax, and he systematised the mathematical functions that now bear his name — the Bessel functions — which arose in his study of planetary motion.

Early life

Bessel was born in Minden, in what is now North Rhine-Westphalia. Largely self-taught, he worked as an apprentice in a trading house in Bremen while studying astronomy and mathematics at night. His calculation of the orbit of Halley's Comet brought him to the attention of Heinrich Wilhelm Olbers, who helped him obtain a post at the Lilienthal Observatory.

Astronomical career

In 1810, at the age of 25, Bessel was appointed director of the new Königsberg Observatory by King Frederick William III of Prussia, and professor of astronomy at the University of Königsberg. He spent the rest of his career there, producing a catalogue of the positions of more than 60,000 stars.

In 1838 Bessel announced that the star 61 Cygni showed a parallax of about 0.314 arcseconds, implying a distance of roughly 10.3 light-years — remarkably close to the modern value. This was the first successful measurement of the distance to a fixed star, a landmark in astronomy. He also predicted, from irregularities in the motion of Sirius and Procyon, that these stars had unseen companions; Sirius B was observed in 1862, after his death.

Mathematics and geodesy

Bessel's work on planetary perturbations led him to develop the functions Jn(x) that solve Bessel's differential equation; they are now fundamental in physics and engineering. In statistics, Bessel's correction — dividing by n − 1 rather than n when estimating variance — is named after him. He also carried out a major geodetic survey of East Prussia and refined the figure of the Earth; the Bessel ellipsoid of 1841 was used as a reference for over a century.

Legacy

The lunar crater Bessel and the asteroid 1552 Bessel are named in his honour, as is the Bessel filter in signal processing.
