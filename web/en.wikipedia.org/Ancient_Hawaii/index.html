Ancient Hawaii

Ancient Hawaii is the period of Hawaiian human history preceding the unification of the Kingdom of Hawaiʻi by Kamehameha the Great in 1795. Traditionally, researchers believed the first settlement of the Hawaiian Islands was by Polynesian long-distance navigators from the Marquesas Islands, arriving sometime before or around 1000 CE; more recent studies place initial settlement around 1000–1200 CE. A second wave of migration from Tahiti in the 14th century is thought to have introduced new religious and social structures.

Settlement and society

Polynesian voyagers brought with them the plants and animals of the "canoe" assemblage: taro, sweet potato, breadfruit, coconut, pigs, dogs, and chickens. Hawaiian society developed into a highly stratified chiefdom system, with society divided between aliʻi (chiefs), kahuna (priests and experts), makaʻāinana (commoners), and kauā (outcasts). Land was managed in units called ahupuaʻa, wedge-shaped divisions running from the mountains to the sea.

Religion and the kapu system

Ancient Hawaiian religion was polytheistic, centred on four great gods — Kū, Kāne, Lono, and Kanaloa — along with many lesser deities and ancestral spirits (ʻaumākua). Daily life was governed by the kapu system, a strict code of religious and social prohibitions regulating everything from fishing seasons to the separation of men and women at meals. Great temples called heiau were built for worship and, in the case of the luakini, human sacrifice.

Agriculture and engineering

Hawaiians developed sophisticated irrigation for taro pondfields (loʻi kalo), dryland field systems such as the Kona and Kohala field systems, and fishponds (loko iʻa) that practised a form of aquaculture unique in Polynesia. These supported population growth that may have reached several hundred thousand by the time of European contact.

Contact and unification

Captain James Cook arrived in 1778, the first documented European contact. In the decades that followed, Kamehameha I used Western weapons and advisors to conquer and unify most of the islands by 1795, founding the Kingdom of Hawaiʻi (the island of Kauaʻi joined by agreement in 1810). The kapu system was abolished in 1819, shortly after Kamehameha's death.
