Julia Pastrana (1834 – 25 March 1860) was a Mexican entertainer who toured the United States and Europe in the mid-19th century. Born with the genetic conditions hypertrichosis terminalis — her face and body were covered with straight black hair — and gingival hyperplasia, which thickened her lips and gums, she was exhibited in freak shows under degrading billing such as "the Ape Woman". Offstage she was accomplished: she sang, danced, and spoke several languages.

Life

Pastrana was born in Sinaloa, Mexico, reportedly of Indigenous descent. She was brought into the household of Pedro Sanchez, a former governor of Sinaloa, as a servant, and in 1854 left for the United States, where she was exhibited in New York and other cities. She married her manager, Theodore Lent, around 1857, and toured with him in Britain and continental Europe, performing songs and dances in multiple costumes. Contemporary medical men examined her; Charles Darwin later cited her case in The Variation of Animals and Plants Under Domestication (1868).

Death and posthumous exhibition

While touring in Moscow, Pastrana gave birth in March 1860 to a son who shared her conditions and died within about two days; Pastrana died of complications of childbirth on 25 March 1860. Lent had both bodies mummified and continued to exhibit them in a glass cabinet for years; he later married another woman with hypertrichosis and billed her as Pastrana's sister. The mummies passed through fairs and collections, were displayed in Norway, and eventually spent decades in storage at the University of Oslo.

Repatriation

In the 21st century, artists and campaigners — notably the Mexican artist Laura Anderson Barbata — pressed for her dignified burial. In February 2013, after approval by the University of Oslo and the Norwegian authorities, Pastrana's remains were returned to Mexico and buried in Sinaloa de Leyva, Sinaloa, more than 150 years after her death. Her story has inspired plays, an opera project by Shaun Prendergast, and continuing discussion of the ethics of human exhibition.
