Fertility is the capacity to produce offspring. In demography, the term is used to measure actual childbearing — most commonly through the total fertility rate (TFR), the average number of children a woman would have over her lifetime at prevailing age-specific birth rates. In biology and medicine, fertility is distinguished from fecundity, the physiological potential for reproduction; a person or population may have high fecundity but low realised fertility because of social, economic or contraceptive factors.

Demography

Global fertility has fallen dramatically since the mid-20th century, when the world average was about five children per woman; by the early 21st century it had declined to roughly 2.3–2.4, close to the replacement level of about 2.1 in low-mortality populations. Fertility remains highest in sub-Saharan Africa and lowest in East Asia and parts of Europe, where rates below 1.5 — and in South Korea below 1.0 — have raised concerns about population ageing and decline. The demographic transition model explains falling fertility as a consequence of declining mortality, urbanisation, education (especially of women), contraception and the changing economics of children.

Factors affecting fertility

Biological fertility in women peaks in the early twenties and declines with age, particularly after 35, ending at menopause; male fertility declines more gradually. Health factors include sexually transmitted infections, endocrine disorders, obesity and environmental exposures. Social determinants of population fertility include age at marriage, contraceptive prevalence, abortion availability, female labour-force participation, housing and childcare costs, and cultural norms about family size.

Infertility

Infertility is generally defined as failure to achieve pregnancy after twelve months of regular unprotected intercourse and affects roughly one in six adults worldwide at some point in their lives. Causes are distributed between male factors, female factors, combined causes and unexplained cases. Treatments range from medication and surgery to assisted reproductive technologies such as intrauterine insemination and in vitro fertilisation (IVF).

Soil fertility

In agriculture, "fertility" also denotes soil fertility: the ability of soil to sustain plant growth, determined by its content of nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, its organic matter, pH, structure and microbial activity. Fertilisers — synthetic or organic — are applied to maintain or restore soil fertility under cultivation.
