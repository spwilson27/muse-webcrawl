Quaternary extinction event

Late Quaternary extinctions
Intervalc. 50,000 – 10,000 years ago (main pulses); continuing on islands later
Most affectedMegafauna — animals over ~44 kg
Proposed causesHuman hunting, climate change, or a combination

The Quaternary extinction event, also called the Late Pleistocene or Late Quaternary extinctions, is the wave of extinctions of mostly large terrestrial animals (megafauna) that occurred during the late Quaternary period, roughly within the last 50,000 years. Unlike the "Big Five" mass extinctions, it selectively removed large land animals while leaving most small animals, marine life, and plants comparatively untouched.

Extinct megafauna of the late Pleistocene

Extinct fauna

Victims included the woolly mammoth and mastodons, woolly rhinoceros, giant ground sloths, glyptodonts, the giant deer (Irish elk), sabre-toothed cats, the American lion, dire wolves, giant kangaroos and Diprotodon in Australia, and the giant flightless moa and Haast's eagle in New Zealand. North and South America lost the great majority of their large mammal genera; Australia lost nearly all of its largest species; Eurasia and Africa were affected more moderately. Island extinctions continued into the Holocene — the mammoths of Wrangel Island survived until about 4,000 years ago, and the moa were exterminated within a few centuries of Polynesian settlement of New Zealand.

Causes

Human hunting ("overkill")

The hypothesis, prominently advanced by Paul S. Martin, holds that newly arriving human hunters caused the extinctions by killing naïve prey faster than they could reproduce, possibly aided by fire and habitat alteration. Extinction pulses on each landmass do follow closely after first human arrival: Australia about 50,000 years ago, the Americas around the end of the last glacial period, and oceanic islands within the last few thousand years.

Climate change

Other researchers attribute the extinctions primarily to the dramatic climate swings at the end of the last Ice Age, including the abrupt Younger Dryas cold reversal, which fragmented habitats and shrank the mammoth steppe. Critics note, however, that megafauna had survived many previous glacial cycles.

The prevailing view in recent research is that the extinctions resulted from a combination in which human hunting pressure fell on populations already stressed by rapid climate change. A proposed impact event around 12,900 years ago remains controversial.

Relation to the Holocene extinction

Some scientists regard the Late Quaternary extinctions as the beginning of the ongoing Holocene extinction, driven by human activity, which continues today and disproportionately affects large animals.
