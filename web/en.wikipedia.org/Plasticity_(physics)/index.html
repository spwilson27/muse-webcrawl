In physics and materials science, plasticity (also known as plastic deformation) is the ability of a solid material to undergo permanent deformation, a non-reversible change of shape in response to applied forces. For example, a solid piece of metal being bent or pounded into a new shape displays plasticity as permanent changes occur within the material itself.

Elastic and plastic deformation

Most materials exhibit elastic behaviour at low stresses: deformation is proportional to stress (Hooke's law) and is recovered when the load is removed. Beyond the yield point, deformation becomes partly permanent. The transition is described by a yield criterion, such as the Tresca or von Mises criterion, which defines the combinations of stress at which plastic flow begins.

Mechanisms

In crystalline materials, plasticity is primarily the result of dislocations — line defects in the crystal lattice — moving through the material on slip planes, and of deformation twinning. In amorphous materials such as polymers and glasses, plastic flow involves the sliding and rearrangement of molecular chains. The motion of dislocations explains why the observed strength of metals is far below the theoretical strength of a perfect crystal.

Strain hardening and necking

As plastic deformation proceeds, the density of dislocations increases and they impede one another's motion, so the material becomes stronger and harder — a phenomenon called strain hardening or work hardening. In tension, deformation eventually localizes in a neck, and fracture follows. Annealing — heating the material — can restore ductility by allowing recrystallization.

Mathematical description

The theory of plasticity was developed by scientists including Tresca, Saint-Venant, Lévy, von Mises, Prandtl and Reuss. It is built on a yield function, a flow rule relating stress to plastic strain rate, and a hardening rule. Unlike deformation theory, flow theory is incremental and path-dependent, which makes plastic behaviour inherently history-dependent.

Applications

Plasticity is exploited in metal forming processes such as forging, rolling, extrusion and stamping, and it is central to structural engineering, where controlled plastic deformation provides ductility and warning before failure. It also underlies the study of soil and rock deformation in geotechnical engineering and tectonics.
