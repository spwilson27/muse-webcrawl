Roll film

Roll film or rollfilm is any type of spool-wound photographic film protected from white light exposure by a backing material linked to the wrap, usually paper or plastic. Unlike sheet film, roll film is wound on a spool and advanced frame by frame through the camera, allowing multiple exposures without reloading in darkness. The term originated in contrast to the photographic plate films and sheet films used in earlier cameras.

History

George Eastman's Kodak company popularised roll film with the Kodak camera of 1888, which came preloaded with a roll for 100 exposures and was returned to the factory for processing under the slogan "You press the button, we do the rest." Early roll film consisted of emulsion on a paper backing ("stripping film"), later replaced by transparent celluloid base. The introduction of standardised formats made roll film the dominant amateur and much professional medium for a century.

Formats

Common formats include 120 film (introduced 1901), still widely used in medium format cameras; 220, its longer double-length variant without full backing paper; 127; and 35mm film, technically a roll film in cassettes, which became the standard for miniature cameras. Disc film and 110 cartridge film (1972) adapted the roll concept to sealed cartridges for simple loading. Although digital photography displaced roll film for most uses, 120 and 35mm formats continue to be manufactured and enjoyed a revival among film enthusiasts.
