Jig

The jig (Irish: port) is a lively folk dance and a corresponding dance tune, with a distinctive rhythm, strongly associated with the traditional music of Ireland and Scotland. The word "jig" also refers to the dance music form in compound metre — most often 6/8 time.


Irish jig dancers

History

The jig developed in England and Ireland in the 16th century; the term derives from the French gigue. During the Baroque period the gigue became a standard movement of the dance suite, used by composers such as Bach and Handel, usually as the energetic final movement.

Types

In Irish tradition, dances and tunes are categorised by metre: the double jig is in 6/8, the slip jig in 9/8 (danced mostly by women in soft shoes), the single jig in 6/8 with a simpler rhythm, and the treble jig danced in hard shoes. Scottish and English traditions have their own variants, and the jig is central to the competitive Irish stepdance tradition.

Music

Jig tunes are typically built of two eight-bar phrases played on fiddle, flute, tin whistle, accordion or uilleann pipes, often in sessions alongside reels (in 4/4). Classic jigs include "The Irish Washerwoman" and "Morrison's Jig".
