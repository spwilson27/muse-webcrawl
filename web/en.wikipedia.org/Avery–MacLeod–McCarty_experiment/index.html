Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiment

The Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiment was a landmark scientific experiment reported in 1944 by Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty at the Rockefeller Institute. It demonstrated that DNA is the substance that causes bacterial transformation — the phenomenon in which dead bacteria of one strain can transform living bacteria of another strain — and thus provided the first strong evidence that DNA is the molecule carrying genetic information.

Background

In 1928, Frederick Griffith had reported that a "transforming principle" from heat-killed virulent pneumococci (Streptococcus pneumoniae) could permanently change harmless bacteria into the virulent form. Avery and his colleagues spent over a decade identifying the chemical nature of this transforming principle.

The experiment

The researchers purified extracts from the heat-killed virulent (S) strain and systematically eliminated candidate components. Transformation activity survived treatment with proteases (destroying proteins), lipases, and RNase (destroying RNA), but was destroyed by DNase (destroying DNA). The purified active material had the chemical composition and physical properties of DNA, leading the team to conclude that DNA was the "fundamental unit of the transforming principle."

Reception and legacy

Although the experiment was rigorous, many scientists initially remained skeptical, believing proteins to be the more plausible carriers of heredity. The 1952 Hershey–Chase experiment provided further confirmation, and the discovery of the double helix in 1953 consolidated DNA's central role. The Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiment is now regarded as a foundational moment in molecular biology.
