Edward Hopper

Edward Hopper (22 July 1882 – 15 May 1967) was an American realist painter and printmaker, among the most significant American artists of the 20th century. His spare, light-filled scenes of modern life — diners, offices, gas stations, hotel rooms — capture a distinctive mood of solitude and quiet tension.

Nighthawks (1942), Hopper's most famous painting

Life

Born in Nyack, New York, Hopper studied illustration and painting at the New York School of Art under Robert Henri. He made three trips to Europe between 1906 and 1910 but remained largely independent of avant-garde movements, supporting himself as a commercial illustrator, which he disliked. His etchings of the early 1920s brought recognition, and in 1924 he married fellow artist Josephine Nivison, who became his manager and the model for nearly all his female figures. The couple lived frugally in a Washington Square studio and summered on Cape Cod.

Work

Hopper's mature paintings — Early Sunday Morning (1930), New York Office (1962), Gas (1940), Automat (1927), and above all Nighthawks (1942), with its lone customers in a fluorescent-lit diner — combine simplified forms, strong light and shadow, and cropped, cinematic viewpoints. Though often read as images of urban loneliness, Hopper resisted symbolic readings, saying he painted "my own reaction to my subject" as honestly as he could.

Legacy

A 1933 retrospective at the Museum of Modern Art and his long association with the Whitney Museum cemented his reputation. His influence extends through Pop art, photography, and cinema, whose directors from Hitchcock to Wim Wenders have quoted his compositions. The Whitney Museum holds the largest collection of his work, bequeathed by his widow.
