In competitive sports, doping is the use of banned athletic performance-enhancing drugs or methods by athletes. The term derives from dop, an alcoholic drink used as a stimulant in eighteenth-century South Africa. Anti-doping rules are justified by the dangers of the substances, the pursuit of a level playing field, and the ideal of sport free from artificial enhancement.

History

Performance enhancement is ancient — Greek Olympians used special diets and potions, and nineteenth-century endurance racers used caffeine, cocaine and strychnine. The modern era began with anabolic steroids: testosterone was isolated in the 1930s, Soviet weightlifters used it in the 1950s, and East Germany later ran a state-sponsored doping programme (State Plan 14.25). The death of cyclist Knud Enemark Jensen at the 1960 Olympics and of Tom Simpson on Mont Ventoux in 1967 spurred the IOC to begin drug testing in 1968. Ben Johnson's positive test after winning the 1988 Olympic 100 metres became the era's defining scandal.

Anti-doping system

The Festina affair at the 1998 Tour de France exposed the limits of federation self-regulation and led to the creation of the World Anti-Doping Agency in 1999. WADA maintains the World Anti-Doping Code and Prohibited List, accredits laboratories, and coordinates in- and out-of-competition testing, the Athlete Biological Passport and whereabouts requirements. High-profile investigations — the BALCO scandal, the Armstrong case culminating in the USADA reasoned decision of 2012, and the McLaren reports into Russian state-sponsored doping (2015–16) — reshaped enforcement.

Methods

Prohibited methods include blood doping and EPO to raise red-cell mass, anabolic steroids for strength, human growth hormone, masking agents and diuretics, stimulants, and gene doping, which is banned pre-emptively. Therapeutic use exemptions permit otherwise banned medicines for genuine medical conditions.
