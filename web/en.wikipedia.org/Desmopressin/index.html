Desmopressin

Desmopressin
Trade names: DDAVP, others
Class: Vasopressin analogue
Routes: Oral, intranasal, intravenous, subcutaneous
First synthesised: 1967

Desmopressin, sold under the trade name DDAVP among others, is a synthetic analogue of the natural hormone vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone). It was developed in the 1960s and first described in 1967. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Medical uses

Desmopressin is used to treat central diabetes insipidus, in which the kidneys produce excessive dilute urine; taken at night, it also treats primary nocturnal enuresis (bedwetting) in children. Because desmopressin raises plasma levels of factor VIII and von Willebrand factor, it is used to manage bleeding in mild haemophilia A and type 1 von Willebrand disease, and it may be given before surgery in patients with such disorders.

Mechanism of action

Compared with vasopressin, desmopressin acts predominantly at V2 receptors in the renal collecting ducts, increasing water reabsorption via aquaporin channels, and has greatly reduced pressor (V1) activity, so it raises blood pressure much less. Structural modifications — deamination of the first amino acid and substitution of D-arginine for L-arginine — also prolong its duration of action.

Adverse effects

The main risks are dilutional hyponatraemia and water intoxication if fluid intake is not restricted, which can rarely cause seizures; headaches, flushing and mild abdominal cramps also occur. Intranasal preparations used for bedwetting were withdrawn in some jurisdictions because of hyponatraemia risk.
