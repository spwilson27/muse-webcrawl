Concerto for Orchestra

A full symphony orchestra, the "soloist" of the concerto for orchestra genre.

Concerto for orchestra is a title used for orchestral compositions that treat the orchestra itself as a virtuoso soloist, giving prominent, demanding passages to individual instruments and sections rather than featuring a single solo instrument as in a traditional concerto. The genre flourished in the 20th century as composers explored the colouristic and technical possibilities of the modern symphony orchestra.

History

Although Paul Hindemith wrote a Concerto for Orchestra, Op. 38, in 1925, the title became famous through Béla Bartók's Concerto for Orchestra (1943), commissioned by Serge Koussevitzky and premiered by the Boston Symphony Orchestra in 1944. Bartók explained that the title reflected the work's tendency "to treat the single orchestral instruments in a concertant or soloistic manner."

Notable examples

Other widely performed concertos for orchestra include Witold Lutosławski's Concerto for Orchestra (1954), Michael Tippett's Concerto for Orchestra (1963), Elliott Carter's Concerto for Orchestra (1969), and Roger Sessions's Concerto for Orchestra (1981), which won the Pulitzer Prize for Music. In recent decades the title has been adopted by composers such as Jennifer Higdon, whose Concerto for Orchestra (2002) is frequently performed, and Zhou Long. The genre overlaps with the older sinfonia concertante and with virtuoso showpieces such as Kodály's and Respighi's orchestral works.

Characteristics

Works in the genre typically demand exceptional technical skill from every section, feature solo passages for principals throughout the orchestra, and use brilliant orchestration and contrasting instrumental combinations as structural devices. Movements often alternate virtuosic display with lyrical or nocturnal episodes, as in the Bartók concerto's famous "Game of Pairs" and its shattering finale.
