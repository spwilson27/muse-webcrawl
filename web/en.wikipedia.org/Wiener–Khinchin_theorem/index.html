Wiener–Khinchin theorem

The Wiener–Khinchin theorem (also known as the Wiener–Khintchine theorem, the Wiener–Khinchin–Einstein theorem or the Khinchin–Kolmogorov theorem) states that the power spectral density of a wide-sense-stationary random process is the Fourier transform of the corresponding autocorrelation function.

History

Norbert Wiener proved this theorem for the case of continuous functions in 1930; Aleksandr Khinchin later proved the result for continuous wide-sense stationary stochastic processes and published it in 1934. Albert Einstein had given an informal statement of the idea, without proof, in a brief two-page memoire in 1914.

Statement

For a continuous-time random process x(t) that is wide-sense stationary, the spectral density Sxx(f) and the autocorrelation function Rxx(τ) form a Fourier transform pair:

Sxx(f) = ∫ Rxx(τ) e−2πifτ dτ

The total power of the process equals the value of the autocorrelation at zero lag, and also equals the integral of the spectral density over all frequencies.

Domain of applicability

The theorem requires only the weak assumption of wide-sense stationarity: the mean is constant and the autocorrelation depends only on the time difference. For deterministic finite-energy signals, an analogous relation holds for the energy spectral density. The discrete-time version, expressed with the discrete-time Fourier transform of the autocorrelation sequence, is the foundation of classical nonparametric spectral estimation methods such as the correlogram.

Applications

The theorem underpins power spectrum estimation in signal processing, communications, optics, and time-series analysis. In practice the power spectral density of a measured signal is often estimated by computing the Fourier transform of an estimated autocorrelation, or equivalently, by periodogram methods related through the theorem.
