Mines in the Battle of Vimy Ridge

During the First World War, extensive tunnelling and mining took place beneath Vimy Ridge in northern France. British and Canadian tunnelling companies dug a network of subways, saps, and mine chambers in the chalk, both to shelter infantry before the attack of April 1917 and to destroy German strongpoints from below.

Background

The ridge's geology — soft chalk over deeper strata — made tunnelling feasible. Both sides began mining soon after the front stabilised in 1914, and an underground war of mines and counter-mines developed, with tunnellers listening for enemy digging and detonating camouflets to destroy opposing galleries.

The 1917 assault

In preparation for the Battle of Vimy Ridge (9–12 April 1917), the British and Canadians excavated some 12 kilometres of subways up to 10 metres deep, complete with light railways, headquarters, and dressing stations, allowing thousands of troops to advance in safety. A small number of mines were fired at zero hour to destroy German positions and create craters, though commanders limited their use because craters could obstruct the assault across the surface.

Legacy

Parts of the tunnel system are preserved at the Canadian National Vimy Memorial, including the Grange Subway. Unexploded mines remain beneath the ridge, as beneath several other Western Front battlefields.
