Blood–cerebrospinal fluid barrier

The blood–cerebrospinal fluid barrier (BCSFB) is a barrier between the blood and the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), formed principally by the epithelial cells of the choroid plexus in the ventricles of the brain. It is one of the three main barrier systems of the central nervous system, alongside the blood–brain barrier and the arachnoid barrier.

Structure

Unlike the capillaries of the blood–brain barrier, choroid plexus capillaries are fenestrated and leaky; the barrier instead resides in the tight junctions between the choroid plexus epithelial cells. These cells also actively secrete CSF and transport nutrients, hormones and waste products between blood and CSF.

Function and significance

The barrier regulates the composition of CSF and provides an additional route — and obstacle — for drug delivery to the brain, as well as a potential entry point for immune cells and pathogens. The total surface area of the choroid plexus epithelium is far smaller than that of the blood–brain barrier endothelium, making the latter the dominant interface for most solutes.
