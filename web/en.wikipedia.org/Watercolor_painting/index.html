Watercolor painting

Watercolor (American English) or watercolour (British English), also aquarelle (French), is a painting method in which the paints are made of pigments suspended in a water-based solution. Watercolor refers to both the medium and the resulting artwork. The conventional and most common support — material to which the paint is applied — for watercolor paintings is watercolor paper. Other supports include papyrus, bark papers, plastics, vellum, leather, fabric, wood, and canvas.

Materials

Watercolor paint consists of finely ground pigment bound with gum arabic, with additives such as glycerin or honey to keep it moist. It is sold in dried pans, tubes, and liquid form. Because the paint is transparent, light reflects off the white of the paper through the pigment layers, giving watercolors their characteristic luminosity.

Techniques

Common techniques include wet-on-wet (applying paint to dampened paper so colours bleed and blend), wet-on-dry (for sharper edges), dry brush, washes and graded washes, lifting, masking with fluid to preserve white areas, and glazing — layering transparent washes to build depth. Gouache, an opaque form of watercolor with added white pigment or chalk, is often used for highlights.

History

Water-based painting is ancient — used in Egyptian papyri and Chinese silk painting — but watercolor as an independent art form flourished in Europe from the Renaissance, in the work of Albrecht Dürer, and reached a golden age in 18th- and 19th-century Britain with artists such as J. M. W. Turner and John Constable. In the United States, Winslow Homer and John Singer Sargent became celebrated watercolorists. The medium remains popular for its portability, spontaneity, and expressive transparency.
