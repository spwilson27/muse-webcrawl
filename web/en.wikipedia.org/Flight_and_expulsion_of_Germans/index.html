Flight and expulsion of Germans (1944–1950)

The flight and expulsion of Germans during and after the Second World War (1944–1950) was the forced migration of ethnic Germans from Central and Eastern Europe — including the former eastern territories of Germany, Czechoslovakia, Poland, Hungary, Yugoslavia, and Romania — to Germany and Austria.

Scale

Between 12 and 14.6 million Germans fled or were expelled, making it the largest forced population transfer in European history. In its first phase, civilians fled the advancing Red Army in 1944–45; after the Potsdam Agreement of August 1945, the Allied powers sanctioned the "orderly and humane" transfer of German populations from Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Hungary, though in practice expulsions were often violent and chaotic.

Deaths and legacy

Estimates of deaths during the flight and expulsions range from about 500,000 to over 2 million, a figure long debated by historians. Expellees (Vertriebene) formed a large, politically organised population in post-war West Germany, and their integration — and later the recognition of the Oder–Neisse line — shaped German politics and Ostpolitik for decades.
