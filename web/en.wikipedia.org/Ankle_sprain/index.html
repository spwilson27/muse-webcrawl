Ankle sprain

An ankle sprain is an injury to one or more ligaments of the ankle, usually caused by the foot turning inward (inversion) beyond its normal range of motion. It is among the most common musculoskeletal injuries, particularly in sports. Symptoms include pain, swelling, bruising, and difficulty bearing weight.

Types

Most sprains involve the lateral ligament complex — the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL), calcaneofibular ligament (CFL), and posterior talofibular ligament — injured by inversion and plantarflexion. Medial (deltoid) ligament injuries arise from eversion and are less common. A "high ankle sprain" injures the syndesmosis between tibia and fibula and heals more slowly.

Grading

Grade I sprains are mild stretches with microscopic tearing; Grade II involve partial tears with moderate pain, swelling, and some instability; Grade III are complete ruptures with marked swelling, bruising, and inability to bear weight.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, with examination of swelling, tenderness, and stability. The Ottawa ankle rules guide whether X-rays are needed to exclude fracture. MRI may be used for suspected high ankle sprains or when recovery stalls.

Treatment

Initial management follows protection, optimal loading, ice, compression, and elevation. Most sprains recover with early mobilisation and rehabilitation emphasising range of motion, strengthening, and proprioceptive (balance) training; bracing or taping reduces recurrence. Severe injuries with persistent instability may require surgical repair. Recurrent sprains can lead to chronic ankle instability.
