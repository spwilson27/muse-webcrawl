Contemplative prayer is a form of Christian prayer characterised by silent, receptive attention to God rather than by words, petitions or discursive meditation. In Catholic spiritual theology it is classed with meditation and vocal prayer as one of the expressions of prayer, and is traditionally considered a gift of grace rather than a technique that can be mastered.

History

Its roots lie in the Desert Fathers and Mothers of the 3rd–5th centuries, who practised short, repeated invocations and watchful stillness (hesychia). John Cassian transmitted their teaching to Western monasticism, and Benedictine lectio divina culminated in contemplation as its final stage. Medieval writers — the anonymous author of The Cloud of Unknowing, Julian of Norwich, and later the Spanish Carmelites Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross — gave classic descriptions of its stages, including the "dark night" of purification.

Modern practice

In the 20th century Thomas Merton popularised contemplative prayer for a wide audience. From the 1970s, Trappist monks William Meninger, Basil Pennington and Thomas Keating developed Centering Prayer, a method of consenting to God's presence using a "sacred word" to return attention when thoughts intervene. The practice has also been taken up in ecumenical and interfaith settings, sometimes drawing criticism from those who see it as too close to Eastern meditation.

Theology

Theologians distinguish acquired contemplation, approached through ordinary grace and disciplined practice, from infused contemplation, given directly by God. The Catechism of the Catholic Church describes contemplative prayer as a gaze of faith fixed on Jesus, a hearing of the word of God, and a silent love.
