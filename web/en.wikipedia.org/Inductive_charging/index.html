Inductive charging (also known as wireless charging) is a type of wireless power transfer that uses electromagnetic induction to provide electricity to portable devices. A charging station creates an alternating electromagnetic field with an induction coil, and a second coil in the device converts that field back into electric current to charge its battery.

Principle

The system functions as a transformer split into two halves: a transmitter coil driven by alternating current generates a magnetic field, which induces a current in a closely coupled receiver coil. Because coupling falls off rapidly with distance, devices must be placed close to and aligned with the pad. Resonant inductive coupling extends the effective range somewhat by tuning both coils to the same resonant frequency.

Standards

The dominant standard is Qi, developed by the Wireless Power Consortium and incorporated into most smartphones; Apple's MagSafe system adds magnetic alignment to Qi for iPhones. The competing AirFuel Alliance promotes resonant technology. For electric vehicles, SAE J2954 standardizes stationary wireless charging at power levels up to 11 kW and above, while experimental "dynamic" systems charge vehicles in motion from coils embedded in the roadway.

Applications and trade-offs

Wireless charging is common in electric toothbrushes, hearing aids, medical implants, smartwatches, earbuds, and phones. Advantages include convenience and the elimination of exposed contacts and ports, improving water resistance and durability. Disadvantages include slower charging speeds, lower efficiency and waste heat compared with wired charging, and sensitivity to misalignment and foreign metal objects.
