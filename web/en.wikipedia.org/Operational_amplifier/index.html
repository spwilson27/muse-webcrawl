Operational amplifier

An operational amplifier (often op amp or opamp) is a DC-coupled electronic voltage amplifier with a differential input, a (usually) single-ended output, and an extremely high gain. By using negative feedback, an op amp's transfer function can be entirely determined by external components, making it a versatile building block of analogue electronics. Modern op amps are manufactured as integrated circuits; the classic 741, introduced in 1968, remains emblematic of the type.

Ideal characteristics

An ideal op amp has infinite open-loop gain, infinite input impedance, zero output impedance, infinite bandwidth and zero offset voltage. Two simplifying rules follow when negative feedback is applied: the amplifier drives its output so as to make the difference between its inputs zero (the "virtual short"), and the inputs draw no current. From these rules the behaviour of standard circuits — inverting and non-inverting amplifiers, summers, integrators, differentiators, active filters and comparators — can be derived straightforwardly.

History

The name derives from the amplifier's use in analogue computers to perform mathematical operations such as addition, subtraction, integration and differentiation. Vacuum-tube designs appeared in the 1940s, including Karl D. Swartzel Jr.'s 1941 Bell Labs design; George Philbrick commercialised tube op amps for analogue computing. Solid-state devices followed in the 1960s: Bob Widlar's µA709 and the Fairchild µA741 made cheap, robust monolithic op amps ubiquitous.

Real-world limitations

Practical op amps have finite gain and bandwidth (a roughly constant gain–bandwidth product), limited slew rate, input offset voltage and bias currents, and output saturation near the supply rails. Designers select among thousands of types — precision, low-noise, high-speed, rail-to-rail, instrumentation — optimised for particular applications in instrumentation, audio, control systems and signal processing.
