Just intonation or harmonic tuning is the tuning of musical intervals as whole number ratios (such as 3:2 or 4:3) of frequencies. Any interval tuned in this way is said to be pure, and its sound a pure interval. In just intonation, the major third is represented by the ratio 5:4 and the perfect fifth by 3:2, relationships drawn from the harmonic series.

History

The principles underlying just intonation were described by Pythagoras in ancient Greece, who related consonance to simple ratios of string lengths. Ptolemy's intense diatonic scale, later endorsed by Gioseffo Zarlino in the sixteenth century, formed the basis of the five-limit system used through much of Western music history. As modulation between distant keys became important, the inflexibility of fixed just ratios led to the adoption of temperaments, culminating in twelve-tone equal temperament in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Five-limit tuning

The most common form, five-limit tuning, builds scales from octaves (2:1), perfect fifths (3:2), and major thirds (5:4). A five-limit major scale contains the ratios 1:1, 9:8, 5:4, 4:3, 3:2, 5:3, 15:8, and 2:1. Such intervals sound smooth because their combined waveforms repeat simply and produce few audible beats.

Difficulties

Just intonation cannot provide consistent ratios in all keys simultaneously: stacking four perfect fifths and reducing by octaves gives a major third of 81:64, not 5:4 — the discrepancy, the syntonic comma (81:80), must be distributed somewhere. Instruments with fixed pitches, such as keyboards, therefore compromise, while voices, string quartets, and a cappella choirs often drift naturally toward just intervals.

Modern use

Composers such as Harry Partch, Ben Johnston, and La Monte Young built extended just-intonation systems, including seven-limit and higher-limit tunings. Barbershop quartets and brass ensembles prize the ringing resonance of purely tuned chords.
