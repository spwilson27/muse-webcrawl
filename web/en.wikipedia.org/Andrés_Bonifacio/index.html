Andrés Bonifacio y de Castro (30 November 1863 – 10 May 1897) was a Filipino revolutionary leader, often called "the Father of the Philippine Revolution" and "the Father of the Philippine Army". He was the founder and Supremo (supreme leader) of the Kataas-taasang, Kagalanggalangang Katipunan ng mga Anak ng Bayan (KKK), the secret society that launched the Philippine Revolution against the Spanish Empire in 1896. He is considered a national hero of the Philippines, and his birthday, 30 November, is a national holiday.

Early life

Bonifacio was born in Tondo, Manila, to a poor family; his father was a tailor and local official, his mother of Spanish mestiza descent. Both parents died while he was in his teens, leaving him to support his younger siblings. Largely self-educated, he read widely — histories of the French Revolution, José Rizal's novels Noli Me Tangere and El Filibusterismo, and works on law and religion — while working as a clerk, warehouse keeper and salesman of fans and canes. In 1892 he joined Rizal's reformist Liga Filipina, and founded the Katipunan on 7 July 1892, the night the Liga was dissolved, dedicated to achieving independence through revolution rather than reform.

The Katipunan and the Revolution

Under Bonifacio's leadership the Katipunan grew rapidly among the urban and rural poor, using a triangle system of recruitment, Masonic-style rituals and a newspaper, Kalayaan. When Spanish authorities discovered the society in August 1896, Bonifacio called his followers to arms at the "Cry of Pugad Lawin" (also associated with Balintawak), where they tore up their cedulas (residence certificates). The revolution spread through Manila and the surrounding provinces, though Bonifacio's own forces met defeats around San Juan and Manila.

Rivalry with Aguinaldo and death

As the revolution progressed, a faction centred on Emilio Aguinaldo in Cavite eclipsed Bonifacio. At the Tejeros Convention of March 1897 a revolutionary government was elected with Aguinaldo as president; Bonifacio, insulted at the proceedings, repudiated the convention and attempted to form his own government. He and his brother Procopio were arrested, tried by a court-martial of the Aguinaldo government for sedition and treason, and executed on 10 May 1897 at Mount Buntis, Maragondon, Cavite. The circumstances of his death remain a subject of historical controversy, but his place as the revolution's founder is widely honoured, notably by the Bonifacio Monument in Caloocan.
