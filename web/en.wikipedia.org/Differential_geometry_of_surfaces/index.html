Differential geometry of surfaces

A saddle point has Gaussian curvature of negative sign.

The differential geometry of surfaces studies smooth surfaces in three-dimensional Euclidean space using calculus. Originating with Euler and Monge in the eighteenth century, it reached a turning point in Gauss's Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas (1827), and it became the seed of modern Riemannian geometry.

Local theory

A surface patch is described by its first fundamental form, which encodes lengths and angles measured within the surface (the metric), and its second fundamental form, which encodes how the surface bends in space. From these come the principal curvatures κ1 and κ2 — the maximum and minimum curvatures of normal sections at a point — and two scalar invariants: the Gaussian curvature K = κ1κ2 and the mean curvature H = (κ1 + κ2)/2. Points are classified as elliptic (K > 0), hyperbolic (K < 0, saddle-like), or parabolic (K = 0).

The Theorema Egregium

Gauss's Theorema Egregium ("remarkable theorem") shows that Gaussian curvature depends only on the first fundamental form — it is an intrinsic property, measurable by inhabitants of the surface without reference to the surrounding space. A consequence is that no flat map of the Earth can preserve all distances, since a sphere has K > 0 while a plane has K = 0.

Geodesics and global theory

The straightest paths on a surface are its geodesics. The Gauss–Bonnet theorem links local geometry to global topology: the total Gaussian curvature of a closed surface equals 2π times its Euler characteristic. Surfaces of constant curvature — sphere, plane, and hyperbolic plane (pseudosphere) — model the three classical geometries, and surfaces of zero mean curvature (minimal surfaces, such as soap films) form another classical topic with modern applications in architecture and materials science.
