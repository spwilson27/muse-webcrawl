Tachykinin receptor

Tachykinin receptors are a family of G protein-coupled receptors that bind tachykinin neuropeptides, including substance P, neurokinin A, and neurokinin B. Three receptor subtypes are recognized in mammals: NK1, NK2, and NK3, encoded by the genes TACR1, TACR2, and TACR3 respectively.

Tachykinin receptors are seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptors

Subtypes and ligands

The NK1 receptor binds preferentially to substance P and is widely distributed in the central nervous system and peripheral tissues, where it mediates pain transmission, inflammation, and the vomiting reflex. The NK2 receptor prefers neurokinin A and is found mainly in smooth muscle of the airways, gut, and urinary tract. The NK3 receptor prefers neurokinin B and is expressed predominantly in the brain, where it participates in the regulation of gonadotropin-releasing hormone secretion.

Signaling

Tachykinin receptors couple mainly to Gq proteins, activating phospholipase C and increasing intracellular calcium. Their activation produces smooth muscle contraction, vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and modulation of neuronal excitability.

Clinical significance

Antagonists of the NK1 receptor, such as aprepitant, are used clinically as antiemetics to prevent chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting. NK3 receptor antagonists such as fezolinetant have been developed for the treatment of menopausal hot flushes, reflecting the role of neurokinin B signaling in thermoregulation. Tachykinin receptors have also been investigated as targets in pain, depression, and inflammatory bowel disease.
