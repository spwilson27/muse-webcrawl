The Western concert flute is a transverse (side-blown) woodwind instrument made of metal — typically silver, nickel silver, gold or platinum — and is the standard flute of the modern orchestra and concert band. The player directs a stream of air across the embouchure hole in the head joint, producing sound from the vibration of the air column.

History

Transverse flutes were made of wood with few keys through the Baroque and Classical eras. In 1847 Theobald Boehm introduced his revolutionary design: a cylindrical bore, large finger holes controlled by a system of ring keys and rods, and a parabolic head joint. The Boehm-system flute made the instrument fully chromatic, more powerful and more even in tone, and remains essentially unchanged today.

Construction and technique

The instrument consists of a head joint, body and foot joint, in either open-hole or closed-hole configuration, with a B foot joint extending the range on professional models. Extended techniques in contemporary music include flutter-tonguing, multiphonics, harmonics and key clicks.

Repertoire

Major repertoire includes concertos by Mozart and Nielsen, sonatas by Hindemith and Prokofiev, Debussy's Syrinx for solo flute, and prominent orchestral solos in works by Bach, Beethoven, Brahms and Ravel. Famous flautists include Jean-Pierre Rampal, James Galway and Emmanuel Pahud.
