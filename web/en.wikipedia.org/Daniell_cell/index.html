Daniell cell

A Daniell cell with its copper and zinc half-cells

The Daniell cell is a type of electrochemical cell invented in 1836 by British chemist and meteorologist John Frederic Daniell. It consists of a copper pot filled with a solution of copper(II) sulfate, in which is immersed an unglazed earthenware container filled with dilute sulfuric acid and a zinc electrode. The Daniell cell was the first reliable source of constant electric current and a great improvement over the voltaic pile for telegraphy and electrotyping.

Operation

At the zinc electrode (the anode), zinc is oxidised: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. The electrons travel through the external circuit to the copper electrode (the cathode), where copper ions from the copper sulfate solution are reduced and deposit as copper metal: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. The porous earthenware pot allows ions to pass to complete the circuit while slowing the mixing of the two solutions. The cell produces a potential of about 1.1 volts.

History

Daniell's design solved the polarisation problem that plagued earlier cells, in which hydrogen bubbles gathered on the copper plate and raised the internal resistance. Because the Daniell cell delivered a steady current for long periods, it became the standard battery of the early electric telegraph networks in Britain and America. Later variants, such as the gravity or "crowfoot" cell, separated the two solutions by density rather than by a porous barrier and remained in telegraph service into the twentieth century.
