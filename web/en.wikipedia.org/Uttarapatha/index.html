Uttarapatha (Sanskrit: "the northern road") was an ancient trade route and the region it traversed in northern India. In early Indian literature the term denotes both the lands north and west of the middle Ganges — including Gandhara and Kamboja — and the great highway that connected the eastern Gangetic plain with Taxila (Takshashila) and, beyond the Hindu Kush, with Central Asia and the Silk Road.

The route

Reconstructions trace the Uttarapatha from Rajagriha and later Pataliputra westward through Varanasi, Prayaga, Mathura, and the Punjab to Taxila, and onward via the Khyber region to Bactria. Together with the Dakshinapatha ("southern road") to the Deccan, it formed the axis of overland commerce in early historic India, carrying horses, gold, lapis lazuli and luxury goods, as well as pilgrims, soldiers and ideas — Buddhism famously travelled northwest along it into Central Asia and China.

Historical role

The route is mentioned in Vedic and early Buddhist texts, in Panini's grammar, and in Kautilya's Arthashastra, which rated the northern route's trade highly. Ashokan edicts and the imperial road-building of the Mauryas, and later the Grand Trunk Road of Sher Shah Suri and the Mughals, followed much the same corridor, which remains the principal east–west highway across northern South Asia.
