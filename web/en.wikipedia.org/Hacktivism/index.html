Hacktivism (a portmanteau of hack and activism) is the use of computer hacking techniques — such as website defacement, distributed denial-of-service attacks, data leaks and online civil disobedience — to promote political goals or social change. The term emerged in the 1990s around the hacker collective Cult of the Dead Cow.

History and notable actions

Early hacktivists included the Chaos Computer Club in Germany and groups that staged "virtual sit-ins" in solidarity with the Zapatistas in the late 1990s. The loose collective Anonymous, born on 4chan in 2003, brought hacktivism global attention: Project Chanology against the Church of Scientology (2008), support for WikiLeaks through Operation Payback (2010), and actions during the Arab Spring. In 2011 the LulzSec splinter group breached entertainment and government sites before its members were arrested. Hacktivist operations surged again after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, when the "IT Army of Ukraine" and Anonymous-aligned groups attacked Russian state and media sites.

Debate

Supporters cast hacktivism as digital civil disobedience defending free expression; critics and prosecutors treat the same acts as computer crime, and laws such as the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act impose severe penalties. The line between hacktivism, cybercrime and state-sponsored operations is often contested.
