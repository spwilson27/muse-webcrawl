Virtual private network

A virtual private network (VPN) extends a private network across a public network, enabling users to send and receive data as if their devices were directly connected to the private network. VPNs are created by establishing a virtual point-to-point connection through dedicated circuits or with tunneling protocols over existing networks, and most modern VPNs encrypt the traffic so that it cannot be read in transit. They are widely used by organizations for secure remote access and site-to-site links, and by individuals seeking privacy or access to region-restricted content.

How it works

A VPN client establishes an authenticated, encrypted tunnel to a VPN server. Traffic destined for the private network — or, in a full-tunnel configuration, all traffic — is encapsulated and encrypted before crossing the public Internet, then decrypted and forwarded by the server. To local networks and websites, the user's traffic appears to originate from the VPN server's address. Common protocol families include IPsec, TLS-based systems such as OpenVPN, the older PPTP and L2TP, and the lightweight modern protocol WireGuard.

Uses

Businesses use remote-access VPNs so employees can reach internal systems from home or while travelling, and site-to-site VPNs to join branch offices over the internet instead of leased lines. Consumer VPN services market privacy from internet service providers, protection on public Wi-Fi, and the ability to appear to be in another country to bypass geographic restrictions on streaming services. VPNs are also used by journalists and activists in countries with internet censorship, though some governments restrict or ban unauthorized VPN use.

Security and limitations

A VPN protects traffic between the user and the VPN server but shifts trust to the VPN operator, which can in principle observe the user's traffic; a VPN alone does not make a user anonymous, nor does it protect against malware or tracking by websites that the user logs into. Performance may suffer from encryption overhead and longer routes. Security researchers advise choosing providers with independently audited no-logging policies.
