Kidney dialysis (from Greek dialysis, "dissolution") is the process of removing excess water, solutes, and toxins from the blood in people whose kidneys can no longer perform these functions naturally. It is a form of renal replacement therapy used in acute kidney injury and in end-stage kidney disease, either long-term or as a bridge to kidney transplantation.

Hemodialysis

In hemodialysis, blood is pumped from the patient's circulation — usually through a surgically created arteriovenous fistula or graft — through a dialyzer ("artificial kidney") in which it flows along one side of a semipermeable membrane while a dialysate solution flows on the other side. Diffusion clears small solutes such as urea and potassium; ultrafiltration under pressure removes excess fluid. A typical in-centre session lasts about four hours and is performed three times weekly; home and nocturnal regimes also exist.

Peritoneal dialysis

Peritoneal dialysis uses the patient's own peritoneum as the membrane: dialysate is instilled into the abdominal cavity through a permanent catheter, dwells for several hours while solutes and fluid exchange, and is then drained. It can be performed manually in exchanges (CAPD) or by an automated cycler overnight (APD), and is done at home.

Indications and complications

Dialysis is initiated for refractory fluid overload, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, uremic symptoms, or very low glomerular filtration rate. Complications include hypotension and cramps during hemodialysis, access infection or thrombosis, peritonitis in peritoneal dialysis, and long-term burdens of cardiovascular disease and mineral-bone disorder. Dialysis replaces only the filtration function of the kidney, not its endocrine functions.

History

The term was coined by Thomas Graham in 1861. Willem Kolff built the first clinically successful rotating-drum artificial kidney in the occupied Netherlands (1943–45); the Scribner shunt (1960) and the Cimino fistula (1966) made chronic dialysis feasible, transforming kidney failure from a fatal condition into a manageable chronic one.
