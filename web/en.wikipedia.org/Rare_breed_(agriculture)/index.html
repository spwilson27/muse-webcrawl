Rare breed (agriculture)

In agriculture, a rare breed is a breed of livestock or poultry that is not common in modern farming, having been displaced by a small number of highly productive commercial breeds. Rare breeds — also called heritage or traditional breeds — are considered part of agricultural biodiversity, and conservation organisations maintain watchlists of breeds at risk of extinction, graded by population numbers of breeding females.

Background

During the twentieth century, intensive agriculture concentrated on a few breeds selected for maximum yield — for example, Holstein cattle for milk, Large White pigs, and hybrid broiler chickens. Hundreds of locally adapted breeds declined sharply; some vanished entirely. Rare breeds often retain traits lost in commercial stock: hardiness, foraging ability, mothering instincts, disease resistance, suitability to marginal land, and distinctive meat, milk or wool qualities.

Conservation

Organisations such as the Rare Breeds Survival Trust (UK, founded 1973) and The Livestock Conservancy (US) monitor breed populations and promote conservation by use — encouraging farmers, smallholders and estates to keep rare breeds commercially viable through premium meat schemes, wool products, conservation grazing and showing. Gene banks also preserve semen and embryos. Rare breeds feature in farm parks and open-air museums, and conservation grazing with hardy breeds is used to manage nature reserves and unimproved grassland.

Examples

British examples include Gloucestershire Old Spots and Tamworth pigs, Herdwick and Soay sheep, Highland and Belted Galloway cattle, and breeds of heavy horse such as the Suffolk Punch. In the United States, heritage breeds include Narragansett turkeys, Randall cattle and Ossabaw Island hogs.
