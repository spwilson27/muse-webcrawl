Lame duck (politics)

A lame duck, in American politics, is an elected official whose successor has already been elected or will soon be, and whose political power is consequently diminished. The term is most often applied to a president in the period between a successor's election and inauguration, and a "lame-duck session" is a sitting of a legislature that takes place after an election but before the newly elected members take office.

The political "lame duck" — an official whose authority wanes as a successor waits to take office.

Origin18th-century London stock market slang
Related conceptTerm limit; caretaker government

Origin of the term

"Lame duck" entered English in the 1760s on the London Stock Exchange, denoting a defaulter who could not pay his debts and was said to "waddle out of the Alley." It was applied to politics by the mid-19th century and became common in the United States, where the long interval between the November election and the March inauguration once allowed defeated officeholders months of unaccountable power.

United States

The Twentieth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution (1933), nicknamed the "Lame Duck Amendment," moved the presidential inauguration to 20 January and the start of Congress to 3 January, shortening the lame-duck period after abuses such as "midnight appointments" and the secession winter of 1860–61. A president becomes a lame duck in the broad sense upon re-election (being barred from a third term by the Twenty-second Amendment), and in the narrow sense after a successor is elected. Lame-duck presidents often issue pardons and executive orders in their final weeks, and lame-duck Congresses have passed measures ranging from major legislation to the provisions endangering the incoming administration's agenda.

Elsewhere

In parliamentary systems, the analogous figure is a caretaker government serving after an election or loss of confidence until a new government forms. The term is also used loosely in business and sport for leaders serving out a final contract.
