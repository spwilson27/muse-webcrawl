Hot working

Hot working is a metalworking process in which metal is plastically deformed above its recrystallisation temperature. Being above the recrystallisation temperature allows the material to recrystallise during deformation, which prevents strain hardening and keeps the yield strength low and ductility high. Hot working includes processes such as hot rolling, forging, extrusion and piercing. Its counterpart, working below the recrystallisation temperature, is called cold working.

Advantages and disadvantages

Because the metal is soft at high temperature, large deformations can be achieved with relatively low forces and power, and internal stresses and directional properties can be reduced or homogenised; porosity in cast structures can be closed and welded shut. The disadvantages include poor surface finish due to oxidation and scaling, less precise dimensional tolerances caused by thermal contraction and uneven cooling, and the energy cost of heating furnaces. For steel, hot working is typically done at temperatures between about 1,100 °C and 1,250 °C.

Recrystallisation temperature

The recrystallisation temperature varies with the metal and its purity; a rough rule is 0.3–0.5 times the melting temperature on the absolute (Kelvin) scale. Lead and tin recrystallise at about room temperature, so they cannot be work-hardened by ordinary deformation, whereas tungsten must be worked at very high temperatures.

Processes

Hot rolling converts cast ingots, slabs and billets into plate, sheet, bar and structural sections and accounts for the great majority of steel production. Hot forging shapes components such as crankshafts and gears. Hot extrusion forces heated metal through a die to produce long products of constant cross-section, and is widely used for aluminium profiles.
