Rogue state

Rogue state (or sometimes outlaw state) is a term applied by some international theorists to states that they consider threatening to the world's peace. These states generally meet certain criteria, such as being ruled by authoritarian or totalitarian governments, severely restricting human rights, sponsoring terrorism, and seeking to proliferate weapons of mass destruction.

Usage by the United States

The term came into prominent use in United States foreign policy during the 1990s. In 1994, National Security Advisor Anthony Lake identified Cuba, North Korea, Iran, Libya, and Iraq as "rogue states" or "backlash states". The label justified policies of dual containment against Iran and Iraq. In 2000, the State Department under Madeleine Albright replaced the term with "states of concern"; after the September 11 attacks, the concept was associated with President George W. Bush's "Axis of evil" (Iraq, Iran, and North Korea), announced in 2002.

Criticism

Critics, including Noam Chomsky, argue that the term is applied selectively to enemies of the United States rather than by consistent criteria, that allies engaging in similar conduct are excluded, and that the concept contradicts principles of state sovereignty in international law. The philosopher Jacques Derrida also criticized the concept, noting that those who denounce "rogue states" may themselves act in disregard of international law.
