Henry Wager Halleck (16 January 1815 – 9 January 1872) was a United States Army officer, scholar, and lawyer. A noted expert in military studies, he was nicknamed "Old Brains" for his scholarly reputation. He served as General-in-Chief of the Union Army during the American Civil War from July 1862 to March 1864, when he became chief of staff under his successor, Ulysses S. Grant.

Early career

Halleck graduated third in his class at West Point in 1839 and was commissioned in the Corps of Engineers. He wrote Elements of Military Art and Science (1846), one of the first American works of military theory, and translated Antoine-Henri Jomini's Life of Napoleon. During the Mexican–American War he served in California, where he became secretary of state of the military government and helped draft the California state constitution. He resigned in 1854 to practise law in San Francisco, becoming wealthy in legal and land affairs and president of a railroad.

Civil War

Recalled in 1861 as a major general, Halleck commanded the Department of the Missouri and then the Department of the Mississippi. His subordinates — notably Grant and Don Carlos Buell — won the victories at Fort Henry, Fort Donelson, and Shiloh, after which Halleck took personal command for the slow, methodical advance on Corinth, Mississippi, which he occupied in May 1862 after the Confederates evacuated.

Summoned to Washington in July 1862, Halleck served as general-in-chief, effectively the army's top administrator and strategist, but proved indecisive in directing field commanders and feuded by telegraph with several of them; President Abraham Lincoln remarked that Halleck was "little more ... than a first rate clerk". When Grant became general-in-chief in March 1864, Halleck stayed on as chief of staff in Washington, where his administrative abilities were valuable during the final campaigns and demobilisation.

Later life

After the war Halleck commanded the Division of the Pacific and then the Division of the South, headquartered at Louisville, Kentucky, where he died in 1872. His reputation suffered from his wartime caution and his role in controversies such as the treatment of Grant after Shiloh, but historians credit his logistical and administrative work.
