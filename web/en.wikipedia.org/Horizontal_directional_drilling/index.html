Directional boring

A directional drilling rig used for trenchless pipe installation

Directional boring, also referred to as horizontal directional drilling (HDD), is a minimal-impact trenchless method of installing underground utilities such as pipes, conduits and cables in a relatively shallow arc or radius along a prescribed underground path. It is used in place of traditional open-cut excavation when trenching or excavation would be impractical, for example beneath rivers, roads, railways and environmentally sensitive areas.

Process

An HDD installation proceeds in three stages. First, a small-diameter pilot hole is drilled along the designed path using a steerable drill head whose position is tracked from the surface by a walk-over locating system or wireline guidance. Second, the pilot hole is enlarged by pulling back progressively larger reamers. Third, the product pipe — commonly high-density polyethylene, steel or ductile iron — is attached behind the final reamer and pulled into the enlarged bore. Drilling fluid, usually a water–bentonite slurry, stabilizes the borehole, carries cuttings to the surface and lubricates the pipe during pullback.

Applications

HDD is widely used for water and sewer lines, oil and gas pipelines, electrical and telecommunications conduits, and river crossings. Modern rigs range from small units for short service connections to "maxi" rigs capable of crossings more than a kilometre long and over a metre in diameter.

History

The technique was developed in the 1970s in California, combining techniques from water-well and oilfield directional drilling; an early landmark job crossed the Pajaro River near Watsonville, California, in 1979.
