Cardinality of the continuum

Notationc, or 2ℵ0
Refers toCardinality of the real numbers
Introduced byGeorg Cantor

In set theory, the cardinality of the continuum is the cardinality or "size" of the set of real numbers, sometimes called the continuum. It is an infinite cardinal number, denoted by c (a lowercase Fraktur letter c) or by 2ℵ0.

The real numbers form a continuum, with more points than any countable set.

History

Georg Cantor discovered in 1874 that the real numbers cannot be put into one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers, proving that there are strictly more reals than naturals. His diagonal argument of 1891 gave a direct proof. Cantor hypothesized that c equals ℵ1, the next cardinal after ℵ0 — the famous continuum hypothesis.

Properties

Almost all real numbers are transcendental, irrational, and normal, in the sense that the sets of algebraic numbers, rational numbers, and non-normal numbers each have cardinality ℵ0, smaller than c. Sets with the cardinality of the continuum include the real numbers, the power set of the natural numbers, the set of points in the plane, and the set of continuous real-valued functions.

The continuum hypothesis

The continuum hypothesis states that there is no set whose cardinality is strictly between that of the integers and that of the real numbers. Kurt Gödel (1940) and Paul Cohen (1963) showed the hypothesis can neither be proved nor disproved from the standard ZFC axioms of set theory. See also cardinality.
