Eusociality

Eusociality
CriteriaReproductive division of labour, overlapping generations, cooperative brood care
Found inAnts, bees, wasps, termites, some beetles, shrimp, naked mole-rats
Term coinedSuzanne Batra (1966); popularised by E. O. Wilson

Eusociality is the highest level of social organisation in animals, defined by three traits: reproductive division of labour (with sterile or less-reproductive castes), overlapping generations within a colony, and cooperative care of the young. Eusocial colonies function in some respects like a single "superorganism."

Distribution

Eusociality evolved many times in the Hymenoptera (all ants, many bees and wasps) and once in the termites. It also occurs in ambrosia beetles, gall-making aphids and thrips, snapping shrimp of the genus Synalpheus, and two mammals, the naked and Damaraland mole-rats.

Evolutionary explanations

W. D. Hamilton's kin-selection theory and inclusive fitness explain sterile castes: workers pass on genes by helping relatives reproduce, aided in Hymenoptera by haplodiploidy, which makes sisters unusually closely related. Later work emphasises monogamy of the ancestral queens, ecology (defensible nests, food), and multilevel selection. E. O. Wilson, who formalised the concept in The Insect Societies (1971), later debated group-selection accounts of its origins.

Consequences

Eusocial insects dominate many terrestrial ecosystems in biomass and labour, driving pollination, soil turnover, and food webs.
