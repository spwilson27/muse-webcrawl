Atomic theory

Atomic theory is the scientific theory that matter is composed of discrete particles called atoms. It developed from an ancient philosophical idea — atomism, proposed by Leucippus and Democritus in the 5th century BC and later elaborated by Epicurus and Lucretius — into the empirical foundation of modern chemistry and physics. The modern theory holds that each chemical element consists of atoms of characteristic mass, that atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds, and that atoms themselves are composed of a dense nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons.

History

Ancient atomism was speculative and remained a minority view against Aristotle's continuum theory of matter. Early modern corpuscular philosophy (Gassendi, Boyle, Newton) revived the idea of particles but without quantitative chemistry. The decisive step came with John Dalton's atomic theory of 1803–1808, which explained the laws of definite and multiple proportions: elements consist of atoms of fixed relative weights (Dalton published the first table of atomic weights), atoms of different elements differ in weight, and chemical reactions merely rearrange atoms.

Through the 19th century the theory was refined by Avogadro's hypothesis (distinguishing atoms from molecules), Cannizzaro's standardisation of atomic weights, and Mendeleev's periodic table (1869), which organised the elements by atomic weight and properties and predicted new elements. Sceptics such as Mach and Ostwald withheld assent until Einstein's 1905 theory of Brownian motion and Jean Perrin's experiments (1908) provided direct quantitative evidence for atoms' existence.

The divisible atom

Meanwhile the atom proved not to be indivisible: J. J. Thomson discovered the electron (1897); Rutherford's gold-foil experiment (1909–1911) revealed the nucleus; Bohr's model (1913) introduced quantised electron orbits; Chadwick discovered the neutron (1932); and quantum mechanics recast electrons as probability distributions (orbitals). Later still, protons and neutrons were found to be made of quarks. The atomic theory underlies chemical bonding, statistical mechanics, solid-state physics and all of molecular science.
