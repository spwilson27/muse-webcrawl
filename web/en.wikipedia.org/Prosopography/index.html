Prosopography

Prosopography is the investigation of the common background characteristics of a group of actors in history by means of a collective study of their lives. Rather than writing the biography of a single individual, the prosopographer gathers standardized data — birth, death, kinship, offices held, wealth, education, religion — about a defined population and analyses the group as a whole, revealing patterns of recruitment, social mobility, and networks of power.

Method

The historian first defines the population to be studied (for example, all senators of the Roman Empire or all members of a parliament), then collects uniform information about each member from the available sources. The aggregated data can answer questions that individual biographies cannot: where elites came from, how they were connected by marriage or patronage, and how institutions reproduced themselves over time.

History

Collective biographical dictionaries have ancient roots, but modern prosopography flourished in the 20th century, notably in Roman and Byzantine studies, and in works such as Lewis Namier's studies of 18th-century British parliamentarians. Large reference projects include the Prosopographia Imperii Romani and the Prosopography of the Byzantine World. Computer databases have greatly expanded the scale of modern prosopographical research.
