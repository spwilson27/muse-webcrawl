Anti-lock braking system

[Image placeholder]

An anti-lock braking system (ABS) is a safety anti-skid braking system used on aircraft and on land vehicles, such as cars, motorcycles, trucks, and buses. ABS operates by preventing the wheels from locking up during braking, thereby maintaining tractive contact with the road surface and allowing the driver to maintain more control over the vehicle than with locked wheels.

Operation

A typical ABS comprises wheel-speed sensors, an electronic control unit, and a hydraulic modulator. When a sensor detects that a wheel is decelerating much faster than the others — indicating impending lock-up — the control unit rapidly reduces, holds and reapplies hydraulic pressure to that wheel's brake, cycling many times per second. The driver may feel a pulsing through the brake pedal. Modern systems integrate electronic brakeforce distribution, traction control and electronic stability control, which share the same hardware.

History

Anti-skid concepts date to early 20th-century railway and aviation work; the Dunlop Maxaret system was used on aircraft from the 1950s, and the 1966 Jensen FF was the first production car with (mechanical) ABS. Electronic systems developed by Bosch and Mercedes-Benz entered production in 1978 in the S-Class. ABS became mandatory on new cars in the European Union in 2004 and in the United States from the 2013 model year (in conjunction with stability control); it is also required on new motorcycles above small engine sizes in the EU, India, Japan and other markets.

Effectiveness

Studies find ABS substantially reduces the risk of fatal crashes for motorcyclists and modestly reduces non-fatal crash involvement for cars, chiefly by preserving steering control on slippery surfaces; on loose gravel or deep snow, locked wheels can sometimes stop a vehicle sooner by building a wedge of material, so some off-road vehicles allow reduced ABS intervention.
