Ricardian equivalence


Under Ricardian equivalence, a tax cut financed by borrowing raises private saving rather than spending

Ricardian equivalence is an economic hypothesis holding that consumers are forward-looking and internalise the government's budget constraint when making consumption decisions. On this view, when a government finances spending by issuing debt rather than raising taxes, rational households anticipate that the debt will eventually be repaid from future taxes, and therefore save the proceeds of any tax cut instead of spending them. As a result, the choice between tax finance and debt finance is "equivalent" — it has no effect on aggregate demand, interest rates or capital accumulation.

History

The argument is named after the British economist David Ricardo, who reasoned in his Essay on the Funding System (1820) that taxpayers should in principle regard a loan and a tax of equal present value as equivalent — though Ricardo himself doubted that people actually behaved this way. The proposition was revived and formalised in the 1970s by Robert Barro, notably in his 1974 paper "Are Government Bonds Net Wealth?", which grounded the result in overlapping generations of altruistic households linked by bequests. For this reason it is also called the Ricardo–Barro equivalence proposition.

Assumptions and criticism

The theorem depends on strong assumptions: households must have effectively infinite horizons (typically via operative bequest motives), perfect capital markets with no liquidity constraints, lump-sum (non-distortionary) taxes, and rational expectations about future tax liabilities. Critics, including James Buchanan and later empiricists, have argued that in practice people are myopic, credit-constrained or uncertain about who will bear future taxes, so deficit-financed tax cuts do partly stimulate consumption. Empirical tests of the proposition have produced mixed and contested results, and it remains a central benchmark in debates over fiscal policy and government debt.
