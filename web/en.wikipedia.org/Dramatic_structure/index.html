Dramatic structure

Dramatic structure (also called dramatic form) is the structure of a dramatic work such as a book, play, or film. Studies of dramatic structure derive from Aristotle's Poetics (c. 335 BC), which held that a tragedy imitates a complete action with a beginning, middle and end, unified by plot.

Freytag's pyramid

The German novelist and critic Gustav Freytag analysed the five-act drama in his Die Technik des Dramas (1863). His "pyramid" divides a play into exposition, rising action, climax, falling action (return), and catastrophe or dénouement. In the exposition the setting and characters are introduced; rising action builds complications toward the climax, the turning point of greatest tension; falling action unwinds the consequences to the resolution.

Three-act structure

Screenwriting commonly uses a three-act structure — setup, confrontation, resolution — popularised by Syd Field's Screenplay (1979), with plot points at each act break. Related models include the hero's journey drawn from Joseph Campbell's monomyth, the seven-point and eight-sequence systems, and Kishōtenketsu, a four-part East Asian structure that proceeds by introduction, development, twist and reconciliation without requiring conflict.
