Congo peafowl

Scientific classification
Kingdom: Animalia
Phylum: Chordata
Class: Aves
Order: Galliformes
Family: Phasianidae
Genus: Afropavo
Species: A. congensis

The Congo peafowl (Afropavo congensis), also known as the African peafowl or mbulu by the Bakongo people, is a species of peafowl native to the Congo Basin in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. It is one of only three peafowl species, the others being the Indian peafowl and the green peafowl of Asia, and it is the only pheasant native to Africa. The species is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN and is the national bird of the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

Discovery

The Congo peafowl was unknown to science until 1936, when the American zoologist James Chapin described it after noticing unusual feathers in a Congolese headdress years earlier and finding mislabelled specimens in a Belgian museum. Its late discovery made it one of the most celebrated ornithological finds of the twentieth century.

Description

The male is a large bird, 64–70 cm (25–28 in) long, with deep metallic blue-black plumage, green and violet tinges, a bare red neck patch, and a distinctive crest of white, hair-like feathers. Unlike its Asian relatives, the male does not carry an extravagant train. The female is chestnut-brown with a glossy green back and a short brown crest.

Habitat and behaviour

The species inhabits lowland primary rainforest, often in dry forest on sandy soils between rivers. It is shy and seldom observed in the wild. Congo peafowl are monogamous; the diet includes fruits, seeds, and invertebrates such as insects and molluscs. Habitat loss from logging and mining, together with hunting, threatens the population, estimated in the low thousands to around ten thousand individuals.
