Charles V of France

Charles V (21 January 1338 – 16 September 1380), called Charles the Wise, was King of France from 1364 to 1380. His reign marked a recovery of French fortunes in the Hundred Years' War: through patient strategy and the generalship of Bertrand du Guesclin, France regained most of the territory ceded to England by the Treaty of Brétigny.

Regency crisis

As dauphin, Charles served as regent after his father John II was captured at the Battle of Poitiers in 1356. He faced the revolt of the Parisian merchants under Étienne Marcel, the peasant Jacquerie of 1358, and the ambitions of Charles of Navarre, surviving all three and learning a lasting caution about open confrontation.

Reign

Becoming king in 1364, Charles stabilised royal finances with regular taxation, rebuilt the army with paid companies, and avoided pitched battles, instead wearing down English garrisons through sieges and harassment. By the late 1370s England retained little more than Calais and a strip of Gascony. He patronised learning, founding the royal library at the Louvre and commissioning translations of Aristotle and Augustine, and reformed the coinage and administration.

Death and legacy

Charles died in 1380 and was succeeded by his twelve-year-old son Charles VI, whose later madness undid much of his father's work. On his deathbed Charles abolished the hearth tax, a decision that left the crown short of revenue and helped trigger revolts in the regency that followed.
