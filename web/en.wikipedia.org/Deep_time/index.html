Deep time

Deep time is the concept of geological time on a scale vastly greater than human experience — the multimillion- and billion-year history of the Earth. It emerged from the work of early geologists who recognised that the processes visible at the Earth's surface, acting at present rates, required immense spans of time to produce the observed rock record.

Development of the idea

James Hutton, examining unconformities such as that at Siccar Point in 1788, concluded that the Earth showed "no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end". Charles Lyell's Principles of Geology (1830–33) popularised uniformitarianism and persuaded Charles Darwin, whose theory of evolution by natural selection required long timescales. Nineteenth-century estimates by Lord Kelvin, based on the cooling of the Earth, were too short; radiometric dating in the twentieth century established the Earth's age at about 4.54 billion years.

Scale

Recorded human history occupies roughly the last 5,000 years, an instant against the 541 million years of the Phanerozoic eon, let alone the full age of the Earth or the 13.8-billion-year age of the universe. The phrase "deep time" was popularised by John McPhee in his 1981 book Basin and Range.
