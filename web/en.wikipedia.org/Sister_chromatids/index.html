Sister chromatids

Sister chromatids are two identical copies of the same chromosome formed by DNA replication during the S phase of interphase, joined together by a common centromere. A pair of sister chromatids is called a dyad. During mitosis and the second division of meiosis, sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles of the cell, so that each daughter cell receives one copy of every chromosome.

Sister chromatids are held together by cohesin protein complexes established during replication. In mitosis, cohesin is removed in stages: most is released from chromosome arms during prophase, while centromeric cohesin is protected until anaphase, when the enzyme separase cleaves it and allows the sisters to part.

Distinction from homologous chromosomes

Sister chromatids are identical copies of a single chromosome, whereas homologous chromosomes are the two different versions (maternal and paternal) of the same chromosome type found in a diploid cell. Non-sister chromatids — chromatids belonging to different homologues — can exchange material by crossing over during meiosis, generating genetic variation. Sister chromatid exchange also occurs but generally produces no genetic change because the copies are identical.

Clinical relevance

Failure of sister chromatids to separate properly (nondisjunction) leads to aneuploidy, an abnormal chromosome number in daughter cells, underlying conditions such as Down syndrome when it occurs in meiosis.
