Coefficient of inbreeding

The coefficient of inbreeding (F) of an individual is the probability that two alleles at a randomly chosen locus in that individual are identical by descent, that is, both descended from a single allele in a common ancestor of the individual's parents. It is a central quantity in population genetics and animal breeding, measuring how inbred an individual is relative to a base population.

Definition and calculation

The concept was formalized by Sewall Wright in the 1920s as part of his method of path coefficients. The inbreeding coefficient of an individual equals the coefficient of coancestry (kinship) of its parents. It is computed from a pedigree by tracing all paths through common ancestors: for each common ancestor A, the contribution is (1/2)n (1 + FA), where n counts the steps in the path from one parent up to A and down to the other parent.

Typical values

Offspring of unrelated parents have F = 0. Matings between first cousins produce offspring with F = 1/16 (0.0625); half-sibling matings give F = 1/8; full-sibling or parent–offspring matings give F = 1/4. Repeated self-fertilization, common in plants, drives F toward 1.

Consequences

Inbreeding raises homozygosity throughout the genome, exposing recessive deleterious alleles and producing inbreeding depression — reduced fertility, survival, and vigour. It also reduces genetic diversity within a population, a concern in conservation genetics for small and endangered populations, where managers use pedigree or genomic estimates of F to plan matings.
