Imperial House Law

The Imperial House Law (皇室典範, Kōshitsu Tenpan) is a Japanese law that governs the line of imperial succession, the membership of the imperial family, and various other matters pertaining to the administration of the Imperial House of Japan.

History

The original Imperial House Law was promulgated in 1889 alongside the Meiji Constitution, placing the imperial house above ordinary legislation and giving the Emperor extensive control over its affairs. The current law was enacted on January 16, 1947, during the Allied occupation, and took effect on May 3, 1947, together with the postwar Constitution of Japan.

Provisions

The 1947 law drastically reduced the size of the imperial family, removing eleven collateral branches (the shinnōke and ōke) from imperial status. It restricts succession to male offspring in the male line of descent, requires imperial marriages to be approved by the Imperial House Council, and provides that female members who marry commoners leave the imperial family. It also governs regency, the coming of age of the Emperor and Crown Prince, and the order of succession.

Succession debate

Because the law limits succession to males of the male line, the small number of male heirs has prompted recurring public debate about amending the law to permit female emperors or the retention of female imperial-family members after marriage.
