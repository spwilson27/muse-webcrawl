True Cross

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The True Cross is the cross on which, according to Christian tradition, Jesus was crucified. It is related by tradition to the remnants of the real cross, fragments of which are venerated as relics in many churches worldwide.

The finding of the Cross

According to accounts written a century or more after the event, Helena, mother of the Emperor Constantine, travelled to the Holy Land around AD 326–328 and discovered the cross during excavations ordered near Golgotha, where the Church of the Holy Sepulchre was then under construction. In the most developed version of the legend, three crosses were found, and the true one was identified by a miraculous healing. The feast of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross (14 September) commemorates the discovery and later recovery of the relic.

History of the relic

The Persian king Khosrow II captured Jerusalem in 614 and carried off the Cross; the Byzantine emperor Heraclius recovered it in 628 and restored it to Jerusalem. After the Muslim conquest and the Crusades, fragments were scattered across Europe, prompting John Calvin's famous complaint that the accumulated pieces could fill a ship. Relics of the True Cross are preserved in, among other places, the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, Santa Croce in Gerusalemme in Rome, and Notre-Dame de Paris. The authenticity of the fragments cannot be verified, but they remain objects of devotion for many Christians.
