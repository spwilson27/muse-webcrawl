Hanja

Hanja is the Korean name for a traditional writing system consisting primarily of Chinese characters.

Hanja (Korean: 한자; Hanja: 漢字; IPA: [ha(ː)ntɕ͈a]) is the Korean name for a traditional writing system consisting primarily of Chinese characters (Han characters) that were incorporated into the Korean language and written alongside the Korean script, Hangul. More specifically, it refers to the Chinese characters that were borrowed from Ancient Chinese and incorporated into the pronunciation of the Korean language, retaining their original forms but read with Korean pronunciation.

Unlike Chinese characters used in China and Japan, hanja were never substantially simplified in Korea, so they closely resemble traditional Chinese characters. A large part of the Korean vocabulary — roughly half or more of the lexicon, especially in academic, legal, and technical registers — is of Sino-Korean origin and can be written in hanja.

History

Chinese characters entered Korea with the spread of Buddhism and Chinese administration from around the 4th century AD, and for centuries they were the only writing system available for Korean. Scribes developed adaptations such as idu and hyangchal to write Korean using characters for their sound or meaning. King Sejong promulgated the phonetic Hangul alphabet in the 1440s, but official and scholarly writing continued to use hanja, usually in mixed script, for centuries thereafter.

Modern usage

South Korea adopted a Hangul-only policy for official documents in 1970, though it was later relaxed; hanja education was restored in schools, and a standard set of about 1,800 "Hanja for Educational Purposes" is taught. Today everyday South Korean writing is overwhelmingly Hangul, but hanja still appear in academic works, legal texts, newspapers (especially in headlines and to disambiguate homophones), personal names, and calligraphy. In names and formal contexts, hanja clarify meaning because many Sino-Korean syllables are homophonous in Hangul. North Korea abolished hanja use in print in 1949, though characters are still taught for reading older and foreign texts.
