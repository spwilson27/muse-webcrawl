Environmental ethics

Environmental ethics is the part of environmental philosophy that considers extending the traditional boundaries of ethics from solely including humans to including the non-human world. It exerts influence on a large range of disciplines including environmental law, environmental sociology, ecotheology, ecological economics, ecology, and environmental geography. Environmental ethics asks what moral obligations humans have toward nature: whether individual animals, species, ecosystems, or the biosphere as a whole have moral standing, and what we owe to future generations.

Does a forest have value in itself, beyond its use to humans?

History

Antecedents include Aldo Leopold's A Sand County Almanac (1949), whose "land ethic" held that "a thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community", and Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962), which helped awaken public environmental concern. The field crystallised in the 1970s: Arne Næss distinguished "deep" from "shallow" ecology in 1973; Richard Routley (Sylvan) posed the "last man" thought experiment to test whether destroying nature could be wrong even with no humans left to be harmed; and the journal Environmental Ethics, founded in 1979, gave the discipline an institutional home. Influences ranged wider historically — from Henry David Thoreau and John Muir to non-Western traditions, including Buddhist, Hindu, and indigenous thought, that never drew a sharp human–nature divide.

Main positions

  • Anthropocentrism values nature instrumentally, for human benefit, though "weak" or enlightened versions support strong environmental protection because healthy ecosystems serve human interests, including the interests of future people.
  • Animal-centred ethics (sentientism), associated with Peter Singer's utilitarianism and Tom Regan's animal rights theory, extends moral consideration to individual sentient animals.
  • Biocentrism, as in Paul Taylor's Respect for Nature (1986), holds that all living things have inherent worth as teleological centres of life.
  • Ecocentrism, following Leopold and developed by J. Baird Callicott, values ecological wholes — species, ecosystems, the land — sometimes over individual organisms, a stance critics have charged with "environmental fascism" for its willingness to sacrifice individuals.
  • Deep ecology (Næss, George Sessions) proposes a platform of biospherical egalitarianism and self-realisation through identification with nature.
  • Ecofeminism (Val Plumwood, Karen Warren) links the domination of nature to the domination of women and other oppressed groups.
  • Social ecology and eco-Marxism locate the roots of ecological crisis in social hierarchy and capitalism.

Applied issues

Environmental ethicists debate climate justice and duties to future generations (the non-identity problem), the ethics of species conservation versus individual welfare, rewilding and de-extinction, the moral status of plants and rivers — some jurisdictions have granted legal personhood to natural features such as New Zealand's Whanganui River — geoengineering, population, consumption, and environmental justice for poor and indigenous communities who bear disproportionate pollution burdens. The field informs environmental law and policy even where its theories disagree: disputes about whether a forest's value is intrinsic or instrumental surface in cost–benefit analysis, endangered-species protection, and climate negotiations alike.
