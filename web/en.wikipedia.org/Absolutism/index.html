Absolute monarchy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Absolute monarchy (or absolutism as doctrine) is a form of monarchy in which the monarch rules in his or her own right with no legal limitations from a constitution, legislature, or other body. The term is most often applied to the European monarchies of the 16th to 18th centuries.

Doctrine and practice

Absolutist rule was commonly justified by the divine right of kings — the claim that the monarch's authority derives directly from God — and theorized by writers such as Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet and, drawing on sovereignty theory, Jean Bodin. Louis XIV of France, who reputedly declared "L'État, c'est moi" ("I am the state") and centralized power at Versailles after 1682, is the archetype. Absolutist states built standing armies, professional bureaucracies and uniform legal codes, curbing the privileges of the nobility and regional estates.

Varieties and decline

Historians note that even "absolute" kings depended on negotiation and patronage, so the label reflects aspiration as much as reality. In the 18th century, "enlightened absolutism" described reforming monarchs such as Frederick the Great of Prussia, Catherine the Great of Russia and Joseph II of Austria. Most European absolute monarchies were curtailed or abolished during the 19th and early 20th centuries; a few absolute monarchies survive today, including Saudi Arabia, Brunei, Oman, Eswatini and Vatican City.
