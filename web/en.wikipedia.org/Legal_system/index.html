Legal system

Legal systems of the world
Principal legal traditions by jurisdiction

A legal system is the framework of laws, institutions and procedures by which a state or other polity creates, interprets and enforces rules. Comparative lawyers generally group the world's legal systems into a small number of major traditions or "families," most importantly the civil law tradition, the common law tradition, religious legal systems, and customary law; many countries combine elements of several traditions in mixed systems.

Major traditions

Civil law

Civil law systems, descended from Roman law and codified most influentially in the Napoleonic Code of 1804, are the most widespread tradition, used across continental Europe, Latin America, much of Africa and parts of Asia. Their hallmark is comprehensive written codes that state general principles, with judges primarily applying the code rather than following precedent.

Common law

Common law developed in medieval England and spread through the British Empire to the United States, Canada, Australia, India and other Commonwealth countries. It is characterised by judge-made law: the doctrine of precedent (stare decisis) binds courts to earlier decisions, alongside statute law enacted by legislatures.

Religious law

Religious legal systems derive their rules from sacred texts and traditions. Examples include Sharia (Islamic law), applied in varying degrees in many Muslim-majority states; Halakha (Jewish law); and canon law within the Roman Catholic Church.

Customary and mixed systems

Customary law, based on long-established local practices, remains important in many African and Pacific jurisdictions. Mixed systems combine traditions: Scotland and South Africa blend civil and common law, while countries such as Japan combine a civil-law code structure with other influences.
