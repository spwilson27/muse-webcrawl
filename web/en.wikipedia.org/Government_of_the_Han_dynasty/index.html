Government of the Han dynasty

The government of the Han dynasty was the imperial administration that ruled China from 202 BC to AD 220, modelled on the preceding Qin dynasty but modified by Confucian ideology. The emperor stood at the apex as Son of Heaven, and a salaried professional bureaucracy — recruited increasingly through recommendation and examination of classical learning — administered an empire whose registered population approached 60 million by AD 2.

The emperor and court

The emperor held supreme legislative, judicial and military authority, issuing edicts that had the force of law. In practice, power could be contested among the emperor, empress dowagers and their consort families, court eunuchs, and the outer-court bureaucracy — tensions that grew acute in the Eastern Han, when a succession of child emperors left government in the hands of regents and palace factions.

Central administration

At the top of the bureaucracy stood the Three Excellencies — the Chancellor, the Imperial Secretary (head of the censorate and nominal deputy chancellor), and the Grand Commandant — each earning a salary rank of "fully 10,000 bushels". Beneath them the Nine Ministers directed specialised portfolios: ceremonies, palace attendants, guards, coachmen (imperial stables), justice, state guests, the imperial clan, finance, and the imperial household treasury. Over the Western Han the Imperial Secretariat (Shangshu), originally a minor palace office, grew into the real centre of decision-making, eclipsing the Chancellor.

Local government

The empire was divided into commanderies (jun) administered by governors and counties (xian) run by magistrates, roughly 1,500 counties in all. Early Han emperors also enfeoffed imperial relatives as kings (wang) over semi-autonomous kingdoms; after the Rebellion of the Seven States (154 BC), reforms under Emperor Wu stripped the kingdoms of administrative power and made their chancellors imperial appointees. Inspectors (cishi) toured thirteen regions to audit local officials and, late in the Eastern Han, were converted into powerful provincial governors.

Recruitment and law

Officials were recruited through recommendation by local administrators (the "filially pious and incorrupt" category), imperial summons, and, from 124 BC, the Imperial University (Taixue), whose students were examined in the Confucian classics. Han law, codified in statutes and ordinances, combined Legalist administrative penalties with Confucian moral norms; punishments ranged from fines and corvée to mutilation (early Han) and death, moderated in principle by imperial amnesties and review of capital cases.
