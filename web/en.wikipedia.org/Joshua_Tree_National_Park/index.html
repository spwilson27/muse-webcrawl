Joshua Tree National Park

Location: Riverside and San Bernardino counties, California, US
Area: 795,156 acres (1,242 sq mi)
Established: 31 October 1994 (national park); national monument 1936
Governing body: National Park Service
Named for: The Joshua tree (Yucca brevifolia)

Joshua Tree National Park is an American national park in southeastern California, east of San Bernardino and Los Angeles and north of Palm Springs. It is named after the Joshua trees (Yucca brevifolia) native to the Mojave Desert. Originally declared a national monument in 1936, Joshua Tree was redesignated as a national park in 1994 when the U.S. Congress passed the California Desert Protection Act.

Geography and ecology

The park straddles two deserts: the higher, cooler Mojave Desert in the west, home to the Joshua tree, and the lower Colorado Desert (part of the Sonoran Desert) in the east, dominated by creosote bush and ocotillo. The Little San Bernardino Mountains cross the southwest edge. The landscape is famous for its surreal rock piles and boulder fields — monzogranite formations eroded into fantastical shapes — making it one of the world's premier rock-climbing destinations, with thousands of named routes. Wildlife includes bighorn sheep, desert tortoises, roadrunners and some 250 species of birds; dark skies make it a popular stargazing site, designated an International Dark Sky Park.

History

The area was inhabited for thousands of years, successively by the Pinto culture, Serrano, Chemehuevi and Cahuilla peoples, and later saw cattle ranchers, miners (some 300 mines were worked) and homesteaders. Conservationist Minerva Hoyt campaigned for protection of the desert flora, leading President Franklin D. Roosevelt to proclaim Joshua Tree National Monument in 1936. The park gained wider fame in popular culture through the Irish rock band U2's 1987 album The Joshua Tree and its association with musician Gram Parsons. Visitation has climbed to around three million people a year.
