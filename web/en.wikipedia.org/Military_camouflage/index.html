Military camouflage

Military camouflage is the use of camouflage by a military force to protect personnel and equipment from observation by enemy forces. In practice, it means the application of colour and materials to any kind of military equipment to conceal it from enemy observation, including uniforms, vehicles, ships, aircraft, positions, and buildings. Camouflage became a systematic military discipline during the First World War, when aerial reconnaissance and modern firepower made concealment essential.

Principles

Effective camouflage defeats detection, recognition, and identification. Pattern design draws on biological principles: disruptive coloration breaks up outlines with high-contrast patches; countershading cancels the shading that reveals three-dimensional form; background matching matches colour and brightness to the environment. Modern evaluation also considers the infrared and radar signatures of materials, since sensors operate beyond visible light.

History

Nineteenth-century armies abandoned bright uniforms as rifle range and accuracy grew: British troops in India dyed uniforms khaki ("dust-coloured") in the 1840s–50s, and drab field dress was general by the Boer War and First World War. France created the first camouflage unit in 1915, employing artists — the original camoufleurs — to paint artillery and design observation posts disguised as trees. In the Second World War, camouflage expanded dramatically: Germany fielded splinter and plane-tree patterns, the Soviet Union and United States developed reversible and frog-skin prints, and naval "dazzle" painting from the earlier war was supplemented by measures against radar.

Uniform patterns

Post-war patterns evolved into national families: the US M81 Woodland (1981), British Disruptive Pattern Material (DPM), and German Flecktarn. From the 2000s, pixelated "digital" patterns such as Canadian CADPAT and US Marine MARPAT, and multi-environment patterns like MultiCam, became common. Specialist patterns exist for desert, jungle, snow, and urban terrain, and many countries now adopt a single multi-terrain pattern for general issue.

Vehicles, ships, and deception

Beyond paint, militaries use camouflage nets, thermal blankets, decoys, and dummy positions to mislead surveillance and precision weapons. Aircraft and ships employ low-observable shaping and coatings as a form of sensor camouflage. Deception operations — from the Second World War's inflatable "Ghost Army" tanks to modern decoy missile launchers — extend camouflage from concealment into misdirection.
