Caprock Escarpment

The Caprock Escarpment is a term used in West Texas and Eastern New Mexico for the escarpment marking the boundary between the Llano Estacado (the "Staked Plain") to the west and the North Central Plains to the east. The escarpment stretches roughly 200 miles (320 km) from the Texas Panhandle toward Central Texas and drops some 300 to 1,000 feet (90–300 m) from the level high plains to the rolling plains below.

Geology

The escarpment is capped by a hard, erosion-resistant layer of caliche — the "caprock" — which protects the softer sedimentary rocks beneath. Erosion of this edge has produced dramatic canyons and breaks, notably at Palo Duro Canyon and Caprock Canyons State Park, the latter known for its free-roaming bison herd descended from the historic Goodnight herd.

Significance

The escarpment forms a sharp ecological and cultural boundary: above it lies the flat, semi-arid Llano Estacado, intensively farmed with water from the Ogallala Aquifer; below it, the breaks and plains support ranching and mesquite and juniper woodland.
