A rhyme is a repetition of similar sounds (usually, the exact same phonemes) in the final stressed syllables and any following syllables of two or more words. Most often, this kind of perfect rhyming is consciously used for a musical and aesthetic effect in the final position of lines within poems or songs. More broadly, a rhyme may also fittingly apply to a set of words having the same ending sound, such as a couplet.

In phonetics, the rhyming part of a syllable is its rime: the vowel nucleus plus any following consonants (the coda). Words rhyme when their rimes match from the last stressed vowel onward — cat/hat, dancing/glancing. The word derives, via Old French rime, from Latin rhythmus, "rhythm".

Rhyming couplets structure verse through matched line endings.

Types of rhyme

Perfect and imperfect rhymes

A perfect rhyme has identical stressed vowel and following sounds with differing onsets (love/dove is imperfect in most accents, being a slant rhyme of near-identical sounds). Slant rhyme (half rhyme, near rhyme) matches only some sounds, as in worm/swarm. An eye rhyme matches in spelling but not pronunciation, e.g. love/move. Identical rhyme repeats the same word.

Position

End rhyme occurs at line endings; internal rhyme occurs within a single line or between internal positions of lines. Rhymes may be classified by the number of matching syllables: masculine (one syllable), feminine (two) and dactylic (three).

Rhyme schemes

The pattern of end rhymes in a stanza is its rhyme scheme, conventionally written with letters: ABAB (alternate), AABB (couplets), ABBA (enclosed). Fixed forms impose schemes such as the sonnet's octave and sestet, the ABaAabAB of ottava rima, and the interlocking ABA BCB of terza rima, invented for Dante's Divine Comedy.

History and across languages

Rhyme appears early in Chinese poetry (the Shijing) and in Arabic verse, entered Latin poetry in late antiquity, and became a hallmark of medieval European verse, replacing the quantitative metre of classical antiquity. Some traditions favour other devices — alliteration in Old English and Finnish verse, tone patterns in Chinese — but rhyme remains central to hymns, nursery rhymes, popular song and hip-hop, where dense internal and multisyllabic rhymes are prized.
