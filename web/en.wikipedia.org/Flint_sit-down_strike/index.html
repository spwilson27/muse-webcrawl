Flint sit-down strike

The Flint sit-down strike of 1936–1937 was a sitdown strike by the United Automobile Workers (UAW) against General Motors (GM) in Flint, Michigan. The strike began on December 30, 1936, when workers occupied several GM plants, and lasted until February 11, 1937. It was one of the most significant strikes in American labour history, leading GM to recognise the UAW as the bargaining agent for its workers and transforming the union into a major force in American industry.

Background

Conditions in the auto plants were harsh, with speedup, job insecurity, and company unions. The passage of the National Labor Relations Act of 1935 gave workers legal protection to organise, and the newly formed UAW, affiliated with the Congress of Industrial Organizations, began campaigns at GM, the industry's dominant firm.

The strike

Rather than picket outside, where they could be replaced, workers sat down inside the plants — first at Fisher Body No. 2 in Flint, then at the key Chevrolet engine plant and Fisher Body No. 1. In the "Battle of the Running Bulls" on January 11, 1937, police attacked strikers with tear gas and gunfire but were repelled. Michigan Governor Frank Murphy deployed the National Guard as a peacekeeping force rather than to evict the strikers, and pressed GM to negotiate.

Outcome

On February 11, 1937, GM signed a one-page agreement recognising the UAW. UAW membership soared from about 30,000 to 500,000 within a year, and the victory encouraged sit-down strikes across American industry.
