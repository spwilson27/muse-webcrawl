Mumps

A child with mumps, showing swelling of the parotid glands

Mumps is a viral disease caused by the mumps virus (MuV), a member of the family Paramyxoviridae. Initial symptoms are often non-specific, such as headache, malaise, and fever, followed within a day or so by the characteristic painful swelling of one or both parotid salivary glands. The disease is highly contagious and spreads through respiratory droplets and direct contact with saliva.

Signs and symptoms

The incubation period is typically 16 to 18 days. About one third of infections are asymptomatic. When symptoms occur, they include swelling and tenderness of the parotid glands (parotitis), fever, headache, muscle aches, fatigue, and loss of appetite. Complications may include inflammation of the testes (orchitis), ovaries, pancreas (pancreatitis), brain (encephalitis), and meninges (meningitis), as well as hearing loss.

Cause and transmission

Mumps is caused by the mumps virus, a single-stranded RNA virus. Humans are the only known natural hosts. The virus spreads via droplets produced by coughing and sneezing, or by contact with contaminated objects and surfaces.

Prevention and treatment

Mumps is preventable by vaccination. The MMR vaccine, which also protects against measles and rubella, is routinely given in two doses in childhood immunization programs. There is no specific antiviral treatment; care is supportive, including rest, fluids, and pain relief.

History

Mumps was described as early as the 5th century BC by Hippocrates. The viral cause was identified in the 1930s, and the first effective vaccine was licensed in 1967, developed by Maurice Hilleman using virus isolated from his daughter Jeryl Lynn.
