Pyongyang

Pyongyang (Korean: 평양) is the capital and largest city of North Korea (the Democratic People's Republic of Korea), located on the Taedong River about 109 km upstream from the Yellow Sea. Its population is estimated at around 3 million. The city is the political, industrial, and cultural center of the country and is administered as a directly governed city.

History

According to legend, the site was founded in 1122 BCE as Seogyeong. Pyongyang served as a capital of Goguryeo from 427 CE until the kingdom's fall in 668. It later flourished as a secondary capital under Goryeo and Joseon, and as a trading and early Christian missionary center in the nineteenth century, sometimes called the "Jerusalem of the East". The city became the capital of North Korea after partition in 1948. It was almost completely destroyed by American bombing during the Korean War and was rebuilt in monumental socialist style with support from other socialist states.

Geography and layout

The Taedong divides the city; broad avenues, grand squares such as Kim Il Sung Square, and large-scale apartment districts characterize its plan. Landmarks include the Juche Tower (170 m), the Arch of Triumph, the twin Koryo Hotel towers, the pyramid-shaped Ryugyong Hotel, the May Day Stadium on Rungra Island—one of the largest stadiums in the world—and the monumental statues of Mansu Hill. The Pyongyang Metro, opened in 1973, has lavishly decorated stations deep underground.

Life and economy

Residence in Pyongyang is a privilege restricted to politically reliable citizens under the songbun classification system; residents enjoy better food rations, services, and access to consumer goods than the rest of the country. The city hosts the regime's elite institutions, including Kim Il Sung University, the Korean Central Broadcasting headquarters, light industry, and the Pyongyang International Film Festival. Tourism, though tightly controlled, brings most foreign visitors through the capital.
