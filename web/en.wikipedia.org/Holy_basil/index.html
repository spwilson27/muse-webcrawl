Holy basil

Holy basil (Ocimum tenuiflorum, syn. Ocimum sanctum), commonly known as tulsi or tulasi, is an aromatic perennial plant in the family Lamiaceae. It is native to the Indian subcontinent and widespread as a cultivated plant throughout the Southeast Asian tropics. Tulsi is cultivated for religious and traditional medicine purposes, and also for its essential oil. It is widely used as an herbal tea, commonly used in Ayurveda, and has a place within the Vaishnava tradition of Hinduism, in which devotees perform worship involving holy basil plants or leaves.

Description

Holy basil is an erect, many-branched subshrub, 30–60 cm (12–24 in) tall with hairy stems. The leaves are green or purple, simple, petioled, with an ovate blade up to 5 cm (2 in) long, which has a slightly toothed margin. The flowers are purplish, in elongate racemes in close whorls. The two main morphotypes cultivated in India and Nepal are green-leaved (Sri or Lakshmi tulasi) and purple-leaved (Krishna tulasi).

Religious significance

Tulsi is sacred in Hinduism and is regarded as an earthly manifestation of the goddess Tulsi, an avatar of Lakshmi. The plant is often grown in a special structure called a Tulsi Vrindavan in the courtyard of Hindu homes, and leaves are offered in worship of Vishnu and Krishna. The Skanda Purana and other texts praise the plant; its stems are carved into prayer beads (mala) worn by devotees.

Uses

In Ayurveda, tulsi is described as an adaptogen and has been used for the treatment of colds, coughs, inflammation and other complaints. Leaves and essential oil contain eugenol and other compounds. Scientific studies of its purported medicinal effects have produced mixed and generally low-quality evidence. In Thai cuisine, the distinct variety called kaphrao is the essential herb of the dish phat kaphrao (stir-fried basil with meat). Hindus also traditionally plant tulsi around temples and homes for its reputed air-purifying properties.
