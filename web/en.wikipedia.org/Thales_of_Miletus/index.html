Thales of Miletus (c. 624 – c. 546 BCE) was a Greek philosopher, mathematician, and astronomer from Miletus in Ionia, Asia Minor. He is traditionally regarded as the first philosopher in the Greek tradition and the founder of the Milesian school, breaking from mythological explanations of nature in favor of naturalistic ones. Aristotle reports that Thales held water to be the arche — the originating principle of all things.

Life and legends

No writings by Thales survive; knowledge of him comes from later authors including Herodotus, Diogenes Laërtius, and Aristotle. Herodotus credits him with predicting the solar eclipse of 585 BCE that halted a battle between the Lydians and the Medes — a claim modern scholars treat cautiously. Other stories have him diverting the river Halys for Croesus's army, cornering the market in olive presses to prove philosophers could be rich if they wished, and falling into a well while stargazing, to the amusement of a Thracian servant girl. He was counted among the Seven Sages of Greece.

Mathematics and astronomy

Later geometers attributed several elementary theorems to Thales, including the theorem that an angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle (Thales's theorem) and the intercept theorem for similar triangles, supposedly used to measure the heights of pyramids and distances of ships at sea. His search for a single underlying substance and for explanations without recourse to the gods marks, for Aristotle and many historians, the beginning of Western philosophy and science.
