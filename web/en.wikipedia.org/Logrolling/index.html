Logrolling

Logrolling is the trading of favours, or vote trading, by legislators to obtain passage of actions of interest to each in a quid pro quo arrangement. The term derives from the pioneer practice of neighbours helping one another roll felled logs — "you roll my log and I'll roll yours" — and entered American politics in the 19th century.

In political economy

In public choice theory, logrolling explains how intense minorities can secure benefits at diffuse public expense: a legislator who cares strongly about one project trades support on projects of little concern to them. James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock analysed the practice in The Calculus of Consent (1962), noting it can both enable mutually beneficial bargains and produce oversized budgets and "pork barrel" spending.

Related practices

Logrolling is related to, but distinct from, bribery and from ordinary compromise in coalition-building. In the United States it is associated with earmarks and omnibus bills; in parliamentary systems, similar trades occur within coalition governments.
