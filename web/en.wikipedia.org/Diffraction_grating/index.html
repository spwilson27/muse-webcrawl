Diffraction grating

A grating disperses white light into a spectrum.

In optics, a diffraction grating is a periodic structure that splits and diffracts light into its component wavelengths, producing a spectrum. A grating consists of a large number of equally spaced parallel slits (transmission grating) or grooves on a reflective surface (reflection grating), with spacings comparable to the wavelength of light — often thousands of lines per millimetre.

Principle

Light diffracted from successive grooves interferes constructively only in directions where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths, given by the grating equation d(sin θi + sin θm) = mλ, where d is the groove spacing, λ the wavelength, and m the diffraction order. Because the angle depends on wavelength, white light is spread into colours, with sharper and more widely dispersed spectra than a prism provides. The resolving power, R = mN (order times number of illuminated grooves), can exceed 100,000 in research instruments.

History

The first gratings were made by the American astronomer David Rittenhouse (1785) and studied by Joseph von Fraunhofer, who used them to measure the dark lines in the solar spectrum. Henry Rowland's ruling engines of the 1880s produced large, precise gratings that transformed spectroscopy. Modern gratings are often made holographically or replicated from a ruled master; "blazed" gratings concentrate light into a chosen order.

Applications

Diffraction gratings are the dispersive element in most spectrometers and monochromators, and are used in tunable lasers, astronomical spectrographs, X-ray and neutron optics, wavelength-division multiplexing in fibre-optic communications, and pulse compression for ultrafast lasers. Everyday examples include the rainbow patterns seen on CDs and DVDs, whose closely spaced tracks act as reflection gratings.
