Younger Dryas
Period~12,900 – 11,700 years ago
Duration~1,200 years
Named afterDryas octopetala (mountain avens)
FollowsBølling–Allerød interstadial

The Younger Dryas was a period of abrupt climatic cooling, lasting roughly from 12,900 to 11,700 years ago, that interrupted the warming trend at the end of the last Ice Age and temporarily returned much of the Northern Hemisphere to near-glacial conditions. It is named after the Arctic-alpine flower Dryas octopetala, whose pollen becomes abundant in sediments of this age in Scandinavia.

Climate record

Ice cores from Greenland show that temperatures there fell by several degrees — estimates around 10 °C regionally — within decades at the onset, and that the event ended equally abruptly around 11,700 years ago, marking the start of the Holocene epoch. Effects were strongest around the North Atlantic but are recorded across much of the globe, with regional variations such as drying in the Levant.

Causes

The leading explanation is a disruption of the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation by a pulse of glacial meltwater — possibly from Lake Agassiz — into the North Atlantic, though the routing and sufficiency of the flood remain debated. A controversial hypothesis attributes the onset to an extraterrestrial impact, citing a layer of impact markers, but it has not gained broad acceptance.

Effects

The cold snap coincided with the extinction of some Pleistocene megafauna and with changes in human societies, including the Natufian culture of the Levant, whose shift toward sedentism and early cultivation has been linked to the drying climate.
