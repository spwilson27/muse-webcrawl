The Kroll process is the principal industrial method for producing metallic titanium. Developed by the Luxembourg metallurgist William J. Kroll in the 1930s and patented in 1940, it displaced the earlier Hunter process and made large-scale titanium production practical, enabling the metal's use in aerospace and chemical industries.

Process

The process begins by converting titanium ore (rutile or upgraded ilmenite) into titanium tetrachloride (TiCl4) by chlorination in the presence of carbon at about 1000 °C. The purified TiCl4 is then reduced with molten magnesium in a steel vessel under an argon atmosphere at roughly 800–850 °C:

TiCl4 + 2 Mg → Ti + 2 MgCl2

The product is a porous mass called titanium sponge, mixed with magnesium chloride and excess magnesium, which are removed by leaching or vacuum distillation. The magnesium chloride is recycled by electrolysis back into magnesium and chlorine.

Characteristics

The Kroll process is a batch process and relatively expensive, which explains titanium's high cost compared with steel or aluminium. Continuous alternatives, such as the electrochemical FFC Cambridge process, have been researched but the Kroll route still accounts for most world titanium sponge production, concentrated in countries such as Japan, Russia, China, Kazakhstan and Ukraine.
