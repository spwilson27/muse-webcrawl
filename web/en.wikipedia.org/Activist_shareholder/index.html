Activist shareholder

TypeInvestor strategy / corporate governance practice
MethodsProxy fights, public letters, board nominations, shareholder proposals
Notable practitionersCarl Icahn, Elliott Management, Third Point, Pershing Square

An activist shareholder is an investor who acquires a stake in a publicly traded company and uses that position to press management and the board for changes intended to increase shareholder value. Demands commonly include replacing directors or executives, returning cash through buybacks or dividends, selling or spinning off divisions, cutting costs, or blocking or forcing acquisitions.

Methods

Activists typically begin with private discussions, then escalate with public letters, media campaigns and regulatory filings. If management refuses to settle, the activist may nominate an alternative slate of directors and solicit other shareholders' proxies — a proxy fight. Campaigns often end in settlement agreements granting the activist board seats. Social and environmental shareholder proposals by smaller investors are a related, usually non-financial, form of activism.

History and debate

Shareholder activism grew out of the corporate raiders of the 1980s and expanded into a mainstream institutional strategy in the 2000s and 2010s, with dedicated hedge funds managing tens of billions of dollars. Supporters argue activism disciplines underperforming managers and improves returns; critics contend it encourages short-term thinking and underinvestment. Empirical studies find mixed but often positive short-term effects on share prices, with long-term effects debated.
