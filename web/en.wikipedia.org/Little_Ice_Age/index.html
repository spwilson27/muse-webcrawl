Little Ice Age

The Little Ice Age (LIA) was a period of regional cooling, particularly in the North Atlantic region, that occurred after the Medieval Climate Anomaly (Medieval Warm Period). It is conventionally dated from about 1300 to about 1850, though its onset and severity varied by region. It was not a true ice age — global temperatures fell modestly, on the order of a few tenths of a degree to about 1 °C in the Northern Hemisphere — but its effects on weather, harvests, and glaciers were dramatic.

Chronology and evidence

The term was introduced by François E. Matthes in 1939 to describe glacier advances. Evidence comes from tree rings, ice cores, lake and ocean sediments, and historical records: frost fairs on the frozen Thames in London (the last in 1814), advancing Alpine glaciers that overran farms and villages, shortened growing seasons, and the decline of Norse settlements in Greenland.

The coldest phases are often associated with grand solar minima — the Spörer Minimum (c. 1450–1550), the Maunder Minimum (1645–1715), when sunspots nearly vanished, and the Dalton Minimum (1790–1830). The year 1816, the "Year Without a Summer," followed the massive 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora, which injected sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere and caused crop failures across the Northern Hemisphere.

Causes

Proposed causes include clusters of large volcanic eruptions, reduced solar output, changes in ocean circulation and sea ice feedbacks, and land-use changes. Modern reconstructions suggest volcanism played a central triggering role, with internal variability sustaining the cooling.

Effects

Colder, more erratic weather contributed to famines (such as the Great Famine of 1315–1317 at the period's onset), shifts in agriculture (declining viticulture in England, greater reliance on potatoes), and social stresses across Europe and China. The period also left a cultural legacy in Dutch winter landscape painting by artists such as Hendrick Avercamp.
