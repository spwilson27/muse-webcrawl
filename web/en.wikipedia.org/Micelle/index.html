Micelle

Typical size2–20 nm diameter
Formed byAmphiphilic molecules (surfactants) above the critical micelle concentration
Key parameterCritical micelle concentration (CMC)

A micelle (also spelled micella, plural micelles or micellae) is an aggregate of surfactant amphipathic lipid molecules dispersed in a liquid, forming a colloidal suspension. In a polar solvent such as water, a typical micelle has the hydrophilic "head" regions facing outward toward the solvent and the hydrophobic single-tail regions sequestered in the center, forming a roughly spherical structure.

Formation

Micelles form only when the concentration of surfactant exceeds the critical micelle concentration (CMC) and the temperature exceeds the Krafft temperature. Below the CMC, surfactant molecules exist mainly as monomers at interfaces; above it, added surfactant assembles into micelles, each containing typically 50–100 molecules (the aggregation number). Formation is driven by the hydrophobic effect: burying the hydrocarbon tails releases ordered water molecules, increasing entropy.

Structure and types

In non-polar solvents, inverse (reverse) micelles form, with hydrophilic heads clustered in the core and tails pointing outward. Depending on surfactant geometry — summarized by the packing parameter — aggregates can also take cylindrical, bilayer, or vesicle forms. Mixed micelles of bile salts, phospholipids, and cholesterol in the intestine solubilize dietary fats and fat-soluble vitamins, enabling their absorption.

Uses

Miceillization underlies the cleaning action of soaps and detergents, which trap oily soil inside micelles that rinse away. Micelles are used in pharmaceutical formulation to solubilize poorly water-soluble drugs, as nanoreactors and templates in materials synthesis, in enhanced oil recovery, and in analytical techniques such as micellar electrokinetic chromatography.
