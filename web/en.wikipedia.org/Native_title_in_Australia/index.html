Native title in Australia

Native title is the Australian legal doctrine that recognises the rights and interests of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in land and waters according to their traditional laws and customs. It was first recognised by the High Court of Australia in Mabo v Queensland (No 2) (1992), which rejected the notion that Australia had been terra nullius at British settlement.

The Mabo decision

Eddie Mabo and other Meriam plaintiffs challenged Queensland's claim to the Murray Islands in the Torres Strait. On 3 June 1992, six of the seven High Court judges held that the Meriam people held native title to their lands, and that native title could survive the acquisition of sovereignty where it had not been extinguished and claimants maintained their connection with the land.

Native Title Act 1993

The federal parliament responded with the Native Title Act 1993, which set up a framework for claims, established the National Native Title Tribunal, and validated certain past acts. The later Wik Peoples v Queensland (1996) decision held that pastoral leases did not necessarily extinguish native title, prompting the controversial 1998 amendments known as the Ten Point Plan.

Operation

Native title may include rights to access, hunt, fish, camp, and conduct ceremonies, and in some cases exclusive possession. Claims are determined by the Federal Court, often after many years of evidence and negotiation, and many are resolved by consent determinations and Indigenous Land Use Agreements.
