Local food

Local food is food that is produced within a short distance of where it is consumed, often through direct-to-consumer channels such as farmers' markets, farm stands and community-supported agriculture (CSA) schemes. The local food movement advocates such production as more environmentally sustainable, economically beneficial to communities, and supportive of fresher, more flavourful eating. Enthusiasts are sometimes called "locavores" — a term coined in San Francisco in 2005 and chosen as the Oxford American Dictionary's word of the year in 2007.

Definitions

There is no single definition of "local". Distances used in practice range from "within 50 miles" to an entire region or state; the 2008 US Food, Conservation, and Energy Act adopted a threshold of 400 miles or within the same state. The "100-mile diet", popularised by Alisa Smith and J. B. MacKinnon, became a well-known personal challenge version of the idea.

Arguments and criticism

Supporters argue that local food keeps money in the local economy, preserves farmland and food traditions, reduces "food miles" and reconnects eaters with producers. Critics respond that transport is only a small share of food's total carbon footprint — production methods usually matter far more — and that efficient large-scale supply chains can outperform fragmented local distribution. Others note that local food can be more expensive and less accessible, raising questions of equity.

Institutions

Farmers' markets, CSA subscriptions, farm-to-table restaurants, school and hospital procurement programmes, and food hubs are the movement's main institutions. The philosophy overlaps with the Slow Food movement founded in Italy in 1986.
