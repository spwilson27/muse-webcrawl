Mine fire

A mine fire is a fire burning in a coal mine or in the seams of a coal deposit, often underground. Coal-seam fires can ignite by spontaneous combustion, lightning, bushfires, welding accidents or deliberate burning, and once established in a network of fractures and mine workings they may smoulder for decades or even centuries, consuming vast quantities of coal and venting heat, smoke and toxic gases through cracks at the surface. Thousands of mine fires are estimated to burn worldwide, notably in China, India, the United States, Australia and South Africa.

Fumaroles at the surface mark the path of underground coal fires.

Causes and behaviour

Coal oxidises exothermically when exposed to air; in crushed, fractured coal with the right balance of ventilation, heat accumulates until ignition — the mechanism of spontaneous combustion. Abandoned mines create ideal conditions: shafts and subsidence cracks supply oxygen, while pillars and unmined coal supply fuel. Fires spread slowly along seams, sometimes only metres per year, and can migrate beneath towns and infrastructure, causing subsidence voids, carbon monoxide hazards and emissions of CO₂, methane, mercury and particulates. Some fires are ancient: Australia's Burning Mountain (Mount Wingen) has burned for an estimated 6,000 years.

Famous examples

  • Centralia, Pennsylvania — a fire ignited in 1962 in a landfill above anthracite workings spread under the borough; the population was bought out and the town largely demolished, inspiring the video game and film Silent Hill.
  • Jharia, India — dozens of fires have burned in the Jharia coalfield since 1916, affecting hundreds of thousands of residents.
  • Burning Mountain, Australia — a naturally ignited seam fire moving slowly southward for millennia.
  • China — fires in Xinjiang and Inner Mongolia (e.g. Wuda) have consumed tens of millions of tonnes of coal; Chinese authorities estimate coal fires burn 10–200 million tonnes annually nationwide.

Detection and extinguishing

Fires are located with satellite thermal imaging, borehole temperature surveys, gas sampling and magnetic surveys (burnt rock retains distinctive magnetism). Control methods include sealing entries to starve oxygen, injecting water, foam, fly ash or inert gases, excavating and quenching shallow burning coal, and grouting barriers. Deep fires are rarely fully extinguished; many projects settle for containment and monitoring. Mine fires in working mines are also an acute safety hazard addressed by fire-fighting plans, refuge chambers and gas monitoring under mining safety law.
