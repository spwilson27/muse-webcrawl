Quartzite

Quartzite is a hard, non-foliated metamorphic rock which was originally pure quartz sandstone. Sandstone is converted into quartzite through heating and pressure usually related to tectonic compression within orogenic belts. Pure quartzite is usually white to grey, though quartzites often occur in various shades of pink and red due to varying amounts of iron oxide; other colours, such as yellow, green and blue, arise from other minerals.

Formation and properties

During metamorphism, the original quartz grains of the sandstone and the silica cement that binds them recrystallise into an interlocking mosaic of quartz crystals. The original textures and sedimentary structures of the sandstone are largely obliterated. Quartzite is extremely resistant to both mechanical and chemical weathering, frequently forms ridges and hilltops, and rates about 7 on the Mohs scale of hardness. Unlike sandstone, it fractures through grains rather than around them, producing a sugary, vitreous surface when broken.

Occurrence and uses

Notable occurrences include the Baraboo Range of Wisconsin, the quartzites of the Appalachian Mountains in the eastern United States, and deposits in Brazil, Norway and Mozambique. Quartzite is used as a decorative dimension stone, for countertops, wall cladding and flooring, and as a source of silica for glass and metallurgy. Historically it was used for stone tools, because it can be worked by knapping, and it is still used as railway ballast and road aggregate where exceptional hardness is required.
