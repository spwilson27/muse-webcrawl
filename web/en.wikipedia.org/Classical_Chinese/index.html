Classical Chinese

Classical Chinese (古文; gǔwén, "ancient text"), also known as Literary Chinese (文言文; wényánwén), is the language of the classic literature of China and the written standard used throughout the Sinosphere from the end of the Spring and Autumn period (early 5th century BC) until the early 20th century. It is a written form modelled on the speech of the late Zhou and Han dynasties, and it remained essentially frozen as speech evolved around it — much as Latin did in Europe — so that for most of its history it was learned as a second, literary language through the study of canonical texts such as the Confucian classics.

Characteristics

Classical Chinese is famously terse and largely monosyllabic: most words are a single character, and grammatical relations are conveyed by word order and a small set of function words (之, 乎, 者, 也, 矣, 焉 and so on) rather than inflection. Pronouns, conjunctions and objects are frequently omitted where context supplies them, and individual characters often carry multiple meanings, so ambiguity is endemic and traditionally resolved through commentary. Texts were written without punctuation; readers supply pauses (句讀) by sense.

History and use

The corpus ranges from the Confucian classics and histories of the Zhou and Han through Tang poetry, Song essays, Ming–Qing novels' formal passages, and the examination essays (including the rigid "eight-legged essay") by which imperial officials were selected. Because the script crossed dialect boundaries, Classical Chinese also served as the written lingua franca of East Asia: Korean, Vietnamese and Japanese literati composed in it, developing local reading traditions (such as Japanese kanbun kundoku, reading the text by re-ordering it into Japanese syntax). In the 1910s–20s, reformers of the New Culture Movement — notably Hu Shih — successfully campaigned to replace it in schools and publishing with written vernacular Chinese (baihua) based on Mandarin speech.

Modern survival

Classical Chinese is still taught in schools in China, Taiwan, Hong Kong and (in heritage form) elsewhere; its idioms (chengyu) pervade modern Chinese, and formal phrases survive in legal, ceremonial and newspaper registers across the Sinosphere.
