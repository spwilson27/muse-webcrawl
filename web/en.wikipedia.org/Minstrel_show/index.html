Minstrel show

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The minstrel show, also called minstrelsy, was an American form of theatre developed in the early nineteenth century in which performers — usually white, in blackface — presented comic skits, variety acts, dancing, and music that caricatured people of African descent. Minstrelsy was the first distinctly American theatrical form and dominated popular entertainment in the United States for much of the 1800s.

History and form

Thomas D. Rice popularized blackface performance around 1830 with his "Jim Crow" song and dance, and in 1843 Dan Emmett's Virginia Minstrels presented the first full evening show in New York. A typical show had three parts: a semicircle of performers exchanging jokes with a central interlocutor and the end men Tambo and Bones, an "olio" of variety acts, and a concluding one-act burlesque, often set on a plantation. Stephen Foster wrote many songs for the minstrel stage, and troupes such as Christy's Minstrels toured nationally.

Legacy

After the Civil War, African American troupes also performed minstrelsy, often still in blackface, and the form influenced vaudeville, musical theatre, radio, and early film. Minstrelsy is now widely understood as a vehicle of racist stereotype — its stock characters and dialect humour entrenched demeaning images of Black Americans — while scholars also note its complex role in transmitting African American music and dance into mainstream culture.
