Coriander (cilantro)

Coriander (Coriandrum sativum) is an annual herb in the family Apiaceae. Its fresh leaves are known in American English as cilantro (from the Spanish name for the plant) and are an essential ingredient in many cuisines, while its dried seeds are used as the spice coriander. The plant is native to a broad region spanning southern Europe, North Africa, and southwestern Asia.

Culinary use

Fresh cilantro leaves are used raw in Mexican cooking (salsas, guacamole, tacos), in Southeast Asian dishes such as Thai salads and Vietnamese pho, in Indian chutneys, and in Middle Eastern and Chinese cuisine. The seeds, with a warm, citrusy flavour quite unlike the leaves, are ground into curry powders and garam masala and used in pickling and sausage making. The roots are used in Thai cooking as a base for curry pastes.

Taste perception

A notable fraction of people perceive cilantro leaves as tasting soapy or unpleasant. This has been linked to genetic variation in olfactory receptor genes, notably OR6A2, which is sensitive to the aldehyde compounds that give the leaves (and soap) part of their aroma. Reported rates of dislike vary widely between ethnic groups and cultures.

Cultivation and history

Coriander is one of the oldest known spices; seeds were found in Neolithic sites and in Tutankhamun's tomb. It grows best in cool weather, bolting quickly in heat, and is grown worldwide as a leafy herb and seed crop.
