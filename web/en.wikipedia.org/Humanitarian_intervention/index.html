Humanitarian intervention

Humanitarian intervention is the use or threat of military force by a state or group of states, without the consent of the target state, aimed at preventing or ending widespread and grave violations of the fundamental human rights of individuals other than its own citizens. It is one of the most contested questions in international law and international relations, pitting the protection of human rights against the principles of state sovereignty and non-intervention in the UN Charter.

History

Interventions justified partly on humanitarian grounds date to the nineteenth century, such as European action over Greek independence and massacres in Ottoman Lebanon. The concept gained prominence after the Cold War: the 1991 no-fly zones over Iraq, operations in Somalia (1992–93), and NATO's 1999 bombing of Yugoslavia over Kosovo — described by an international commission as "illegal but legitimate." Failures to intervene, above all during the 1994 Rwandan genocide, also shaped the debate.

Responsibility to Protect

In 2001 the International Commission on Intervention and State Sovereignty proposed the Responsibility to Protect (R2P), reframing sovereignty as a responsibility. The 2005 UN World Summit adopted a version of R2P limited to genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity, with military action to be authorized by the Security Council as a last resort. R2P was invoked in the 2011 intervention in Libya, though the conduct of that campaign led several states to question its implementation.

Debate

Supporters argue intervention can halt atrocities and that sovereignty cannot shield mass murder. Critics cite the selectivity of interventions, risks of escalation and mission creep, doubtful legality without Security Council authorization, and the possibility that humanitarian rhetoric masks strategic motives.
