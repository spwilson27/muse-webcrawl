Alkaline earth metal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Samples of alkaline earth metals

The alkaline earth metals are six chemical elements in group 2 of the periodic table. They are beryllium (Be), magnesium (Mg), calcium (Ca), strontium (Sr), barium (Ba), and radium (Ra). The elements have very similar properties: they are all shiny, silvery-white, somewhat reactive metals at standard temperature and pressure.

Together with the alkali metals, they form the s-block of the periodic table. All of the alkaline earth metals have two electrons in their outermost shell, and readily lose them to form cations with a charge of +2 and an electron configuration of a noble gas.

The alkaline earth metals are all silver-colored and soft, and have relatively low densities, melting points, and boiling points. Chemically, they react with halogens to form ionic halides and with water (not as rapidly as the alkali metals) to form strongly alkaline hydroxides.

History

The alkaline earths were named after their oxides, the alkaline earths, whose old names were beryllia, magnesia, lime, strontia, and baryta. These oxides are basic (alkaline) when combined with water. The term "earth" was an old term applied by early chemists to nonmetallic substances that were insoluble in water and remained unchanged by heating.

Humphry Davy first isolated magnesium, calcium, strontium and barium by electrolysis in 1808. Radium was discovered by Marie and Pierre Curie in 1898, and beryllium was independently isolated by Friedrich Wöhler and Antoine Bussy in 1828.

Occurrence and applications

Magnesium and calcium are among the most abundant elements in the Earth's crust and have major biological roles. Calcium compounds form shells, bones and limestone. Magnesium alloys are used in aircraft and pyrotechnics; strontium and barium salts give red and green colours to fireworks. Radium is highly radioactive and was once used in luminous paint.
