Autoclave

A bench-top laboratory autoclave used to sterilise instruments and media.

An autoclave is a machine used to carry out industrial and scientific processes requiring elevated temperature and pressure, most commonly the steam sterilisation of equipment and supplies. Unlike boiling, steam under pressure can reach temperatures of 121–134 °C (250–273 °F), sufficient to kill bacteria, viruses, fungi and highly resistant bacterial spores.

History

The steam digester, an ancestor of both the autoclave and the domestic pressure cooker, was invented by Denis Papin in 1679. Charles Chamberland, working in Louis Pasteur's laboratory, developed the modern autoclave in 1879, and it became a foundation of aseptic technique in medicine and microbiology.

Operation

Items are placed in a sealed pressure vessel; air is removed (by gravity displacement or vacuum pump) and saturated steam is admitted. A standard sterilising cycle holds the load at 121 °C at about 15 psi above atmospheric pressure for at least 15 minutes, or at 134 °C for shorter periods. Chemical indicators and biological indicators using Geobacillus stearothermophilus spores are used to verify that sterilisation has been achieved.

Uses

Autoclaves sterilise surgical instruments, laboratory glassware and microbiological culture media, and are used to decontaminate regulated medical waste before disposal. Industrial autoclaves cure composites for aerospace, vulcanise rubber, process food and treat timber; in construction, autoclaved aerated concrete takes its name from the process. Not all materials can be autoclaved — prions, for example, require extended cycles or chemical treatment, and some plastics and sharp instruments are damaged by repeated exposure.
