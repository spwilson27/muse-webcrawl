The Black Paintings (Spanish: Pinturas negras) are a group of fourteen works by Spanish artist Francisco Goya, created between about 1819 and 1823. Goya, then in his seventies and profoundly disillusioned, painted them directly onto the walls of his house, the Quinta del Sordo ("Villa of the Deaf Man"), on the outskirts of Madrid. They were never intended for public display and were not given titles by the artist; the names by which they are known were assigned later by art historians.

Subjects

The paintings occupy two floors of the house and are dominated by dark tones and disturbing themes: Saturn Devouring His Son, Witches' Sabbath, Judith and Holofernes, A Manola: Leocadia, The Dog, Fight with Cudgels, Atropos (The Fates) and Two Old Men Eating Soup among them. Interpretations link their bleakness to Goya's deafness, his fears of insanity, the political repression of Ferdinand VII's Spain, and memories of the Peninsular War.

Transfer and legacy

After Goya's exile to France, the house passed through several owners. In the 1870s the murals were transferred from plaster to canvas under the direction of Salvador Martínez Cubells of the Prado — a process that damaged and altered parts of the works — and in 1881 they were donated to the Museo del Prado, where they are now among its most visited exhibits. The Black Paintings are regarded as precursors of modern art and expressionism, and questions persist about their dating, arrangement and even, controversially, their attribution.
