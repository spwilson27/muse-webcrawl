Rafflesia
Genus of parasitic flowering plants
Family Rafflesiaceae; native to Southeast Asian rainforests

Rafflesia is a genus of parasitic flowering plants in the family Rafflesiaceae. The species have enormous flowers, the buds emerging from the ground or directly from lower stems of host plants; one species has the largest flower in the world. The genus, native to the rainforests of Southeast Asia, contains some 28 species.

Description

The plants have no stems, leaves or true roots; they live inside vines of the genus Tetrastigma, spreading thread-like tissue through the host and appearing only to flower. Rafflesia arnoldii produces individual flowers over one metre across and weighing up to 11 kilograms. The flowers smell of rotting flesh — hence local names like "corpse flower" — attracting carrion flies that pollinate them.

History and conservation

The genus is named for Sir Stamford Raffles, in whose 1818 expedition to Sumatra the plant was found by Joseph Arnold. Many species are threatened by habitat loss and by their dependence on a specific host vine, forest trees for seed dispersal, and both male and female flowers blooming simultaneously nearby.

Classification

Despite their strange form, molecular evidence places Rafflesiaceae within the order Malpighiales, close to the spurge family; they have undergone extreme genome changes, even losing their chloroplast genome entirely.
