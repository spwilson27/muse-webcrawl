Murray Gell-Mann (September 15, 1929 – May 24, 2019) was an American theoretical physicist who received the 1969 Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on the theory of elementary particles. He was the Robert Andrews Millikan Professor of Theoretical Physics Emeritus at the California Institute of Technology, a co-founder of the Santa Fe Institute, and a frequent contributor to the study of complex systems.

Early life and education

Gell-Mann was born in Manhattan, New York City, to Jewish immigrant parents from Austria-Hungary. A child prodigy, he entered Yale University at the age of 15 and graduated in 1948. He earned his PhD in physics in 1951 from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology under Victor Weisskopf, at the age of 21.

Career

After postdoctoral work at the Institute for Advanced Study and the University of Chicago, Gell-Mann joined the faculty of Caltech in 1955, where he remained for the rest of his career. In collaboration with Richard Feynman he developed the V–A theory of the weak interaction. In 1961 he introduced a classification scheme for elementary particles known as the Eightfold Way, and in 1964 he proposed the existence of quarks — fundamental constituents of protons and neutrons — independently of George Zweig. His work laid the foundations of quantum chromodynamics, the theory of the strong nuclear force.

Later interests

Outside particle physics, Gell-Mann pursued interests in linguistics, ornithology, archaeology, and complexity science. His popular book The Quark and the Jaguar (1994) explored the relationship between the fundamental laws of physics and the complexity of the natural world. He co-founded the Santa Fe Institute in 1984 to promote interdisciplinary research on complex adaptive systems.

Legacy

Gell-Mann is regarded as one of the most influential physicists of the 20th century. The word "quark," which he borrowed from James Joyce's Finnegans Wake, became a permanent part of scientific vocabulary, and his classification schemes transformed the chaotic "particle zoo" of the 1950s into an orderly system.
