Wigner-Seitz cell

The Wigner-Seitz cell is a type of primitive cell used in the study of crystals, named after physicists Eugene Wigner and Frederick Seitz. It is defined as the set of all points in space that are closer to one particular lattice point than to any of the others, making it identical to the Voronoi cell of the lattice.

Definition and construction

To construct the cell, lines are drawn from a chosen lattice point to its nearest neighbours, and perpendicular bisector planes are placed at the midpoints of those lines. The polyhedron enclosed by these planes around the point is the Wigner-Seitz cell. Each cell contains exactly one lattice point, so the cell is a primitive cell, and by construction it displays the full symmetry of the lattice.

Use in solid-state physics

Applied to the reciprocal lattice, the construction yields the first Brillouin zone, which governs electron and phonon states in a periodic crystal. In their original 1933 work on metallic sodium, Wigner and Seitz also approximated the cell by a sphere of equal volume — the Wigner-Seitz radius — a quantity still used throughout condensed matter physics, often expressed through the density parameter rs.

Shapes

The cell takes characteristic polyhedral shapes for common lattices: a cube for the simple cubic lattice, a rhombic dodecahedron for the face-centred cubic lattice, and a truncated octahedron for the body-centred cubic lattice.
