The French Foreign Legion (French: Légion étrangère) is a corps of the French Army comprising several units — infantry, cavalry, engineers, and airborne troops — that are commanded by French officers but open to foreign volunteers from any nation. Founded in 1831, it is famous for its esprit de corps, its anonymity tradition, and its role in France's colonial and modern expeditionary wars.

History

King Louis Philippe created the Legion in 1831 to employ foreign soldiers left in France after the July Revolution and to fight in Algeria. Legionnaires subsequently served in the Crimean War, Italy, Mexico — where at the Battle of Camarón (1863), 65 legionnaires under Captain Danjou held off some 2,000 Mexican troops, an episode commemorated every 30 April on the Legion's main holiday — Indochina (including Dien Bien Phu), both World Wars, the Gulf War, Afghanistan, and Mali.

Recruitment and traditions

Volunteers enlist under a declared identity for an initial five-year contract and may apply for French citizenship after three years of service (or immediately if wounded in action, under the principle Français par le sang versé). Distinctive traditions include the white kepi, the slow ceremonial marching pace of 88 steps per minute, the song Le Boudin, and the code of honour Honneur et Fidélité. Modern legionnaires come from more than 140 countries.
