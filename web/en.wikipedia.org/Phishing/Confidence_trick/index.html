Confidence trick

A confidence trick is an attempt to defraud a person or group after first gaining their trust — hence the perpetrator's traditional name, the "confidence man" or con artist. Phishing is the confidence trick translated to electronic communication: instead of a plausible stranger in a nineteenth-century street, the victim faces a plausible email, text message or website wearing the branding of a bank, a delivery company or an employer.

Shared anatomy

Classic cons and phishing campaigns share the same stages. The approach establishes a trustworthy persona — a forged sender address, a cloned login page, a spoofed caller ID. The build-up creates pressure: an account about to be closed, a parcel held at customs, a superior demanding secrecy, as in business email compromise. The payoff extracts money or credentials, and the cooling-out phase delays the victim's realisation, for instance with a fake "processing" page, giving the fraudster time to exploit the haul — passwords, personal data or access to payment cards (see credit card fraud).

From street to inbox

Historical schemes such as the Spanish Prisoner letter evolved directly into advance-fee email fraud, and legendary hackers such as Kevin Mitnick popularised the term "social engineering" for the same art applied to computer systems. The lesson security trainers draw from the parallel is that phishing exploits human trust rather than technical flaws, so defences must include verification habits and phishing-resistant authentication, not merely better software.
