Phishing


A phishing message typically imitates a trusted sender and demands urgent action.

Phishing is a form of social engineering and a scam in which attackers deceive people into revealing sensitive information or installing malware, usually by impersonating a trustworthy entity in an electronic communication. The name is a play on "fishing": attackers cast out fraudulent lures hoping someone bites. Most phishing is delivered by email, but the term covers attacks by text message (smishing), voice call (vishing), social media, and malicious websites.

Techniques

Common features include spoofed sender addresses, near-identical copies of legitimate login pages hosted on look-alike domains, manufactured urgency ("your account will be closed"), and attachments carrying malware. Spear phishing targets a specific individual or organisation with personalised content; whaling targets senior executives; business email compromise impersonates a CEO or supplier to redirect payments. Attackers increasingly use QR codes, adversary-in-the-middle proxies that relay multi-factor authentication, and AI-generated text and voice to improve believability.

History and scale

The term emerged in the mid-1990s among hackers stealing AOL account credentials, and the practice industrialised in the 2000s with phishing kits sold to low-skill criminals. Industry groups such as the Anti-Phishing Working Group track hundreds of thousands of unique phishing sites per quarter, and phishing is consistently among the most common initial vectors in reported data breaches.

Countermeasures

Defences combine technology and behaviour: email authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC), browser and mail filters that block known malicious sites, hardware-backed multi-factor authentication and passkeys that resist credential replay, organisational training and simulated-phishing programmes, and rapid takedown of fraudulent domains. Because phishing exploits human trust rather than software flaws, no purely technical control eliminates it.
