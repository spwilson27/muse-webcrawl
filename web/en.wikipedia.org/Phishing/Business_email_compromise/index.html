Business email compromise

Business email compromise (BEC) is a form of financially motivated social engineering in which an attacker impersonates — or gains control of — the email account of an executive, supplier or lawyer in order to induce an employee to transfer money or disclose sensitive information. It is a targeted descendant of phishing, relying less on malicious software than on the classic techniques of the confidence trick.

How it works

In the common "CEO fraud" variant, a finance employee receives a plausible, urgent request, apparently from the chief executive, to pay an invoice or complete a confidential acquisition payment to a new bank account controlled by the criminals. Attackers may first harvest credentials through phishing — defeating weak authentication — and then study genuine correspondence, sometimes obtained in a wider data breach, so that the fraudulent message matches the victim company's style and ongoing transactions. Look-alike domains differing by a single character are frequently registered for the same purpose.

Scale and countermeasures

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center has attributed tens of billions of dollars in exposed losses to BEC schemes, which often exceed the damage of mass phishing because a single success can yield a six- or seven-figure transfer. Defences are largely procedural: out-of-band verification of payment changes, dual authorisation, and phishing-resistant authentication for mailboxes, since technical filters struggle against messages that contain no malware or malicious links at all. BEC directed at senior executives overlaps with whaling.
