Reparations for slavery are the proposed application of the concept of reparations to victims of the transatlantic slave trade and of slavery in the Americas and elsewhere, and to their descendants. Proposals range from formal apologies and debt cancellation to direct financial compensation, development programmes, and institutional reforms.

Historical background

At abolition, compensation was frequently paid not to the enslaved but to slave owners. Britain's Slavery Abolition Act 1833 allocated £20 million (about 40% of the national budget) to compensate owners, a loan not finally repaid by British taxpayers until 2015. In the United States, the short-lived wartime order known as "forty acres and a mule" (Special Field Orders No. 15, 1865) granted land to freed families but was soon reversed; no federal reparations were paid to the formerly enslaved. Haiti, conversely, was forced to pay indemnities to France for generations in return for recognition of its independence (1825–1947).

The modern debate

In the United States, Representative John Conyers introduced H.R. 40 — the Commission to Study and Develop Reparation Proposals for African-Americans Act — in every Congress from 1989 until 2017; Ta-Nehisi Coates's 2014 essay "The Case for Reparations" revived public discussion. In the 2020s, local and state initiatives proceeded in places such as Evanston, Illinois, and through California's Reparations Task Force, which reported in 2023.

In the Caribbean, the CARICOM Reparations Commission adopted a Ten-Point Plan in 2013 seeking, among other things, a formal apology, debt cancellation, repatriation support, and development funding from European former slave-trading nations. In Africa, demands were articulated at the 2001 World Conference against Racism in Durban, which declared slavery a crime against humanity.

Arguments

Supporters argue that slavery and subsequent discriminatory policies created durable wealth gaps, that institutions and nations were unjustly enriched, and that precedents exist — German reparations to Jewish survivors of the Holocaust, and US payments to Japanese Americans interned during the Second World War. Critics respond that identifying eligible recipients and payers after many generations is impractical, that present taxpayers did not themselves own slaves, and that programmes of general social investment would be fairer or more effective. Public opinion remains divided in most countries where the question is polled.
