Heavy water (deuterium oxide, D2O) is a form of water in which the hydrogen atoms are the isotope deuterium (²H or D) rather than the common isotope protium (¹H). It is chemically similar to ordinary water but has somewhat different physical properties: it is about 10.6 percent denser, melts at 3.82 °C and boils at 101.4 °C.

History and production

Deuterium was discovered by Harold Urey in 1931, and heavy water was first isolated soon afterwards. Natural water contains only a small fraction of deuterium — about one deuterium atom per 6,400 hydrogen atoms — so enrichment is energy-intensive. Industrial production historically used the Girdler sulfide process, electrolysis, or distillation. During the Second World War, the Norsk Hydro plant at Vemork in Norway was the world's main source of heavy water; Allied concern about a German nuclear weapons programme led to a famous series of commando sabotage operations against the plant, celebrated as the Heroes of Telemark.

Uses

Heavy water's most important use is as a neutron moderator in certain nuclear reactors, such as the Canadian CANDU design. Because deuterium absorbs far fewer neutrons than ordinary hydrogen, heavy-water reactors can sustain a chain reaction with natural, unenriched uranium. Heavy water is also used in nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, in neutrino observatories such as the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory (which used 1,000 tonnes of heavy water), and as a tracer in biological studies.

Biological effects

Although not radioactive, heavy water is mildly toxic in large proportions: replacing a substantial fraction of body water with D2O disrupts cell division and enzyme kinetics, and high levels are lethal to most organisms. The small amounts encountered naturally are harmless.
