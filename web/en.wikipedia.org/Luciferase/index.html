Luciferase

Luciferase is a generic term for the class of oxidative enzymes that produce bioluminescence. The name is derived from the Latin lucifer, "light-bringer". The best-studied example is the luciferase of the firefly Photinus pyralis. Luciferases catalyse the oxidation of a substrate called luciferin, in the presence of oxygen and (for the firefly enzyme) ATP, producing light.

Reaction

In fireflies, luciferase catalyses the reaction of D-luciferin with oxygen and ATP to produce oxyluciferin, carbon dioxide, AMP, pyrophosphate and photons, typically yellow-green light around 560 nm. Bacterial luciferases use a reduced flavin mononucleotide and a long-chain aldehyde, and emit blue-green light.

Uses in research

Luciferase genes are widely used as reporter genes in molecular biology: because light output can be measured extremely sensitively, luciferase expression reports on gene expression, promoter activity and cell viability. Luciferase-based assays are used in drug discovery, in vivo imaging of tumours and infections, and in hygiene monitoring (ATP bioluminescence). Variants such as NanoLuc have been engineered for brightness and stability.

Diversity

Bioluminescence evolved independently many times, and luciferases from different lineages — insects, bacteria, dinoflagellates, marine animals — are not homologous, an example of convergent evolution. The sea pansy enzyme, which uses coelenterazine, is another widely used reporter.
