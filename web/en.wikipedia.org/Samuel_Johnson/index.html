Samuel Johnson

Born: 18 September 1709 (O.S. 7 September), Lichfield, Staffordshire, England

Died: 13 December 1784, London

Occupations: Lexicographer, essayist, poet, critic, biographer

Notable works: A Dictionary of the English Language (1755), Lives of the Poets (1779–81), The Rambler

Biographer: James Boswell, Life of Samuel Johnson (1791)

Samuel Johnson (18 September 1709 – 13 December 1784), often called Dr Johnson, was an English writer who made lasting contributions to English literature as a poet, playwright, essayist, moralist, critic, sermonist, biographer, editor and lexicographer. The Oxford Dictionary of National Biography calls him “arguably the most distinguished man of letters in English history”. His A Dictionary of the English Language (1755) was the pre-eminent English dictionary for more than a century, and James Boswell’s Life of Samuel Johnson (1791) made his conversation — witty, combative and quotable — as famous as his books.

Life

Johnson was born in Lichfield, the son of a bookseller, and was educated at Lichfield Grammar School and Pembroke College, Oxford, which poverty forced him to leave without a degree after thirteen months. After years of struggle as a schoolmaster and hack writer in London — to which he walked in 1737 with his former pupil David Garrick — he slowly built a reputation through his poem London (1738), his parliamentary reports for The Gentleman’s Magazine, and the twice-weekly essays of The Rambler (1750–52), which he wrote almost single-handed.

The Dictionary

In 1746 a consortium of booksellers contracted Johnson to produce a dictionary of the English language. Working with only a handful of amanuenses in Gough Square, he completed in nine years what the French Academy, with forty members, had needed forty years to attempt for French. Published in April 1755 in two huge folio volumes, the Dictionary contained about 42,773 entries illustrated by some 114,000 quotations, and personal touches that have kept it readable ever since: oats is defined as “a grain, which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland supports the people”, and lexicographer as “a harmless drudge”.

Later work and conversation

A royal pension in 1762 freed Johnson from hackwork. His later achievements include a celebrated edition of Shakespeare (1765), the political pamphlets of the 1770s, A Journey to the Western Islands of Scotland (1775), recounting his tour of the Hebrides with Boswell, and the Lives of the Most Eminent English Poets (1779–81), a masterpiece of literary biography and criticism. The Literary Club, founded with Sir Joshua Reynolds in 1764, gathered Burke, Goldsmith, Gibbon and Garrick around his table. Johnson died in December 1784 and was buried in Westminster Abbey.
