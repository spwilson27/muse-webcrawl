Eurocentrism

Eurocentrism (also Eurocentricity) is a worldview centred on Europe and Europeans, in which European history, culture and values are treated — explicitly or implicitly — as the universal standard against which other societies are measured. The term is used mainly as a critique, in history, anthropology, postcolonial studies and education.

Usage and critique

Critics argue that Eurocentrism projects European categories onto world history: for example, narrating global modernity as a purely European achievement diffusing outward, treating "feudalism" or "the Renaissance" as universal stages, or presenting colonial expansion as a civilising mission. Samir Amin's Eurocentrism (1988) gave the concept wide currency, arguing it was an ideological component of capitalist modernity. Jack Goody's The Theft of History (2006) similarly accused European scholarship of appropriating inventions such as capitalism, democracy and individualism as uniquely European.

Responses

Alternatives proposed include world-systems analysis, connected and global history, and curriculum reforms giving weight to non-European perspectives. Debates continue over whether rejecting Eurocentrism risks falling into romanticisation or a mirror-image essentialism about other cultures.
