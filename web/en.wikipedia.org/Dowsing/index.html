Dowsing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dowsing is a type of divination employed in attempts to locate ground water, buried metals or ores, gemstones, oil, and other objects and materials, usually without the use of a scientific apparatus. It is also known as divining, doodlebugging (in the US) or water witching when specifically searching for water.

Practice

The dowser typically walks over an area holding a forked (Y- or L-shaped) rod — traditionally hazel, willow or copper — or a pendulum, watching for a dip, swing or crossing of the rods said to indicate the target. Dowsing for water to site wells remains common in rural areas, and some utility workers historically used rods to find pipes. Map dowsing, using a pendulum over a map, is also practised.

Scientific assessment

Dowsing has been practised for centuries — it is illustrated in Agricola's De re metallica (1556) — but scientific studies have found no evidence that it works better than chance. Controlled tests, including the large Munich barn experiments of the late 1980s (whose positive early claims were not replicated on re-analysis) and later blinded trials, attribute rod movements to the ideomotor effect: unconscious muscular movements by the dowser. Dowsing is therefore generally classified as a pseudoscience, though it persists in folk practice worldwide.
