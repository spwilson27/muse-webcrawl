Dot matrix printing

Dot matrix printing, sometimes called impact matrix printing, is a computer printing process in which ink is applied to a surface using a relatively low-resolution dot matrix layout. A dot matrix printer uses a print head that moves back and forth, or in an up-and-down motion, on the page and prints by impact, striking an ink-soaked cloth ribbon against the paper, much like the print mechanism on a typewriter or line printer.

Mechanism

The print head carries a vertical column of fine metal pins (commonly 9, or 24 for "near letter quality" output), each driven by a small electromagnetic solenoid or hammer. As the head traverses the page, selected pins fire against the ribbon, impressing one column of dots at a time; characters and graphics are built up column by column. Paper is usually fed by a tractor mechanism engaging sprocket holes along the edges of fan-fold continuous stationery. Because printing is by impact, dot matrix printers can produce carbon copies and multi-part forms in a single pass, something inkjet and laser printers cannot do.

History

The first dot matrix printer was OKI's Wiredot (1968). Digital Equipment Corporation's LA30 (1970), printing 30 characters per second, helped popularise the technology for computer terminals. In the personal computer era, models such as the Epson MX-80 (1980) and the long-lived Epson FX and LQ series, and printers from Star Micronics and Citizen, became ubiquitous in homes and offices. 24-pin models offered much improved text quality, but by the mid-1990s cheap inkjet and laser printers had displaced dot matrix machines for general use.

Modern uses

Dot matrix printers remain in use for invoices, delivery notes, receipts, cheques, and other multipart business forms, in banking passbook printers, and in industrial and point-of-sale environments where their robustness, cheap consumables, and ability to print through copies are valued.
