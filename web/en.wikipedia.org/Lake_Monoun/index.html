Lake Monoun is a crater lake in the West Region of Cameroon, lying in the Oku Volcanic Field along the Cameroon volcanic line. It is one of three lakes in Cameroon known to contain large amounts of dissolved carbon dioxide in their deep waters, the others being Lake Nyos and Lake Kivu (on the border of Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of Congo, which contains methane as well).

The 1984 disaster

On 15 August 1984, Lake Monoun underwent a limnic eruption — a sudden overturn of the lake that released a large volume of dissolved carbon dioxide gas. The gas cloud, heavier than air, flowed into a nearby low-lying populated area, suffocating 37 people. At the time the cause was mysterious; initial speculation involved a volcanic eruption or sabotage. Investigation of the Monoun event, and of the far deadlier eruption at Lake Nyos two years later in which about 1,746 people died, established the limnic eruption mechanism: volcanic CO2 seeps into and accumulates in the still bottom waters of deep crater lakes until a disturbance triggers an explosive release.

Degassing

To reduce the risk of recurrence, engineers installed degassing pipes in Lake Monoun in 2003, following the pioneering degassing column at Lake Nyos. The pipes siphon gas-rich water from the depths to the surface, where the carbon dioxide is released gradually at safe rates. By the 2010s the dissolved gas concentration in Monoun had been substantially reduced, and scientists judged the lake much safer, though monitoring continues.
