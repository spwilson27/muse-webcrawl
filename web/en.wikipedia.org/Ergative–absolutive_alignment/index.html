Ergative–absolutive alignment

Ergative–absolutive alignment groups the intransitive subject (S) with the object (O)

In linguistic typology, ergative–absolutive alignment is a type of morphosyntactic alignment in which the subject of an intransitive verb behaves like the object of a transitive verb, and differently from the subject of a transitive verb. The transitive subject receives a special ergative case, while the intransitive subject and transitive object share the unmarked absolutive case.

Terminology

The three core arguments are conventionally labelled S (the single argument of an intransitive clause), A (the agent-like argument of a transitive clause), and O (the patient-like argument). In nominative–accusative languages such as English, S and A are grouped as subjects. In ergative–absolutive languages, S and O are grouped instead.

Distribution

Fully ergative languages are a minority worldwide but are found in many regions: Basque in Europe; Georgian and other Kartvelian and Northeast Caucasian languages; Mayan languages in Mesoamerica; Eskimo–Aleut languages in the Arctic; many Australian Aboriginal languages; and various languages of New Guinea and South Asia. Many more languages, including Hindi-Urdu, Kurdish (in past tenses), and some Polynesian languages, show partial or split ergativity.

Morphological and syntactic ergativity

Ergativity can appear in case marking, in verb agreement, or in syntax. Most ergative languages are only morphologically ergative: their syntax (for coordination, relativisation, and control) still groups S with A. Dyirbal of Australia is the classic case of deep syntactic ergativity, where coordination requires the shared argument to be in the S/O function. Antipassive constructions are common in ergative languages, serving to promote the A argument into the absolutive so it can participate in syntactic processes reserved for S and O.
