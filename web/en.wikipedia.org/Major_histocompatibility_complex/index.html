The major histocompatibility complex (MHC) is a large locus on vertebrate DNA containing a set of closely linked polymorphic genes that code for cell surface proteins essential for the adaptive immune system. These cell surface proteins are called MHC molecules. The MHC has been identified in all jawed vertebrates. In humans the MHC region — also called the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) complex — lies on chromosome 6 and spans about 4 million base pairs, containing more than 200 genes.

MHC molecules display peptide fragments derived from pathogens (or from the cell itself) on the cell surface, where they are recognised by T cells. This antigen presentation is central to the immune system's discrimination between self and non-self, and the extreme diversity of MHC genes in populations is thought to be maintained by pathogen-driven balancing selection.

Classes

Class I

MHC class I molecules (HLA-A, -B, -C in humans) are expressed on nearly all nucleated cells. They present peptides from intracellular proteins — including viral proteins in infected cells — to cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. A cell displaying foreign peptides is targeted for killing.

Class II

MHC class II molecules (HLA-DP, -DQ, -DR) are expressed mainly on professional antigen-presenting cells such as dendritic cells, macrophages, and B cells. They present peptides from extracellular material taken up by endocytosis or phagocytosis to helper CD4+ T cells.

Class III

The class III region, lying between classes I and II, contains genes for complement components (C2, C4, factor B), tumour necrosis factor, and other immune-related proteins that are not themselves antigen-presenting molecules.

Discovery and naming

The MHC was discovered through studies of tissue transplantation: grafts between genetically dissimilar individuals were rejected because of differing "histocompatibility" antigens. Peter Gorer identified the mouse H-2 complex in the 1930s–40s, George Snell developed congenic mouse strains that defined it genetically, and Jean Dausset described the first human leukocyte antigen in 1958. Snell, Dausset, and Baruj Benacerraf shared the 1980 Nobel Prize for this work. Baruj Benacerraf showed that immune responsiveness is genetically controlled via the MHC.

Medical significance

Matching of HLA types is crucial for organ and haematopoietic stem-cell transplantation. Particular HLA alleles are strongly associated with autoimmune diseases — for example HLA-B27 with ankylosing spondylitis and HLA-DQ2/DQ8 with coeliac disease — and with adverse drug reactions and susceptibility to infections. MHC diversity also influences mate choice in some animals via odour cues.
