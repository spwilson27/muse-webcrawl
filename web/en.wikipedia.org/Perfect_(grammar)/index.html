Perfect (grammar)

CategoryGrammatical aspect / tense form
English formsPresent perfect, past perfect (pluperfect), future perfect
Construction (English)Auxiliary have + past participle

The perfect is a grammatical construction used to describe an event that occurred before a given point of reference, often with results or relevance at that point. In English it is formed with an inflected form of the auxiliary verb have together with the past participle of the main verb: "I have eaten," "she had left," "they will have finished."

Forms

English grammars commonly distinguish the present perfect ("I have seen it"), the past perfect or pluperfect ("I had seen it (before then)"), and the future perfect ("I will have seen it (by then)"). Because the auxiliary carries tense, many analyses treat the perfect as an aspect rather than a tense, and the combined forms as tense–aspect combinations. Usage restrictions vary: standard English disallows the present perfect with definite past-time adverbials ("*I have seen it yesterday").

Meanings

Linguists describe several characteristic uses of the present perfect: the resultative ("I have lost my keys" — they are lost now), the experiential ("She has visited Japan twice"), the recent-past or "hot news" use ("The minister has resigned"), and the persistent situation use ("We have lived here since 2010"). Theories of the perfect generally appeal to a notion of "current relevance" linking a prior event to the reference time.

In other languages

Many European languages have perfect constructions built with "have" or "be" plus a participle, such as the French passé composé and the German Perfekt; in several languages these have expanded into general past tenses, a development also visible in colloquial English in some dialects. Ancient Greek and Latin had distinct perfect forms: the Latin perfect tense combined perfective past and perfect functions, and the Greek perfect often denoted a resulting state.
