How the Other Half Lives

How the Other Half Lives
Author: Jacob Riis
Published: 1890
Genre: Photojournalism / social documentary
Publisher: Charles Scribner's Sons

How the Other Half Lives: Studies Among the Tenements of New York (1890) is an early work of photojournalism by the Danish-American reporter and photographer Jacob Riis. The book documents the squalid living conditions of the slums of New York City in the 1880s and is credited with helping to spur tenement reform in the Progressive Era.

Content

Riis, a police reporter for the New-York Tribune, drew on his nightly rounds of the city's poorest districts — the Lower East Side, Five Points and Mulberry Bend among them. Using the newly developed technique of flash powder photography, he captured images of dark, overcrowded tenement rooms, lodging houses, sweatshops, street children ("street Arabs") and cellar dwellings, pairing them with statistics and vivid narrative. The title derives from the saying that "one half of the world does not know how the other half lives".

Impact

The book was a bestseller and provoked public debate. Among its effects were the demolition of Mulberry Bend — replaced by a park — and reforms in water supply, sanitation and building codes; New York police commissioner Theodore Roosevelt, who accompanied Riis on some rounds, closed the police lodging houses Riis had exposed. Together with later muckraking works, it became a landmark of reform literature and of documentary photography, though modern critics also examine its period prejudices toward immigrant groups.
