Dickerson v. United States

Dickerson v. United States
530 U.S. 428 (2000)
Decided June 26, 2000
Chief Justice Rehnquist delivered the opinion of the Court (7–2)

Dickerson v. United States, 530 U.S. 428 (2000), was a United States Supreme Court case that upheld the requirement that law enforcement officers give Miranda warnings to suspects in custody before interrogation. The Court held that Miranda v. Arizona (1966) had announced a constitutional rule that Congress could not supersede by statute.

Background

In 1968, Congress enacted 18 U.S.C. § 3501, which made the admissibility of confessions in federal court turn solely on voluntariness, effectively attempting to overrule Miranda. The provision was rarely invoked for decades, until the Fourth Circuit upheld it in the case of Charles Dickerson, who had been charged with bank robbery and related offences and whose statements were admitted without a challenge to their voluntariness.

Holding

In a 7–2 decision written by Chief Justice William Rehnquist — a longstanding critic of Miranda — the Court held that Miranda was a constitutional decision and that its warnings had become embedded in routine police practice and national culture. Section 3501 was therefore unconstitutional insofar as it conflicted with Miranda.
