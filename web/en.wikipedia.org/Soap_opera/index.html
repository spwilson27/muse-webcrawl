Soap opera

A soap opera set; the genre is named for the soap sponsors of early radio serials

A soap opera, sometimes shortened to soap, is a radio or television serial drama characterised by melodrama, ensemble casts and open-ended storylines that continue over multiple episodes and seasons. The name derives from the original dramatic serials broadcast on American radio during the 1930s, which were sponsored by soap and detergent manufacturers such as Procter & Gamble and aimed at a predominantly female daytime audience.

Characteristics

Soap operas focus on the relationships, romances, family conflicts and moral dilemmas of a community of characters. Unlike episodic series, their plots are serialised and effectively never-ending: storylines interweave, cliffhangers close most episodes, and characters may return from apparent death or be recast. Production is rapid and economical — US daytime soaps traditionally produced an episode a day on standing sets — which creates the genre's distinctive visual style of close-ups and dialogue-driven scenes.

History and examples

The genre began on American radio with programmes such as Painted Dreams (1930) and moved to television in the late 1940s. Landmark examples include Guiding Light (1937–2009 across radio and TV, the longest-running soap), Coronation Street (UK, from 1960), EastEnders (UK, from 1985), Neighbours and Home and Away (Australia), and prime-time soaps such as Dallas and Dynasty. Latin American telenovelas are a related form but usually run for a fixed term with a definite ending. Audiences for traditional daytime soaps have declined sharply since the 2000s, with many long-running series cancelled, though the format persists internationally and in streaming.
