Inbreeding depression

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fitness typically declines as inbreeding accumulates over generations.

Inbreeding depression is the reduced biological fitness in a given population as a result of inbreeding (the breeding of related individuals). Biological fitness refers to an organism's ability to survive and reproduce. Inbreeding depression is often the result of a population bottleneck: with a small breeding population, mating between relatives becomes more likely, and deleterious recessive alleles are more often expressed in homozygous form.

Causes

Two main genetic mechanisms are proposed. Under the partial dominance hypothesis, inbreeding exposes mildly deleterious recessive alleles that are normally masked in heterozygotes. Under the overdominance hypothesis, the loss of heterozygosity itself reduces fitness because heterozygous genotypes are superior at some loci. Evidence favours partial dominance as the major cause. Inbreeding also fixes alleles by genetic drift in small populations, further reducing variation.

Effects

Inbreeding depression has been documented in plants and animals, including humans, and affects traits such as fertility, survival, growth rate, disease resistance, and sperm quality. Charles Darwin documented reduced vigour in self-fertilised plants in his 1876 book The Effects of Cross and Self Fertilisation in the Vegetable Kingdom. In animals, Florida panthers, Isle Royale wolves, and cheetahs have shown inbreeding-associated defects, and conservation programmes counter it through outcrossing and genetic rescue. In humans, offspring of consanguineous marriages show modestly elevated rates of congenital disorders and early mortality.

Measurement

The severity of inbreeding depression is commonly expressed with the inbreeding coefficient F, and the number of "lethal equivalents" per genome. Fitness decline is often roughly exponential with F: W = W0e−BF, where B is the number of lethal equivalents.

Purging

Sustained inbreeding can expose and eliminate strongly deleterious recessive alleles through selection, a process called purging. Purging is more effective for highly deleterious alleles and in slowly inbreeding populations, but it is unreliable for mildly deleterious alleles and does not fully protect small populations.
