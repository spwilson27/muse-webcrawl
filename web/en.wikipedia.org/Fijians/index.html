Fijians

Fijians (Fijian: iTaukei, 'owners [of the land]') are a nation and ethnic group indigenous to Fiji, who speak Fijian and English and share a common history and culture. Indigenous Fijians make up a slight majority of Fiji's population of roughly 900,000, with most of the remainder being Indo-Fijians, descendants of indentured labourers brought from British India between 1879 and 1916.

History

The ancestors of the Fijians settled the islands around 1500–1000 BCE as part of the Austronesian expansion associated with the Lapita culture. Traditional society was organised into chiefdoms (vanua), led by hereditary chiefs (turaga). Fiji was ceded to Britain in 1874 and became independent in 1970. Constitutional crises, including coups in 1987, 2000, and 2006, were closely tied to relations between indigenous Fijians and the Indo-Fijian community.

Culture

Traditional Fijian culture is centred on the extended family (tokatoka), the clan (mataqali), and customary ceremonies involving yaqona (kava) and the lovo earth oven. Christianity, primarily Methodism, is the majority faith among indigenous Fijians. Rugby — especially rugby sevens, in which the Fiji national rugby sevens team has won two Olympic gold medals — is the national sport and a deep source of pride.

Language

Fijian is an Austronesian language with several dialects; the standard form is based on the dialect of Bau. Fiji Hindi, developed by Indo-Fijians, is the other major language, alongside English, which is used in government, business, and education.
