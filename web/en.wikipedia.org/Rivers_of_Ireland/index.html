Rivers of Ireland

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The island of Ireland — comprising the Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland — is drained by a dense network of rivers, most of them relatively short but fed by high rainfall. The longest is the River Shannon, which flows 360 kilometres from County Cavan through the midlands to the Atlantic, and drains about a fifth of the island.

Principal rivers

Irish rivers are traditionally grouped by the sea they reach. Flowing into the Atlantic are the Shannon, the Moy, the Corrib, the Erne, the Foyle, and the Blackwater of Munster. Draining to the Irish Sea are the Liffey, the Boyne, the Slaney, and the Lagan at Belfast. The Barrow, Nore, and Suir — the "Three Sisters" — meet in the estuary at Waterford, while the Lee reaches the sea at Cork Harbour.

Character and use

Because the central plain is low and poorly drained, Irish rivers are often slow, lake-dotted, and subject to flooding of their callows — seasonal flood meadows of high ecological value. Many rivers are famous for salmon and trout fishing. The Grand Canal and Royal Canal link Dublin to the Shannon, and the Shannon–Erne Waterway connects the two largest river systems, making Ireland's inland waterways a single navigable network.
