Harappa is an archaeological site in Punjab, Pakistan, about 24 km west of Sahiwal. The site takes its name from a modern village near the former course of the Ravi River. It was one of the great cities of the Indus Valley Civilisation and the first of its sites to be excavated in the 1920s, giving its name to the entire "Harappan" culture.

The city

The mature city (c. 2600–1900 BCE) covered more than 150 hectares and comprised a raised citadel mound to the west and a lower town with a grid of streets, working-class housing, granaries, furnaces, and circular threshing platforms. Harappa produced fine seals in steatite carved with animal motifs and signs of the still-undeciphered Indus script, cubical chert weights in standard ratios, and long-distance trade goods including carnelian beads exported to Mesopotamia. Extensive cemeteries (R-37 and Cemetery H) document burial customs and population health.

Discovery and damage

The ruins were described by the deserter-explorer Charles Masson in 1842 and visited by Alexander Cunningham in the 1850s–70s, but enormous quantities of Harappan brick were carted away as ballast for the Lahore–Multan railway in the 1850s, destroying much of the upper city. Systematic excavation began under the Archaeological Survey of India in 1921–22 under Daya Ram Sahni; its results, announced in 1924 alongside Mohenjo-daro's, revealed a previously unknown Bronze Age civilisation. Modern work by the Harappa Archaeological Research Project (since 1986) has traced the site's growth from an early farming settlement (from c. 3300 BCE) into a city, and its decline as trade networks and river systems shifted after 1900 BCE.
