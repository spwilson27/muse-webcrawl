In probability theory and statistics, the Poisson distribution is a discrete probability distribution that expresses the probability of a given number of events occurring in a fixed interval of time or space, if these events occur with a known constant mean rate and independently of the time since the last event. It is named after the French mathematician Siméon Denis Poisson, who published it in 1838 in his work Recherches sur la probabilité des jugements.

Definition

A discrete random variable X is said to have a Poisson distribution with parameter λ > 0 if the probability of observing exactly k events is

P(X = k) = λk e−λ / k!

Here e is Euler's number and k! is the factorial of k. The parameter λ equals both the expected value and the variance of the distribution — a property known as equidispersion.

Applications

The Poisson distribution models counts of independent events occurring at a steady average rate: the number of phone calls arriving at a call centre per hour, radioactive decays per second, misprints per page, or goals in a football match. A classic early application was Ladislaus Bortkiewicz's 1898 study of the number of Prussian cavalry soldiers killed by horse kicks each year. The distribution arises as a limiting case of the binomial distribution when the number of trials grows large and the probability of success per trial is small, with the product held constant.

Poisson processes

The distribution is the counting distribution of the Poisson point process, in which events occur continuously and independently at a constant average rate. Related distributions include the exponential distribution for the waiting time between events and the gamma distribution for the waiting time until a given number of events have occurred.
