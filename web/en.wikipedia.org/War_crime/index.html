War crime

War crime

A war crime is a violation of the laws and customs of war (international humanitarian law) that gives rise to individual criminal responsibility under international law. It consists of acts such as murder, ill-treatment or deportation of civilians in occupied territory; murder or ill-treatment of prisoners of war; killing of hostages; torture; plunder; wanton destruction of cities and villages not justified by military necessity; and the use of prohibited weapons.

Legal foundations

The modern law of war crimes developed from the Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907, which regulated the conduct of hostilities, and the four Geneva Conventions of 1949 and their Additional Protocols of 1977, which protect wounded and sick combatants, prisoners of war and civilians. "Grave breaches" of the Geneva Conventions must be prosecuted by every state party under the principle of universal jurisdiction. Article 8 of the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court (1998) provides the most comprehensive modern codification, covering crimes in both international and non-international armed conflict.

Examples

Prohibited conduct includes intentionally directing attacks against civilians or civilian objects; torture and inhuman treatment; wilfully killing protected persons; taking hostages; using human shields; employing poison, poisoned weapons or chemical and biological weapons; conscripting children under 15; sexual violence including rape and sexual slavery; and attacking buildings dedicated to religion, education, art, science or historic monuments, or hospitals displaying the protective emblems.

Prosecution

The first modern prosecutions followed the Second World War: the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg (1945–46) and the Tokyo Tribunal tried Axis leaders, establishing that individuals — not only states — bear responsibility, and that superior orders are no defence. Ad hoc tribunals for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) and Rwanda (ICTR) in the 1990s revived international criminal justice and led to the permanent International Criminal Court at The Hague, which began work in 2002. War crimes are distinct from crimes against humanity (widespread or systematic attacks on civilians, in war or peace) and genocide (acts intended to destroy a protected group in whole or in part).
