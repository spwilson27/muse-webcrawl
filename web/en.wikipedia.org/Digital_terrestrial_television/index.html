Digital terrestrial television

Digital terrestrial television (DTT) is a technology for terrestrial television in which television stations broadcast television content in a digital format. It is a major technological advance over analog television, and has largely replaced it worldwide. Digital signals use spectrum far more efficiently, allowing more channels — and additional services such as electronic programme guides and interactive features — to fit in the same bandwidth.

Standards

Several incompatible standards were developed. DVB-T and its successor DVB-T2 are used across Europe, much of Asia, Africa, and Australia. ATSC is used in North America and South Korea. ISDB-T originated in Japan and spread to much of Latin America, while DTMB is used in China. Second-generation standards such as DVB-T2 and ATSC 3.0 offer higher capacity and support for ultra-high-definition broadcasts.

Transition

The switchover from analog required viewers to own a digital television or a set-top box converter. Luxembourg and the Netherlands completed the transition in 2006, among the first countries; the United States ended full-power analog broadcasts in 2009, and the United Kingdom in 2012. Freed spectrum, known as the "digital dividend", was reallocated in many countries to mobile broadband.

Advantages and disadvantages

Digital reception provides a sharper picture and better sound, but suffers from the "cliff effect": near the edge of coverage the picture breaks up or disappears entirely, whereas analog signals degrade gradually into static.
