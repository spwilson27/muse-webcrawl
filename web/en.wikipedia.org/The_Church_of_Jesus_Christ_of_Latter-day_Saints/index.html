The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, informally known as the LDS Church or Mormon Church, is a restorationist Christian church and the largest denomination of the Latter Day Saint movement founded by Joseph Smith in upstate New York in 1830. It is headquartered in Salt Lake City, Utah, and reports more than 17 million members worldwide.

History

Smith taught that he had been visited by God the Father and Jesus Christ (the First Vision) and was later led to golden plates, whose translated text was published in 1830 as the Book of Mormon. The young church gathered converts in Ohio, Missouri and Illinois, facing violent persecution; Smith was killed by a mob at Carthage Jail in 1844. Most Saints followed Brigham Young west, making the epic trek to the Salt Lake Valley in 1847 and building a theocratic commonwealth in the Great Basin.

Doctrine and practice

The church teaches that it is the restoration of Christ's original church through living prophets. Distinctive doctrines include continuing revelation, temples and ordinances such as baptism for the dead and eternal marriage, a three-degree heaven, the Word of Wisdom health code (abstinence from alcohol, tobacco, coffee and tea), tithing, and a global missionary programme staffed largely by young volunteers. Polygamy, practised in the nineteenth century, was renounced in the 1890 Manifesto, a step toward Utah statehood in 1896.

Organisation and reach

Leadership is by a lay, hierarchical priesthood headed by the First Presidency and the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles. The church operates thousands of congregations, more than 300 temples announced or built, universities (including Brigham Young University), a large welfare and humanitarian system, and one of the world's largest genealogical archives through FamilySearch.
