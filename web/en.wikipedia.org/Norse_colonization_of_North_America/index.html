Norse colonization of North America

The Norse colonization of North America began in the late 10th century, when Norse (Viking) explorers from Iceland and Greenland reached the northeastern coast of North America, roughly five centuries before the voyages of Christopher Columbus. The only confirmed Norse site in North America outside Greenland is L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, Canada.

The sagas

The principal written sources are two Icelandic sagas, the Saga of the Greenlanders and the Saga of Erik the Red, composed centuries after the events. They describe voyages by Bjarni Herjólfsson, who sighted land west of Greenland after being blown off course, and by Leif Erikson, who around the year 1000 explored lands the Norse named Helluland, Markland and Vinland. Subsequent expeditions, including one led by Thorfinn Karlsefni, attempted settlement but were abandoned, partly because of conflict with Indigenous peoples, whom the Norse called skrælingjar.

Archaeological evidence

In 1960 the Norwegian explorer Helge Ingstad and archaeologist Anne Stine Ingstad excavated a Norse settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows, at the northern tip of Newfoundland. The site contained turf-walled halls, a forge with evidence of iron smelting from bog iron, and Norse artefacts including a bronze pin and a spindle whorl. Tree-ring analysis published in 2021 dated Norse wood-cutting at the site precisely to the year 1021 CE, the earliest confirmed European presence in the Americas.

Greenland settlements

The Norse also maintained settlements in Greenland — the Eastern and Western Settlements, founded from about 985 — for roughly 450 years, trading in walrus ivory, furs and falcons. The Greenland colonies disappeared by the mid-15th century, likely through a combination of climate cooling during the Little Ice Age, soil erosion, shifting trade and conflict.
