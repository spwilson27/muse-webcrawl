Bronze Age China

The Bronze Age in China spans roughly the period from about 2000 BCE to 771 BCE, encompassing the Erlitou culture and the Shang and Western Zhou dynasties. Unlike in western Eurasia, where bronze was used first for tools and weapons, Chinese bronze metallurgy developed above all around the casting of elaborate ritual vessels used in ancestor worship, which became central symbols of political and religious authority.

Erlitou and the Xia tradition

The Erlitou culture (c. 1900–1500 BCE) in the Yellow River valley produced the earliest large Chinese bronze vessels and palatial foundations, and is frequently associated — controversially — with the semi-legendary Xia dynasty of traditional histories. Chinese smiths pioneered piece-mould casting, in which a vessel was cast in a sectional ceramic mould assembled around a model and core, a technique distinct from the lost-wax method and capable of producing intricate relief decoration.

Shang dynasty

Under the Shang dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE), bronze working reached a monumental scale. Great hoards of ritual vessels — ding tripods, jue wine cups, and bells — were cast for ancestral sacrifices, often bearing the earliest surviving Chinese inscriptions. The largest known vessel, the Houmuwu ding (833 kg), dates to this period. Shang kings also cast weapons such as dagger-axes (ge) and developed the war chariot. Oracle bone inscriptions record divinations conducted for the royal house, providing the first substantial corpus of Chinese writing.

Western Zhou

The Zhou conquest (c. 1046 BCE) was justified by the doctrine of the Mandate of Heaven. Western Zhou bronzes increasingly carried long inscriptions commemorating appointments, land grants, and battles, making them key historical sources. Ritual reforms standardised sets of vessels by rank, and bronze bells (bianzhong) were cast in elaborate musical sets. The period ends in 771 BCE with the fall of the western capital; the subsequent Spring and Autumn period saw the transition toward iron.

Regional cultures

Bronze Age China was not a single tradition: the Sanxingdui culture of Sichuan produced astonishing bronze heads, masks, and a bronze tree, stylistically quite distinct from the Central Plains, while the cultures of the lower Yangtze and the steppe frontier developed their own metalworking styles.
