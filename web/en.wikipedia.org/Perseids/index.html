Perseids

Parent body: Comet Swift–Tuttle
Radiant: Constellation Perseus
Peak activity: 12–13 August
Zenithal hourly rate: typically 60–110

The Perseids are a prolific meteor shower associated with the comet Swift–Tuttle, which orbits the Sun once every 133 years and last reached perihelion in 1992. The meteors are called Perseids because their radiant — the point in the sky from which they appear to radiate — lies in the constellation Perseus. The shower is active from mid-July to late August and usually peaks on the night of 12–13 August.

Origin

As Swift–Tuttle passes through the inner Solar System it sheds dust and debris along its orbit. Each summer the Earth passes through this stream; particles typically the size of grains of sand enter the atmosphere at about 59 km/s (133,000 mph) and burn up at heights of 80–100 km, producing the bright streaks popularly called "shooting stars". Because the dust stream has been renewed by successive returns of the comet, outbursts with much higher rates sometimes occur.

Observation

The Perseids are among the most popular showers of the year in the northern hemisphere because they occur during warm summer nights. Under dark skies observers may see 60 or more meteors per hour at the peak, which is best watched in the pre-dawn hours when the radiant is high. The shower has been observed for some two thousand years, with the earliest recorded observations from China, and in European folklore the meteors are sometimes called the "tears of Saint Lawrence", whose feast day falls on 10 August.
