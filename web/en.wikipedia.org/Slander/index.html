Slander

This article discusses the legal concept. Slander is the spoken form of defamation; the written form is libel.
[Image: A courtroom scene illustrating a defamation trial]

Area of lawTort law (defamation)
DefinitionFalse spoken statement damaging a person's reputation

Slander is the communication of a false statement, in spoken or other transitory form, that harms the reputation of another person. Together with libel, which covers statements in writing or other permanent form, it forms the tort of defamation in common law jurisdictions. Historically, slander was treated as less serious than libel, and in many legal systems a claimant in a slander case had to prove actual (special) damage, whereas damage was presumed in libel.

Elements

A slander claim generally requires: (1) a false statement presented as fact; (2) publication — communication to at least one third party; (3) fault, ranging from negligence to actual malice depending on the subject's status; and (4) damage to reputation. Statements of pure opinion based on disclosed facts are protected in many jurisdictions.

Slander per se

Certain categories of spoken statement have traditionally been treated as so damaging that harm is presumed without proof of special damage ("slander per se"). These classically include imputing a serious crime, a loathsome disease, unchastity or adultery, and uncleanliness or unfitness in one's trade or profession.

Defences and public figures

Truth is an absolute defence. In the United States, following New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964), public officials and public figures must prove "actual malice" — that the defendant knew the statement was false or acted with reckless disregard for the truth. Statements made in legislative or judicial proceedings are typically protected by absolute privilege, and fair reports of public proceedings by qualified privilege.

Modern developments

The distinction between slander and libel has blurred with broadcasting and the internet: recorded speech may be treated as libel in some jurisdictions, and England and Wales reformed defamation law with the Defamation Act 2013, introducing a threshold of "serious harm".
