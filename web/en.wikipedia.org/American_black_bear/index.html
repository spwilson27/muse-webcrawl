The American black bear (Ursus americanus) is a species of medium-sized bear endemic to North America. It is the continent's smallest and most widely distributed bear species. The American black bear is an omnivore, with a diet varying greatly depending on season and location. It typically lives in largely forested areas but will leave forests in search of food; it is sometimes attracted to human communities due to the immediate availability of food.

Description

Despite the common name, black bears show considerable colour variation: individuals may be black, brown, cinnamon, blond, or even white, as in the Kermode bear of coastal British Columbia. Adults have small eyes, rounded ears, a long brown snout, and short, non-retractable claws well suited to climbing, which black bears do expertly at all ages.

Behaviour

Black bears are generally solitary except for mothers with cubs and concentrations at rich food sources. In cold climates they hibernate through the winter in dens, entering a state of torpor during which they neither eat, drink, urinate, nor defecate. Females give birth to typically two or three cubs during winter hibernation, and cubs remain with the mother for about 17 months. Black bears possess remarkable colour vision, memory, and navigational ability, and studies indicate capacities for counting and tool use.

Relationship with humans

Populations are estimated at roughly twice those of all other bear species combined; the IUCN lists the species as Least Concern. Attacks on humans are rare and usually defensive or predatory in isolated incidents, though bears habituated to human food cause substantial property damage. The black bear features prominently in the folklore of Indigenous peoples of North America and is the state mammal or animal of several U.S. states.
