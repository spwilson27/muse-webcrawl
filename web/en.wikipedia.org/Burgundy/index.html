Burgundy

Burgundy (French: Bourgogne)
Historical region of France
Capital (historical): Dijon
Now part of: Bourgogne-Franche-Comté

Burgundy (French: Bourgogne) is a historical territory and former administrative region of east-central France. It takes its name from the Burgundians, an East Germanic people who settled there in late antiquity. Since 2016 it has formed part of the region of Bourgogne-Franche-Comté.

History

Burgundy was successively a kingdom of the Burgundians, a Frankish duchy, and — under the Valois dukes of the 14th and 15th centuries — one of the most powerful states in Europe, at times a rival to the French crown itself. After the death of Charles the Bold at Nancy in 1477, the duchy was absorbed by France, while the Burgundian Netherlands passed to the Habsburgs.

Wine

Burgundy is world-famous for its wines, produced mainly from Pinot noir and Chardonnay grapes along the Côte d'Or. The region's vineyard classification emphasizes terroir, with grand cru and premier cru designations, and its climats were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2015.

Geography

The region covers the departments of Côte-d'Or, Nièvre, Saône-et-Loire and Yonne. Its capital is Dijon, known for its mustard and its well-preserved medieval and Renaissance centre.
