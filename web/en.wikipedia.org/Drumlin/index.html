Drumlin

A drumlin, from the Irish word droimnín ("smallest ridge"), is a glacial landform: an elongated, streamlined hill composed largely of glacial till, formed by the movement of glacial ice sheets. Drumlins typically range from a few hundred metres to several kilometres long and up to about 50 metres high, with a blunt, steeper end facing the direction from which the ice advanced and a gentler tapered tail pointing down-ice.

Formation

Drumlins form beneath flowing ice. Theories include the accretion of sediment around an obstruction, the moulding of pre-existing deposits by ice flow, and catastrophic subglacial meltwater floods. Their long axes record the direction of ice movement, making drumlin fields valuable evidence for reconstructing past ice sheets.

Distribution

Drumlins occur in "fields" or "swarms" of tens to thousands in formerly glaciated regions: Ireland (giving them their name; Clew Bay is said to be full of drowned drumlins forming islands), central New York State, the Puget Lowland of Washington, Wisconsin, Finland, Estonia, and Patagonia. Boston's harbour islands are drumlins partly submerged by postglacial sea-level rise, and Bunker Hill is a drumlin.
