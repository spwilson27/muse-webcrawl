Parliamentary privilege

Parliamentary privilege is a legal immunity enjoyed by members of certain legislatures, allowing them to act freely in carrying out their duties without fear of civil or criminal liability. It is a protection of the legislature as an institution, rather than a personal perk: its purpose is to safeguard free debate and to prevent the executive or the courts from interfering with the work of parliament. The best-known element is freedom of speech in debate — members cannot be sued or prosecuted for anything said on the floor of the house.

Free speech in debate is the core of parliamentary privilege.

Origins

The privilege has its roots in England, where the House of Commons asserted its right to debate without royal interference. Article 9 of the Bill of Rights 1689 declares "that the freedom of speech and debates or proceedings in Parliament ought not to be impeached or questioned in any court or place out of Parliament", a provision still in force in the United Kingdom and mirrored in many Commonwealth constitutions. The Constitution of the United States contains a similar protection in the Speech or Debate Clause.

Scope

Beyond freedom of speech, parliamentary privilege typically includes:

  • Freedom from arrest in civil cases while attending parliament (a protection now largely obsolete).
  • The exclusive right of each house to regulate its own internal proceedings and discipline its members.
  • Protection for witnesses giving evidence to parliamentary committees, and for official reports and papers published by order of the house.

Privilege does not cover everything a member does: statements repeated outside the chamber, ordinary crimes, and administrative acts outside proceedings may fall outside its protection, and legislatures have punished members who abuse privilege.

Controversies

Privilege is periodically controversial when members use the floor to make allegations against individuals who have no right of reply inside the house, or when it appears to shield wrongdoing — the United Kingdom's parliamentary expenses scandal and cases such as the naming of individuals subject to court injunctions have prompted repeated reviews of its scope. Reform proposals generally seek to preserve the core protection of debate while clarifying its limits.
