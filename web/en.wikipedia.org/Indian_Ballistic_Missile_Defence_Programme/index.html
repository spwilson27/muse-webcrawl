The Indian Ballistic Missile Defence Programme is an initiative by India to develop and deploy a multi-layered ballistic missile defence system to protect the country from ballistic missile attacks. It was launched by the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) in the late 1990s, following growing missile threats in the region. The programme is designed as a two-tiered shield, with interceptor missiles for both exo-atmospheric and endo-atmospheric interceptions.

Background

India began developing ballistic missile defences after the Kargil War and amid concerns about missile arsenals in neighbouring Pakistan and China. The system is intended to intercept incoming missiles at ranges of up to about 5,000 km in its later phases. Development drew partly on technologies acquired from abroad, including the Israeli Green Pine radar used in the Swordfish long-range tracking radar.

Phase I

The first phase uses two interceptors: the Prithvi Air Defence (PAD) missile, later replaced by the Prithvi Defence Vehicle (PDV), for exo-atmospheric interception at altitudes of 50–180 km, and the Advanced Air Defence (AAD) missile for endo-atmospheric interception at altitudes of 15–30 km. The first successful test took place on 27 November 2006, when a PAD missile intercepted a modified Prithvi missile at an altitude of about 48 km.

Phase II

The second phase is aimed at intercepting longer-range missiles of the 3,500–5,000 km class. It includes the AD-1 and AD-2 interceptor missiles, intended to engage targets in both endo-atmospheric and exo-atmospheric regimes. The AD-1 was first flight-tested in November 2022.

Naval component

A ship-based variant, sometimes referred to as Phase-II naval BMD, was demonstrated in April 2023 when India successfully tested a sea-based endo-atmospheric interceptor from INS Anvesha, placing India among a small group of nations with naval ballistic missile defence capability.
