The San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge, known locally as the Bay Bridge, is a complex of bridges spanning San Francisco Bay in California, connecting the cities of San Francisco and Oakland as part of Interstate 80. It opened in 1936, six months before its more famous neighbour, the Golden Gate Bridge.

Design and construction

The bridge consists of two major crossings joined by a tunnel through Yerba Buena Island. The western crossing to San Francisco is a pair of suspension bridges; the eastern crossing to Oakland was originally a cantilever truss bridge. Construction, led by chief engineer Charles H. Purcell, employed thousands during the Great Depression.

History

The bridge originally carried automobiles on its upper deck and trains and trucks below; the Key System trains stopped running in 1958 and the bridge was reconfigured for five lanes of one-way traffic on each deck. A section of the eastern span's upper deck collapsed during the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake, closing the bridge for a month.

Eastern span replacement

Because the old eastern span was seismically unsafe, it was replaced by a new self-anchored suspension bridge — the largest of its kind — which opened on September 2, 2013, alongside the old structure, which was then demolished. The replacement project cost roughly $6.5 billion and is among the most expensive public works projects in California history.
