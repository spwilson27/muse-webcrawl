Ci poetry

Ci (Chinese: 詞; pinyin: cí) is a type of lyric poetry in the classical Chinese tradition, originating in the Tang dynasty and reaching its height in the Song dynasty. Ci poems are written to fixed tunes — known patterns called cipai (詞牌) — which determine the number of lines, the number of characters in each line, the rhyme scheme and the tonal pattern.

Form

Unlike shi poetry, whose lines are of regular length, ci lines vary in length according to the tune pattern, which is why ci is sometimes called the "poetry of irregular lines" (changduanju). Each tune pattern has a name, often borrowed from an older song, a place, or the opening words of an early poem written to it. A poem composed to a pattern is identified by the pattern name, followed by a title or subtitle of the poet's choosing.

History

The ci form grew out of songs performed by entertainers in the pleasure quarters of Tang-dynasty cities, with lyrics set to popular music, some of it imported from Central Asia. The earliest surviving collection is the Huajian ji (Among the Flowers), compiled in 940. In the Song dynasty the form was taken up by scholar-officials and became one of the major genres of Chinese literature. Celebrated ci poets include Liu Yong, Su Shi, who broadened the form's subject matter beyond love and longing, Zhou Bangyan, Li Qingzhao, generally regarded as the greatest female poet in Chinese history, and Xin Qiji.
