Switched reluctance motor

Image placeholder: rotor and stator of a switched reluctance motor.

The switched reluctance motor (SRM) is a type of electric motor that runs by reluctance torque. Unlike brushed DC motors, power is delivered to the windings of the stator rather than the rotor. The rotor has no magnets or windings — it is made of soft magnetic material with salient (projecting) poles — which makes the motor mechanically simple and robust.

Principle of operation

When a stator phase is energised, the rotor turns so as to minimise the reluctance of the magnetic circuit, aligning its poles with the energised stator pole. By switching current between stator phases in sequence, timed to the rotor position measured by sensors or sensorless estimation, continuous rotation is produced. Torque is independent of the direction of current, simplifying the power electronics.

Advantages and drawbacks

SRMs are cheap to build, fault-tolerant, and capable of very high speeds and operation in harsh environments, suiting them to appliances, industrial drives and some electric-vehicle applications. Their main drawbacks are torque ripple, acoustic noise, and the need for sophisticated electronic control — a problem only solved by modern power electronics.
