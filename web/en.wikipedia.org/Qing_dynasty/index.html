The Qing dynasty (Chinese: 清朝; pinyin: Qīng cháo), officially the Great Qing, was the last imperial dynasty of China. It was established in 1636 by the Manchu Aisin Gioro clan in Manchuria, ruled China proper from 1644 to 1912, and was succeeded by the Republic of China. The multi-ethnic Qing empire surpassed the land area and population of the preceding Ming dynasty, encompassing China, Mongolia, Tibet, Xinjiang, and Taiwan at its peak.

Under the Kangxi, Yongzheng, and Qianlong emperors — the so-called High Qing era — the empire reached the zenith of its territorial and cultural influence, with China's population growing to roughly 400 million by 1900. The dynasty's final century was marked by internal rebellions and foreign encroachment, ending with the Xinhai Revolution.

History

Founding and conquest

Nurhaci unified the Jurchen tribes and organized the Eight Banners; his son Hong Taiji renamed the people Manchu and proclaimed the Qing in 1636. In 1644, taking advantage of the fall of Beijing to rebel leader Li Zicheng, Qing forces under regent Dorgon passed through Shanhai Pass with the defection of Ming general Wu Sangui, seized the capital, and completed the conquest of the Ming over the following decades. Han men were ordered to adopt the Manchu queue hairstyle as a sign of submission.

High Qing

The Kangxi Emperor (r. 1661–1722) consolidated rule, defeating the Revolt of the Three Feudatories and annexing Taiwan. The Qianlong Emperor (r. 1735–1796) oversaw the Ten Great Campaigns that destroyed the Dzungar Khanate and fixed the empire's borders, while presiding over immense literary projects such as the Siku Quanshu.

Decline

The 19th century brought the Opium Wars with Britain (1839–42, 1856–60), which forced open treaty ports and ceded Hong Kong; the catastrophic Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864), which killed tens of millions; defeat by Japan in 1895; and the Boxer Rebellion of 1900. Reform efforts — the Self-Strengthening Movement and the Hundred Days' Reform — failed to save the dynasty.

Fall

The Xinhai Revolution broke out in October 1911. On 12 February 1912, the last emperor, the six-year-old Puyi, abdicated, ending over two thousand years of imperial rule in China.

Government and society

The Qing preserved much of the Ming administrative system, with Manchu–Han "dyarchy" staffing, Grand Council decision-making from the Yongzheng era, and banners garrisoned across the empire. Its frontiers were administered flexibly through the Lifan Yuan, incorporating Mongol, Tibetan, and Muslim elites into a plural imperial order.
