Nag Hammadi library

Nag Hammadi library
Discovered: December 1945, near Nag Hammadi, Egypt
Contents: 13 leather-bound papyrus codices
Language: Coptic
Date of texts: 2nd–4th centuries CE
Notable text: Gospel of Thomas

The Nag Hammadi library is a collection of early Christian and Gnostic texts discovered in a sealed jar near the Egyptian town of Nag Hammadi in December 1945. The find, made by local farmer Muhammad Ali al-Samman, comprised thirteen leather-bound papyrus codices containing over fifty tractates, mostly Coptic translations of Greek works composed between the 2nd and 4th centuries.

Contents

The codices include the only complete copy of the Gospel of Thomas, a sayings gospel; the Gospel of Philip; the Apocryphon of John; the Gospel of Truth; and the Apocalypse of Paul, alongside Hermetic writings and even a partial Coptic translation of Plato's Republic. Most works are Gnostic — presenting salvation as secret knowledge (gnosis) revealed by a savior — and many were previously known only through hostile quotations by early church heresiologists such as Irenaeus.

Significance

Before 1945, knowledge of Gnosticism came largely from its opponents. The Nag Hammadi texts revolutionized the study of early Christianity, revealing its diversity and fueling scholarly and popular interest in "lost" gospels. The codices are now housed in the Coptic Museum in Cairo; a complete English edition, The Nag Hammadi Library in English, appeared in 1977 under the editorship of James M. Robinson. Scholars often connect the burial of the codices to the late-4th-century suppression of non-canonical books following Athanasius's Festal Letter of 367. See also Gnosticism and Gospel of Thomas.
