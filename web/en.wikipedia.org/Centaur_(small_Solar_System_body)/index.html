Centaur (small Solar System body)

In planetary astronomy, a centaur is a small Solar System body that orbits the Sun between Jupiter and Neptune and crosses or has crossed the orbit of one or more of the giant planets. Centaurs generally have unstable orbits because they have crossed or will cross the orbits of one or more of the giant planets; almost all their orbits have dynamic lifetimes of only a few million years.

Centaurs typically exhibit the characteristics of both asteroids and comets. They are named after the mythical centaurs, who were a hybrid of man and horse. It has been estimated that there are around 44,000 centaurs in the Solar System with diameters larger than 1 km.

History of study

The first centaur discovered was 944 Hidalgo in 1920, though the class was only recognised after the discovery of 2060 Chiron in 1977 by Charles Kowal. The largest confirmed centaur is 10199 Chariklo, about 250 km in diameter, found in 2013–2014 to possess a system of two narrow rings — the first rings discovered around a small body. 60558 Echeclus and Chiron both display comet-like comae and hold dual designations as comets (174P and 95P).

Orbits and fate

Centaurs occupy orbital space between about 5.2 and 30 AU with perihelia beyond Jupiter. Gravitational encounters with the giant planets make their orbits chaotic; simulations suggest about half will eventually be ejected, while some migrate inward to become Jupiter-family comets, and a few may collide with planets or the Sun.

Physical properties

Surfaces range from neutral grey to very red, reflecting differing histories of irradiation and resurfacing. Spectroscopic studies show water ice on several objects, and Chariklo's rings may consist of water-ice particles.
