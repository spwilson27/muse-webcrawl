Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938

The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 (FLSA) is a United States labor law that creates the right to a minimum wage, and "time-and-a-half" overtime pay when people work over forty hours a week. It also prohibits employment of minors in "oppressive child labor". It applies to employees engaged in interstate commerce or employed by an enterprise engaged in commerce or in the production of goods for commerce, unless the employer can claim an exemption from coverage. The law is administered by the Wage and Hour Division of the Department of Labor.

The FLSA was a cornerstone of Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal.

History

Signed on June 25, 1938, by President Franklin D. Roosevelt, the Act initially set a minimum wage of 25 cents per hour, a maximum workweek of 44 hours (falling to 40), and banned child labor in certain industries. It was the product of long campaigns against "sweated labor" and of the Black-Connery bill debates; its constitutionality followed the Supreme Court's post-1937 acceptance of New Deal economic regulation, confirmed in United States v. Darby Lumber Co. (1941).

Provisions and amendments

The Equal Pay Act of 1963 amended the FLSA to prohibit sex-based wage discrimination. Coverage was progressively extended to retail, service, agricultural, and public-sector workers. The federal minimum wage was last raised to $7.25 per hour in 2009; many states and cities set higher rates. Major recurring controversies concern the classification of employees versus independent contractors, exemptions for executive, administrative and professional employees, and the treatment of tipped workers, whose federal cash wage floor remains much lower, offset by a tip credit.
