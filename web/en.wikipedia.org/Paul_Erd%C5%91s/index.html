Paul Erdős (26 March 1913 – 20 September 1996) was a Hungarian mathematician. He was one of the most prolific mathematicians and producers of mathematical conjectures of the 20th century, publishing more than 1,500 articles with some 500 collaborators. He pursued a famously itinerant life, travelling between universities and colleagues' homes with his possessions in a suitcase, working on problems in combinatorics, graph theory, number theory, and probability.

Work

Erdős co-developed the probabilistic method in combinatorics, proved the prime number theorem by elementary means with Atle Selberg, and founded the field of extremal graph theory with results such as the Erdős–Stone theorem. With Alfréd Rényi he introduced random graphs (the Erdős–Rényi model). He was famed for offering cash prizes for problems he considered difficult, and the whimsical "Erdős number" measures collaborative distance from him in the co-authorship graph.

Life and personality

A child prodigy who could multiply three-digit numbers at age three, Erdős never married, held no permanent post for most of his career, and spoke in a private vocabulary — children were "epsilons", and to die was to "leave", to stop doing mathematics was to "die". He died of a heart attack after a mathematics conference in Warsaw. Paul Hoffman's biography The Man Who Loved Only Numbers (1998) popularized his life.
