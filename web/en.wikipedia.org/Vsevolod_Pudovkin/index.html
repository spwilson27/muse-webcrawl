Vsevolod Illarionovich Pudovkin (1893–1953) was a Soviet film director, screenwriter, and theorist who, alongside Sergei Eisenstein, pioneered the theory and practice of montage. His silent films Mother, The End of St. Petersburg, and Storm over Asia are classics of early cinema.

Early life

Pudovkin studied physics and chemistry at Moscow University and served in the First World War, spending three years as a German prisoner of war. After the Revolution he joined Lev Kuleshov's film workshop at the State Film School, acting in and helping to make early Soviet experiments, including the science film Mechanics of the Brain (1926).

Montage theory

Where Eisenstein favoured the collision of shots, Pudovkin theorised editing as linkage — building a scene brick by brick to guide the spectator's emotion. His books Film Technique and Film Acting (from lectures of the late 1920s) became standard texts worldwide, emphasising the "plastic material" of the shot, the actor's craft, and the director's control of the viewer's attention.

Career

Mother (1926), adapted from Gorky's novel, established his international reputation with its lyrical, psychologically focused montage. After the coming of sound and the tightening of socialist realism, his later films, such as the wartime Suvorov (1941) and Admiral Nakhimov (1946), were more conventional historical epics. He remained a leading figure of Soviet cinema until his death in 1953.
