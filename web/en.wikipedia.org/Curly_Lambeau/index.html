Curly Lambeau

Earl Louis "Curly" Lambeau (April 9, 1898 – June 1, 1965) was an American professional football player and coach in the National Football League (NFL). He is best known as the co-founder, player, and first head coach of the Green Bay Packers, leading the team from its founding in 1919 through 1949 and winning six NFL championships.

Early life and founding of the Packers

Lambeau was born and raised in Green Bay, Wisconsin, and starred as a fullback at Green Bay East High School. He played one season at Notre Dame under Knute Rockne in 1918 before tonsillitis ended his college career. Returning home, he and sportswriter George Whitney Calhoun founded the Packers in 1919, securing sponsorship from the Indian Packing Company. The team joined the new American Professional Football Association (later the NFL) in 1921.

Playing and coaching career

As a player from 1921 to 1929, Lambeau was a running back and one of the league's earliest prolific passers, captaining the Packers to their first NFL championship in 1929 with an undefeated season. As head coach (1921–1949), he won six league titles (1929, 1930, 1931, 1936, 1939, 1944) — three of them consecutively — and compiled a record of 209 wins, 104 losses, and 21 ties with Green Bay. He pioneered aspects of the modern passing game, including pass patterns and the use of pass-oriented offenses in an era dominated by running.

After a bitter departure from Green Bay following the 1949 season, Lambeau coached the Chicago Cardinals (1950–1951) and the Washington Redskins (1952–1953). He retired with 229 career coaching wins, a record at the time.

Legacy

Lambeau was a charter member of the Pro Football Hall of Fame in 1963. The Packers' stadium, opened in 1957 as City Stadium, was renamed Lambeau Field in his honor in 1965, shortly after his death. A statue of Lambeau stands outside the stadium's atrium.
