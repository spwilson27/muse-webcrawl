The Grand Council (Manchu: coohai nashūn i ba; Chinese: 軍機處, Junjichu, literally "Office of Military Secrets") was an important policy-making body in Qing dynasty China. Established under the Yongzheng Emperor in the early 1730s, initially to handle the military campaign against the Dzungars in the northwest, it became the de facto highest organ of the imperial government for the rest of the dynasty.

Function

The Grand Council consisted of a small number of grand councillors — initially three to five, Manchu and Han officials holding concurrent posts — assisted by a staff of secretaries (zhangjing). It met daily with the emperor, received memorials, drafted edicts in the emperor's name, and processed urgent business through the palace memorial system and "court letters" (tingji), which moved far faster than the routine bureaucracy of the Six Ministries.

Significance

Critics, notably the historian of Qing institutions, have stressed that the Council never had formal statutory authority: it was an instrument of the emperor's personal rule, enabling the Yongzheng and Qianlong emperors to bypass the Grand Secretariat and the deliberative processes inherited from the Ming. Its unassuming headquarters — a small building just outside the Gate of Heavenly Purity in the Forbidden City — belied its power. The Council was abolished in 1911 when the Qing court created a modern cabinet.
