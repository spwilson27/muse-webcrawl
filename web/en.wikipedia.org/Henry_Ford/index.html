Henry Ford (July 30, 1863 – April 7, 1947) was an American industrialist and business magnate, founder of the Ford Motor Company, and chief developer of the assembly line technique of mass production. By creating the first automobile that middle-class Americans could afford, the Model T (1908), he converted the automobile from an expensive luxury into an accessible conveyance that profoundly reshaped 20th-century society.

Early life

Ford was born on a farm in Springwells Township (now part of Dearborn), Michigan, to Irish immigrant parents. Fascinated by machinery from boyhood, he left home at 16 to work as an apprentice machinist in Detroit. He became an engineer with the Edison Illuminating Company in 1891 and, in his spare time, built experimental gasoline engines — completing his first self-propelled vehicle, the Quadricycle, in 1896.

Ford Motor Company and the Model T

After two failed ventures, Ford founded the Ford Motor Company in 1903 with backing from investors including the Dodge brothers. The Model T, introduced in 1908, was simple, durable, and cheap. In 1913 Ford's Highland Park plant introduced the moving assembly line, cutting chassis assembly time from 12½ hours to about 1 hour 33 minutes. The price of the Model T fell from $850 in 1908 to under $300 by the mid-1920s; more than 15 million were sold before production ended in 1927.

Labour and the five-dollar day

In 1914 Ford announced a $5 daily wage — roughly double the prevailing rate — and an eight-hour day. The policy reduced turnover, attracted skilled workers, and enabled his own employees to buy the cars they built. Ford's later relations with organised labour were hostile: he resisted unionisation until 1941, when the company finally recognised the United Auto Workers after years of conflict, including the Battle of the Overpass in 1937.

Later life and controversies

Ford's newspaper, The Dearborn Independent, published antisemitic articles in the early 1920s, collected as "The International Jew", for which he later publicly apologised in 1927. A pacifist who opposed American entry into both world wars, he nevertheless converted his factories to war production in the 1940s, building bombers at Willow Run. His son Edsel died in 1943, and Ford resumed the presidency until 1945, when his grandson Henry Ford II took over. He died in 1947.

Legacy

Ford's system of mass production — sometimes called "Fordism" — transformed manufacturing worldwide, and his name remains synonymous with the assembly line and the democratisation of the automobile.
