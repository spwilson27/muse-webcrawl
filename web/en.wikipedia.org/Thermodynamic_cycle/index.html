Thermodynamic cycle

Thermodynamic cycle
BranchThermodynamics
ExamplesCarnot, Otto, Diesel, Rankine, Brayton cycles
DevicesHeat engines, refrigerators, heat pumps

A thermodynamic cycle consists of a linked sequence of thermodynamic processes that involve transfer of heat and work into and out of a system, while varying pressure, temperature, and other state variables within the system, and that eventually return the system to its initial state. Because the system ends where it began, its net change in internal energy over one cycle is zero, so by the first law of thermodynamics the net work done equals the net heat absorbed.

Heat engines and refrigeration

Run clockwise on a pressure–volume diagram, a cycle represents a heat engine, which absorbs heat from a hot reservoir, converts part of it to work, and rejects the remainder to a cold reservoir; its thermal efficiency is limited by the Carnot efficiency, 1 − TC/TH. Run counterclockwise, the cycle represents a refrigerator or heat pump, consuming work to move heat from a cold space to a warmer one, characterized by a coefficient of performance.

Well-known cycles

The idealized Carnot cycle of two isotherms and two adiabats sets the maximum possible efficiency. Practical engines are modeled by the Otto cycle (spark-ignition gasoline engines), the Diesel cycle, the Rankine cycle (steam power plants), and the Brayton cycle (gas turbines and jet engines). Refrigeration commonly uses the vapor-compression cycle. Real cycles deviate from the ideals because of friction, irreversibility, and finite-rate heat transfer.
