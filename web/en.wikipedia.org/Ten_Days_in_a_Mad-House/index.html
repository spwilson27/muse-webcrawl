Ten Days in a Mad-House

Ten Days in a Mad-House
Author: Nellie Bly
Published: 1887
Publisher: Ian L. Munro
Genre: Investigative journalism, exposé

Ten Days in a Mad-House is a book by American journalist Nellie Bly, published in 1887. It recounts Bly's undercover assignment for Joseph Pulitzer's newspaper the New York World, in which she feigned insanity in order to be committed to the Women's Lunatic Asylum on Blackwell's Island (now Roosevelt Island) in New York City and report on conditions inside. The work is a landmark of investigative and immersion journalism.

Background

Bly, born Elizabeth Cochran, had joined the New York World in 1887 after working at the Pittsburgh Dispatch. Seeking a sensational first assignment, she agreed to have herself committed to an asylum to expose its treatment of patients. To prepare, she practised dishevelled expressions before a mirror and checked into a boarding house for women, where her erratic behaviour led police and a judge to have her examined by doctors, several of whom declared her insane.

Contents

Bly spent ten days in the asylum. She reported that once admitted, she dropped all pretence of madness, yet staff treated her as insane regardless. She described spoiled and meagre food, icy baths, filthy conditions, rats, and physical abuse of patients by nurses, as well as many inmates who appeared to be sane but were confined because of poverty, illness, or inability to speak English. Her release was arranged by an attorney sent by the newspaper.

Impact

The series, published in the World in October 1887 and then in book form, made Bly famous and prompted a grand jury investigation of the asylum. The inquiry led New York City to appropriate an additional $850,000 for the care of the insane and to institute reforms in examinations and conditions. The success of the exposé helped establish undercover "stunt reporting" as a feature of the era's competitive newspaper journalism, and Bly went on to her celebrated trip around the world in 72 days in 1889–90.
