Telemachus (Ancient Greek: Τηλέμαχος, Tēlemakhos) is a figure in Greek mythology, the son of Odysseus and Penelope, and a central character in Homer's Odyssey. The first four books of the epic, sometimes called the Telemachy, follow his coming of age as he searches for news of his father.

In the Odyssey

Telemachus was an infant when Odysseus left for the Trojan War. Twenty years later, the palace at Ithaca is beset by suitors consuming Odysseus's wealth and pressing Penelope to remarry. Prompted by the goddess Athena (disguised as Mentor and Mentes), Telemachus calls an assembly, rebukes the suitors, and sails secretly to Pylos and Sparta to question Nestor and Menelaus about his father. On returning home — narrowly escaping an ambush by the suitors — he reunites with the disguised Odysseus at the swineherd Eumaeus's hut, and father and son plot the suitors' destruction. Telemachus fights beside Odysseus in the slaughter of the suitors in Book 22.

Later traditions and legacy

In the lost epic Telegony, Telemachus later marries Circe, while his half-brother Telegonus marries Penelope. He is the central figure of Fénelon's 1699 didactic novel The Adventures of Telemachus, a bestseller of the Enlightenment, and a presence in Joyce's Ulysses, whose Stephen Dedalus corresponds to him. His name has become shorthand for a son searching for an absent father.
