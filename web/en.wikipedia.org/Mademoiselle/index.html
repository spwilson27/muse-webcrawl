Mademoiselle

Mademoiselle (French pronunciation: [madmwazɛl]; abbreviated Mlle or Mlle) is a French courtesy title, literally "my lady," traditionally given to an unmarried woman. It is composed of the possessive adjective ma (my) and demoiselle (young lady, originally "little lady," from Latin dominicella). The corresponding title for a married woman—and, in modern usage, for any adult woman—is Madame.

Use in France

Historically the title marked marital status in the same way that English distinguished Miss from Mrs. Feminist campaigners in France long objected to this asymmetry, noting that men are addressed as Monsieur regardless of marital status. In February 2012, the French government, under Prime Minister François Fillon, directed that the term mademoiselle be removed from official administrative forms and replaced by madame, following similar reforms in other francophone jurisdictions such as Quebec, Belgium, and Switzerland, where madame had already become standard for adult women. The word remains common in everyday spoken French, particularly for young women and girls, and in polite address in service settings.

Other uses

The title was historically applied within the French royal household: "Mademoiselle" par excellence was the eldest unmarried daughter of the king's eldest brother (Monsieur), most famously Anne Marie Louise d'Orléans, duchess of Montpensier, known as la Grande Mademoiselle. The word has been borrowed into other languages as a marker of French elegance, notably as the name of the American women's magazine Mademoiselle (1935–2001) and in fashion branding, including Chanel's "Mademoiselle" perfume.
