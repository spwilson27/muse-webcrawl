A ring system is a disc or ring of dust, ice and rocky debris orbiting an astronomical body such as a planet, dwarf planet or minor planet. The most famous example is the bright, broad system of rings around Saturn, visible from Earth in a small telescope.

Formation

Rings form within a body's Roche limit, where tidal forces prevent orbiting particles from accreting into a moon. Material may come from a shattered moon or comet, from a moon disrupted by impact, or from material left over from formation that never coalesced. Saturn's rings are thought to be relatively young — perhaps 100–400 million years old — and composed of nearly pure water ice.

Ring systems of the Solar System

All four giant planets have rings. Jupiter's are faint and dusty; Uranus's are narrow, dark and confined by shepherd moons; Neptune's include incomplete arcs. Beyond the planets, rings have been discovered around the centaur 10199 Chariklo (2013), the dwarf planet Haumea (2017), and the trans-Neptunian object Quaoar (2023), whose ring lies surprisingly far outside the classical Roche limit.

Structure and dynamics

Ring particles range from micrometres to metres across. Gaps and divisions — such as Saturn's Cassini Division — are carved by resonances with moons and by embedded moonlets; fine structure including spokes, waves and braided strands arises from gravitational and electromagnetic interactions. Individual particles orbit according to Kepler's laws, inner particles moving fastest.
