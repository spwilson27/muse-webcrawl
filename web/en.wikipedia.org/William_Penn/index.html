William Penn

William Penn (24 October 1644 – 30 July 1718) was an English writer, religious thinker, and influential Quaker who founded the Province of Pennsylvania during the British colonial era in North America.

Early life and conversion

Penn was the son of Admiral Sir William Penn. Expelled from Oxford for religious nonconformity, he converted to Quakerism in his twenties and was repeatedly imprisoned for his preaching and writings, including No Cross, No Crown (1669). His 1670 trial helped establish the principle of jury independence in English law.

Founding Pennsylvania

In 1681, King Charles II granted Penn a large tract of American land in settlement of a debt owed to his father. Penn named it Pennsylvania ("Penn's woods") and planned the city of Philadelphia on a grid between the Delaware and Schuylkill rivers. His Frame of Government embodied liberal principles — representative assembly, fair trials, and broad religious toleration — that attracted persecuted groups from across Europe.

Relations with Native Americans and legacy

Penn made a point of buying land from the Lenape and treating them fairly; his 1683 treaty of friendship with Tamanend became legendary. He spent only about four years in America (1682–84 and 1699–1701), later suffering financial ruin through a dishonest steward. Nevertheless Pennsylvania was named an honorary citizen of the United States in 1984, and his ideas influenced later American democratic thought.
