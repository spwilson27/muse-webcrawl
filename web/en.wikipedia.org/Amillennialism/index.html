Amillennialism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Amillennialism, in Christian eschatology, involves the rejection of the belief that Jesus Christ will have a thousand-year, physical reign on the Earth. This is in contrast to premillennialist interpretations of chapter 20 of the Book of Revelation, which hold that Christ's Second Coming will precede a literal millennial kingdom.

Amillennialists interpret the "thousand years" of Revelation 20 symbolically, as the present church age between Christ's first and second comings, during which Christ reigns spiritually from heaven and through the Church, while Satan is "bound" in the limited sense that he cannot prevent the spread of the gospel or deceive the nations into destroying the Church. The general resurrection, the return of Christ, the last judgment, and the new heavens and new earth all occur together at the end of this age.

History

Although early church fathers such as Justin Martyr and Irenaeus held chiliastic (premillennial) views, an allegorical reading developed under Origen and was defended by Augustine of Hippo in The City of God. Augustine's view became dominant in medieval Christianity and was retained by most Reformation traditions: it is the historic position of Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Lutheran, Anglican, and Reformed churches, though the label "amillennial" itself dates only from later centuries.

Relation to other views

Amillennialism differs from postmillennialism, which expects the world to be progressively Christianised before Christ's return, and from premillennialism, which expects Christ to return before establishing a literal thousand-year reign. Amillennialists share with postmillennialists the belief that the millennium is the present age, but are generally more reserved about expecting the triumph of the gospel over world cultures before the end.
