Seebeck effect

The Seebeck effect is the direct conversion of temperature differences into electric voltage: when two dissimilar electrical conductors or semiconductors are joined and their junctions are held at different temperatures, a voltage develops proportional to the temperature difference. It is one of the thermoelectric effects, discovered by Thomas Johann Seebeck in 1821.

Principle

The size of the effect is described by the Seebeck coefficient, measured in microvolts per kelvin. In a circuit of two different metals with junctions at different temperatures, a current flows; a single material develops a voltage along a temperature gradient because charge carriers diffuse from hot to cold.

Applications

The effect underlies the thermocouple, the standard device for measuring temperature, and thermoelectric generators, which power deep-space probes (radioisotope thermoelectric generators) and some remote installations by converting heat directly into electricity. Its inverse is the Peltier effect, used in solid-state cooling.
