Closed captioning

Closed captioning (CC) is the process of displaying text on a television, video screen, or other visual display to provide additional or interpretive information, most commonly a transcription of the audio portion of a program as it occurs — including dialogue, speaker identification, and descriptions of sound effects and music — for viewers who are deaf or hard of hearing. The term "closed" indicates that the captions are not visible until activated by the viewer, usually via a remote control or menu option, in contrast to "open" captions, which are permanently embedded in the image.

History

Experiments with captioning broadcast television began in the United States in the early 1970s. The Public Broadcasting Service (PBS) station WGBH in Boston pioneered open-captioned broadcasts, and in 1979 the National Captioning Institute was founded to provide closed captioning services for network programming. The first closed-captioned broadcasts aired in 1980, with decoders sold to consumers as separate set-top boxes. The Television Decoder Circuitry Act of 1990 required all televisions with screens 13 inches or larger sold in the United States after July 1993 to contain built-in caption decoder chips. As television moved to digital transmission and internet streaming, captioning obligations were extended to new platforms by legislation such as the Twenty-First Century Communications and Video Accessibility Act of 2010.

Technology

In analog NTSC broadcasts, captions were encoded in line 21 of the vertical blanking interval under the EIA-608 standard, carrying up to four caption and text streams in a form that could also transmit rating and program information. Digital television adopted the CEA-708 standard, which supports multiple caption services, greater character sets, and viewer control over font, size, colour, and placement. Captions may be produced live — using stenographic reporters or automatic speech recognition — or prepared in advance for recorded programming, sometimes called offline captioning. On the web, caption files are commonly delivered in formats such as WebVTT, SubRip (SRT), and Timed Text (TTML).

Styles and terminology

Roll-up captions scroll upward line by line and are typical of live broadcasts, whereas pop-on captions appear in complete blocks and are used for pre-recorded material. In the United Kingdom and much of Europe, the equivalent service is called subtitling for the hard of hearing; teletext-based systems such as line 777 on British broadcasts served a similar purpose. Captions for the deaf and hard of hearing (SDH) go beyond dialogue to include non-speech elements such as "[door slams]" or "[ominous music]".

Legislation

In the United States, the Federal Communications Commission requires nearly all English- and Spanish-language television programming to be captioned, and rules on caption quality — covering accuracy, synchronicity, completeness, and placement — took effect in 2014. Many other countries impose comparable quotas on broadcasters, and captioning has become a standard expectation for streaming services.
