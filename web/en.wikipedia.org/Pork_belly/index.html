Pork belly

Pork belly is a boneless, fatty cut of meat taken from the underside (belly) of the pig, after the loin and spareribs are removed. It consists of alternating layers of fat and lean muscle, giving it a rich flavour and a texture that becomes tender and succulent with slow cooking. Pork belly is one of the most widely used cuts in world cuisines and is also the raw material for bacon and pancetta.

Culinary uses

In East Asia, pork belly is central to dishes such as Chinese hong shao rou (red-braised pork), Dongpo pork, Japanese kakuni and chashu for ramen, and Korean samgyeopsal, in which thick slices are grilled at the table. In Europe it appears as Italian pancetta, Spanish panceta, and slow-roasted British pork belly with crisp crackling. In the United States, uncured belly is cured and smoked to make bacon, while fresh belly has become a staple of modern restaurant cooking, often braised, roasted, or cooked sous vide and then seared.

Commodity trade

Frozen pork bellies were famously traded as a futures contract on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange from 1961 until 2011, because bellies were stored in winter for summer bacon demand. The contract became a cultural shorthand for commodity speculation, though by the time it was delisted the physical trade had largely shifted to fresh, never-frozen bellies and the contract had fallen into disuse.
