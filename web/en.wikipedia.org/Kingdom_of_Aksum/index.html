Kingdom of Aksum

The Kingdom of Aksum (also Axum) was an ancient kingdom centred in what is now northern Ethiopia and Eritrea, which flourished from roughly 100 AD to 960 AD. Growing from the proto-Aksumite period, it became one of the great powers of the ancient world — the Persian prophet Mani named it one of the four great kingdoms alongside Rome, Persia, and China.

Trade and power

Aksum controlled the Red Sea port of Adulis and dominated trade between Africa, Arabia, and India, exporting ivory, gold, frankincense, obsidian, and tortoiseshell in exchange for Roman wine, cloth, glass, and metalware. Aksumite kings minted their own gold, silver, and bronze coinage from the reign of Endubis (c. 270 AD) — the first sub-Saharan African state to do so — with inscriptions in Greek and later Ge'ez.

Christianity and monuments

King Ezana converted to Christianity around 330–360 AD, making Aksum one of the earliest Christian states; the missionary Frumentius became its first bishop. The kingdom is famous for its monumental carved granite stelae at Aksum, the largest over 33 metres tall, and for churches cut from rock. In the 6th century King Kaleb intervened in Yemen to protect Christians, briefly extending Aksumite rule across the Red Sea.

Decline

From the 7th century Aksum declined as Islamic expansion cut its Red Sea trade, soils eroded, and rainfall patterns shifted. The capital was eventually abandoned, but the kingdom's legacy endured in Ethiopian Christianity, the Ge'ez language and liturgy, and the tradition that the Ark of the Covenant rests at Aksum's Church of Our Lady Mary of Zion.
