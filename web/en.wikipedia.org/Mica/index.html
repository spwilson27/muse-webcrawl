Mica

Mica is a group of sheet silicate (phyllosilicate) minerals characterised by near-perfect basal cleavage, allowing them to be split into very thin, flexible, transparent sheets. Chemically, micas are complex hydrous potassium-aluminium silicates. The most common members are muscovite (pale), biotite (dark), phlogopite and lepidolite.

Properties and occurrence

Mica has a Mohs hardness of about 2–4, a pearly to vitreous lustre and excellent dielectric strength. It occurs in igneous, metamorphic and sedimentary rocks; large sheet crystals come from pegmatites, historically mined in India, and today also produced in countries including China, Russia and Brazil.

Uses

Ground mica is used as a filler and shimmering pigment in paints, plastics, cosmetics and joint compound, and to strengthen rubber and asphalt shingles. Sheet mica's combination of transparency, heat resistance and electrical insulation made it valuable for furnace and stove windows and as insulation in electrical equipment; synthetic mica is now also manufactured.

Labour concerns

Mica mining, particularly in parts of India and Madagascar, has been linked in reporting to child labour and hazardous informal work, prompting supply-chain scrutiny by cosmetics and automotive industries.
