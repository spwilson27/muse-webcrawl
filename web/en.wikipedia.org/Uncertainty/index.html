Uncertainty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Uncertainty refers to epistemic situations involving imperfect or incomplete information. It applies to predictions of future events, to physical measurements that are already made, and to the unknown. Uncertainty arises in partially observable or stochastic environments, as well as due to ignorance, indolence, or both. It arises in any number of fields, including insurance, philosophy, physics, statistics, economics, finance, medicine, psychology, and the social sciences.

Concepts

Although the terms are used in various ways among the general public, experts in decision theory, statistics, and other quantitative fields have arrived at more precise definitions. In 1921 the economist Frank Knight distinguished risk, where the probabilities of outcomes are known or can be estimated, from uncertainty proper (Knightian uncertainty), where they cannot. Other useful distinctions include:

  • Aleatory uncertainty — uncertainty due to inherent randomness or variability, which cannot be reduced by gathering more information
  • Epistemic uncertainty — uncertainty due to lack of knowledge, which can in principle be reduced by further study or measurement
  • Measurement uncertainty — the dispersion of values that could reasonably be attributed to a measured quantity, formally evaluated in metrology

Measurement and quantification

In science and engineering, uncertainty is quantified, for example, by standard deviations, confidence intervals, and probability distributions. In meteorology, ensemble forecasting runs a model many times with slightly different starting conditions so that the spread of the results represents forecast uncertainty. Financial analysts use measures such as volatility and value at risk to characterise the uncertainty of returns.

Decision making under uncertainty

Decision theory studies how agents should choose when outcomes are uncertain. Expected utility theory, maximin criteria, and Bayesian methods for updating beliefs in light of evidence are central tools. Psychologists such as Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that real human judgements under uncertainty systematically depart from these normative rules, relying on heuristics that can introduce biases.

Uncertainty in physics

Quantum mechanics places a fundamental limit on the precision with which certain pairs of physical properties can be known simultaneously — the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. This is a property of nature itself, rather than merely a limitation of measurement instruments.
