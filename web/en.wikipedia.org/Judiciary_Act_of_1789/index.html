Judiciary Act of 1789

Judiciary Act of 1789
Enacted24 September 1789
Passed by1st United States Congress
Signed byPresident George Washington
EffectEstablished the federal judiciary of the United States

The Judiciary Act of 1789 (1 Stat. 73) was a landmark statute adopted by the first United States Congress that established the federal court system. Article III of the Constitution had created the Supreme Court and authorized Congress to create lower federal courts; the Act exercised that power and laid out the structure, jurisdiction and procedures of the new judiciary.

Provisions

The Act, principally drafted by Senator Oliver Ellsworth of Connecticut, created 13 judicial districts (one for each state then in the Union), 13 district courts and three circuit courts. It fixed the Supreme Court at six justices — a chief justice and five associates — and required them to "ride circuit" and sit on the circuit courts. Section 13 empowered the Supreme Court to issue writs of mandamus; in Marbury v. Madison (1803) the Court held that this grant, as applied, exceeded Congress's authority under the Constitution, in the first assertion of judicial review over an Act of Congress. Section 25 allowed Supreme Court review of state-court decisions on federal questions, a provision upheld in Martin v. Hunter's Lessee and Cohens v. Virginia.

Office of Attorney General

The Act also created the office of Attorney General of the United States (first held by Edmund Randolph) and provided for United States attorneys and marshals in each district.

Legacy

Although amended many times — notably by the Judiciary Act of 1801, the Judiciary Act of 1869 and the Evarts Act of 1891 — the basic three-tier structure of district courts, circuit courts of appeals and a single Supreme Court descends from the 1789 Act, whose core provisions survive in Title 28 of the United States Code.
