Neural crest

The neural crest is a ridge-like structure that is formed on either side of the neural plate in the early vertebrate embryo, at the border between the neural plate and the epidermis. Neural crest cells originate from the ectoderm and, after an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, migrate extensively throughout the embryo to give rise to a remarkably diverse set of cell types. Because of the breadth of its derivatives, the neural crest is sometimes informally called the "fourth germ layer".

Derivatives

Neural crest cells differentiate into, among others: neurons and glia of the peripheral nervous system, including sensory, sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia; the medulla of the adrenal gland; pigment cells (melanocytes) of the skin; Schwann cells; and much of the cartilage, bone and connective tissue of the face and skull (from cranial neural crest). Trunk neural crest also contributes to the dorsal root ganglia and the enteric nervous system of the gut. Signals such as BMP, Wnt and FGF, and transcription factors including Sox10, Pax3 and Snail family proteins, govern crest specification, migration and differentiation.

Clinical significance

Defects in neural crest development, termed neurocristopathies, include Hirschsprung's disease (absence of enteric ganglia in part of the colon), Waardenburg syndrome (pigmentation and hearing abnormalities), Treacher Collins syndrome (craniofacial malformation), and DiGeorge syndrome, in which cardiac outflow and thymic defects reflect abnormal crest contributions. Neural crest cells also give rise to tumours such as melanoma and neuroblastoma, and the crest's migratory behaviour is frequently used as a model for cancer metastasis.

Evolution

The neural crest is a vertebrate innovation; its appearance is linked to the evolution of the "new head" — jaws, paired sense organs and a large brain — that distinguishes vertebrates from other chordates. Crest-like cells have been identified in tunicates, suggesting the developmental programme was assembled stepwise in early chordate evolution.
