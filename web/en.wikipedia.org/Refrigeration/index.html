Refrigeration

Refrigeration is the process of removing heat from an enclosed space or substance and transferring it elsewhere in order to lower and maintain temperature below that of the surroundings. Refrigeration slows the growth of bacteria and chemical reactions that spoil food, and it also serves industrial processes, air conditioning, and cryogenics. The "cold chain" of refrigerated storage and transport underpins the modern global food supply.

Methods

Most refrigeration uses the vapour-compression cycle, in which a refrigerant is compressed, cooled and condensed to a liquid, expanded through a valve so that it partially vaporises and cools, and then evaporated in coils that absorb heat from the refrigerated space before returning to the compressor. Alternatives include absorption refrigeration (driven by heat rather than mechanical work, common in gas-fired and recreational-vehicle fridges), thermoelectric (Peltier) cooling for small devices, and evaporative and magnetic refrigeration in specialised applications.

History and impact

Before mechanical refrigeration, cold storage relied on harvested natural ice, shipped in the 19th century from North American lakes as far as India and Australia. Practical vapour-compression machines were developed from the 1850s onward by inventors including James Harrison, Carl von Linde, and Ferdinand Carré; refrigerated ships from the 1880s enabled the export of frozen meat from Australia, New Zealand, and Argentina to Europe. Household electric refrigerators spread in the 1920s–1930s. Choice of refrigerant has been shaped by safety and environment: toxic early refrigerants gave way to chlorofluorocarbons (Freon), which were phased out under the Montreal Protocol because they destroy stratospheric ozone and replaced by HFCs and, increasingly, low-global-warming alternatives such as hydrocarbons, ammonia, carbon dioxide, and HFOs.
