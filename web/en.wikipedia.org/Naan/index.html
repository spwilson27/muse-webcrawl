Naan is a leavened, oven-baked flatbread of Central and South Asian origin, traditionally cooked in a tandoor, a cylindrical clay oven. It is a staple accompaniment to meals across India, Pakistan, Afghanistan, Iran, Bangladesh and the surrounding regions, and is widely eaten in South Asian restaurants worldwide, typically torn and used to scoop up curries and gravies.

History

The word naan derives from the Persian nān, simply meaning "bread". Naan-like breads spread through the region with Persian and Mughal culinary influence; references to naan appear in the notes of the Indo-Persian poet Amir Khusrau in the 1300s, and the bread was served at the Mughal court in India. The leavening with yeast distinguishes it from unleavened flatbreads such as roti and chapati.

Preparation

Naan dough is made from wheat flour, water, yeast and salt, often enriched with yogurt and milk, then kneaded, proofed, and rolled into an oval or teardrop shape. In a tandoor the flattened dough is slapped onto the oven's inner wall, where it bakes in under a minute at very high heat, blistering and charring in spots. Finished naan is frequently brushed with butter or ghee.

Varieties

Common variants include garlic naan, butter naan, cheese naan, and stuffed versions such as Peshwari naan (with coconut, almonds and raisins) and keema naan (with spiced minced meat). Regional relatives include Afghan noni and the leavened breads of Central Asia.
