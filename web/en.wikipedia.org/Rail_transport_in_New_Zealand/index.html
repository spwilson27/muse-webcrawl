Rail transport in New Zealand

New Zealand's railways run on the narrow Cape gauge of 1,067 mm (3 ft 6 in).

Rail transport in New Zealand is an integral part of the country's transport network, with a nationwide system of about 4,100 kilometres of track built to the narrow gauge of 1,067 mm (3 ft 6 in), known as Cape gauge. The network is operated by the state-owned enterprise KiwiRail, which also owns the track infrastructure. Rail plays a significant role in transporting freight between ports and regions and in urban passenger services in Wellington and Auckland.

The first railway opened in 1863 between Christchurch and the port of Ferrymead. Provincial governments initially built lines to various gauges, but in 1870 the central government under Julius Vogel adopted 1,067 mm as the national standard as part of a great programme of public works and immigration.

History

The network reached its greatest extent of about 5,700 km in the 1950s before road competition led to decades of branch-line closures. The railways were corporatised in 1982, privatised in 1993, and renationalised in stages, with the infrastructure returning to public ownership in 2004 and the operating company bought back as KiwiRail in 2008.

Services

Freight dominates the network, moving export goods such as coal, logs, milk and containers. Long-distance passenger services are largely scenic tourist trains — the TranzAlpine across the Southern Alps, the Northern Explorer between Auckland and Wellington, and the Coastal Pacific — while Auckland and Wellington operate suburban commuter networks, both progressively electrified and expanded.

Engineering features

The mountainous terrain required notable feats of engineering, including the Raurimu Spiral on the North Island Main Trunk (completed 1908), which allows trains to gain height on a steep grade, and the 8.5 km Otira Tunnel through the Southern Alps (opened 1923).
