Factor V Leiden

Factor V Leiden (rs6025 or F5 p.R506Q) is a variant (mutated form) of human factor V (one of several substances that helps blood clot), which causes an increase in blood clotting (hypercoagulability). Due to this mutation, protein C, an anticoagulant protein that normally inhibits the pro-clotting activity of factor V, is unable to bind normally to factor V, leading to a hypercoagulable state, i.e. an increased tendency for the thrombosis. Factor V Leiden is the most common hereditary hypercoagulability disorder amongst ethnic Europeans.

Signs and symptoms

Most carriers never develop abnormal clots. When thrombosis occurs, it is typically venous thromboembolism: deep vein thrombosis of the leg and pulmonary embolism. The risk is higher in people with two copies of the variant (homozygotes) than in those with one copy (heterozygotes), and is increased further by other risk factors such as surgery, pregnancy, immobility and estrogen-containing contraceptives.

Genetics

The disorder is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. It is caused by a single nucleotide polymorphism in the F5 gene, in which guanine is replaced by adenine, changing the amino acid arginine to glutamine at position 506 (in the newer numbering, 534) of the factor V protein. Position 506 is one of the cleavage sites at which activated protein C inactivates factor Va; the mutation makes factor Va resistant to this cleavage, a phenomenon called activated protein C resistance.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is by genetic testing or by a functional activated protein C resistance assay. Management centres on anticoagulation when thrombosis occurs; routine lifelong anticoagulation is generally not recommended for asymptomatic carriers, though prophylaxis may be used in high-risk situations.

History

The variant was identified in 1994 by researchers in Leiden, Netherlands, from which it takes its name.
