Pope Alexander VI

Pope Alexander VI (born Rodrigo de Borja; 1 January 1431 – 18 August 1503) was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 11 August 1492 until his death in 1503. Born into the prominent Borgia family in Xàtiva in the Kingdom of Valencia, he is one of the most controversial Renaissance popes, remembered for nepotism, corruption, and the worldly ambitions he pursued on behalf of his children, including Cesare and Lucrezia Borgia.

Early life and election

Rodrigo Borja rose under his uncle, Pope Callixtus III, who made him a cardinal in 1456 and vice-chancellor of the Church, a lucrative post he held under five popes. He accumulated enormous wealth and influence, fathered several children (whom he openly acknowledged), and after the death of Innocent VIII won the conclave of 1492 — reportedly with extensive bribery — taking the name Alexander VI.

Pontificate

As pope he issued the bulls Inter caetera (1493) dividing the newly encountered lands outside Europe between Spain and Portugal, a settlement adjusted by the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494). He navigated the Italian Wars after the French invasion of 1494, shifting alliances to preserve the Papal States. He relentlessly advanced his family: his son Cesare was made a cardinal at 17, later resigned to pursue a military career carving out a principality in the Romagna — a career that inspired Machiavelli's The Prince — and his daughter Lucrezia was married three times for political advantage. The Florentine friar Savonarola, who denounced his corruption, was excommunicated and executed in 1498. Alexander died in August 1503, probably of fever (malaria), amid rumours of poisoning.

Legacy

Alexander VI became the archetype of the corrupt Renaissance pope, though modern historians note that his administrative and diplomatic skills were considerable and that some lurid stories derive from hostile sources. His patronage included commissioning Pinturicchio to decorate the Borgia Apartments in the Vatican.
