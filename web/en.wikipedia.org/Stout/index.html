Stout

A pint of dry stout

Stout is a type of dark beer brewed with roasted malt or roasted barley, hops, water, and yeast. Stouts were traditionally the generic term for the strongest (most "stout") porters, typically 7% or 8% alcohol by volume, produced by a brewery. The term was first documented in 1677.

History

Stout developed from London porter in the 18th century: a "stout porter" was a stronger version of the style. Irish brewers, most famously Arthur Guinness in Dublin, made the style their own, producing the dry Irish stout with unmalted roasted barley that became its signature. Stout was exported worldwide and brewed under licence in many countries.

Varieties

Recognised variants include dry or Irish stout, milk (sweet) stout brewed with lactose, oatmeal stout, oyster stout, chocolate stout, coffee stout, and the stronger imperial stout originally brewed for export to the Russian imperial court. Modern craft brewers produce many flavoured "pastry stouts".

Characteristics

Stouts are nearly black, with flavours of coffee, chocolate, and roasted grain, a full body, and a creamy head famously enhanced in draught service by nitrogen gas rather than carbon dioxide alone.
