Carbyne

Carbyne is an allotrope of carbon composed of long chains of carbon atoms joined by alternating single and triple bonds (polyyne form) or by consecutive double bonds (cumulene form). It is the one-dimensional member of the family of sp-hybridised carbon allotropes, alongside the familiar sp2 forms (graphite, graphene, fullerenes) and sp3 diamond. Infinitely long carbyne is also called linear acetylenic carbon.

Properties

Theoretical studies predict that carbyne would have extraordinary properties: a tensile strength exceeding that of graphene and diamond, very high stiffness, and electronic behaviour tunable by bending or stretching the chain, which could switch it between conductor and insulator. However, free carbyne chains are highly reactive and unstable; long chains tend to cross-link or decompose, so claims of bulk carbyne synthesis since the 1960s have been controversial.

Synthesis and occurrence

Stable finite polyynes with protective end-groups have been synthesised with dozens of carbon atoms, and in 2016 researchers reported chains of thousands of atoms stabilised inside double-walled carbon nanotubes. Carbyne-like chains have also been proposed to occur in interstellar dust and shocked graphite. In organic chemistry, the term "carbyne" is also used for species with a carbon atom bearing three nonbonding electrons, and for metal complexes with carbon–metal triple bonds (alkylidynes).
