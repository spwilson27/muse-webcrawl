Society of Jesus

Founded: 27 September 1540
Founder: Ignatius of Loyola
Type: Religious order of the Catholic Church
Motto: Ad maiorem Dei gloriam

The Society of Jesus (Latin: Societas Iesu; abbreviated SJ), also known as the Jesuits, is a religious order of clerks regular of the Catholic Church headquartered in Rome. It was founded in 1540 by Ignatius of Loyola and six companions with the approval of Pope Paul III.

The Society is engaged in evangelization and apostolic ministry in 112 nations. Jesuits work in education, research, and cultural pursuits, and are known for their missionary work, their role in the Counter-Reformation, and their founding of schools, colleges, and universities around the world.

History

Ignatius of Loyola, a Basque nobleman and soldier, formulated the Spiritual Exercises following his conversion after being wounded at the Battle of Pamplona in 1521. In 1534, Ignatius and six companions — including Francis Xavier and Peter Faber — took vows at Montmartre in Paris. The order was formally approved by Pope Paul III in 1540 with the bull Regimini militantis Ecclesiae. Jesuit missionaries travelled widely, including Francis Xavier to India and Japan, Matteo Ricci to China, and missions throughout the Americas.

Suppression and restoration

Under political pressure from European courts, Pope Clement XIV suppressed the Society in 1773 with the brief Dominus ac Redemptor. The order survived in Russia and Prussia, where rulers refused to promulgate the brief. Pope Pius VII restored the Society universally in 1814.

Education and characteristics

Jesuit formation is lengthy, often lasting more than a decade, and includes studies in philosophy and theology. The order's educational tradition is codified in the Ratio Studiorum of 1599. Members take vows of poverty, chastity, and obedience, and professed members take a fourth vow of obedience to the pope regarding missions. The Superior General is elected for life. In 2013, Jorge Mario Bergoglio became Pope Francis, the first Jesuit pope.
