Pulao

Pulao (also called pilaf, pilav, palaw, or palav) is a rice dish in which rice is cooked in a seasoned broth, often with spices, vegetables, meat, or seafood. The grains are typically sautéed in oil or ghee before liquid is added, a technique that keeps them separate and fluffy rather than sticky. Pulao is one of the most widespread dishes in the world, with regional versions across South Asia, Central Asia, the Middle East, the Balkans, East Africa, and the Caribbean.

History

Cooking rice in broth with spices is described in ancient Sanskrit texts and in Arabic cookery books from the medieval period. The dish spread widely with Persian and Turkic influence; the Persian polow and Turkish pilav are direct relatives. Alexander the Great is legendarily associated with an early version served in Bactria. In South Asia, pulao became a fixture of Mughlai cuisine, distinguished from biryani by its subtler spicing and the single-pot absorption method of cooking.

Preparation

Long-grain rice such as basmati is washed (and sometimes soaked), then fried briefly with whole spices such as cardamom, cloves, cinnamon, cumin, and bay leaves. Stock or water is added in a measured ratio, and the pot is covered and simmered until the liquid is absorbed. Common additions include peas, carrots, onions, potatoes, chicken, lamb, and chickpeas. The finished dish is often garnished with fried onions, nuts, raisins, and fresh herbs.

Regional varieties

In India and Pakistan, varieties include yakhni pulao (cooked in meat stock), vegetable pulao, Kashmiri pulao with fruit and nuts, and peas pulao (matar pulao). Central Asian plov, such as Uzbek osh, is cooked with carrots and lamb in a large kazan. Afghan kabuli pulao is topped with raisins and julienned carrots. In Turkey, iç pilav includes currants and pine nuts, while in the Caribbean, pelau is made with coconut milk and pigeon peas.
