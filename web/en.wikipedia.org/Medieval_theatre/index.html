Medieval theatre

Medieval theatre refers to the theatrical performance in the period between the fall of the Western Roman Empire in the 5th century and the beginning of the Renaissance in approximately the 15th century. The category of "medieval theatre" covers theatre in this era: liturgical drama, mystery plays, miracle plays, morality plays, passion plays, farces, and the performances of minstrels, mummers, and touring players.

Mystery plays were performed on pageant wagons or fixed stages in town squares.

Development

After the Church suppressed the theatres of late antiquity, drama re-emerged within the liturgy itself. From about the 10th century, short biblical scenes — notably the Quem quaeritis trope at Easter, showing the three Marys at Christ's empty tomb — were enacted during services. These liturgical dramas grew longer and more elaborate, moved from the altar to the church porch and then to the marketplace, and began to be written in the vernacular rather than Latin.

Principal forms

  • Mystery plays — cycles dramatizing the whole of biblical history, from the Creation to the Last Judgment, performed by craft guilds (the "mysteries") on feast days such as Corpus Christi. Famous English cycles survive from York, Chester, Wakefield (Towneley), and N-Town.
  • Miracle plays — plays about the lives and miracles of saints.
  • Morality plays — allegorical dramas, such as Everyman (c. 1510), in which personified virtues and vices struggle for a protagonist's soul.
  • Passion plays — dramas of Christ's suffering and death, of which the Oberammergau Passion Play, performed since 1634, is the most famous survivor.
  • Secular theatre — farces, sotties, mummings, and interludes performed by minstrels and, later, professional companies.

Staging

Performances took place on fixed scaffold stages in a square (the platea with localized booths or "mansions") or on mobile pageant wagons that processed from station to station through a town. Special effects — trapdoors, flying angels, fire-breathing hell-mouths — were a celebrated feature.

Legacy

Guild cycles flourished for over two centuries before being suppressed during the Reformation in Protestant regions and declining elsewhere. Medieval drama laid the groundwork for the secular professional theatre of the Renaissance in England and Spain.
