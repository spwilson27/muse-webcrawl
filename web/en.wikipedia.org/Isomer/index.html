Isomer

In chemistry, isomers are molecules or polyatomic ions with identical molecular formulae — the same number of atoms of each element — but distinct arrangements of atoms in space. Isomerism refers to the existence or possibility of isomers. Isomers do not necessarily share similar chemical or physical properties.

Structural isomers

Structural (constitutional) isomers differ in the connectivity of their atoms. For example, butane and isobutane (both C4H10) differ in their carbon skeleton; ethanol and dimethyl ether (both C2H6O) are functional isomers with different functional groups. Tautomers are structural isomers that interconvert readily, such as keto and enol forms.

Stereoisomers

Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but differ in the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms. Enantiomers are non-superposable mirror images, often arising from a chiral carbon center, and rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions. Diastereomers are stereoisomers not related as mirror images, including cis–trans (E/Z) isomers about double bonds.

History and importance

The concept arose in the 1820s–30s when compounds such as silver fulminate and silver cyanate were found to share a composition but not properties; Jöns Jacob Berzelius coined the term "isomer". Isomerism is crucial in pharmacology, since enantiomers of a drug may differ greatly in effect — one may be therapeutic while the other is inactive or harmful.
