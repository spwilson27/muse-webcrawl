Pachamama

Pachamama is a goddess revered by the indigenous peoples of the central Andes, including in Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador and northern Chile and Argentina. She is an Earth and fertility goddess — "World Mother" or "Mother Earth", from the Quechua pacha (world, earth, time) and mama (mother) — who presides over planting and harvesting, and is associated with mountains, earthquakes and the sustenance of life.

Beliefs and rituals

In Andean cosmology, Pachamama is one of the central deities, paired in some traditions with Pachacamac or the sun god Inti. Worshippers make offerings (pagapu) to her, burying food, coca leaves, chicha (corn beer) and other goods in the ground, or pouring drink onto the earth, especially at the start of the planting season in August, the beginning of the Andean year. The challa — sprinkling alcohol and coca on the ground — is performed before drinking on many occasions in Bolivia and Peru, and offerings are made before building houses, travelling or mining, since taking resources from the earth requires reciprocity (ayni).

After the Spanish conquest, Catholic evangelisation identified Pachamama with the Virgin Mary, and her veneration became syncretised with Marian feasts such as Candlemas; devotion to her continues strongly alongside Roman Catholic practice.

Modern significance

Pachamama has become a symbol of indigenous identity and environmental ethics. The constitutions of Ecuador (2008) and Bolivia (2009) recognise rights of nature, or "Pachamama", and Bolivia's 2010 Law of the Rights of Mother Earth gives legal expression to the concept. Her image has also been adopted by environmental movements worldwide; in 2019, carved Pachamama figures displayed during the Vatican's Amazon Synod provoked controversy when they were thrown into the Tiber by critics who considered them idolatrous.
