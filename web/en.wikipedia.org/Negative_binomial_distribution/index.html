In probability theory and statistics, the negative binomial distribution is a discrete probability distribution that models the number of failures in a sequence of independent Bernoulli trials before a specified number of successes occurs. Its name comes from the negative binomial expansion used in its probability mass function. With r = 1 it reduces to the geometric distribution.

Definition

If each trial succeeds with probability p, the probability that the r-th success occurs after exactly k failures is C(k + r − 1, k) (1 − p)k pr, where C is the binomial coefficient. The distribution can be extended to non-integer r using the gamma function, which is the form commonly used in statistical modelling.

Overdispersion and applications

The negative binomial distribution is widely used to model count data whose variance exceeds the mean (overdispersion), where the Poisson distribution would be inadequate. It arises as a gamma–Poisson mixture: if event rates themselves vary according to a gamma distribution, the counts follow a negative binomial. Applications include epidemiology (modelling numbers of disease cases), ecology (species counts), insurance claims, and RNA sequencing analysis, where negative binomial regression — a generalised linear model — is a standard tool.

History

The distribution appeared in Pascal's work on the "problem of points" in the seventeenth century in the context of gambling problems. Its modern statistical use was developed in the early twentieth century, notably in Greenwood and Yule's 1920 study of accident proneness.
