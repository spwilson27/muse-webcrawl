Fatimid Caliphate
Capital: Mahdia (909–948); al-Mansuriya (948–973); Cairo (973–1171)
Government: Hereditary caliphate
Religion: Isma'ili Shia Islam
Established: 909 · Ended: 1171
Cairo, founded by the Fatimids in 969, became the caliphate's capital and a great centre of learning.

The Fatimid Caliphate was an Arab empire and Isma'ili Shia caliphate that ruled large parts of North Africa, Egypt, and the Levant from 909 to 1171. The dynasty claimed descent from Fatima, the daughter of the Prophet Muhammad, through the line of the Isma'ili imams, and its caliphs were recognised by their followers as both political rulers and divinely guided religious leaders (imam-caliphs). At its height the Fatimid state stretched from the Atlantic coast of Morocco to the Red Sea and Syria.

Origins and rise

The caliphate was founded in Ifriqiya (modern Tunisia) in 909 by Abd Allah al-Mahdi Billah after Isma'ili missionaries (da'is) won over the Kutama Berbers and overthrew the Aghlabid emirate. The early capital was Mahdia, a fortified peninsula city. In 969 the Fatimid general Jawhar al-Siqilli conquered Egypt from the Ikhshidids and founded a new capital, Cairo (al-Qahira, "the Victorious"), to which the caliph al-Mu'izz moved in 973.

Golden age

Under caliphs such as al-Aziz and al-Hakim, the Fatimids made Egypt the centre of a prosperous trading empire linking the Mediterranean with the Indian Ocean. Cairo's al-Azhar Mosque, founded in 970–972, developed into one of the world's oldest universities and a major centre of Islamic learning. The Fatimids were notable for relative religious tolerance: Christians and Jews could reach high office, and Sunni Muslims remained the majority of the population. The reign of al-Hakim (996–1021) was marked by erratic decrees and ended in his mysterious disappearance — an event central to the origin of the Druze faith.

Decline and fall

From the mid-eleventh century the caliphate weakened through military factionalism, the loss of Syria to the Seljuks and then the Crusaders, fiscal crises, and the growing power of viziers such as Badr al-Jamali, who effectively ruled as military dictators. In 1171 the last Fatimid caliph, al-Adid, died while the Kurdish general Salah al-Din (Saladin), nominally his vizier, abolished the caliphate, restored Sunni Islam in Egypt, and founded the Ayyubid dynasty.

Legacy

The Fatimids left a rich architectural and artistic heritage, including the mosques of al-Azhar and al-Hakim in Cairo, Fatimid rock crystal and lusterware ceramics, and a legacy in Isma'ili thought and literature that continues in the traditions of the Nizari and Tayyibi Isma'ili communities.
