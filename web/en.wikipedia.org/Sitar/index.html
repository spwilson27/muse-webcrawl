Sitar

ClassificationString instrument (plucked lute)
OriginIndian subcontinent, c. 18th century
Strings18–21 (6–7 played, rest sympathetic)
RelatedVeena, tanpura, surbahar

The sitar is a plucked stringed instrument used in Hindustani classical music. Recognisable by its long hollow neck, gourd-shaped resonating chamber and shimmering tone, it is one of the most widely known Indian instruments in the world.

Construction

The body is typically carved from teak or tun wood with a large resonating gourd (often a specially grown pumpkin shell) at the base. A sitar usually has 18 to 21 strings: six or seven main playing strings that run over curved, movable metal frets, and eleven or more sympathetic strings that vibrate in resonance and produce the instrument's characteristic halo of sound. The main strings are played with a wire plectrum called a mizrab.

Playing technique

The player sits cross-legged, holding the instrument at an angle across the body. The index and middle fingers of the left hand press the main string against the frets while the right hand plucks. A defining technique is meend, pulling the string laterally across a fret to bend a note continuously — by as much as a fifth — allowing the gliding ornaments essential to a raga.

History and performers

The modern sitar developed in the 18th century, blending features of Indian lutes with Persian instruments (its name derives from the Persian sehtar, "three-stringed"). In the 20th century, Ravi Shankar brought the sitar to international prominence, performing at major Western festivals and influencing rock musicians; George Harrison of the Beatles studied with Shankar and played sitar on songs such as "Norwegian Wood". Other leading exponents include Vilayat Khan, Nikhil Banerjee and Anoushka Shankar.
