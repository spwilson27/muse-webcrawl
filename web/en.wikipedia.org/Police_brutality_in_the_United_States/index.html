Police brutality in the United States

Police brutality in the United States is the unwarranted or excessive and often illegal use of force against civilians by U.S. police officers. Forms of police brutality range from assault and battery to mayhem, torture, and murder. Some broader definitions also include harassment, intimidation, and verbal abuse, as well as the use of racial profiling.

According to databases maintained by journalists and researchers, such as the Washington Post's Fatal Force database, roughly 1,000 people are shot and killed by police in the United States each year. Studies published in medical and social-science journals have found that Black Americans are killed by police at substantially higher rates than white Americans, and that police use of force is a leading cause of death for young men of color.

History

Complaints of police violence date to the earliest American police forces in the 19th century, when officers were frequently implicated in breaking strikes and enforcing segregation. During the civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s, televised images of police attacking peaceful demonstrators — for example in Birmingham, Alabama, in 1963 — galvanized national opinion. Later flashpoints included the 1991 beating of Rodney King in Los Angeles, whose officers' acquittal sparked the 1992 Los Angeles riots; the 2014 killings of Michael Brown in Ferguson, Missouri, and Eric Garner in New York; and the 2020 murder of George Floyd by Minneapolis officer Derek Chauvin, which triggered worldwide protests and Chauvin's conviction for murder.

Causes and legal framework

Commentators cite factors including the standard of "qualified immunity," which shields officers from civil liability unless they violated clearly established law; the Supreme Court's objective-reasonableness standard for force (Graham v. Connor, 1989); police union contracts; and the difficulty of prosecution by local district attorneys who work closely with police. The federal government may prosecute under 18 U.S.C. § 242 or impose reform through pattern-or-practice investigations and consent decrees.

Reform efforts

Reforms debated and adopted in various jurisdictions include body-worn cameras, bans on chokeholds, duty-to-intervene rules, de-escalation training, changes to use-of-force standards, independent investigation of critical incidents, and civilian review. The movement for reform, associated in part with Black Lives Matter, has made police accountability a continuing subject of national debate.
