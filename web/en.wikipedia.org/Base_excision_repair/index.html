Base excision repair

Base excision repair (BER) is a cellular mechanism that repairs damaged DNA throughout the cell cycle. It is responsible primarily for removing small, non-helix-distorting base lesions from the genome, such as those caused by oxidation, deamination, and alkylation — damage that arises spontaneously at a rate of thousands of lesions per cell per day. BER is important for preventing mutations and genomic instability, and defects in the pathway are associated with cancer and neurological disease.

Overview of the base excision repair pathway.

Mechanism

The pathway begins with a DNA glycosylase, which recognises and removes the damaged base by cleaving the N-glycosidic bond, leaving an abasic (AP) site. Humans have eleven known DNA glycosylases, each with a characteristic lesion specificity; for example, uracil-DNA glycosylase removes uracil arising from cytosine deamination, and OGG1 removes 8-oxoguanine. An AP endonuclease (APE1 in humans) then cuts the phosphodiester backbone at the AP site. In short-patch BER, DNA polymerase β inserts the single correct nucleotide and removes the residual deoxyribose phosphate, after which a ligase (the XRCC1–Ligase III complex) seals the nick. In long-patch BER, a stretch of two to ten nucleotides is synthesised, the displaced flap is removed by FEN1, and Ligase I seals the strand.
