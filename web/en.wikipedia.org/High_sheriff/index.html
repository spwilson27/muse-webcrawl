High sheriff

A high sheriff is a ceremonial officer for each shrieval county of England, Wales and Northern Ireland, or the chief or most senior sheriff in a larger urban area. The office is unpaid and largely ceremonial today, though the holder retains certain formal duties, including attending on judges at the Crown Court and acting as returning officer in parliamentary elections in some areas.

History

The office of sheriff — from "shire reeve" — dates to Anglo-Saxon England, when the sheriff was the king's representative in a county, responsible for keeping the peace, collecting revenues, and executing writs. By the late medieval period many executive functions had passed to justices of the peace and lords lieutenant. The Sheriffs Act 1887 consolidated the law governing the office in England and Wales.

Appointment

High sheriffs are appointed annually; in England and Wales the monarch formally selects ("pricks") names from a list during the Pricking Ceremony, using a bodkin to pierce the parchment roll — a tradition said to date from Elizabeth I. The term of office runs for one year, typically from March or April.
