Lagrange's four-square theorem

Lagrange's four-square theorem, also known as Bachet's conjecture, states that every natural number can be represented as the sum of four integer squares. That is, for every natural number n there exist integers a, b, c, d such that n = a² + b² + c² + d². For example, 3 = 1² + 1² + 1² + 0², and 31 = 5² + 2² + 1² + 1².

History

The theorem was first stated without proof by Claude Gaspard Bachet de Méziriac in 1621, in his translation of Diophantus's Arithmetica. Fermat claimed to have a proof but never published it. Joseph Louis Lagrange gave the first published proof in 1770. Adrien-Marie Legendre strengthened the result in 1797–98 with his three-square theorem, characterising the numbers needing only three squares.

Proof outline

Classical proofs use Euler's four-square identity, which shows that the product of two sums of four squares is itself a sum of four squares, reducing the problem to prime numbers, together with a descent argument showing every prime is a sum of four squares.

Generalisations

The theorem is the g(2) = 4 case of Waring's problem. Jacobi's four-square theorem counts the number of representations of a number as a sum of four squares. Ramanujan gave a list of the 54 universal diagonal quadratic forms in four variables.
