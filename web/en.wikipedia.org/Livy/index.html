Titus Livius (59 BC – AD 17), known in English as Livy, was a Roman historian. His history of Rome, Ab Urbe Condita libri — "Books from the Founding of the City" — chronicled the city from its legendary foundation in 753 BC through the reign of Augustus, and it is one of the principal surviving narrative sources for early Rome and the Roman Republic.

Life

Livy was born in Patavium (modern Padua) in Cisalpine Gaul and spent most of his life in Rome, where he enjoyed the acquaintance, though not the patronage in any formal sense, of the emperor Augustus. He tutored the future emperor Claudius in historical writing. Little else is known of his life; he appears to have held no public office and devoted himself entirely to his history, which he began around 27–25 BC.

The History of Rome

The work comprised 142 books, of which 35 survive complete: Books 1–10 (from the founding to 293 BC) and Books 21–45 (218–167 BC, covering the Second Punic War and the Macedonian wars), plus fragments and summaries (periochae) of the rest. Livy wrote in a grand, periodic Latin style modeled on Cicero, composing speeches and dramatic scenes in the manner of ancient historiography. His stated purpose was moral: to show readers the examples of Rome's rise — the virtues that made the republic great — and to contemplate its decline.

Sources and reliability

Livy drew on earlier annalists such as Fabius Pictor, Valerius Antias, and Polybius (for eastern affairs). Modern scholars fault his uncritical use of sources, chronological confusions, and patriotic embellishment, but his narrative remains foundational: for long stretches of Republican history, his account is the only continuous one that survives.

Legacy

Livy was read intensively in the Renaissance — Dante called him "Livy who does not err" — and Machiavelli's Discourses on Livy made his first ten books the basis of modern republican political thought.
