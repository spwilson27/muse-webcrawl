Indonesian language

Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia) is the official and national language of Indonesia. It is a standardized variety of Malay, an Austronesian language that has been used as a lingua franca in the multilingual Indonesian archipelago for centuries. Indonesian is the language of national identity, government, education and media for a population of over 280 million people, most of whom speak it as a second language alongside more than 700 local languages such as Javanese, Sundanese and Balinese.

History

Malay served for centuries as the trade language of maritime Southeast Asia. In 1928, nationalist youth at the Second Youth Congress in Batavia proclaimed the Youth Pledge (Sumpah Pemuda), declaring Malay — renamed Bahasa Indonesia — the language of unity. Upon Indonesia's declaration of independence in 1945, it became the official language of the republic. Its selection over Javanese (spoken by the largest ethnic group) avoided privileging one ethnicity and aided national cohesion.

Relationship to Malay

Indonesian and Malaysian Malay are mutually intelligible standardized registers of the same language, differing mainly in vocabulary influences — Indonesian absorbed many Dutch loanwords during colonial rule, while Malaysian drew more on English — and in some spelling history and idiom. Indonesia reformed its spelling in 1972 (the EYD system), in parallel with Malaysia, making the two orthographies largely convergent.

Grammar and features

Indonesian grammar is relatively simple for learners: there is no grammatical gender, no plural inflection (reduplication may mark plurality), no verb conjugation for tense or person — time is indicated by adverbs and aspect markers such as sudah (already) and sedang (in progress). Word order is generally subject–verb–object. Meaning is elaborated through a rich system of affixes (prefixes such as me-, ber-, di- and suffixes such as -kan and -i) that derive verbs and nouns from roots.

Vocabulary

The lexicon reflects Indonesia's history: layers of loanwords from Sanskrit (from early Indian influence), Arabic (with Islam), Chinese, Portuguese, Dutch (thousands of words from 350 years of colonial contact) and increasingly English for technology and popular culture.

Usage

Formal Indonesian is used in law, education and national media; informal registers (Bahasa gaul) flourish in cities and online. Indonesian is also taught abroad, notably in Australia and Vietnam, and serves as a working language within ASEAN contexts.
