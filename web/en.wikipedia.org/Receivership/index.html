Receivership

In receivership, a court- or creditor-appointed receiver takes control of a debtor's assets to protect or realise their value.

In law, receivership is a situation in which an institution or enterprise is held by a receiver — a person "placed in the custodial responsibility for the property of others, including tangible and intangible assets and rights" — especially in cases where a company cannot meet its financial obligations or enters bankruptcy. The receiver may be appointed by a court, by a government regulator, or under the terms of a security instrument such as a mortgage debenture.

Functions of a receiver

A receiver's powers depend on the instrument of appointment. Typically the receiver takes possession of and manages the charged assets, receives income arising from them, and may sell assets and distribute the proceeds to creditors in order of priority. Because a receiver displaces the company's directors over the assets under the receivership, the device historically gave secured creditors — especially holders of floating charges in Commonwealth jurisdictions — a powerful, swift remedy outside formal insolvency proceedings.

Types

Common forms include the administrative receiver under English law (greatly restricted by the Enterprise Act 2002, which favoured administration instead), the fixed-charge or Law of Property Act receiver over real property, and court-appointed receivers in litigation, who preserve disputed assets pending judgment. In the United States, receivership is an equitable remedy used by courts in shareholder disputes, fraud cases and regulatory enforcement — the Securities and Exchange Commission frequently seeks receivers over fraudulent enterprises — and by regulators such as the FDIC over failed banks. A receivership differs from bankruptcy in that it may cover only part of a debtor's property and is driven by a secured creditor or the court rather than a collective process for all creditors.
