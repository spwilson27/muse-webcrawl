Argon

Argon is a chemical element with the symbol Ar and atomic number 18. It is in group 18 of the periodic table and is a noble gas. Argon is the third-most abundant gas in Earth's atmosphere, at 0.934% by volume. It is colourless, odourless, and extremely unreactive under most conditions, because its outer electron shell is complete.

History

Argon was suspected to exist by Henry Cavendish in 1785, but it was not isolated until 1894, when Lord Rayleigh and Sir William Ramsay removed oxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, and water from air and characterised the inert residue. The name comes from the Greek word ἀργόν, meaning "inactive" or "lazy", referring to its lack of chemical reactivity. Rayleigh and Ramsay received Nobel Prizes for the discovery in 1904.

Properties

Argon is produced industrially by the fractional distillation of liquid air. It is monatomic, with a boiling point of −185.8 °C and a melting point of −189.3 °C. Because it is inert and denser than air, argon is widely used as a shielding gas in welding (such as TIG welding), as a protective atmosphere in metallurgy and semiconductor manufacturing, and as a filler gas in incandescent and fluorescent lighting. It is also used in double-glazed windows for insulation and in the preservation of historical documents.

Isotopes

The main isotopes of argon in Earth's atmosphere are argon-40 (99.6%), argon-36, and argon-38. Argon-40 is largely radiogenic, produced by the decay of potassium-40 in Earth's crust, which underlies the potassium-argon dating method used in geology.
