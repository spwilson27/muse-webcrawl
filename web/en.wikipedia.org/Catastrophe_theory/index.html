Catastrophe theory

Catastrophe theory is a branch of bifurcation theory in the study of dynamical systems; it is a special case of the more general singularity theory in geometry. It studies how small, smooth changes in the parameters of a system can cause sudden, large, qualitative changes in its behaviour.

Elementary catastrophes

The theory was developed chiefly by the French mathematician René Thom in the 1960s and popularised by Christopher Zeeman in the 1970s. Thom classified the elementary catastrophes — the fold, cusp, swallowtail, butterfly, and the hyperbolic, elliptic, and parabolic umbilics — which describe the ways equilibria of a potential function can appear or vanish as up to five control parameters vary.

Applications and criticism

Catastrophe theory has been applied to optics (caustics), structural buckling, phase transitions, and more speculatively to biology and the social sciences. The broader applications were criticised in the late 1970s for overreach, but the underlying mathematics remains a standard part of singularity and bifurcation theory.
