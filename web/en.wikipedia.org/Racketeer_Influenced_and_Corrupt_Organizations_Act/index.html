Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act

RICO Act
Long titleAn Act relating to the control of organized crime in the United States

The Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO) is a United States federal law, enacted in 1970 as Title IX of the Organized Crime Control Act, that provides for extended criminal penalties and a civil cause of action for acts performed as part of an ongoing criminal organization. It is codified at 18 U.S.C. §§ 1961–1968. The law was designed to allow leaders of criminal enterprises — in particular the American Mafia — to be tried for crimes they ordered, not only those they personally committed.

Elements of a RICO offense

Under § 1962, it is unlawful for any person employed by or associated with an "enterprise" engaged in or affecting interstate or foreign commerce to conduct or participate in the enterprise's affairs through a "pattern of racketeering activity." Racketeering activity is defined in § 1961 as a list of predicate offenses, including murder, kidnapping, gambling, arson, robbery, bribery, extortion, drug trafficking, mail and wire fraud, and securities fraud. A "pattern" requires at least two predicate acts within ten years that are related and amount to or threaten continued criminal activity.

Penalties and remedies

Criminal RICO violations carry up to 20 years' imprisonment (or life imprisonment where the predicate act permits), fines, and mandatory forfeiture of interests acquired through the racketeering. Section 1964(c) also gives private plaintiffs a civil cause of action, with treble damages and attorney's fees for persons injured in their business or property by RICO violations.

History and major cases

Drafted chiefly by Notre Dame law professor G. Robert Blakey — the name "RICO" is often said to be a nod to the gangster character Rico in the 1931 film Little Caesar — the statute was little used during its first decade. In the 1980s it became a central weapon against La Cosa Nostra: the Mafia Commission Trial (1985–86) convicted the heads of New York's Five Families, and Rudolph Giuliani's prosecution of the Commission is considered a landmark. RICO has since been applied to street gangs, corrupt police departments, sports organizations (including the FIFA corruption case), tobacco companies, and financial fraud schemes.

Civil RICO and criticism

The civil provisions have been used far beyond traditional organized crime, in ordinary commercial disputes, prompting criticism that the "racketeer" label stigmatizes defendants in what are essentially fraud cases. Courts have narrowed civil RICO through requirements such as a direct injury and, for certain predicates, a domestic injury in private securities cases.

State RICO laws

Many states have enacted "little RICO" statutes modeled on the federal law, sometimes with broader predicate lists or lower thresholds. Georgia's RICO Act, for example, has been used in high-profile gang and public-corruption prosecutions.
