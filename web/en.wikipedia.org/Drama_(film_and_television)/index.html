Drama (film and television)

In film and television, drama is a category or genre of narrative fiction (or semi-fiction) intended to be more serious than humorous in tone. The term derives from the Greek drama (δρᾶμα), meaning "action" or "deed", from the verb draō, "to do". Drama in this sense typically depends on in-depth development of realistic characters dealing with emotional themes such as loss, addiction, moral conflict, or social injustice.

All forms of cinema or television that involve fictional stories are forms of drama in the broader sense if their storytelling is achieved by means of actors who represent characters. In this broader sense, drama is a mode distinct from other genres such as comedy, tragedy and melodrama, though in practice "drama" most often labels works that are neither primarily comic nor sensational.

Subgenres

  • Crime drama and legal drama — centred on criminals, police, or lawyers and trials (e.g., Law & Order).
  • Historical drama — period settings and real events (e.g., Downton Abbey, Schindler's List).
  • Medical drama — set in hospitals (e.g., ER, Grey's Anatomy).
  • Melodrama — heightened emotion and moral polarisation; includes soap operas and telenovelas.
  • Teen drama and family drama — focusing on young people or domestic life.
  • Docudrama and biographical drama — dramatisations of real persons and events.

Television drama

Serialised television drama became a dominant cultural form in the late 20th and 21st centuries, with shows such as The Sopranos, The Wire, and Breaking Bad credited with a "Golden Age of Television" in the United States, while British, Korean (K-drama), and other national drama industries developed large international audiences through streaming.
