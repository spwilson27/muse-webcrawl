Acoustic stealth

Acoustic stealth is a set of techniques used to reduce the sound emitted or reflected by a vehicle or structure, making it harder to detect by sonar and other acoustic sensors. It is a central concern in the design of military submarines, whose survival depends on remaining quieter than the surrounding ocean, and is also applied to surface ships, aircraft, and machinery.

Techniques

Methods include isolating vibrating machinery on resilient mounts ("rafts"), using flexible couplings, and enclosing noisy equipment. Submarine hulls may be covered with anechoic tiles — rubber coatings that absorb active sonar pulses and damp internal noise. Propeller design is critical: skewed, many-bladed propellers and pump-jet propulsors delay the onset of cavitation, a major source of noise. Crews also practise "silent running," shutting down non-essential equipment and restricting movement.

Detection

Passive sonar listens for radiated noise with hydrophone arrays, while active sonar sends out pulses and listens for echoes. Reducing a vessel's acoustic signature narrows the range at which either can detect it. The pursuit of quieter submarines drove much Cold War undersea competition, including efforts to acquire precision machining technology for quieter propellers.
