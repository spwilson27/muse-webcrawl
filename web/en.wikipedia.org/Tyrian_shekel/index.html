Tyrian shekel

The Tyrian shekel was a silver coin issued by the ancient Phoenician city of Tyre from 126 BC until AD 19. It weighed about 14.2 grams and was struck in a very fine silver (over 94% pure), making it the most trusted currency in the ancient Levant.

History

Tyre began minting its own shekels after gaining independence from the Seleucid Empire in 126 BC. The coins featured the head of Melqart, the patron deity of Tyre, on the obverse and an eagle standing on the prow of a ship on the reverse, together with a Greek inscription giving the year of issue.

Religious significance

Because of its purity, the Tyrian shekel was the prescribed coin for paying the half-shekel temple tax at the Temple in Jerusalem; money changers in the Temple courts exchanged other currencies for it. Many scholars associate the "thirty pieces of silver" paid to Judas Iscariot in the New Testament with Tyrian shekels.

Decline

Production continued until Roman rule, when the coinage ceased around AD 19; later issues may have been struck by the Jerusalem authorities to keep the temple tax supplied.
