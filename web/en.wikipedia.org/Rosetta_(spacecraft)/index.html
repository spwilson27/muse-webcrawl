Rosetta (spacecraft)

Rosetta was a space probe built and launched by the European Space Agency (ESA) to perform the first rendezvous with, and long-term study of, a comet. On August 6, 2014, it arrived at comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko after a ten-year journey through the Solar System, becoming the first spacecraft to orbit a comet nucleus. On November 12, 2014, its lander Philae made the first soft landing on a comet.

Mission profile

Rosetta launched on an Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou on March 2, 2004. Because no launcher could send it directly to its target, the spacecraft used four gravity assists—three from Earth and one from Mars—and flew by the asteroids 2867 Šteins (2008) and 21 Lutetia (2010). It spent 957 days in deep-space hibernation before being successfully reactivated in January 2014.

The mission was named after the Rosetta Stone, the inscribed slab that enabled the decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs; scientists hoped the probe would similarly unlock the secrets of the early Solar System, since comets are primitive leftovers from its formation 4.6 billion years ago. The lander was named for Philae, the island in the Nile where an obelisk used in decipherment was found.

Findings

Rosetta revealed 67P to be a dark, porous, rubber-duck-shaped body of two joined lobes, with cliffs, pits and dunes. Its instruments found that the comet's water has a deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio quite different from Earth's oceans—arguing against Jupiter-family comets as the main source of Earth's water—and detected molecular oxygen and the amino acid glycine in the coma. Philae, which bounced twice before coming to rest in shadow, operated for about 57 hours on battery power; it was finally located in images in September 2016.

End of mission

As the comet receded from the Sun and power dwindled, ESA ended the mission by deliberately crash-landing Rosetta onto the comet's surface on September 30, 2016, gathering unique close-up data on the way down.
