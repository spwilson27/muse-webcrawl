Cerebral cortex

The cerebral cortex, also known as the cerebral mantle, is the outer layer of neural tissue of the cerebrum of the brain in humans and other mammals. It is the largest site of neural integration in the central nervous system and plays a key role in attention, perception, awareness, thought, memory, language, and consciousness. The cerebral cortex consists of the two cerebral hemispheres and their cortices (outer layers of grey matter), plus the underlying subcortical white matter.

Structure

In humans the cortex is a sheet of neural tissue 2 to 4 millimetres thick, folded into gyri and sulci to fit within the skull; its surface area is roughly 2,000 square centimetres. It is composed of six horizontal layers of neurons in the neocortex, which constitutes about 90% of the cortex. The cortex is divided into four lobes — frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital — and contains an estimated 14–16 billion neurons in humans, interconnected by an immense network of synapses.

Functions

Different cortical areas serve different functions: the primary motor cortex controls voluntary movement; primary sensory areas receive touch, vision, hearing, taste and smell; and association areas integrate information for higher cognition. Language is typically lateralised to the left hemisphere in most people, involving Broca's area and Wernicke's area. The prefrontal cortex supports planning, decision-making and personality.

Development and evolution

The cortex develops from the embryonic telencephalon, with neurons migrating outward to form its layers. Cortical expansion and folding are greatly increased in primates, and especially in humans, relative to other mammals. Damage to cortical areas — from stroke, trauma or neurodegenerative disease — produces characteristic deficits in movement, sensation, language or cognition.
