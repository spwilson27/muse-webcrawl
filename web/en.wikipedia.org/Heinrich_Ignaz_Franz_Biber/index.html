Heinrich Ignaz Franz Biber

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Heinrich Ignaz Franz Biber von Biberburg (baptised 12 August 1644 – 3 May 1704) was a Bohemian-Austrian composer and violinist. Generally regarded as one of the most important composers for the violin in the history of the instrument, he served most of his career at the court of the Prince-Archbishop of Salzburg. His works include operas, sacred music, ensemble sonatas, and solo violin music famous for its use of scordatura — deliberate mistuning of the strings.

Life

Biber was born in Wartenberg in Bohemia (now Stráž pod Ralskem, Czech Republic). He worked for Prince Johann Seyfried von Eggenberg in Graz and then for Karl II von Liechtenstein-Kastelkorn, Bishop of Olomouc, at Kroměříž. In about 1670 he travelled to Salzburg — reportedly to buy instruments from the luthier Jacob Stainer — and, without returning, entered the service of Archbishop Maximilian Gandolph von Kuenburg, an act of desertion his former employer never forgave. In Salzburg he rose to deputy Kapellmeister in 1679 and Kapellmeister in 1684, and in 1692 he was ennobled by Emperor Leopold I, taking the name "von Biberburg". He died in Salzburg in 1704.

Music

Biber's best-known works are his violin compositions. The Rosary Sonatas (also called the Mystery Sonatas, c. 1676) are a cycle of fifteen sonatas for violin and continuo, each depicting one of the Mysteries of the Rosary, plus a concluding passacaglia for solo violin — one of the earliest major works for unaccompanied violin. Almost every sonata uses a different scordatura tuning, producing unusual resonances and, in the "Resurrection" sonata, even requiring the middle strings to be crossed. His eight Sonatae violino solo of 1681 and the virtuosic Harmonia artificioso-ariosa (1696) consolidated his fame.

His programme music includes the Battalia (1673), a vivid depiction of a battle in which the strings imitate drums, cannon, and drunken soldiers singing in several keys at once, and sonatas imitating animals such as the nightingale, cuckoo, frog, hen, and cat. His sacred output includes large-scale polychoral works for Salzburg Cathedral, among them the 53-part Missa Salisburgensis (long attributed to Orazio Benevoli but now credited to Biber).

Legacy

Biber's violin technique — extensive double-stopping, high positions, and scordatura — influenced the south German and Austrian violin schools and composers such as Johann Jakob Walther and Johann Paul von Westhoff. Interest in his music revived strongly with the early-music movement of the late 20th century.
