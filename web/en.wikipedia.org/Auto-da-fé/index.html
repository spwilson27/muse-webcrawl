Auto-da-fé

An auto-da-fé, the public ceremony at which the Inquisition announced its sentences.

An auto-da-fé (from Portuguese auto da fé and Spanish auto de fe, "act of faith") was the ritual of public penance, carried out between the 15th and 19th centuries, of condemned heretics and apostates imposed by the Spanish, Portuguese or Mexican Inquisitions. It was the most solemn stage of the inquisitorial process: a public ceremony in which the sentences of the accused were read out, reconciliation or penance was imposed, and those condemned to death were handed over ("relaxed") to the secular authorities for execution, usually burning at the stake outside the ceremony itself.

The ceremony

Autos were staged with great elaboration in main squares or esplanades, often on feast days and attended by royalty, clergy and crowds. A procession of penitents, wearing the penitential sanbenito garment — sometimes painted with flames for those impenitent or relapsed — processed before officials of the Inquisition, followed by a Mass, a sermon, the public reading of each case and sentence, and acts of abjuration. Those who reconciled were assigned penances ranging from fines and confiscation to imprisonment and the galleys. Effigies of the dead or fugitive condemned could be burned in their place.

History

The first Spanish auto-da-fé was held in Seville in 1481; the last in Spain took place in the early 19th century, the Inquisition being definitively abolished in 1834. The Portuguese Inquisition held autos in Lisbon, Évora, Coimbra and Goa. Autos also occurred in Spanish America, notably in Mexico City. In modern usage, auto-da-fé has come by extension to mean the burning of books or heretics, though strictly it denotes the ceremony of judgment itself.
