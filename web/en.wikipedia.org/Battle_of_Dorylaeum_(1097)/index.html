Battle of Dorylaeum (1097)

Date1 July 1097
LocationDorylaeum (near modern Eskişehir), Anatolia
ResultCrusader victory
BelligerentsCrusaders (First Crusade) vs. Sultanate of Rûm
CommandersBohemond of Taranto, Godfrey of Bouillon, Raymond IV of Toulouse; Kilij Arslan I

The Battle of Dorylaeum took place during the First Crusade on 1 July 1097, between the Crusaders and the Seljuk Turks of the Sultanate of Rûm, near the ruined Byzantine city of Dorylaeum in Anatolia. Coming shortly after the successful siege of Nicaea, it was the first major open-field test of the Crusader army in Asia Minor and ended in a decisive Crusader victory that opened the road across Anatolia toward Antioch.

The battle

Advancing in two widely separated columns, the Crusaders exposed the vanguard under Bohemond of Taranto — with Tancred, Robert of Normandy, and perhaps 10,000–20,000 men including noncombatants — to attack near Dorylaeum by Kilij Arslan I, whose army included his own Turks and allies of the Danishmend emir. The Seljuk horse archers surrounded Bohemond's camp by the river, poured arrows into it, and cut down many infantry and camp followers. Bohemond's knights made repeated charges but could not break the mobile Turkish encirclement, and the vanguard held a desperate defensive position for hours.

Around midday the second Crusader column — Godfrey of Bouillon, Raymond IV of Toulouse, Hugh of Vermandois, and the papal legate Adhemar of Le Puy — arrived on the field. Bishop Adhemar led a force around the Turkish flank and fell on their rear, capturing the sultan's camp. The combined Crusader charge then routed the Seljuks, who abandoned their baggage and fled.

Aftermath

Kilij Arslan adopted scorched-earth tactics thereafter, devastating the countryside along the Crusader route, but he could not again risk battle in force. The victory at Dorylaeum demonstrated the devastating effect of the Crusader heavy cavalry charge against Turkish tactics in pitched battle, and the Crusade marched on across the Anatolian plateau toward Heraclea and Antioch, suffering greatly from heat and thirst but largely unopposed in the field.
