Scientific method

The scientific method is an empirical method for acquiring knowledge that has characterised the development of science since at least the 17th century. It involves careful observation, rigorous scepticism about what is observed, the formulation of hypotheses, testing them by experiment and measurement, and refining or rejecting hypotheses in the light of the results. Although procedures vary from one field of inquiry to another, the process is commonly described as an iterative cycle.

The process

A typical characterisation of the method comprises the following elements:

  • Observation and description of a phenomenon or problem.
  • Formulation of a hypothesis — a proposed explanation that can, in principle, be tested.
  • Prediction — deducing consequences that should follow if the hypothesis is true.
  • Experimentation and measurement — testing the predictions under controlled conditions, ideally with repeatable procedures.
  • Analysis — comparing results with predictions and drawing conclusions; results are published so that other researchers can replicate, criticise and extend them through peer review.

Because each step is iterated, the method is better pictured as a self-correcting loop than as a one-way recipe.

History

Elements of empirical inquiry appear in Aristotle and reached a sophisticated form in the work of the medieval scholar Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen), who emphasised experiment in his Book of Optics. In the 17th century Francis Bacon promoted inductive reasoning, Galileo combined experiment with mathematics, and in the 19th and 20th centuries philosophers of science including Charles Sanders Peirce, Karl Popper (who stressed falsifiability) and Thomas Kuhn (who described "paradigm shifts") debated how science actually progresses.

Key concepts

Central notions include empirical evidence, reproducibility, controlled comparison, statistical inference, and the distinction between a hypothesis, a law and a theory — in scientific usage a theory is a well-substantiated explanatory framework, not a mere guess. Science is also a social enterprise: consensus, replication studies and retraction mechanisms all form part of how scientific claims are validated.
