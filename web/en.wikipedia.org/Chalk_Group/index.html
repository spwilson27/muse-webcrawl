Chalk Group
TypeGeological group
AgeLate Cretaceous (Cenomanian–Maastrichtian)
LithologyChalk limestone, marl, flint
RegionEngland and north-west Europe

The Chalk Group (often just called the Chalk) is a lithostratigraphic unit made up of the pure white limestones of the Upper Cretaceous of England and equivalent strata across north-west Europe. It forms prominent landscapes such as the White Cliffs of Dover, the North and South Downs, and Salisbury Plain.

Deposition

The chalk was deposited roughly 100 to 66 million years ago in a warm epicontinental sea that covered much of Europe, from the accumulated calcareous skeletons of coccolithophores raining down onto the sea floor. Bands of dark flint nodules, formed from dissolved silica, occur throughout the sequence.

Subdivisions

The group is divided into the Grey Chalk Subgroup below and the White Chalk Subgroup above, each comprising several formations distinguished by their marl content, flint development, and fossil assemblages. The sequence reaches more than 500 metres in thickness in parts of the English Channel basin.

Economic importance

Chalk is quarried for cement manufacture and agricultural lime, forms a major aquifer supplying water to south-east England, and its fossils — including ammonites, echinoids, and sponges — have been collected since the earliest days of geology.
