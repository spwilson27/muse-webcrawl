Ojibwe (Anishinaabemowin) is an Indigenous language of North America belonging to the Algonquian language family. It is spoken by the Ojibwe (Chippewa) people in the region surrounding the Great Lakes, in the U.S. states of Michigan, Wisconsin, Minnesota, and North Dakota, and in the Canadian provinces of Ontario, Manitoba, and Saskatchewan. It is the second-most widely spoken Indigenous language in Canada and one of the most widely spoken in North America north of Mexico.

Dialects

Ojibwe forms a dialect continuum including Southwestern Ojibwe (Chippewa), Northwestern Ojibwe, Eastern Ojibwe, Ottawa (Odawa), Algonquin, and Oji-Cree (Severn Ojibwe). Some varieties, such as Ottawa, show extensive syncope of unstressed vowels. Neighbouring Potawatomi is closely related and is sometimes grouped with Ojibwe.

Grammar

Ojibwe is a polysynthetic, agglutinative language with complex verb morphology; a single verb can express what requires a full sentence in English. Nouns are classified as animate or inanimate, a distinction that runs through agreement and plural marking. The language distinguishes proximate and obviative third persons, direct and inverse verb forms, and inclusive versus exclusive first-person plural ("we including you" vs. "we excluding you"). Word order is relatively free, with verb-initial orders common.

Writing and revitalisation

The most common Latin orthography is the double-vowel system developed by Charles Fiero, which writes long vowels by doubling. In northern Ontario and Manitoba, many communities use Canadian Aboriginal syllabics, originally adapted from the Cree syllabary. Like many Indigenous languages, Ojibwe lost speakers through residential and boarding schools, but immersion schools, master-apprentice programmes, and university courses in Minnesota, Wisconsin, and Ontario are training new speakers.
