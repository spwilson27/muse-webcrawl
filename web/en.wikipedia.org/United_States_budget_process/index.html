The United States budget process is the framework by which the U.S. federal government formulates and enacts its annual budget. Under the Constitution, Congress holds the "power of the purse": no money may be drawn from the Treasury except by appropriation made by law. The modern process is governed chiefly by the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974. The federal fiscal year runs from 1 October to 30 September.

President's budget

The process begins with federal agencies submitting funding requests to the Office of Management and Budget (OMB), which prepares the President's budget proposal. By law, the President submits the budget to Congress on or before the first Monday in February. The budget is a comprehensive plan covering discretionary spending (subject to annual appropriations) and mandatory spending (such as Social Security, Medicare, and interest on the debt, which is governed by permanent law and constitutes the majority of outlays).

Budget resolution

The House and Senate Budget Committees hold hearings, drawing on analysis from the nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office (CBO), and each chamber passes a concurrent budget resolution setting aggregate levels of revenue, spending, and deficits, and allocations to committees. A conference committee reconciles the two versions. The budget resolution is not a law and is not signed by the President; it may include reconciliation instructions directing committees to change mandatory spending or revenue law, which are later combined into a reconciliation bill that cannot be filibustered in the Senate.

Appropriations

Each chamber's Appropriations Committee divides the discretionary allocation among its 12 subcommittees, which draft the 12 annual appropriations bills covering areas from Defense to Agriculture to Transportation–HUD. The bills must be passed by both chambers and signed by the President before the fiscal year begins on 1 October. In practice, Congress frequently misses the deadline, funding the government through continuing resolutions that maintain prior-year levels; if funding lapses entirely, a government shutdown occurs, as in 1995–96, 2013, and the 35-day shutdown of 2018–19.

Execution and oversight

After enactment, OMB apportions funds to agencies, which obligate and spend them under the Antideficiency Act, which prohibits spending in excess of appropriations. The Impoundment Control Act limits the President's ability to withhold appropriated funds, permitting deferrals and proposed rescissions subject to congressional approval. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) audits federal spending.

History

Before 1921, agencies submitted requests directly to Congress. The Budget and Accounting Act of 1921 created the Bureau of the Budget (now OMB) and required a unified presidential budget. The 1974 Act, passed amid conflict with President Nixon over impoundments, created the Budget Committees and the CBO and established the contemporary timetable.
