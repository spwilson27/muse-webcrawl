Scientific nameMeleagris ocellata
FamilyPhasianidae
RangeYucatán Peninsula (Mexico, Belize, northern Guatemala)

The ocellated turkey (Meleagris ocellata) is a species of turkey native to the Yucatán Peninsula of Mexico, Belize and Guatemala. It is a relative of the more widespread wild turkey (Meleagris gallopavo) of North America, but is smaller and far more brightly coloured.

Description

Both sexes have iridescent bronze-green body plumage. The tail feathers are bluish-grey tipped with distinctive eye-spots (ocelli) of blue and bronze, which give the species its name and resemble the eyespots of a peacock's train. The bare skin of the head is blue, with orange-red nodules that become more pronounced in breeding males; males also develop a fleshy blue crown and produce a gobbling call quite unlike that of the wild turkey, beginning with low drumming notes. Hens lay eight to fifteen eggs in a ground nest.

Habitat and behaviour

Ocellated turkeys inhabit tropical forests, forest edges and clearings, feeding on seeds, nuts, leaves, berries and insects. They roost in trees at night and are generally shy. The species was significant in Maya culture, and its remains and depictions occur at Maya sites such as Tikal, within whose national park in Guatemala the birds are now a conspicuous attraction.

Conservation

The ocellated turkey is listed as Near Threatened by the IUCN. Populations have declined because of heavy subsistence and sport hunting and the clearing of forest for agriculture. It persists in protected areas including Tikal National Park (Guatemala) and reserves in Belize and Mexico.
