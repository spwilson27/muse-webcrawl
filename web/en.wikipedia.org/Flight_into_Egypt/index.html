Flight into Egypt

The flight into Egypt is a story recounted in the Gospel of Matthew (Matthew 2:13–23) and in New Testament apocrypha. Soon after the visit by the Magi, an angel appeared to Joseph in a dream telling him to flee to Egypt with Mary and the infant Jesus, since King Herod would seek the child to kill him. The episode is a prominent theme in Christian art.

Biblical account

According to Matthew, after the Magi departed without reporting back to Herod, an angel warned Joseph: "Rise, take the child and his mother, and flee to Egypt, and remain there until I tell you, for Herod is about to search for the child, to destroy him." The family departed by night and remained in Egypt until Herod's death, when they returned and settled in Nazareth. Matthew presents the journey as fulfilling the prophecy of Hosea: "Out of Egypt I called my son."

Apocryphal traditions

Later infancy gospels expanded the story with miracles along the way: palm trees bending to give Mary fruit, idols falling as the child entered Egyptian temples, and encounters with the two thieves who would later be crucified beside Jesus. Coptic tradition preserves an extended itinerary of the Holy Family's journey through Egypt, and many churches and monasteries claim sites associated with their stay.

In art

The flight into Egypt was a favourite subject of Renaissance and Baroque painters, including Giotto, Rembrandt, Nicolas Poussin, and Adam Elsheimer, whose Flight into Egypt (1609) is noted for its naturalistic night sky. The related scene of the Rest on the Flight into Egypt depicts the family pausing during the journey.
