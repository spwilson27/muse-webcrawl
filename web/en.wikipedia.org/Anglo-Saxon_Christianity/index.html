Anglo-Saxon Christianity

Periodc. 597 – 1066
Key eventsGregorian mission (597); Synod of Whitby (664)
Key figuresAugustine of Canterbury; Bede; Theodore of Tarsus; Boniface
RegionAnglo-Saxon England

Anglo-Saxon Christianity is the form of Christianity practised in England between about 600 and the Norman Conquest of 1066, during which the pagan Anglo-Saxon kingdoms were converted and a distinctive English church culture flourished.

Conversion

In 597 Pope Gregory the Great sent Augustine of Canterbury to convert the kingdom of Kent; King Æthelberht accepted baptism and Canterbury became the seat of the archbishopric. Irish missionaries from Iona, such as Aidan of Lindisfarne, evangelized Northumbria, creating tensions over the dating of Easter and other customs resolved in Rome's favour at the Synod of Whitby in 664. Archbishop Theodore of Tarsus organized the English church into dioceses.

Culture and missions

Anglo-Saxon monasteries such as Wearmouth-Jarrow, where the Venerable Bede wrote his Ecclesiastical History, became centres of learning and art, producing illuminated manuscripts and stone sculpture. English missionaries, notably Boniface, carried Christianity to Frisia and Germany in the eighth century. The church was disrupted by Viking invasions, revived in the tenth-century Benedictine reform, and remained central to English life until the Conquest.
