Mammillary body

The mammillary bodies are a pair of small round structures on the underside of the brain, forming part of the posterior hypothalamus. Their name comes from the Latin mammilla ("little breast"), referring to their shape. Each body contains two groups of nuclei, the medial and lateral mammillary nuclei.

Connections and function

The mammillary bodies are an important relay in the limbic circuitry of the brain, classically described as part of the Papez circuit. They receive their main input from the hippocampal formation (especially the subiculum) via the fornix, and project to the anterior thalamic nuclei via the mammillothalamic tract, and to the midbrain tegmentum via the mammillotegmental tract. They are involved in spatial and episodic memory.

Clinical significance

The mammillary bodies are damaged in Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome, caused by thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, often associated with chronic alcoholism; atrophy of the mammillary bodies is a characteristic finding and correlates with the severe memory deficits of the syndrome. Damage can also arise from tumours, infarction or trauma.
