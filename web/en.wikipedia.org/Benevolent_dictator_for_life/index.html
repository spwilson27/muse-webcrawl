Benevolent dictator for life

Benevolent dictator for life (BDFL) is a title — usually jocular — given to a small number of open-source software development leaders, typically project founders who retain the final say in disputes or arguments within the community. The phrase originated in 1995 with reference to Guido van Rossum, founder and long-time leader of the Python programming language, and was popularised by Eric S. Raymond's essay on open-source governance. BDFLs typically retain authority because of the community's trust in their judgement rather than by formal rule, and in principle dissatisfied users may fork the project.

Well-known BDFLs

Notable holders of the role include Linus Torvalds (Linux kernel), Larry Wall (Perl), Yukihiro Matsumoto (Ruby), Dries Buytaert (Drupal), and David Heinemeier Hansson (Ruby on Rails). Van Rossum stepped down as Python's BDFL in 2018 after the contentious adoption of PEP 572 (the "walrus operator"), and the project subsequently adopted an elected steering council model of governance.

Variants and criticism

Some communities have adopted variations, such as Ubuntu's self-described "self-appointed benevolent dictator for life" (Mark Shuttleworth's SABDFL title). Critics note that the model depends heavily on the founder's competence and temperament, creates succession risks, and may discourage contribution; defenders reply that it gives projects coherent direction and a fast way to resolve disputes, with forking acting as the ultimate check on abuse.

Succession

Several projects have transitioned away from the model: Python to a steering council, and others to foundations or elected technical committees, reflecting the maturation of open-source governance.
