Irregular verb

An irregular verb is a verb whose conjugation does not follow the typical patterns of the language to which it belongs. In English, regular verbs form their past tense and past participle by adding -ed, whereas roughly 200 irregular verbs use other means, such as vowel changes (sing–sang–sung), no change at all (cut–cut–cut), or wholly different forms (go–went–gone, be–was–been).

Origins

Most English irregular verbs descend from the Old English strong verb system, in which seven classes of verbs formed tenses by systematic vowel gradation (ablaut). Over centuries many strong verbs have been regularised, but the most frequent verbs — which resist change — remain irregular in most languages; the verbs meaning "to be" are typically the most irregular of all, often being suppletive.

In other languages

Languages differ greatly in how much irregularity they tolerate. Highly inflected languages such as Spanish and German have large classes of stem-changing verbs, while some languages, such as Turkish, have almost entirely regular verb systems. See also irrealis mood.
