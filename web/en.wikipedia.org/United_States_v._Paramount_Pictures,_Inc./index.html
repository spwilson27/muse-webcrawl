United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc., 334 U.S. 131 (1948), commonly known as the Paramount Decree case or the Hollywood Antitrust Case of 1948, is a landmark United States Supreme Court decision that reshaped the American film industry. The Court held that the major Hollywood studios' control over production, distribution and exhibition violated the Sherman Antitrust Act, and the resulting consent decrees forced the studios to divest their theatre chains.

Background

During Hollywood's Golden Age, the "Big Five" studios — Paramount, MGM (through Loew's), Warner Bros., 20th Century Fox and RKO — owned large chains of theatres and used practices such as block booking (requiring exhibitors to license films in bundles) and blind bidding to dominate the market. The Justice Department first sued in 1938; after a 1940 consent decree expired without satisfying the government, the case proceeded to trial.

Decision

Writing for the Court, Justice William O. Douglas held that the studios' system of vertical integration and restrictive licensing practices were unlawful restraints of trade. The Court affirmed findings that practices including block booking, price fixing of admissions, and discriminatory clearances between affiliated and independent theatres violated the Sherman Act, and it required divorcement of exhibition from production and distribution.

Impact

The Paramount Decrees are widely credited with ending the classical studio system. Deprived of guaranteed outlets for their films, the studios cut production, the star contract system collapsed, and independent producers and exhibitors gained new opportunities. The decrees, administered for decades by the Justice Department, were formally terminated in 2020 after the Department concluded that changes in the industry had made them obsolete.
