Transit-oriented development

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In urban planning, transit-oriented development (TOD) is a type of urban development that maximises the amount of residential, business and leisure space within walking distance of public transport. It promotes a symbiotic relationship between dense, compact urban form and public transport use. A TOD typically includes a mix of housing, offices, retail and amenities integrated into a walkable neighbourhood located within roughly 400–800 metres (a half-mile) of a transit station.

Principles

The concept was popularised by architect and urban designer Peter Calthorpe in the early 1990s. Core principles include higher-density housing near stations, mixed land uses, pedestrian-friendly street design, reduced and managed parking, and high-quality public transport service. Agencies such as Transport for London apply accessibility measures (such as PTAL ratings) to guide density decisions.

Examples

Notable examples include Curitiba, Brazil, where bus rapid transit corridors structure the city's growth; Hong Kong's "rail plus property" model; Copenhagen's Finger Plan, which channels development along suburban rail lines; and Arlington County, Virginia's Rosslyn–Ballston corridor along the Washington Metro.

Benefits and criticism

Proponents argue TOD reduces car dependence, traffic congestion, air pollution and infrastructure costs, and can preserve open land. Critics point to risks of gentrification and displacement near stations, arguing that affordability requirements and tenant protections must accompany upzoning for benefits to be broadly shared.
