Swiss cuisine

Swiss cuisine comprises the cooking traditions of Switzerland, shaped by the country's Alpine geography and by its four language regions — German, French, Italian, and Romansh — each drawing on the neighbouring cuisines of Germany, France, and Italy. Dairy products, cheese, potatoes, and cured meats are central, and Switzerland is internationally famous for its chocolate.

Cheese dishes

Switzerland produces hundreds of cheese varieties, including Gruyère, Emmental, Appenzeller, and Tête de Moine. The best-known cheese dishes are fondue — melted cheese with white wine eaten communally by dipping bread, said to have been promoted in the 1930s by the Swiss cheese union — and raclette, in which a half wheel of cheese is heated and scraped over potatoes and pickles. Rösti, a fried grated-potato cake originally a Bernese farmers' breakfast, is often treated as the national dish of German-speaking Switzerland, and the cultural-political divide between language regions is jokingly called the Röstigraben ("rösti ditch").

Regional dishes

Other notable dishes include Zürcher Geschnetzeltes (sliced veal in cream sauce, from Zurich), Älplermagronen (an Alpine macaroni with potatoes, cheese, cream, and applesauce), Capuns and Maluns from Graubünden, polenta and risotto in Ticino, and leckerli honey-spice biscuits from Basel. Breakfast of muesli — invented around 1900 by the Swiss physician Maximilian Bircher-Benner — has spread worldwide from Switzerland.

Chocolate

Swiss chocolatiers pioneered milk chocolate (Daniel Peter, 1875) and the conching process (Rodolphe Lindt, 1879), and Switzerland has among the highest per-capita chocolate consumption in the world. Famous firms include Lindt, Nestlé, and Tobler, whose Toblerone bar (1908) is shaped, by legend, after the Matterhorn.
