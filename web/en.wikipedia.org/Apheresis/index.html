Apheresis

Apheresis (from Greek aphairesis, "a taking away") is a medical procedure in which blood is drawn from a donor or patient, one component is separated and retained, and the remainder is returned to the circulation. Separation is usually by centrifugation or membrane filtration.

Types

Named forms include plasmapheresis (plasma), plateletpheresis (platelets), leukapheresis (white blood cells), erythrocytapheresis (red blood cells), and LDL apheresis (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol).

Uses

In blood donation, apheresis allows collection of specific components — most commonly platelets or plasma — from a single donor. Therapeutically, plasma exchange is used in autoimmune and neurological conditions such as myasthenia gravis, Guillain–Barré syndrome, and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura; leukapheresis can reduce dangerously high white-cell counts, and peripheral blood stem cells are collected by apheresis for transplantation.

Procedure

Blood flows from a venous access into a separator, where it is mixed with an anticoagulant such as citrate. The desired component is collected in a bag and the rest returned, either continuously or in cycles. Common side effects include tingling from citrate-induced hypocalcaemia and light-headedness.
