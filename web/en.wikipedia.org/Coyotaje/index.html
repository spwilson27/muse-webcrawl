Coyotaje

Coyotaje is the Spanish-language term for the practice of human smuggling across the Mexico–United States border, carried out by guides known as coyotes (also called polleros). It refers to the paid service of guiding undocumented migrants through border crossings, across deserts and rivers, and onward to destinations in the United States. Fees, once a few hundred dollars, have risen over the decades to many thousands of dollars per person, reflecting tighter border enforcement.

History

Guides have assisted cross-border movement since early 20th-century labour migration, and the trade expanded with the Bracero Program era (1942–1964) and after its end. From the 1990s, United States enforcement operations concentrated patrols at urban crossing points, pushing migrants — and their coyotes — into remote and dangerous terrain such as the Sonoran Desert, a policy sometimes described as "prevention through deterrence." Smuggling networks grew more professional and increasingly intertwined with drug-trafficking organizations, which in many sectors tax or control the routes.

Risks and controversies

Migrants relying on coyotes face risks of abandonment, extortion, kidnapping, sexual violence, and death from exposure; thousands of remains have been recovered in borderlands counties. Abandoned loads and stash houses are recurring features of enforcement reporting. Defenders of migrants argue that restrictive migration policies create the market for coyotaje, while officials frame the trade as organized crime exploiting vulnerable people.

In culture

The coyote is a familiar figure in border ballads (corridos), literature, and film about migration, portrayed variously as folk hero, swindler, or predator. The term also has broader Spanish slang uses for a fixer or intermediary who expedites bureaucracy for a fee.
