Piezoelectricity

Piezoelectricity is the electric charge that accumulates in certain solid materials—such as crystals, particular ceramics, and biological matter like bone and DNA—in response to applied mechanical stress. The word piezoelectricity means electricity resulting from pressure and latent heat, from the Greek piezein, "to squeeze or press". Conversely, these materials deform when an electric field is applied (the inverse piezoelectric effect).

History

The direct piezoelectric effect was discovered in 1880 by French physicists Jacques and Pierre Curie, working with crystals including quartz, tourmaline, and Rochelle salt. The converse effect was predicted mathematically by Gabriel Lippmann in 1881 and confirmed by the Curies. The first major application was sonar during the First World War, pioneered by Paul Langevin using quartz transducers.

Materials and mechanism

Piezoelectricity arises in crystals lacking a center of symmetry: mechanical strain displaces ions, producing a net polarization. Quartz is a natural piezoelectric crystal; widely used manufactured materials include lead zirconate titanate (PZT) ceramics and the polymer polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF).

Applications

Applications include ultrasonic transducers and medical imaging, sonar, quartz crystal oscillators that keep time in watches and electronics, sensors and accelerometers, actuators and precision positioning, gas lighters and cigarette lighters (spark generation), loudspeakers and microphones, inkjet printer heads, and energy harvesting devices.
