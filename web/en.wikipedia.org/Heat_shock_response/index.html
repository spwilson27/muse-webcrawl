Heat shock response

FieldCell biology, molecular biology
Key regulatorsHeat shock factor 1 (HSF1)
Key productsHeat shock proteins (Hsp70, Hsp90, small Hsps)
Discovered1962, in Drosophila (Ferruccio Ritossa)

The heat shock response (HSR) is a cell stress response that increases the number of molecular chaperones to combat the negative effects of denatured proteins caused by stressors such as increased temperature, oxidative stress and heavy metals. It is an ancient and highly conserved protective mechanism found in virtually all organisms, from bacteria to humans.

Mechanism

In a normal cell, heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) is kept inactive in a complex with chaperones such as Hsp70 and Hsp90. When stress causes proteins to unfold, the chaperones are recruited to the damaged proteins, freeing HSF1. HSF1 trimerises, enters the nucleus and binds heat shock elements in the promoters of heat shock genes, driving rapid transcription of heat shock proteins. These chaperones then refold damaged proteins or target them for degradation, restoring proteostasis.

Discovery

The response was first observed in 1962 by Ferruccio Ritossa, who noticed new patterns of chromosome "puffing" in the salivary glands of fruit flies exposed to elevated temperature — the first known example of transcriptional regulation by environmental stress.

Significance

Beyond heat, the response is triggered by infection, inflammation, toxins and exercise. It declines with age, and its impairment is implicated in neurodegenerative diseases, while its overactivation in cancer cells is a target of drug development.
