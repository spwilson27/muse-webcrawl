Treebank

In linguistics, a treebank is a parsed text corpus in which each sentence is annotated with syntactic structure. The syntactic structure is commonly represented as a tree structure, hence the name. Treebanks are often created on top of a text corpus that has already been annotated with part-of-speech tags. In turn, treebanks are sometimes enhanced with semantic or other linguistic information. Treebanks can be created completely manually, or semi-automatically, where a parser assigns a structure which is then checked by linguists.

Uses

Treebanks are a central resource in computational linguistics and natural language processing. They are used to train and evaluate statistical parsers, to induce probabilistic grammars, and to investigate linguistic hypotheses quantitatively against large amounts of attested language.

Notable treebanks

The best-known treebank is the Penn Treebank, developed at the University of Pennsylvania, which includes over 4.5 million words of American English annotated with part-of-speech tags and skeletal syntactic parses, drawn mainly from the Wall Street Journal. Other significant resources include the Prague Dependency Treebank for Czech, the TIGER corpus for German, and the Universal Dependencies project, which provides consistently annotated treebanks for well over one hundred languages using dependency grammar.

Annotation schemes

Treebanks differ in whether they represent syntax with phrase-structure (constituency) trees or dependency trees, in the tag sets they use, and in how they handle phenomena such as empty categories, coordination, and discontinuous constituents. These choices strongly affect the parsers trained on them and the comparability of results across corpora.
