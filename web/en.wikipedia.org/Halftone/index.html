Halftone

Halftone is the reprographic technique that simulates continuous-tone imagery through the use of dots, varying either in size or in spacing, thus generating a gradient-like effect. It allows photographs and other tonal images to be printed with a single colour of ink: where the image is dark, the dots are large and merge together; where it is light, they are small and sparse. Viewed from a normal distance, the eye integrates the dots into smooth tones.

History

Photographic halftone processes were developed in the late 19th century; William Henry Fox Talbot patented an early screen concept in 1852, and practical commercial processes by Frederic Ives, Georg Meisenbach and others in the 1880s enabled newspapers and books to reproduce photographs for the first time. The first halftone photograph in a newspaper is generally credited to the New York Daily Graphic in 1880.

Process

In traditional halftone, a photograph is re-photographed through a glass screen ruled with a fine grid, producing a plate of dots. Screen ruling is measured in lines per inch (lpi): newspapers typically use 65–85 lpi, magazines 133–150 lpi, and fine art printing 175 lpi or more. For colour printing, separate halftone plates for cyan, magenta, yellow and black are printed at slightly different screen angles to minimise moiré patterns; misalignment produces the familiar rosette pattern visible under magnification.

Digital halftoning

Digital imaging replaced screens with algorithms: ordered dithering, error diffusion (such as Floyd–Steinberg) and frequency-modulated (stochastic) screening, in which dots are of fixed small size and their density varies. Digital halftoning is used by laser and inkjet printers to render greyscale and colour with a limited number of ink levels.
