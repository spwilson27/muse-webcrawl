Occupation of Alcatraz

Alcatraz Island, occupied by Native American activists from 1969 to 1971

The Occupation of Alcatraz (November 20, 1969 – June 11, 1971) was a 19-month-long protest when 89 Native Americans and their supporters occupied Alcatraz Island. The protest was led by Richard Oakes, LaNada War Jack, and others, while John Trudell served as spokesman. The group lived on the island together with thousands of Native American supporters, and the protest received national media attention.

The occupants called themselves the "Indians of All Tribes" (IOAT), a reference to the many different Native American tribes and nations who supported the protest. They claimed the island by right of discovery under the terms of the 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie, which granted Native Americans the right to unused federal land. Alcatraz had been closed as a federal prison in 1963 and declared surplus property in 1964, five years before the occupation began.

Background

Alcatraz was considered surplus federal land after the closure of Alcatraz Federal Penitentiary. In San Francisco, Native American students and activists began organizing around reclaiming the island. A brief occupation attempt occurred on March 8, 1964, when a group of Lakota Sioux landed on the island and stayed for a few hours. The 1969 occupation grew out of this earlier action and out of frustration with federal Indian policy, including termination policy, which sought to end the special status of tribes.

The occupation

The occupiers issued the sardonic "Alcatraz Proclamation," offering to purchase the island for $24 in glass beads and red cloth, a reference to the legendary purchase of Manhattan. They proposed turning the island into a Native American cultural and educational center. The federal government refused their demands but initially avoided a forced removal. Conditions on the island deteriorated over time: Oakes left after his stepdaughter died in a fall in January 1970, utilities and water were cut off, and a fire in June 1970 destroyed several buildings. On June 11, 1971, federal marshals and Coast Guard personnel removed the remaining 15 occupants without resistance.

Legacy

Although the occupiers did not win title to Alcatraz, the protest is widely credited with awakening public consciousness about Native American issues and helping to end the federal policy of termination. It inspired a wave of Native American activism, including the occupation of Wounded Knee in 1973, and contributed to the passage of legislation such as the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975. Alcatraz later became part of the Golden Gate National Recreation Area, and an annual Indigenous Peoples' Day sunrise ceremony is held on the island.
