The monkeypox virus (MPXV) is a double-stranded DNA virus in the genus Orthopoxvirus, the same genus as the variola virus that causes smallpox. It is the causative agent of mpox (formerly called monkeypox), a zoonotic disease that produces fever and a characteristic pustular rash.

History and clades

The virus was first identified in 1958 in laboratory monkeys in Copenhagen — hence its name, though monkeys are not its natural reservoir; African rodents are suspected hosts. The first human case was recorded in 1970 in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Genetic studies distinguish two major clades: Clade I, historically found in Central Africa and associated with more severe disease, and Clade II, found in West Africa; the virus responsible for the global outbreak that began in 2022 belongs to Clade IIb.

Transmission and disease

Transmission occurs through close contact with infectious lesions, bodily fluids, respiratory secretions during prolonged face-to-face contact, and contaminated materials such as bedding. After an incubation period of roughly one to two weeks, illness typically begins with fever, headache, muscle aches, and swollen lymph nodes — lymphadenopathy helps distinguish mpox from smallpox — followed by a rash that progresses through macules, papules, vesicles, and pustules before scabbing. Most cases are self-limiting over two to four weeks. Because of antigenic cross-reactivity among orthopoxviruses, smallpox vaccines (such as JYNNEOS/Imvanex) provide substantial protection against mpox.
