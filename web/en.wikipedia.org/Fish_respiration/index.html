Fish respiration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fish respiration is the process by which fish exchange gases with their environment, taking up dissolved oxygen from water and releasing carbon dioxide. Most fish exchange gases exclusively through their gills, feathery organs located on either side of the pharynx and protected in bony fish by a bony cover, the operculum.

Mechanism

A fish breathes by taking water in through its mouth and pumping it over the gills. In bony fish this is achieved by a two-part pump: expansion of the buccal cavity draws water in, and compression, coordinated with the opening of the opercula, drives it across the gill filaments and out. Many fast-swimming pelagic fish, such as tunas and some sharks, instead use ram ventilation, swimming with the mouth open to force water over the gills, and must swim continuously to breathe.

Countercurrent exchange

Each gill is composed of filaments covered in thin lamellae richly supplied with capillaries. Blood in the lamellae flows in the opposite direction to the water passing over them — a countercurrent exchange arrangement that maintains a favorable oxygen concentration gradient along the whole exchange surface. As a result, fish can extract a large proportion — often 80 percent or more — of the oxygen dissolved in the water, far more efficiently than a concurrent-flow system could.

Air breathing

Some fish supplement or replace gill respiration with air breathing, an adaptation to oxygen-poor waters. Lungfish possess true lungs; labyrinth fish such as gouramis and bettas use a labyrinth organ above the gills; and various catfishes and loaches gulp air and absorb oxygen through the gut. These fishes may suffocate if denied access to the surface. A few species, such as mudskippers, can also exchange gases through moist skin while out of water.
