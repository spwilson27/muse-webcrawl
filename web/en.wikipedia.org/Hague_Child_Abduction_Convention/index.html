Hague Child Abduction Convention

The Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, commonly called the Hague Child Abduction Convention, is a multilateral treaty concluded on 25 October 1980 under the auspices of the Hague Conference on Private International Law. It entered into force on 1 December 1983 and has been ratified by more than 100 states.

Purpose and mechanism

The Convention seeks to protect children from the harmful effects of wrongful removal or retention across international borders by providing a procedure for their prompt return to the state of their habitual residence, so that custody disputes are decided by the courts of that state. Each contracting state designates a Central Authority to cooperate in locating children and processing return applications. Courts are generally required to order a child's return if the removal breached rights of custody under the law of the child's habitual residence and the application is made within one year.

Exceptions

Return may be refused in narrow circumstances: where the applicant was not actually exercising custody rights or consented to the removal; where there is a grave risk that return would expose the child to physical or psychological harm or place the child in an intolerable situation; where the child objects to return and has attained an appropriate age and maturity; or where return would violate fundamental principles of human rights. The Convention applies to children under 16 and does not decide the merits of custody itself.
