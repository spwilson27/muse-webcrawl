Theodor Hermann Meynert (15 June 1833 – 31 May 1892) was a German-born Austrian psychiatrist, neuropathologist, and anatomist. A professor at the University of Vienna and the most influential teacher of his generation in German-language neuropsychiatry, Meynert sought to ground psychiatry in the anatomy of the brain and is regarded as a founder of cerebral cytoarchitectonics.

Career

Born in Dresden, Meynert moved to Vienna as a child and studied medicine there, becoming assistant to Carl von Rokitansky. He qualified as a lecturer in the anatomy and physiology of the nervous system in 1865 and in 1870 became associate professor of psychiatry and director of the psychiatric clinic attached to the Vienna General Hospital; in 1873 he gained the full chair. Among the young physicians who trained under or were influenced by him were Sigmund Freud, who worked in his laboratory, Carl Wernicke, Auguste Forel, and Paul Flechsig.

Work

Meynert described the layered cellular architecture of the cerebral cortex and distinguished projection fibres, linking cortical areas to lower centres, from association fibres connecting cortical regions — a framework that shaped thinking about higher mental functions. His name survives in several anatomical eponyms, including the nucleus basalis of Meynert (the magnocellular basal forebrain nucleus, later important in Alzheimer's disease research), the fasciculus retroflexus (Meynert's bundle) between the habenula and interpeduncular nucleus, and the tectospinal decussation called Meynert's fountain decussation. In his textbook Psychiatrie (1884) he classified mental disorders by anatomical and physiological principles, coining the term "amentia" for acute confusional states.

Later life

Meynert's later years were marked by strained relations with some former pupils and by personal tragedy following the death of his son. He died at Klosterneuburg in 1892. Freud, despite later differences, credited the intellectual stimulation of Meynert's clinic and laboratory in his own formation.
