Harold Clayton Urey (April 29, 1893 – January 5, 1981) was an American physical chemist. His pioneering work on isotopes earned him the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1934 for the discovery of deuterium. During the Second World War, he played a significant role in the development of the atom bomb, leading work on isotope separation methods for uranium enrichment. After the war, he became a leading figure in planetary science and the study of the origin of life; the Miller–Urey experiment on the synthesis of organic compounds under early-Earth conditions grew out of his research group.

Early life and education

Urey was born in Walkerton, Indiana, to Samuel Clayton Urey, a schoolteacher and minister in the Church of the Brethren, and Cora Emeline Reinoehl. After studying zoology at Earlham College and the University of Montana, he worked as a chemist for the Barrett Chemical Company during the First World War and then earned a Ph.D. in chemistry from the University of California, Berkeley, in 1923 under Gilbert N. Lewis. He spent a year at Niels Bohr's institute in Copenhagen before joining the faculty of Johns Hopkins University and later Columbia University.

Deuterium

Working with Ferdinand Brickwedde and George M. Murphy, Urey investigated the predicted heavy isotope of hydrogen by carefully fractionating liquid hydrogen and examining its spectrum. In 1931–1932 the group demonstrated the existence of deuterium, showing spectral lines shifted exactly as theory predicted. The discovery won Urey the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1934. During the Second World War he directed the SAM Laboratories at Columbia, which developed the gaseous diffusion process for separating uranium-235.

Later career

Urey moved to the University of Chicago after the war, advocating civilian control of atomic energy and international cooperation. He turned to geochemistry, developing an oxygen-isotope paleotemperature scale for reconstructing past climates from fossil shells. In the 1950s he moved to the new University of California, San Diego, at La Jolla. Inspired by his ideas about a reducing early atmosphere, his graduate student Stanley Miller performed the famous 1953 Miller–Urey experiment, producing amino acids from simple gases and electric discharge. Urey also wrote The Planets: Their Origin and Development (1952), a foundational work of planetary science, and advised NASA on lunar samples returned by Apollo. The lunar crater Urey and asteroid 4716 Urey are named in his honor.
