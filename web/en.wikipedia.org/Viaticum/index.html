Viaticum

Viaticum is a term used in the Catholic Church for the Eucharist (Holy Communion) given to a person who is dying or in danger of death. The word is Latin for "provision for a journey," and in this usage it signifies food for the passage through death to eternal life. It is administered as part of the Last Rites, together with the sacraments of Penance and Anointing of the Sick.

Practice

Viaticum may be given by a priest, deacon, or an authorised extraordinary minister of Holy Communion. The dying person renews the profession of faith, and the communicant may receive under both kinds (bread and wine) even where lay communion is normally under one kind. Canon law urges that Viaticum not be delayed until the very last moment, and it may be received even by someone who has already received Communion that day. Historically it was carried in procession to the sick person's home, sometimes with a bell and lantern, and the faithful were expected to show reverence as it passed.

History

The practice is ancient: the First Council of Nicaea (325) decreed that the dying should not be deprived of "the last and most necessary Viaticum." In the ancient world, viaticum also denoted money or provisions given for a journey, and some early Christians placed the consecrated host with the dead — a practice the Church later discouraged. The concept resonates with older customs of providing the dead with sustenance or coinage for their journey, such as Charon's obol in Greek tradition.
