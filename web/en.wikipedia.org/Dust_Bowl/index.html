Dust Bowl

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Dust Bowl
Period1930–1936 (some regions to 1940)
RegionGreat Plains: Oklahoma and Texas panhandles, Kansas, Colorado, New Mexico
CausesSevere drought; deep ploughing and removal of native grasses
EffectsMass crop failure, dust storms ("black blizzards"), migration of ~2.5 million people

The Dust Bowl was a period of severe dust storms that greatly damaged the ecology and agriculture of the American and Canadian prairies during the 1930s. The phenomenon was caused by a combination of natural drought and decades of extensive farming without crop rotation, fallow fields, cover crops or other techniques to prevent wind erosion. Deep ploughing had displaced the native deep-rooted grasses that normally trapped soil and moisture even in dry periods.

The drought came in three waves — 1934, 1936 and 1939–1940. On "Black Sunday", 14 April 1935, one of the worst "black blizzards" turned day into night across the plains. With crops failing, areas like the Oklahoma panhandle were depopulated: about 2.5 million people left the Plains states, many of them "Okies" who migrated to California in search of work, a journey immortalised in John Steinbeck's novel The Grapes of Wrath (1939) and the photographs of Dorothea Lange.

Government response

The Roosevelt administration responded with New Deal programmes: the Soil Conservation Service (now the Natural Resources Conservation Service) was established in 1935, farmers were taught contour ploughing and other conservation methods, the federal government bought and retired marginal land, and a massive shelterbelt programme planted millions of trees to break the wind.

Legacy

The Dust Bowl remains one of the worst man-made ecological disasters in American history and a lasting influence on soil conservation policy and American culture.
