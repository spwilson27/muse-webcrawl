Genotype

The genotype of an organism is its complete set of genetic material. However, the term is often used to refer to a single gene or set of genes, such as the genotype for eye color. The genes partly determine the observable characteristics of an organism (its phenotype), such as hair color, height, etc. An example of a characteristic determined by genotype is the petal color in a pea plant. The collection of all genetic possibilities for a single trait are called alleles; two alleles for petal color are purple and white.

The genotype is one of three factors that determine phenotype. The other two are environmental factors and epigenetic factors. Not all individuals with the same genotype look or act the same way because appearance and behavior are modified by environmental and growing conditions. Likewise, not all organisms that look alike necessarily have the same genotype.

The term genotype was introduced by the Danish botanist Wilhelm Johannsen in 1909, along with the term phenotype, in his work on plant heredity. He coined the terms to clarify that an organism's hereditary constitution must be distinguished from the characteristics it actually displays.

Genotype and phenotype

The genotype–phenotype distinction is fundamental in genetics. The genotype is the full hereditary information carried by an organism, while the phenotype is the organism's actual observed properties, such as morphology, development, or behavior. This distinction is necessary for the study of inheritance of traits and their evolution. The phenotype is influenced by the genotype, the environment, and interactions between the two.

A given genotype is often described by the alleles an individual carries at a locus. An individual with two copies of the same allele is homozygous for that gene; an individual with two different alleles is heterozygous. Dominant alleles are expressed in heterozygotes, masking the effect of recessive alleles, which are expressed only when homozygous.

Genotypic variation

Variation in genotype contributes to variation in phenotype. Mutations introduce new alleles into a population, and recombination during meiosis shuffles alleles into new combinations. The distribution of genotypes in a population can be described by genotype frequencies, which under idealized conditions follow the proportions predicted by the Hardy–Weinberg principle.

Determining genotype

The process of determining an individual's genotype is called genotyping. Techniques range from classical genetic crosses and Punnett squares, which infer genotype from the phenotypes of offspring, to modern molecular methods such as polymerase chain reaction assays, DNA microarrays, and whole-genome sequencing. Genotyping is used in medicine to diagnose genetic disorders and predict disease risk, in forensics for identification, and in agriculture for selective breeding.

Genotype and environment

The same genotype can produce different phenotypes in different environments, a phenomenon described by the norm of reaction. Such genotype–environment interactions mean that the effect of an allele may depend on the conditions in which an organism develops. A classic example is the human disease phenylketonuria, in which a harmful genotype produces its effects only when the diet contains phenylalanine.
