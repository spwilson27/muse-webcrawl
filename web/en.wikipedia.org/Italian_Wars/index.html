Italian Wars

The Italian Wars were a series of conflicts fought between 1494 and 1559, primarily in the Italian Peninsula, involving most of the city-states of Italy together with France, Spain, the Holy Roman Empire, England, Scotland, Switzerland, the Papal States, Venice and the Ottoman Empire. Sparked by the French invasion of Charles VIII in 1494 to press a dynastic claim to Naples, the wars became a prolonged struggle between the House of Valois and the House of Habsburg for dominance in Italy and in Europe, ending with the Peace of Cateau-Cambrésis (1559) and the confirmation of Spanish Habsburg ascendancy in Italy.

The Battle of Pavia (1525) was the decisive engagement of the wars, ending in the capture of Francis I.

Course of the wars

Charles VIII's rapid advance to Naples in 1494–95, initially unopposed, shattered the Italian balance of power maintained since the Peace of Lodi (1454) and alarmed the peninsula's states into the League of Venice, forcing his retreat. Louis XII added a claim to Milan and partitioned Naples with Ferdinand of Spain in 1500, but the partners fell out, and Spanish victory at the Garigliano (1503) under Gonzalo de Córdoba left Spain holding Naples. Pope Julius II's League of Cambrai (1508) against Venice gave way to the Holy League against France; the Swiss infantry proved dominant at Novara (1513), while Francis I's victory at Marignano (1515) recovered Milan for France.

The election of Charles of Spain as Emperor Charles V in 1519 fused the French wars with the Habsburg–Valois rivalry. Key engagements followed: Bicocca (1522), the catastrophic French defeat at Pavia (1525) — where Francis I was captured and taken to Madrid — and the brutal Sack of Rome by imperial troops in 1527. Later phases included the campaigns of the 1530s and 1540s (Ceresole, 1544), the imperial failure at Metz (1552), and the final battles of St. Quentin (1557) and Gravelines (1558), won by Spain and England respectively, leading to the peace of 1559.

Consequences

The wars ended the era of independent Italian great powers: Milan and Naples passed under Spanish rule for a century and a half, Florence fell under Medici dukes backed by the Empire, and only Venice retained full independence. Militarily, the wars were a laboratory of the "military revolution" — Spanish tercios combining pike, arquebus and entrenchment displaced traditional heavy cavalry, artillery fortresses (trace italienne) spread across Europe, and the Battle of Ravenna (1512) saw the first major use of field artillery between armies. Machiavelli, who served Florence as diplomat and militia organiser during the wars, drew many of his political lessons from them.
