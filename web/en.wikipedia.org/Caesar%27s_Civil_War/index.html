Caesar's Civil War

Caesar's Civil War (49–45 BC) was a major civil war of the late Roman Republic, fought between Julius Caesar and the forces of the Roman Senate, led by Pompey the Great. Caesar's victory ended the political order of the Republic and set the stage for the Roman Empire under his heir Augustus.

Background

The war grew out of the breakdown of the First Triumvirate and escalating confrontation between Caesar and the Senate, which demanded that he give up his command in Gaul and return to Rome as a private citizen, exposed to prosecution. Negotiations failed, and the Senate passed its ultimate decree against him.

Course

On 10 January 49 BC, Caesar crossed the Rubicon river into Italy, beginning the war. Pompey and the Senate abandoned Rome and withdrew to Greece. Caesar secured Italy and defeated Pompeian forces in Hispania before crossing the Adriatic. After an initial defeat at Dyrrhachium, he decisively beat Pompey at Pharsalus in 48 BC. Pompey fled to Egypt, where he was murdered. Caesar campaigned in Egypt and Asia Minor, then defeated the remaining Pompeian armies at Thapsus in Africa (46 BC) and at Munda in Hispania (45 BC).

Aftermath

Caesar became dictator of Rome, eventually dictator in perpetuity, and enacted wide-ranging reforms. He was assassinated on the Ides of March 44 BC, triggering further civil wars that ended with the establishment of the Principate.
