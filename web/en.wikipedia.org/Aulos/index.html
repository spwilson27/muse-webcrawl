Aulos

An aulos (plural auloi) was a wind instrument in ancient Greece, often depicted in art and also attested by archaeology. It was usually a double-reed instrument played in pairs, with two separate pipes held one in each hand. The Roman version was the tibia. Despite frequently being translated as "flute", the aulos sounded more like an oboe or shawm.

An aulete performing with a phorbeia (cheek strap).

Description

Each pipe of the aulos had a cylindrical bore and a double reed, with typically four or five finger holes; the two pipes could play melody and drone or two melodic lines. Players sometimes wore a leather cheek strap, the phorbeia, to support continuous circular breathing and strong cheeks. Surviving examples in wood, bone and ivory show that sophisticated instruments with rotating sleeves and extra keys existed.

Use and associations

The aulos accompanied sacrifices, theatrical performances — including the choruses of tragedy and comedy — athletic training, drinking parties and military music. It was especially associated with the worship of Dionysus and Cybele and with ecstatic, emotional music, in contrast to the lyre of Apollo. Plato excluded the aulos from his ideal city in the Republic, and Aristotle criticised its orgiastic character, while professional auletes such as Pronomos of Thebes became celebrities.
