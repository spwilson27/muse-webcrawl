Transit of Venus

A transit of Venus takes place when Venus passes directly between the Sun and a superior planet. During a transit, Venus can be seen from Earth as a small black disc moving slowly across the face of the Sun. Transits of Venus are among the rarest of predictable astronomical phenomena, occurring in a pattern that repeats every 243 years, with pairs of transits eight years apart separated by gaps of 121.5 and 105.5 years.

Historical significance

The first observed transit was in 1639, recorded by Jeremiah Horrocks and William Crabtree. Edmond Halley proposed in 1716 that carefully timed observations of transits from widely separated locations could be used with parallax to determine the distance to the Sun. Expeditions mounted for the 1761 and 1769 transits — including Captain James Cook's voyage to Tahiti — produced the first accurate measurements of the astronomical unit. The 2012 transit was the last of this 243-year cycle's pair; the next pair will occur in December 2117 and December 2125.

Scientific use

Modern transits serve as analogues for detecting exoplanets by the transit method, and observations in 2004 and 2012 helped astronomers refine techniques for studying exoplanet atmospheres, as well as probing Venus's own atmosphere through the aureole visible during ingress and egress.
