Federal Rules of Evidence

The Federal Rules of Evidence are the rules of evidence that govern the admission of facts in evidence in proceedings in the United States federal courts. Adopted by the Supreme Court and enacted by Congress, they took effect on July 1, 1975, and are organised into eleven articles covering subjects from judicial notice and relevance to privileges, witnesses, hearsay and authentication.

History

Before 1975, federal evidence law was a patchwork of statutes and common law. A drafting committee appointed in 1961, chaired by Albert Jenner, produced proposed rules that the Supreme Court transmitted to Congress in 1972; after controversy over the privilege rules, Congress enacted a revised version as a statute in 1975.

Key provisions

Rule 401 defines relevant evidence, Rule 403 allows exclusion of evidence whose probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice, and Article VIII governs hearsay and its many exceptions (Rules 801–807). The rules have been amended periodically, including a comprehensive restyling in 2011, and most U.S. states have adopted evidence codes modelled on them.
