B. F. Skinner

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Burrhus Frederic Skinner (March 20, 1904 – August 18, 1990) was an American psychologist and a leading figure in behaviorism. He developed the theory of operant conditioning — the idea that behaviour is shaped by its consequences — and invented the operant conditioning chamber (the "Skinner box") and the teaching machine. His books include The Behavior of Organisms (1938), the utopian novel Walden Two (1948), and Beyond Freedom and Dignity (1971).

Operant conditioning

Where classical conditioning (Pavlov's dogs) deals with reflexive responses to stimuli, Skinner studied voluntary behaviour emitted by an organism. Through experiments with rats and pigeons he described reinforcement, punishment, extinction and schedules of reinforcement — showing, for instance, that variable-ratio schedules produce steady, high rates of responding, a principle later compared to gambling behaviour.

Radical behaviorism

Skinner's philosophy, radical behaviorism, held that private events such as thoughts and feelings could be studied as behaviour without appeal to internal "mental" causes, and that talk of free will obscured the environmental determinants of action. The stance drew sharp criticism from the emerging cognitive psychology; Noam Chomsky's 1959 review of Skinner's Verbal Behavior is often cited as a landmark of the cognitive revolution, though behavior analysis remained influential in education, animal training and applied behavior analysis therapy.

Later life and legacy

During the Second World War, Skinner pursued a project to guide missiles with trained pigeons, and later invented an "air crib" for infants that became the subject of persistent urban legends. He spent most of his career at Harvard and ranked among the most cited psychologists of the 20th century.
