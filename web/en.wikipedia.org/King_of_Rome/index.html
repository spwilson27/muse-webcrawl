King of Rome (Latin: Rex Romae) is a title associated with several distinct uses in European history. In antiquity, the kings who ruled the Roman Kingdom before the founding of the Roman Republic in 509 BC were known as kings of Rome, beginning with the legendary Romulus. In the medieval Holy Roman Empire, the related title "King of the Romans" (Rex Romanorum) was borne by an elected king who had not yet been crowned emperor by the pope.

Napoleon II

The title is most commonly associated with Napoleon Bonaparte's only legitimate son, Napoleon François Charles Joseph Bonaparte (1811–1832), who was granted the title King of Rome at his birth on 20 March 1811. Napoleon I gave the title to his heir to evoke the prestige of Rome and to echo the Holy Roman imperial tradition, in which the emperor's designated successor was styled King of the Romans. A grand palace, the Palais du Roi de Rome, was projected at Chaillot in Paris but never completed.

After Napoleon's abdication in 1814 and again in 1815 — when the infant was briefly proclaimed Napoleon II — the child was taken to Austria and raised at the court of his grandfather, Emperor Francis I, under the title Duke of Reichstadt. He died of tuberculosis in 1832 at Schönbrunn Palace, aged 21, known affectionately in France as L'Aiglon ("the Eaglet").

Ancient kings

Roman tradition listed seven kings of Rome: Romulus, Numa Pompilius, Tullus Hostilius, Ancus Marcius, Lucius Tarquinius Priscus, Servius Tullius and Lucius Tarquinius Superbus, whose expulsion led to the Republic. The Roman Senate retained a ceremonial priestly office, the rex sacrorum, to perform the religious duties once held by the kings.
