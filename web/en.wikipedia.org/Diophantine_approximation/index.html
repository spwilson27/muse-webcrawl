Diophantine approximation

In number theory, the study of Diophantine approximation deals with the approximation of real numbers by rational numbers. It is named after Diophantus of Alexandria.

The first problem was to know how well a real number can be approximated by rational numbers. For a rational number a/b to be a "good" approximation of a real number α, it is not sufficient that it lies close to α: the absolute value of the difference between α and a/b should be small relative to the size of the denominator b. Informally, one asks for rational approximations whose accuracy is high compared with the complexity (denominator) required to write them down.

Approximation by rationals

Dirichlet's approximation theorem states that for any real α and positive integer N there exist integers p and q with 1 ≤ q ≤ N such that |α − p/q| < 1/(qN) ≤ 1/q². Hence every irrational number admits infinitely many rational approximations with |α − p/q| < 1/q². The theory of continued fractions provides the best such approximations: the convergents of the continued fraction of α are precisely the rationals that approximate α better than any rational with a smaller denominator.

Hurwitz's theorem sharpens Dirichlet's result: for every irrational α there are infinitely many rationals p/q with |α − p/q| < 1/(√5 q²), and the constant √5 is best possible, with the golden ratio being the hardest number to approximate. Numbers that are exceptionally well approximable are characterised by having unbounded partial quotients in their continued fractions; Liouville numbers are approximated so well that they must be transcendental — indeed, Diophantine approximation gave the first proofs that specific numbers such as Liouville's constant are transcendental.

Roth's theorem and lower bounds

In the other direction, one asks how badly a number can be approximated. Algebraic numbers are hard to approximate too well: Roth's theorem (1955), for which Klaus Roth received the Fields Medal, states that for an algebraic irrational α and any ε > 0 there are only finitely many rationals p/q with |α − p/q| < 1/q2+ε. This exponent of 2 is optimal. The theory connects approximation to the geometry of numbers, the theory of linear forms in logarithms (Baker's theory), and the study of Diophantine equations such as Thue equations.

Simultaneous and inhomogeneous approximation

The subject also studies simultaneous approximation of several real numbers by rationals with a common denominator (Dirichlet's theorem generalises via the pigeonhole principle and Minkowski's geometry of numbers), inhomogeneous approximation in which the target is shifted, and approximation on manifolds and by algebraic numbers. Metric Diophantine approximation asks what happens for almost all numbers, as in Khinchin's theorem.
