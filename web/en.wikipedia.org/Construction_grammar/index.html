Construction grammar

In construction grammar, all units of language pair form with meaning.

Construction grammar (often abbreviated CxG) is a family of theories within cognitive linguistics which holds that the basic units of language are constructions — conventionalised pairings of form and meaning that range in size and complexity from individual morphemes and words through idioms to abstract sentence patterns such as the ditransitive construction ("She gave him a book").

Principles

Construction grammarians, notably Charles Fillmore, Paul Kay and Adele Goldberg, argue against a strict separation between lexicon and syntax: both words and grammatical patterns are constructions, differing only in complexity and schematicity. Constructions are learned from usage, organised in inheritance networks, and can carry meaning not predictable from their parts — as in Goldberg's classic example "She sneezed the napkin off the table", where the caused-motion construction contributes the sense of movement.

Variants

Approaches include Berkeley construction grammar, sign-based construction grammar, radical construction grammar (William Croft), cognitive construction grammar (Goldberg), and embodied construction grammar. Constructionist approaches have also been applied to language acquisition, language change, and computational modelling of grammar.
