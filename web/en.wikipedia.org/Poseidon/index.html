Poseidon

Poseidon (Greek: Ποσειδῶν) is one of the Twelve Olympians in ancient Greek religion and mythology, presiding over the sea, storms, earthquakes and horses. He was the protector of seafarers and the guardian of many Hellenic cities and colonies. In Roman myth he was known as Neptune.

Mythology

Poseidon was the second son of the Titans Cronus and Rhea. Like his siblings, he was swallowed by his father and later freed by Zeus. After the defeat of the Titans, the three brothers divided the world: Zeus took the sky, Hades the underworld, and Poseidon the sea. His weapon and symbol was the trident, with which he could shake the earth and shatter any object.

Poseidon and Athens

In a famous contest, Poseidon vied with Athena for patronage of Athens. Poseidon struck the Acropolis with his trident, producing a spring of salt water (in some versions a horse), while Athena offered the olive tree. The judges chose Athena, and the city took her name — though Poseidon remained worshipped on the Acropolis in the Erechtheion.

Worship

His principal sanctuary stood on the Isthmus of Corinth, where the Isthmian Games were held in his honour. Sailors prayed to him before voyages, and he was worshipped as the deep-sea god (Pelagaeus), the earth-shaker (Enosichthon), and the tamer of horses (Hippios).

In literature

Poseidon's wrath drives much of Homer's Odyssey: angered that Odysseus blinded his son, the Cyclops Polyphemus, the god tormented the hero's voyage home from Troy for ten years.
