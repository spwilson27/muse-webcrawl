Staged combustion cycle

Flow diagram of a staged combustion cycle.

The staged combustion cycle (sometimes known as the topping cycle, preburner cycle, or closed cycle) is a power cycle of a bipropellant rocket engine. In staged combustion, some or all of the propellants pass through one or more preburners — preliminary combustion chambers — whose hot gas drives the engine's turbopumps before being injected into the main combustion chamber, where combustion completes. Because no propellant is dumped overboard (unlike the gas-generator cycle), the cycle achieves high efficiency, and because all the exhaust passes through the main chamber it can operate at very high chamber pressures, further increasing performance.

Variants

In a fuel-rich staged combustion cycle, all the fuel and part of the oxidizer react in the preburner; this is easier to manage with hydrocarbon fuels, which produce soot in fuel-rich conditions that are nonetheless workable. In an oxidizer-rich cycle, used by many Soviet and Russian engines such as the RD-180, all the oxidizer and part of the fuel pass through the preburner, requiring exotic metallurgy to withstand hot, high-pressure oxygen. The full-flow staged combustion cycle, used by the SpaceX Raptor, has separate oxidizer-rich and fuel-rich preburners driving the two turbopumps, so that the full flow of both propellants powers the pumps and all gas enters the main chamber; this maximises efficiency and reduces coking on turbine blades.

Examples

  • RS-25 (Space Shuttle Main Engine) — fuel-rich staged combustion with liquid hydrogen/oxygen.
  • RD-180, RD-170 — oxidizer-rich cycles on kerosene/oxygen.
  • NK-33 — Soviet oxidizer-rich engine developed for the N1 Moon rocket.
  • Raptor — full-flow cycle on methane/oxygen.
  • BE-4 — oxidizer-rich staged combustion on methane/oxygen.

Trade-offs

Staged combustion offers the highest performance of the conventional turbopump cycles but demands complex plumbing, high-temperature high-pressure turbines, tight seals between propellants, and difficult start transients, making development costly and failure modes severe. For these reasons the simpler, lower-performing gas-generator cycle remains common.
