Madeira wine

Madeira is a fortified wine produced on the Portuguese island of Madeira in the Atlantic Ocean. It is made in a range of styles from dry wines drunk as an apéritif to sweet wines served with dessert, and is famous for its extraordinary longevity and for the deliberate heating and oxidation it undergoes during production, a process called maderisation.

History

Winegrowing on Madeira began after Portuguese settlement in the 15th century. Ships bound for the East Indies and the Americas called at Funchal and took on casks of wine fortified with brandy to survive the voyage; the heat of tropical crossings transformed the wine, and the vinho da roda ("round-trip wine") commanded premium prices. Madeira was immensely popular in colonial America — it was used to toast the signing of the United States Declaration of Independence. The industry was devastated in the 19th century by powdery mildew and phylloxera but recovered in the 20th century.

Production

The principal noble grape varieties are Sercial (driest), Verdelho, Bual, and Malmsey (Malvasia, sweetest), along with the workhorse Tinta Negra. After fortification with grape spirit to about 17–22% alcohol, wines are matured either by the estufagem method — heating in tanks for several months — or the traditional canteiro method, ageing in casks in warm lodge lofts for years or decades. Deliberate oxidation makes Madeira almost indestructible: opened bottles keep for months, and old vintages remain drinkable after more than a century.

Styles

Styles range from dry Sercial and medium-dry Verdelho through medium-sweet Bual to sweet Malmsey, sold at quality levels including 5-, 10-, 15- and 20-year-old blends, single-harvest colheita wines, and rare vintage frasqueira wines aged at least 20 years in cask.
