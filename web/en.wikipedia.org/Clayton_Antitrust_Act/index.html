Clayton Antitrust Act

The Clayton Antitrust Act of 1914 (Pub. L. 63–212, 38 Stat. 730, enacted October 15, 1914) is a part of United States antitrust law with the goal of adding further substance to the U.S. antitrust law regime; the Clayton Act seeks to prevent anticompetitive practices in their incipiency. Drafted by Henry De Lamar Clayton Jr., the Act supplemented the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 by specifying particular prohibited conduct.

Provisions

The Act prohibits certain discriminatory prices, services and allowances in dealings between merchants; exclusive dealing and tying arrangements where they substantially lessen competition; and mergers and acquisitions whose effect "may be substantially to lessen competition, or to tend to create a monopoly". It also restricts interlocking directorates — the same person making business decisions for competing companies. Section 6 exempted labour unions and agricultural organisations from antitrust liability, and Section 20 limited the use of injunctions in labour disputes, which Samuel Gompers called labour's "Magna Charta".

Enforcement and amendments

The Act is enforced by the Federal Trade Commission (created by a companion statute the same year) and the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice, and it gives private parties a right to sue for treble damages. The Robinson–Patman Act of 1936 amended the price-discrimination provisions, and the Celler–Kefauver Act of 1950 closed a loophole in the merger provision by extending it to asset acquisitions; the Hart–Scott–Rodino Act of 1976 added premerger notification requirements.

Significance

Together with the Sherman Act and the Federal Trade Commission Act, the Clayton Act forms the core of modern U.S. federal antitrust law.
