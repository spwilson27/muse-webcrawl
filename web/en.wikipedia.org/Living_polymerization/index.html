Living polymerization

Living polymerization is a form of chain growth polymerization where the ability of a growing polymer chain to terminate has been removed. This can be accomplished in a variety of ways. Chain termination and chain transfer reactions are absent in living polymerization, in comparison with conventional chain growth polymerization, so the rate of chain initiation is also much larger than the rate of chain propagation.

Characteristics

In an ideal living polymerization, the polymer chains continue to grow for as long as monomer is available. Molecular weight increases linearly with conversion, the molecular weight distribution (dispersity) is narrow and approaches a Poisson distribution, and block copolymers can be prepared by sequential addition of different monomers. Because the chain ends retain their activity, polymerization can resume when more monomer is added.

History

Living polymerization was first described by Michael Szwarc in 1956 for the anionic polymerization of styrene in tetrahydrofuran, where he observed that the polystyryl anions remained active indefinitely in the absence of terminating impurities. The concept was later extended to cationic, radical, ring-opening and coordination polymerizations.

Types

Major classes include living anionic polymerization, living cationic polymerization, ring-opening metathesis polymerization (ROMP), and controlled radical techniques such as atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP), reversible addition-fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT), and nitroxide-mediated polymerization (NMP), which are sometimes called quasi-living because a small amount of termination still occurs.

Applications

Living polymerization is used commercially to produce block copolymers such as styrene-butadiene-styrene (SBS) thermoplastic elastomers, and is widely used in research to make polymers with precisely defined architectures, including star, comb and brush polymers.
