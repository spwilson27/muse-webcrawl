Myeloproliferative neoplasm

Peripheral blood in a myeloproliferative neoplasm

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are a group of rare blood cancers in which excess red blood cells, white blood cells or platelets are produced in the bone marrow. Myelo refers to the bone marrow, proliferative describes the fast growth of blood cells and neoplasm describes that growth as abnormal and uncontrolled.

The overproduction of blood cells is often associated with a somatic mutation, for example in the JAK2, CALR, or MPL genes, which may be a diagnostic criterion. The MPNs include chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), polycythaemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythaemia (ET), and primary myelofibrosis (PMF), which are the "classic" four, as well as chronic neutrophilic leukaemia (CNL), chronic eosinophilic leukaemia (CEL) and mastocytosis.

Classification

Most institutions refer to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of MPNs. In the most recent 2022 classification, the MPNs include:

  • Chronic myeloid leukaemia, BCR-ABL1-positive
  • Chronic neutrophilic leukaemia
  • Polycythaemia vera
  • Primary myelofibrosis
  • Essential thrombocythaemia
  • Chronic eosinophilic leukaemia
  • Myeloproliferative neoplasm, not otherwise specified
  • Juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia

Signs and symptoms

Patients with an MPN can be asymptomatic at diagnosis, with the disease discovered incidentally on a routine blood count. Symptoms, when present, may include fatigue, night sweats, weight loss, itching (particularly after a warm bath, characteristic of polycythaemia vera), gout, and symptoms related to an enlarged spleen such as early satiety and abdominal discomfort. Complications include thrombosis (blood clots), bleeding, and progression to myelofibrosis or acute leukaemia.

Diagnosis

Depending on the nature of the myeloproliferative neoplasm, diagnostic tests may include full blood count, bone marrow aspirate and trephine biopsy, cytogenetic analysis, and molecular testing for mutations such as JAK2 V617F, CALR and MPL. In 2005, the discovery of the JAK2 V617F mutation provided a major breakthrough in understanding the pathogenesis of Philadelphia chromosome-negative MPNs.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the specific MPN and its risk category. Options include observation, phlebotomy (for polycythaemia vera), low-dose aspirin, cytoreductive therapy such as hydroxycarbamide, interferon, JAK inhibitors such as ruxolitinib, and allogeneic stem cell transplantation, which is the only potentially curative option for myelofibrosis. Imatinib and other tyrosine kinase inhibitors have transformed the prognosis of chronic myeloid leukaemia.
