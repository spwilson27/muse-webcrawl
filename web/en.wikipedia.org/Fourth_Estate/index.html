Fourth Estate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Fourth Estate or fourth power refers to the press and news media, both in their explicit capacity of advocacy and in their implicit ability to frame political issues. The term derives from the three traditional estates of the realm — the clergy, the nobility, and the commoners — and designates the press as a fourth institutional power alongside them.

Origin of the term

The phrase is commonly attributed to Edmund Burke, as reported by Thomas Carlyle in On Heroes and Hero-Worship (1841): "Burke said there were Three Estates in Parliament; but, in the Reporters' Gallery yonder, there sat a Fourth Estate more important far than they all." Earlier, Lord Macaulay had written in 1828 that "the gallery in which the reporters sit has become a fourth estate of the realm." In France the corresponding term quatrième pouvoir is used alongside the classic three powers of Montesquieu — legislative, executive, and judicial.

Role in democracy

The Fourth Estate is understood as a watchdog: by reporting on government and other powerful institutions, the media enable public scrutiny and accountability outside the formal separation of powers. This conception underlies constitutional protections for press freedom, such as the First Amendment in the United States. In the digital era the concept has been extended by some writers to networked publics and social media, occasionally called a "fifth estate".
