Infrared (IR) radiation is electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths longer than those of visible light and shorter than those of microwaves — roughly from 700 nanometres (the red edge of the visible spectrum) to 1 millimetre. Infrared is emitted by all objects with a temperature above absolute zero (thermal radiation); roughly half of the Sun's energy reaching Earth arrives as infrared.

Discovery

In 1800 the astronomer William Herschel, measuring the temperature of each colour of sunlight dispersed by a prism, found that a thermometer placed just beyond the red end registered the highest temperature, revealing invisible "calorific rays" beyond red light — hence "infrared" (Latin infra, below).

Bands

The infrared range is conventionally subdivided: near-infrared (0.7–1.4 μm), used in fibre-optic communications and night-vision devices; short-wave and mid-wave infrared (1.4–8 μm); long-wave or thermal infrared (8–15 μm), in which objects at everyday temperatures radiate most strongly and which thermal cameras exploit; and far-infrared (15 μm–1 mm), bordering the terahertz and microwave bands. Earth's atmosphere transmits infrared only in certain "windows"; greenhouse gases such as water vapour, carbon dioxide and methane absorb strongly in others, producing the greenhouse effect.

Applications

Infrared technology underlies thermal imaging and night vision, remote controls (IR LEDs), short-range data links, fibre-optic telecommunications (at 1.3 and 1.55 μm), infrared heating and cooking, weather and Earth-observation satellites, and infrared spectroscopy in chemistry. Infrared astronomy — conducted from high, dry sites and space telescopes such as Spitzer, Herschel and the James Webb Space Telescope — penetrates dust clouds and reveals cool objects and the distant, redshifted universe.
