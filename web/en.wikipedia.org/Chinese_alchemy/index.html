Chinese alchemy

Chinese alchemy is the ancient Chinese tradition of alchemical practice, largely associated with Taoism, which sought physical immortality through the creation of elixirs and the transformation of substances. It is conventionally divided into waidan (external alchemy), concerned with compounding elixirs in furnaces, and neidan (internal alchemy), which pursued longevity through meditation, breath control, and inner transformation.

External alchemy

Waidan emerged by the Han dynasty and flourished under the Tang. Practitioners compounded minerals — especially cinnabar (mercuric sulfide) — into "golden elixirs" (jindan) believed to confer immortality or long life. Texts such as Ge Hong's Baopuzi (4th century CE) describe recipes and laboratory procedures. Ironically, many elixirs contained mercury, lead, and arsenic, and several Tang emperors are believed to have died from elixir poisoning.

Internal alchemy

As the dangers of mineral elixirs became apparent, emphasis shifted to neidan, in which the adept's own body became the laboratory: the "elixir" was refined internally through meditation, breathing exercises, diet, and sexual practices. Neidan developed a rich symbolic vocabulary borrowing the terms of the external furnace and remains influential in Taoist practice and qigong.

Contributions

Chinese alchemical experimentation produced important technological by-products. Gunpowder is famously said to have resulted from alchemists seeking an elixir of life during the Tang dynasty, and alchemical work contributed to Chinese medicine, metallurgy, and ceramics.
