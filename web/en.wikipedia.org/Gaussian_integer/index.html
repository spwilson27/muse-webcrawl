Gaussian integer

In number theory, a Gaussian integer is a complex number whose real and imaginary parts are both integers. The Gaussian integers, with ordinary addition and multiplication of complex numbers, form an integral domain, usually written Z[i]. They are named after the German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss, who developed their theory around 1831.

Basic properties

The Gaussian integers form a lattice in the complex plane. The norm N(a + bi) = a² + b² is multiplicative, and Z[i] is a Euclidean domain with respect to this norm, hence a principal ideal domain and a unique factorization domain. The only units are 1, −1, i and −i.

Gaussian primes

A Gaussian integer is a Gaussian prime if it cannot be written as a product of two non-unit Gaussian integers. The rational primes behave in three ways in Z[i]:

  • The prime 2 ramifies: 2 = −i(1 + i)², and 1 + i is prime.
  • Primes p ≡ 1 (mod 4) split: p = π·π̄, e.g. 5 = (2 + i)(2 − i). This is equivalent to Fermat's theorem that such primes are sums of two squares.
  • Primes p ≡ 3 (mod 4) remain prime (inert) in Z[i].

History

Gauss introduced these numbers in his second monograph on biquadratic reciprocity (1832), using them to state and prove the biquadratic reciprocity law and to develop the theory of sums of two squares. Gaussian integers were among the first examples motivating the general theory of algebraic number theory.
