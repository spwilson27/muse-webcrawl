Metagenomics

Metagenomics is the study of genetic material recovered directly from environmental or clinical samples. Instead of isolating and culturing individual organisms — the vast majority of microbes cannot be grown in the laboratory — metagenomics sequences DNA from an entire community at once, revealing which organisms are present and what functions they encode.

Approaches

Two main strategies are used. In amplicon sequencing, a marker gene such as the 16S rRNA gene is amplified and sequenced to profile community composition. In shotgun metagenomics, all DNA in the sample is fragmented and sequenced; computational assembly and binning then reconstruct genomes of individual community members, called metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), and annotate their metabolic capabilities.

Applications

Metagenomics has transformed the study of the human microbiome, ocean and soil ecology, and biotechnology, where it is used to discover new enzymes and antibiotics. Large projects include the Human Microbiome Project, Tara Oceans and the Earth Microbiome Project.

History

The term was coined in 1998 by Jo Handelsman and colleagues, and early landmark studies included the sequencing of the Sargasso Sea by J. Craig Venter's team in 2004, which revealed hundreds of new species and over a million new genes.
