King of the Romans

King of the Romans (Latin: Rex Romanorum; German: König der Römer) was the title used by the elected king of Germany in the Holy Roman Empire after his election by the prince-electors but before, or instead of, his coronation as emperor by the pope in Rome.

History

The title emerged in the 11th and 12th centuries, replacing the simple "king" (rex) and distinguishing the ruler's claim to the imperial dignity. From the early modern period, the title was often conferred on the emperor's designated heir during the emperor's lifetime—for example, Ferdinand I was elected King of the Romans in 1531, long before succeeding Charles V. The last coronation of an emperor by a pope was that of Charles V in 1530; afterwards rulers styled themselves "elected Roman emperor", while "King of the Romans" persisted as the heir's title until the Empire's dissolution in 1806. Napoleon later revived a version of the title for his son, Napoleon II.
