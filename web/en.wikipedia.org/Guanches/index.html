Guanches

The Guanches were the indigenous inhabitants of the Canary Islands in the Atlantic Ocean, off the coast of north-west Africa. Strictly, "Guanche" referred to the people of Tenerife, but the term is commonly applied to the native peoples of all the islands. They are believed to have migrated from North Africa, with genetic and linguistic links to the Berber (Amazigh) peoples, arriving in the islands over the first millennium BC and first millennium AD.

Society and culture

The Guanches lived in a Neolithic-level society without metalworking or the wheel, herding goats, sheep, and pigs, and farming barley and wheat. They lived in caves and stone houses, mummified their dead (a practice especially developed on Tenerife), and organised society into kingdoms ruled by chiefs (menceys on Tenerife, one per district, nine at the time of the conquest). Their languages, collectively called Guanche, died out by about the 18th century, though words survive in Canarian Spanish and in place names across the archipelago.

Conquest

The Castilian conquest of the Canaries unfolded over the 15th century, beginning with Jean de Béthencourt's expeditions in 1402 and ending with the fall of Tenerife in 1496. Guanche resistance was fierce on several islands — notably Tenerife, where the invaders were defeated at the First Battle of Acentejo in 1494 before winning the second battle the following year. Disease, warfare, slavery, and intermarriage greatly reduced the native population, but genetic studies show substantial Guanche ancestry persists in modern Canarians, particularly in maternal lineages.
