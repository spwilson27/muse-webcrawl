The Ilkhanate (also spelled Il Khanate) was a khanate ruled by the Mongol House of Hulagu that ruled Persia and surrounding lands from 1256 to 1335. It was one of the four main successor states of the Mongol Empire, alongside the Yuan dynasty, the Chagatai Khanate, and the Golden Horde. Its ruler bore the title Ilkhan ("subordinate khan"), acknowledging, at first, the seniority of the Great Khan in the east.

Foundation

Hulagu, grandson of Genghis Khan, was sent west by his brother Möngke Khan to subdue Persia and the Muslim powers of the Middle East. His armies destroyed the Nizari Ismaili state (the "Assassins") in 1256 and sacked Baghdad in 1258, killing the last Abbasid caliph there and devastating the city. Mongol expansion into Syria was checked by the Mamluks of Egypt at the Battle of Ain Jalut in 1260, shortly after Hulagu's death in 1265 the khanate settled into a recognisably Persianate state centred on Tabriz.

Islamisation and culture

The seventh Ilkhan, Ghazan (reigned 1295–1304), converted to Islam, making the state officially Muslim, and with his vizier Rashid al-Din undertook sweeping administrative and fiscal reforms. Rashid al-Din's Jami' al-Tawarikh ("Compendium of Chronicles") was the first world history of its kind. The Ilkhanid court patronised Persian literature, miniature painting, and monumental architecture such as the dome of Soltaniyeh built under Öljaitü — and maintained diplomatic correspondence with European rulers about an alliance against the Mamluks, though no joint campaign ever materialised.

Decline

After the death of Abu Sa'id in 1335 without an heir, the Ilkhanate fragmented into competing regional dynasties — Jalayirids, Chobanids, Muzaffarids, and others — ending effective Mongol rule in Persia, though successor states preserved much of its administrative tradition.
