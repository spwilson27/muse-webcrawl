Roger B. Taney

Roger Brooke Taney
Born: 17 March 1777, Calvert County, Maryland
Died: 12 October 1864, Washington, D.C.
Office: 5th Chief Justice of the United States (1836–1864)

Roger Brooke Taney (March 17, 1777 – October 12, 1864) was an American lawyer and politician who served as the fifth chief justice of the United States from 1836 until his death in 1864. His Supreme Court opinions supported states' rights and the expansion of slaveholder rights, most infamously in Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857).

Early career

Taney was born into a wealthy Maryland planter family and educated at Dickinson College. He served as Attorney General of Maryland and as a leader of the Democratic Party. President Andrew Jackson appointed him United States Attorney General in 1831 and Secretary of the Treasury in 1833, in which post he carried out Jackson's removal of federal deposits from the Second Bank of the United States.

Chief Justice

Appointed Chief Justice in 1836, Taney presided over a Court that favored dual federalism, limiting federal power while expanding the police powers of the states. His opinion in Charles River Bridge v. Warren Bridge (1837) supported economic development over implied monopoly rights.

Dred Scott

In Dred Scott v. Sandford, Taney wrote for the majority that African Americans, whether enslaved or free, were not and could not become citizens of the United States, and that Congress had no power to prohibit slavery in the territories, rendering the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional. The decision intensified sectional conflict and is widely regarded as one of the worst in Supreme Court history. It was effectively overturned by the Civil War Amendments.

Civil War and death

During the Civil War, Taney, sitting as a circuit judge, challenged President Abraham Lincoln's suspension of habeas corpus in Ex parte Merryman (1861), which Lincoln ignored. Taney died in 1864, on the same day his home state of Maryland abolished slavery.
