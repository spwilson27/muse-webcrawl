The St. Lawrence River (French: Fleuve Saint-Laurent) is a large international river in the middle latitudes of North America, connecting the Great Lakes to the Atlantic Ocean. Its flow begins at the mouth of Lake Ontario near Kingston, Ontario, and it flows northeast past Montreal and Quebec City to the Gulf of St. Lawrence. The river is the primary drainage outlet of the Great Lakes Basin and the principal means of access to the North American interior for ocean shipping via the St. Lawrence Seaway.

Geography

Including the estuary, the St. Lawrence system extends about 1,197 km (744 mi). The river proper runs roughly 500 km from Lake Ontario to the point where it widens into the estuary near Île d'Orléans. It passes through the Thousand Islands archipelago, along the border between Ontario and New York State, and then wholly within the province of Quebec. Major tributaries include the Ottawa, Richelieu, Saint-Maurice, and Saguenay rivers. Cities on its banks include Montreal, Trois-Rivières, Quebec City, and Sorel-Tracy.

History

Indigenous peoples lived along the river for thousands of years; the St. Lawrence Iroquoians encountered by Jacques Cartier in 1534 farmed the valley and lived in villages such as Stadacona and Hochelaga. Cartier named the gulf and river for Saint Lawrence, whose feast day fell near the date of his exploration. Samuel de Champlain founded Quebec in 1608 and Montreal was founded in 1642, making the river the axis of New France. After the British conquest of 1760–63, the river remained the commercial spine of British North America and later of Canada.

Navigation and ecology

The completion of the St. Lawrence Seaway in 1959 allowed ocean-going vessels to reach the Great Lakes, transforming ports from Montreal to Duluth. The river and its estuary support rich fisheries and populations of beluga whales, and the surrounding wetlands are important migratory bird habitat. Ice cover in winter historically halted navigation; icebreaking now keeps the channel to Montreal open year-round.
