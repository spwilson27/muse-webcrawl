Molokai

Molokaʻi (Hawaiian: Molokaʻi) is the fifth most populous of the eight major islands that make up the Hawaiian Islands in the middle of the Pacific Ocean. It is 38 by 10 miles (61 by 16 km) in size, with a land area of about 260 square miles (673 km²), making it the fifth-largest of the main Hawaiian Islands and the 27th-largest island in the United States. It lies east of Oʻahu across the 25-mile-wide Kaiwi Channel and north of Lānaʻi. The island's largest town is Kaunakakai, and most of its roughly 7,000 residents are of Native Hawaiian ancestry — the highest proportion of any Hawaiian island — earning Molokai the nickname "the Friendly Isle" and a reputation as the most traditionally Hawaiian island.

Geography

Molokai was formed by two shield volcanoes: the smaller West Molokai and the larger East Molokai, whose summit, Kamakou, rises 4,970 feet (1,515 m) above sea level. The northern coast of East Molokai features some of the highest sea cliffs in the world, rising up to about 3,900 feet (1,200 m) above the Pacific. Between the two volcanoes lies the Hoʻolehua plain. The isolated Kalaupapa Peninsula juts from the northern cliffs.

Kalaupapa

From 1866 to 1969, the peninsula of Kalaupapa was the site of a settlement for people exiled because they suffered from Hansen's disease (leprosy). At its peak about 1,100 patients lived there. The Belgian priest Saint Damien worked among them from 1873 until his death from the disease in 1889; Mother Marianne Cope also served there. The area is now preserved as Kalaupapa National Historical Park, and a small number of former patients still choose to live there.

Economy and culture

Molokai has resisted large-scale tourism and cruise ships, and its economy centres on ranching, small farms (coffee, macadamia nuts, papaya and seed corn) and subsistence fishing and farming. The Molokai Hoe canoe race, finishing at Waikīkī, and the island's fishponds — among the best preserved in Hawaiʻi — are notable cultural features.
