Cloister

A cloister (from Latin claustrum, "enclosure") is a covered walkway, open gallery, or open arcade running along the walls of buildings and forming a quadrangle or garth, the open court at its centre. Cloisters are most closely associated with monasteries and convents, where they formed the heart of the religious community's daily life, but they are also found in cathedrals, colleges, and palaces. The word gives the modern sense of seclusion, as in "cloistered" life.

Monastic cloisters

In a medieval monastery the cloister connected the principal communal buildings: the church, the chapter house, the refectory (dining hall), and the dormitory, arranged around its four walks. The standard layout was codified early, appearing in the ninth-century Plan of Saint Gall. Monks walked, read, copied manuscripts, and meditated in the sheltered galleries, while the central garth often contained a garden, a well, or a lavabo for washing. Romanesque and Gothic cloisters are renowned for the carved capitals of their paired columns, which frequently depict biblical scenes, beasts, and foliage — famous examples survive at Monreale in Sicily and Santo Domingo de Silos in Spain.

Symbolism and later use

The cloister garth evoked paradise and the heavenly Jerusalem for medieval monks, and its ordered calm embodied the contemplative ideal. The architectural form passed to universities, hospitals, and civic buildings; The Cloisters museum in New York, for example, incorporates genuine medieval cloisters transported from France. Enclosed religious orders still observe "cloister" as the part of the house reserved to the community, from which members traditionally do not leave.
