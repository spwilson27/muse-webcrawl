Islay (Scottish Gaelic: Ìle) is the southernmost island of the Inner Hebrides of Scotland. Known as "The Queen of the Hebrides", it lies in Argyll and Bute just south west of Jura and around 40 kilometres (25 mi) north of the Northern Irish coast. The island is widely famed for its peated single malt Scotch whiskies, produced by distilleries including Ardbeg, Bowmore, Bruichladdich, Bunnahabhain, Caol Ila, Kilchoman, Lagavulin, Laphroaig, and Ardnahoe.

Geography

Islay is 40 kilometres (25 mi) long from north to south and up to 30 kilometres (19 mi) wide. Much of its coastline is indented with sea lochs, notably Loch Gruinart and Loch Indaal, and extensive raised beaches and peat bogs characterise the interior. The highest point is Beinn Bheigeir at 491 metres (1,611 ft). The island's mild climate, moderated by the Gulf Stream, supports a rich birdlife, including large wintering flocks of barnacle and Greenland white-fronted geese.

History

Islay has been inhabited since Mesolithic times; the island contains Neolithic and Bronze Age remains and prominent Iron Age forts. In the early medieval period it formed part of the Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata. From the 12th to the 15th centuries Islay was the political heart of the Lordship of the Isles: the MacDonald Lords of the Isles governed from Finlaggan, near present-day Ballygrant, whose council island and castle ruins survive. After the forfeiture of the Lordship in 1493, the island passed through periods of Campbell control, and in the 18th and 19th centuries the lairds of the Shawfield/Islay estate embarked on agricultural improvement, village building (including the planned circular village of Bowmore, founded 1768), and eventually clearances and emigration.

Whisky

Whisky distilling on Islay is documented from the early 18th century, and the island's whiskies are prized for intense peat smoke, medicinal, and maritime flavours imparted by peat-fired malt and sea air. Distilleries cluster in two traditional groups: the southern Kildalton coast (Ardbeg, Lagavulin, Laphroaig) and the north (Bunnahabhain, Caol Ila, Ardnahoe). The annual Fèis Ìle festival each May draws whisky enthusiasts from around the world.

Transport

Ferry services operated by Caledonian MacBrayde connect Port Askaig and Port Ellen with Kennacraig on the Kintyre peninsula, with additional sailings to Colonsay and Oban. Islay Airport (Glenegedale) offers scheduled flights to Glasgow.

Language and culture

Scottish Gaelic remains spoken by a minority of residents, and Islay has a strong tradition of Gaelic song and piping. The island is the ancestral home of Clan Donald. The Islay Gaelic choir and local heritage centre at Islay House Square in Bridgend reflect a continuing cultural identity.
