The Enūma Eliš (Akkadian: Enūma eliš, "When on high"), also known as The Seven Tablets of Creation, is the Babylonian creation myth, named after its opening words. It was recovered by English archaeologist Austen Henry Layard in 1849 in the ruined Library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (in modern Iraq), and published by George Smith in 1876.

The epic runs to roughly a thousand lines on seven clay tablets written in the Akkadian language in cuneiform script. It tells of the origin of the world from primordial waters, the conflict between the older gods and the sea goddess Tiamat, and the rise of the god Marduk, who defeats Tiamat, fashions the cosmos from her body, and creates humankind to serve the gods.

Summary

In the beginning, only the fresh-water god Apsu and the salt-water goddess Tiamat exist, mingling their waters. Generations of younger gods arise and disturb Apsu, who plots to destroy them; the god Ea learns of the plot and kills Apsu first. Tiamat, enraged, raises an army of monsters led by her champion Kingu. The gods turn in desperation to Marduk, who agrees to fight on condition that he be granted supreme authority. Marduk defeats Tiamat, splits her body to form heaven and earth, establishes the order of the stars and calendar, and creates humans from Kingu's blood. The epic concludes with the building of Babylon and its temple, and a long recitation of Marduk's fifty names.

Significance

The Enūma Eliš was recited during the Babylonian New Year festival (Akitu). Scholars have compared its account of creation — the division of waters, the ordering of chaos — with the creation narratives of the Hebrew Bible, though the extent and direction of influence remain debated.
