Stereotype threat

Field: Social psychology
Proposed by: Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson (1995)
Effect: Anxiety about confirming a negative group stereotype impairs performance

Stereotype threat is a situational predicament in which people feel at risk of conforming to stereotypes about their social group. The term was coined by social psychologist Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson in 1995, in studies showing that African American students underperformed on tests when the tests were presented as diagnostic of intellectual ability.

Mechanism and evidence

The proposed mechanisms include anxiety, reduced working-memory capacity, heightened self-monitoring and negative thinking, triggered when a situation makes a negative stereotype salient — for example, women taking a difficult mathematics test described as showing gender differences. The effect has been investigated across many groups and domains, including gender and mathematics, race and academic testing, and age and memory.

Interventions and debate

Interventions studied for reducing stereotype threat include values-affirmation exercises, teaching that intelligence is malleable (growth mindset), and reframing tests as non-diagnostic or fair. The size and robustness of the effect have been debated, with some large replication attempts finding smaller effects than early studies, and meta-analyses arguing over publication bias; stereotype threat nonetheless remains an influential framework in the psychology of achievement gaps.
