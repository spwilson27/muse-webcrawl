Greek War of Independence

The Greeks fight for independence from Ottoman rule, 1821–1829.

The Greek War of Independence, also known as the Greek Revolution of 1821, was a successful war of independence waged by Greek revolutionaries against the Ottoman Empire between 1821 and 1829. It led to the establishment of modern Greece, the first independent Hellenic state since the fall of the Byzantine Empire.

Background

Greece had been under Ottoman rule since the mid-15th century. During the late 18th and early 19th centuries, Greek national consciousness grew, fuelled by the Greek Enlightenment, the activities of the diaspora, and the secret revolutionary society Filiki Eteria (Society of Friends), founded in Odessa in 1814.

The outbreak

The revolt began in March 1821. On 25 March 1821 — now celebrated as Greek Independence Day — Bishop Germanos of Patras is said to have raised the Greek flag at the monastery of Agia Lavra. Uprisings spread across the Peloponnese, central Greece and the Aegean islands. Alexandros Ypsilantis simultaneously led a failed campaign in the Danubian Principalities.

Course of the war

Greek forces achieved early victories, capturing Tripolitsa in 1821, while Ottoman reprisals included the Chios massacre of 1822. Civil wars among the Greeks in 1823–1825 weakened the revolution just as Sultan Mahmud II called on Muhammad Ali of Egypt, whose son Ibrahim Pasha landed in the Peloponnese in 1825 and devastated the region. The fall of Missolonghi in 1826, after a year-long siege, galvanised European public opinion; philhellenes such as Lord Byron, who died at Missolonghi in 1824, had already raised awareness of the Greek cause.

Foreign intervention and independence

The Great Powers — Britain, France and Russia — intervened. Their combined fleets destroyed the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet at the Battle of Navarino in October 1827. A French expeditionary force expelled Ibrahim's troops from the Peloponnese in 1828, and Russia's advance during the Russo-Turkish War of 1828–29 forced the Ottomans to accept Greek autonomy. The London Protocol of 1830 recognised Greece as an independent state, and Ioannis Kapodistrias served as its first governor until his assassination in 1831. In 1832 the Bavarian prince Otto became the first King of Greece.

Legacy

The war reshaped the Balkans and inspired nationalist movements across Europe. Its heroes — Theodoros Kolokotronis, Georgios Karaiskakis, Laskarina Bouboulina and Konstantinos Kanaris among them — remain central figures of Greek national memory.
