Situated cognition

FieldCognitive science, education
Key proponentsJean Lave, Etienne Wenger, John Seely Brown, Lucy Suchman
Key conceptsCommunities of practice; legitimate peripheral participation; cognitive apprenticeship

Situated cognition is a theory of learning that argues knowledge is not simply stored in the mind and applied to situations, but is fundamentally bound to the activity, context and culture in which it is developed and used. Knowing, on this view, is inseparable from doing.

Origins

The theory grew out of anthropological and psychological work in the 1980s. Lucy Suchman's Plans and Situated Actions (1987) showed how human action responds to circumstances rather than simply following abstract plans. Jean Lave's studies of everyday arithmetic — shoppers computing best buys in supermarkets, tailors apprenticing in Liberia — demonstrated that people who performed poorly on school-style tests handled the same mathematics competently in real settings.

Communities of practice

In Situated Learning (1991), Lave and Etienne Wenger proposed that learning is a process of legitimate peripheral participation: newcomers join a community of practice, begin with simple, peripheral tasks, and gradually move toward full participation as they absorb the community's skills, language and identity. Learning is thus as much socialisation as information transfer.

Implications for education

Situated cognition has influenced instructional design through ideas such as cognitive apprenticeship, in which experts model skills and coach learners in authentic tasks, and through problem-based and project-based learning. Critics respond that some knowledge does transfer across contexts, and that situated approaches can understate the value of abstract instruction.
