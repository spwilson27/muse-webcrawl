Sinusitis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


CT scan of chronic sinusitis

Sinusitis, also known as rhinosinusitis, is an inflammation of the mucous membranes lining the sinuses that results in symptoms including thick nasal mucus, a plugged nose and facial pain. Other signs and symptoms may include fever, headaches, a poor sense of smell, sore throat, a cough and fatigue.

Sinusitis is classified by duration: acute sinusitis lasts less than four weeks, subacute between four and twelve weeks, and chronic sinusitis longer than twelve weeks. Most acute cases follow a viral common cold; only a small proportion are complicated by bacterial infection.

Causes

Inflammation of the sinuses may be triggered by viral or bacterial infections, allergies, air pollution, or structural problems in the nose such as a deviated septum. When the sinus openings become blocked and mucus builds up, micro-organisms can multiply and cause infection. Chronic sinusitis involves persistent inflammation and may be associated with nasal polyps, asthma or immune system problems; a subset of chronic cases are linked to fungal colonisation.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually based on symptoms and physical examination. Features suggesting bacterial rather than viral infection include symptoms persisting beyond ten days, severe onset with high fever and purulent discharge, or a pattern of initial improvement followed by worsening ("double worsening"). Imaging such as a CT scan and nasal endoscopy are used mainly in chronic or complicated cases.

Treatment

Most cases resolve without antibiotics, and symptomatic treatment — saline irrigation, intranasal corticosteroids, analgesics and rest — is recommended first. Antibiotics are reserved for cases likely to be bacterial. Chronic sinusitis is managed with topical steroids and saline irrigation, and functional endoscopic sinus surgery may be offered when medical therapy fails.

Epidemiology

Sinusitis is a common condition, affecting roughly 10–30 per cent of people in the United States and Europe each year, and is one of the most frequent reasons for antibiotic prescriptions in primary care.
