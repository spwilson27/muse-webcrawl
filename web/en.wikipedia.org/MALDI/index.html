Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) is an ionization technique used in mass spectrometry that employs a laser energy-absorbing matrix to create ions from large molecules with minimal fragmentation. It allows the analysis of biomolecules such as DNA, proteins, peptides, and carbohydrates, as well as synthetic polymers, which tend to fragment when ionized by more conventional methods.

Principle

The analyte is mixed with a large excess of a matrix compound, commonly a small organic acid such as α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid or 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid, and the mixture is dried to form co-crystals. A pulsed laser irradiates the spot; the matrix absorbs the laser energy and desorbs, carrying analyte molecules into the gas phase and ionizing them, typically by protonation. The ions are then analysed, most often with a time-of-flight (TOF) analyser in the MALDI-TOF configuration.

History and applications

The technique grew out of work by Koichi Tanaka (soft laser desorption, recognised by a share of the 2002 Nobel Prize in Chemistry) and by Franz Hillenkamp and Michael Karas, who developed the matrix-assisted method in the late 1980s. MALDI imaging mass spectrometry and microbial identification by MALDI-TOF fingerprinting are now routine applications.
