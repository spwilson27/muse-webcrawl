Fortified wine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fortified wine is a wine to which a distilled spirit, usually brandy, has been added. In the course of this process, the wine is often aged in wooden barrels. The original reason for fortification was to preserve the wine on long sea voyages: the added alcohol inhibited spoilage and allowed wines to survive transport in cask. Many different styles of fortified wine have been developed, including port, sherry, Madeira, Marsala, and vermouth (an aromatised fortified wine). Fortified wines typically contain 15–22% alcohol by volume.

Production

The timing of the spirit addition determines the style. In port, brandy is added during fermentation, killing the yeast and leaving residual sugar, so the wine is sweet. In most sherries, the wine is fermented dry and fortified afterwards; the resulting strength guides ageing style — lighter fino under a layer of flor yeast, stronger oloroso by oxidation. Madeira is fortified and then heated (the estufagem process), giving it exceptional longevity. Vermouth is fortified and infused with botanicals, including wormwood.

Major types

  • Port — from Portugal's Douro Valley; styles include ruby, tawny, vintage, and late bottled vintage.
  • Sherry — from Jerez in Andalusia, Spain; aged in the solera system; styles from bone-dry fino and manzanilla to sweetened cream sherries.
  • Madeira — from the Portuguese island of Madeira; made from Sercial, Verdelho, Bual, and Malmsey grapes in increasing order of sweetness.
  • Marsala — from Sicily, used in both drinking and cooking.
  • Vermouth — aromatised fortified wine from Italy and France, a key cocktail ingredient.

Service and storage

Fortified wines are traditionally served as aperitifs (dry styles, chilled) or with dessert and cheese (sweet styles). Because of their higher alcohol and (in oxidised styles) prior exposure to air, opened bottles keep far longer than table wines — typically weeks to months.
