Oil spill

An oil spill is the release of a liquid petroleum hydrocarbon into the environment, especially the marine environment, due to human activity, and is a form of pollution. The term is usually given to marine oil spills, where oil is released into the ocean or coastal waters, but spills may also occur on land. Oil spills may result from releases of crude oil from tankers, offshore platforms, drilling rigs and wells, as well as spills of refined petroleum products and their by-products.

Environmental effects

Oil penetrating the plumage of birds and the fur of marine mammals reduces their insulating and water-repelling abilities, causing hypothermia and drowning. Oil also smothers intertidal organisms, contaminates beaches and salt marshes, and its toxic components can persist in sediments for decades, harming fish, shellfish, and the fisheries and tourism that depend on them.

Notable spills

  • Deepwater Horizon (2010), Gulf of Mexico — the largest marine oil spill in U.S. history, roughly 134 million gallons over 87 days from a blown-out well.
  • Exxon Valdez (1989), Prince William Sound, Alaska — about 11 million gallons of crude oil.
  • Ixtoc I (1979), Bay of Campeche — a blowout releasing roughly 140 million gallons.
  • Torrey Canyon (1967) and Amoco Cadiz (1978) — tanker wrecks off the United Kingdom and France respectively.
  • Kuwaiti oil fires and spills (1991) during the Gulf War, including a deliberate release into the Persian Gulf.

Cleanup and recovery

Response methods include controlled in-situ burning, mechanical containment and skimming, chemical dispersants, hot-water washing, and bioremediation using oil-degrading microbes. Recovery of affected ecosystems can take years to decades. Spills led to regulatory changes such as the U.S. Oil Pollution Act of 1990, which required double hulls for tankers in U.S. waters.
