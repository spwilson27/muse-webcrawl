Indian Arm is a steep-sided glacial fjord adjacent to the city of Vancouver in southwestern British Columbia, Canada. It extends about 20 kilometres (12 miles) north from Burrard Inlet, between the communities of Belcarra on the west and Deep Cove on the east, into mountainous terrain largely inaccessible by road. It lies within the traditional territories of the Tsleil-Waututh and Squamish First Nations, for whom it has been a place of settlement, food gathering and spiritual significance for thousands of years.

Geography

Carved by glaciers and flooded by the sea as the ice retreated, Indian Arm is the southernmost fjord on the west coast of North America. Granite cliffs and thickly forested slopes rise abruptly from the water to peaks of over 1,000 metres. The Indian River empties into the fjord's head, and numerous creeks cascade down its sides, several harnessed in the early 20th century for hydroelectric power at the Buntzen Lake stations, whose intakes and powerhouse occupy the fjord's western shore. The fjord's waters support salmon, shellfish beds and harbour seals, and its quiet upper reaches are a favourite destination for kayakers and boaters from the Vancouver area.

History and use

The Tsleil-Waututh Nation, whose main community sits at the fjord's mouth on Burrard Inlet, calls the inlet by names reflecting its identity as their ancestral waterway; archaeological sites around its shores attest to long occupation. In the colonial era the arm saw logging, quarrying and small settlements, some reachable only by boat — as several waterfront properties still are. The eastern and northern shores are now largely protected within Say Nuth Khaw Yum Provincial Park (also known as Indian Arm Provincial Park), established in 1995 and co-managed with the Tsleil-Waututh Nation, while the Wigwam Inn, a historic resort near the head of the inlet accessible only by water or air, remains a local landmark.
