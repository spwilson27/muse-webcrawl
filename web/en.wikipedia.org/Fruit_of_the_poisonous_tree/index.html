Fruit of the Poisonous Tree

Fruit of the poisonous tree is a legal metaphor used to describe evidence that is obtained illegally. The logic of the terminology is that if the source (the "tree") of the evidence or evidence itself is tainted, then anything gained (the "fruit") from it is tainted as well. The doctrine is an extension of the exclusionary rule in United States constitutional law.

Doctrine

The metaphor was coined by Justice Felix Frankfurter in his opinion in Nardone v. United States (1939). The doctrine holds that evidence derived from an unconstitutional search, seizure, or interrogation must be excluded, even if that derived evidence was itself obtained lawfully. Key cases include Silverthorne Lumber Co. v. United States (1920) and Wong Sun v. United States (1963), which developed the idea of attenuation.

Exceptions

Courts recognise several exceptions. Under the independent source doctrine, evidence discovered by lawful means independent of the illegality is admissible. Under the inevitable discovery doctrine, evidence that would lawfully have been found anyway may be admitted. Evidence may also be admitted where the connection between the illegality and the discovery is sufficiently attenuated, as with intervening acts of free will. The doctrine applies in U.S. law, while other legal systems weigh illegally obtained evidence differently, often balancing its probative value against the impropriety of its acquisition.
