Human spaceflight
Spaceflight with a crew or passengers aboard a spacecraft; began in 1961 with Yuri Gagarin's orbital flight.

Human spaceflight (also referred to as manned spaceflight or crewed spaceflight) is spaceflight with a crew or passengers aboard a spacecraft, often with the spacecraft being operated directly by the onboard human crew. It is distinguished from robotic space probes and remotely controlled satellites.

History

The first human in space was Soviet cosmonaut Yuri Gagarin, who completed one orbit of Earth aboard Vostok 1 on 12 April 1961. The United States followed with Alan Shepard's suborbital flight weeks later, and John Glenn's orbital flight in 1962. The Apollo programme landed the first humans on the Moon in 1969; Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walked on the lunar surface while Michael Collins remained in orbit. Twelve astronauts walked on the Moon between 1969 and 1972.

Space stations and long duration

From Salyut 1 (1971) through Mir to the International Space Station (continuously occupied since 2000), crews have lived and worked in orbit for months at a time. Long-duration flight studies the effects of microgravity on human anatomy — bone loss, muscle atrophy and fluid shifts — knowledge essential for future missions to Mars. Life-support systems must carefully manage oxygen; crew hypoxia is a critical emergency scenario in spacecraft design.

Commercial era

Since 2020, SpaceX's Crew Dragon has flown professional and private astronauts to orbit, opening an era of commercial human spaceflight and space tourism, while China operates the Tiangong station with its taikonaut corps.
