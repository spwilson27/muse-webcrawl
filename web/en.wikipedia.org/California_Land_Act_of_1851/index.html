California Land Act of 1851

The California Land Act of 1851 (9 Stat. 631), formally entitled "An Act to ascertain and settle the private Land Claims in the State of California", was an act passed by the United States Congress on 3 March 1851. It required holders of Spanish and Mexican land grants in the newly admitted state of California to present their claims to a federal commission for confirmation, even though the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) had promised to respect Mexican property rights.

The Land Commission

The Act created a three-member Board of Land Commissioners to review claims. Of 813 claims filed, most were ultimately confirmed, but the process was slow and expensive: each claim took an average of about 17 years to resolve through appeals to the federal courts, and claimants bore the burden of proof. Many Californio rancheros were forced to sell or mortgage their lands to pay legal fees and taxes, and a large share of the ranchos passed into Anglo-American hands.

Effects

Historians regard the Act as a major cause of the dispossession of the Californio population and the transformation of California's land tenure in the decades after the Mexican–American War. Squatters occupied disputed grants while claims were pending, and the confirmed patents often came too late to restore the old rancho economy, which was also undermined by the collapse of the hide-and-tallow trade and the droughts of the 1860s.
