Rapid eye movement sleep

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


An EEG during REM sleep resembles that of waking

Rapid eye movement sleep (REM sleep or REMS) is a unique phase of sleep in mammals and birds, characterised by random rapid movement of the eyes, accompanied by low muscle tone throughout the body, and the propensity of the sleeper to dream vividly. It is also known as paradoxical sleep or desynchronised sleep, because of the physiological similarities to waking states including rapid, low-voltage desynchronised brain waves.

REM sleep was first described in 1953 by Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman at the University of Chicago. In adults it occupies roughly 20–25 per cent of total sleep, occurring in episodes that recur about every 90 minutes through the night and grow longer toward morning; newborn infants spend around half their sleep in REM.

Physiology

During REM sleep the brain's electrical activity resembles wakefulness, while most voluntary muscles are paralysed — a state called atonia, which prevents the sleeper from acting out dreams. Breathing and heart rate become irregular, body temperature regulation is reduced, and the eyes dart in rapid movements that give the stage its name. Activity in the pons and related brainstem structures, involving cholinergic and monoaminergic neurotransmitter systems, drives the state.

Dreaming and function

Most vivid, story-like dreaming occurs during REM sleep, and awakening from REM commonly yields detailed dream reports. Proposed functions of REM sleep include memory consolidation — especially for procedural and emotional memories — brain development in infancy, and the processing of emotions. The full function remains debated among sleep researchers.

Disorders

REM sleep is implicated in several disorders. In REM sleep behaviour disorder, muscle atonia is lost and sleepers physically act out dreams; the condition is associated with later development of Parkinson's disease and related synucleinopathies. Narcolepsy involves intrusions of REM phenomena, such as cataplexy and sleep paralysis, into wakefulness. Suppression of REM is also a known effect of many antidepressant medications.
