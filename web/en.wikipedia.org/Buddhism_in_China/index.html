Buddhism in China

Introduced1st century CE (Han dynasty)
Major schoolsMahayana, Chan (Zen), Pure Land, Tiantai, Huayan
Key figuresKumārajīva, Xuanzang, Bodhidharma, Huineng

Buddhism in China has a history of roughly two thousand years. Buddhism entered China from India and Central Asia along the Silk Road during the Han dynasty, traditionally dated to the reign of Emperor Ming in the 1st century CE. Over the centuries it was profoundly transformed by Chinese culture, giving rise to distinctly Chinese schools such as Tiantai, Huayan, Chan and Pure Land, and it became one of the "three teachings" alongside Confucianism and Taoism.

Transmission and translation

Early translators such as Kumārajīva (344–413) rendered Sanskrit sutras into Chinese with great skill, shaping East Asian Buddhist vocabulary. The pilgrim Xuanzang (602–664) travelled to India and returned with hundreds of texts, founding the Faxiang school. Monasteries became centres of scholarship, art and printing; the world's earliest dated printed book, the Diamond Sutra (868 CE), is a Chinese Buddhist text.

Development

Buddhism flourished under the Tang dynasty, though it also faced state persecutions, most severely in 845 CE under Emperor Wuzong. Chan (better known by its Japanese name, Zen) and Pure Land became the most popular forms in later dynasties. Buddhist ideas deeply influenced Chinese philosophy, poetry, painting and architecture, producing landmarks such as the Longmen and Yungang grottoes and the Leshan Giant Buddha.

Modern period

Suppressed during the Cultural Revolution, Buddhism revived after the late 1970s and is today one of China's officially recognised religions, with tens of thousands of temples and a large lay following. It also spread from China to Korea, Japan and Vietnam, forming the East Asian Mahayana tradition. See also: Buddhism in Japan, Chan Buddhism.
