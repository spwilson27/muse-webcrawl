In Greco-Roman mythology, Aeneas was a Trojan hero, the son of the prince Anchises and the goddess Aphrodite (Venus). His father was a first cousin of King Priam of Troy, making Aeneas a second cousin to Priam's children. He is a character in Greek mythology and is mentioned in Homer's Iliad. Aeneas receives full treatment in Roman mythology, most extensively in Virgil's Aeneid, where he is cast as an ancestor of Romulus and Remus and thus of Rome itself.

In Greek tradition

In the Iliad, Aeneas is a leading Trojan warrior, repeatedly rescued by the gods; Poseidon foretells that he and his descendants will rule the Trojans. The Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite recounts his conception. He does not die at Troy in the Greek sources, and local traditions made him a king in the Troad after the war.

In the Aeneid

Virgil's epic follows Aeneas, epitomising pietas (dutiful piety), as he escapes the sack of Troy carrying his father and the household gods, loses his wife Creusa, and wanders the Mediterranean for seven years. Storm-driven to Carthage, he loves and then, at Jupiter's command, abandons Queen Dido, who curses him and kills herself. After a visit to the underworld at Cumae, where Anchises reveals Rome's future glory, Aeneas reaches Latium, marries Lavinia, and defeats Turnus in a war against the Rutulians — founding the line that leads to Alba Longa and Rome.

Legacy

Aeneas became a symbol of duty and Roman destiny; the Julian family claimed descent from his son Iulus. He appears in medieval Trojan romances, Dante (who places him among the virtuous), Shakespeare, and the operas of Purcell (Dido and Aeneas) and Berlioz (Les Troyens).
