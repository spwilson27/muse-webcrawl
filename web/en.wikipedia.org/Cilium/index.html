Cilium

TypeCell organelle
LocationEukaryotic cell surface
ComponentsMicrotubules, dynein, basal body

The cilium (plural cilia, from the Latin word for "eyelash") is a membrane-bound organelle found on most types of eukaryotic cells. Cilia are slender protuberances that project from the cell body and are built on a core scaffold of microtubules called the axoneme.

Types

Motile cilia beat in coordinated waves and serve to move fluid over a cell surface — as in the airways, where they clear mucus — or to propel single cells such as ciliated protists and sperm-like cells. Motile cilia usually have a "9+2" axoneme arrangement of nine outer microtubule doublets around a central pair. Primary (non-motile) cilia generally have a "9+0" arrangement and function as cellular antennae, sensing chemical and mechanical signals; nearly all vertebrate cells possess one.

Structure and movement

Each cilium grows from a basal body, a modified centriole. Bending is driven by the motor protein dynein, whose arms cause adjacent microtubule doublets to slide, constrained by links that convert sliding into bending. Proteins are moved along the cilium by intraflagellar transport (IFT).

Medical significance

Defects in ciliary structure or function cause a group of disorders called ciliopathies, including primary ciliary dyskinesia, polycystic kidney disease, and Bardet–Biedl syndrome.
