Molecular self-assembly

Molecular self-assembly is the process by which molecules adopt a defined arrangement without guidance or management from an outside source. It is a central concept in supramolecular chemistry and nanotechnology, describing how disordered collections of molecules spontaneously form ordered structures through local non-covalent interactions.

In molecular self-assembly, the final (stable) structure is encoded in the shape and properties of the molecules themselves; the components must be correctly designed so that the assembled state is thermodynamically favoured.

Types

Two types are distinguished: intramolecular self-assembly, in which a single molecule folds into a new shape (as in protein folding), and intermolecular self-assembly, in which two or more molecules combine to form a larger complex.

Self-assembly may also be described as static, where the ordered state appears as the system reaches equilibrium and requires no further energy input, or dynamic, where the ordered state is maintained only by continuous dissipation of energy, as in many biological systems.

Interactions and examples

The weak interactions exploited in self-assembly include hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, hydrophobic effects, pi-stacking, and electrostatic interactions; stronger, directional metal coordination bonds are also used.

Familiar examples include the formation of micelles and lipid bilayer membranes from amphiphiles, the folding of proteins, the crystallisation of molecules into ordered solids, and laboratory systems such as DNA nanotechnology structures, rotaxanes, catenanes, and metal–organic frameworks.

Applications

Because self-assembly constructs structures from the bottom up, it is pursued for nanofabrication, drug delivery (for example self-assembling peptide hydrogels), photovoltaics, and materials that can heal or reconfigure themselves. The concept was popularised in the broader literature by George Whitesides and others from the 1990s onward.
