Precarious work

Precarious work is a term that describes non-standard employment which is poorly paid, insecure, unprotected, and cannot support a household. In more recent terms, it may be described as "gig work" carried out through digital platforms. Though definitions vary, precarious work is generally characterised by short-term or temporary contracts, low wages, limited or no social benefits such as pensions and sick pay, lack of job security, and little protection against dismissal.

The growth of precarious work since the late twentieth century is associated with neoliberal labour-market deregulation, globalisation, the decline of trade union density, and the rise of the gig economy. Sociologists such as Guy Standing have argued that precarious workers form an emerging social class, the "precariat", whose insecurity extends beyond the workplace into housing, income and social identity.

Forms

Precarious work includes fixed-term and casual contracts, agency and on-call work, involuntary part-time work, dependent self-employment, and platform-mediated tasks such as ride-hailing and food delivery. Zero-hours contracts in the United Kingdom and "mini-jobs" in Germany are frequently cited national examples.

Effects

Research links precarious employment to poorer physical and mental health, income volatility, difficulty obtaining housing and credit, and delayed family formation. Insecurity is unevenly distributed: women, young people, migrants and ethnic minorities are over-represented in precarious jobs in most developed economies.

Responses

Policy responses have included minimum-wage legislation, restrictions on abusive contract forms, portable benefit schemes, platform-worker reclassification laws (such as in Spain and under EU directives), and renewed organising by unions among gig and service workers.
