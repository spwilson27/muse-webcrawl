Lunar north pole

The lunar north polar region imaged by orbiters

Location: 90° N on the Moon

Nearby features: Peary crater, Rozhdestvenskiy crater

The lunar north pole is the point on the Moon's northern hemisphere where the Moon's axis of rotation meets its surface. Because the Moon's axial tilt relative to the ecliptic is only about 1.54°, sunlight reaches the polar regions at very shallow angles, so crater floors near the poles can lie in permanent shadow while nearby crater rims enjoy near-perpetual illumination — the so-called peaks of eternal light.

Exploration and significance

Permanently shadowed craters near both lunar poles act as cold traps for volatiles. Radar and neutron-spectrometer data from orbiters such as Clementine, Lunar Prospector, Chandrayaan-1, and the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter indicate deposits of water ice in these shadowed regions, especially near the south pole but also around the north pole. The crater Peary, close to the north pole, has rims that are sunlit for an unusually large fraction of the lunar day, making the area a candidate site for a future lunar outpost with solar power and access to ice resources.

Detailed mosaics of the north polar region produced from Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Camera images have mapped terrain down to metre scales, supporting studies of polar illumination, temperature, and volatile stability.
