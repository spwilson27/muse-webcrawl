Thomas Robert Malthus (1766–1834) was an English cleric, scholar and influential economist in the fields of political economy and demography. In An Essay on the Principle of Population, first published anonymously in 1798 and greatly expanded in later editions, he argued that population, when unchecked, increases geometrically (exponentially) while food supply grows only arithmetically (linearly), so population growth will inevitably be checked by famine, disease and war ("positive checks") or by moral restraint and delayed marriage ("preventive checks").

Influence

The resulting prediction of inevitable scarcity became known as the "Malthusian trap" and influenced poor-law reform debates, classical economics, and evolutionary biology: both Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace credited reading Malthus with inspiring the theory of natural selection. Malthus also debated David Ricardo on rent, gluts and the Corn Laws, and from 1805 he taught at the East India Company's college at Haileybury, one of the first academic posts in political economy.

Criticism

Critics from Marx onward disputed Malthus's assumptions; agricultural productivity growth and the demographic transition have so far outrun his predictions in industrialised societies, though neo-Malthusian concerns about resources and environment periodically revive his name.
