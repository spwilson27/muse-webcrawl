Groundhog Day

Groundhog Day is a popular North American tradition observed in the United States and Canada on 2 February. According to folklore, if a groundhog emerges from its burrow on that day and sees its shadow, it will retreat into its den and winter will continue for six more weeks; if it does not see its shadow, spring will arrive early.

Origins

The tradition has roots in ancient European weather lore, in which a sacred animal — a bear, badger, or hedgehog — cast a shadow on Candlemas (2 February), predicting a longer winter. German settlers in Pennsylvania brought the custom with them, substituting the abundant local groundhog (woodchuck) as the prognosticator.

Punxsutawney Phil

The most famous celebration takes place in Punxsutawney, Pennsylvania, where a groundhog named Punxsutawney Phil is presented at Gobbler's Knob by members of the Punxsutawney Groundhog Club, some in top hats and tails. The ceremony, first recorded as a trek in the 1880s, now draws tens of thousands of visitors and extensive media coverage. Phil's handlers claim he communicates his prediction in "Groundhogese." Other notable groundhogs include Wiarton Willie in Ontario and Staten Island Chuck in New York.

Accuracy and popular culture

Studies by meteorological services have found the groundhogs' predictions accurate only about 30–40% of the time — no better than chance. The 1993 film Groundhog Day, starring Bill Murray as a weatherman reliving the same day repeatedly, popularised the holiday worldwide and made "Groundhog Day" a common metaphor for a monotonous, repeating situation.
