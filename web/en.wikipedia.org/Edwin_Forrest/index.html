Edwin Forrest

Edwin Forrest (March 9, 1806 – December 12, 1872) was an American actor of international renown, celebrated for his muscular, passionate performances in Shakespearean and heroic roles. The first American-born theatrical star, he became a symbol of Jacksonian democracy on stage. His rivalry with the English tragedian William Charles Macready culminated in the Astor Place Riot of 1849 in New York, in which more than twenty people were killed.

Career

Forrest made his professional debut in Philadelphia in 1820 and rose to fame in the later 1820s with roles including Othello, King Lear, Coriolanus, and Spartacus in The Gladiator, written for him by Robert Montgomery Bird. Determined to foster native drama, he offered prizes for American plays, producing works such as John Augustus Stone's Metamora (1829), in which his portrayal of the Wampanoag chief became one of his signature roles. Successful London engagements in 1836 and 1845 initially won him British acclaim.

Rivalry with Macready and the Astor Place Riot

A perceived slight during his second London tour — Forrest believed Macready had engineered a hostile reception, and hissed Macready's Hamlet in Edinburgh — turned professional rivalry into feud. When Macready performed Macbeth at New York's Astor Place Opera House on May 10, 1849, Forrest's working-class supporters, inflamed by nativist and class resentment against the genteel venue, rioted outside while Forrest played nearby. The state militia fired on the crowd, killing at least 22 people.

Later life

Forrest's protracted and scandalous divorce from his wife Catherine Sinclair in the 1850s damaged his reputation, though he remained a wealthy box-office draw and returned triumphantly in the 1860s. Childless, he endowed the Edwin Forrest Home for retired actors in Philadelphia, and the Forrest Theatre there is named for him.
