Freezing-point depression

Freezing-point depression is a drop in the maximum temperature at which a substance freezes, caused when a small amount of another, non-volatile substance is added. Examples include adding salt into water (used to de-ice roads), alcohol in water, or any foreign substance to a pure solvent. The phenomenon is a colligative property: it depends on the number of dissolved particles rather than their identity.

Explanation

Dissolving a solute lowers the chemical potential (and vapour pressure) of the liquid solvent while leaving the pure solid phase unaffected, so equilibrium between the solid and liquid is reached at a lower temperature. The magnitude of the depression is proportional to the molality of the solute: ΔTf = Kf · b · i, where Kf is the cryoscopic constant of the solvent and i is the van 't Hoff factor accounting for dissociation into ions.

Uses

Road salt exploits freezing-point depression to melt ice; calcium chloride can depress the freezing point of water far below that achievable with sodium chloride. Antifreeze such as ethylene glycol uses the same principle in engine coolant. Historically, measuring freezing-point depression (cryoscopy) was used to determine the molar mass of unknown substances.

Natural examples

Seawater freezes at about −2 °C because of its dissolved salts. Some polar fish produce antifreeze proteins that inhibit ice crystal growth in their blood, a related but distinct adaptation.
