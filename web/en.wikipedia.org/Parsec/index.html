A parsec is the distance at which one AU subtends one arcsecond.

The parsec (symbol: pc) is a unit of length used to measure the large distances to astronomical objects outside the Solar System. One parsec is approximately 3.26 light-years, or about 30.9 trillion kilometres. The name is a contraction of "parallax of one second" (or "parallax of one arcsecond"): a star at a distance of one parsec exhibits a parallax of one arcsecond as the Earth moves across its orbit, i.e. the distance at which one astronomical unit subtends an angle of one second of arc.

History and definition

The concept emerged from the first successful measurements of stellar parallax in 1838, by Friedrich Bessel, Friedrich Georg Wilhelm von Struve and Thomas Henderson. The term "parsec" itself was coined by the British astronomer Herbert Hall Turner in 1913. In 2015 the International Astronomical Union defined the parsec exactly as 648,000/π astronomical units, equal to 3.08567758149137 × 1016 metres.

Use

Distances within the Milky Way are commonly given in parsecs or kiloparsecs (kpc); distances to other galaxies in megaparsecs (Mpc) and gigaparsecs (Gpc). The nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is about 1.30 pc away. The Milky Way is roughly 30 kpc across. Despite the physical convenience of the light-year, professional astronomers prefer the parsec because it is directly tied to the observational technique of parallax, as embodied in the distance modulus and in data from missions such as Gaia.
