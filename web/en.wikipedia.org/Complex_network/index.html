Complex network

FieldNetwork science, graph theory, statistical physics
FeaturesNon-trivial topology: heavy-tailed degree distributions, clustering, community structure, short path lengths
LandmarksWatts & Strogatz (1998), Barabási & Albert (1999)
ApplicationsInternet, social networks, protein interactions, epidemics, power grids

In the context of network theory, a complex network is a graph (network) with non-trivial topological features — features that do not arise in simple lattices or Erdős–Rényi random graphs but do occur in networks representing real systems. The modern study of complex networks is a young, interdisciplinary field drawing on graph theory, statistical mechanics, computer science, sociology, and biology.

A complex network with hubs and community structure

Defining properties

  • Scale-free degree distribution — many real networks have degree distributions following a power law, with a few highly connected hubs.
  • Small-world effect — short average path lengths between nodes, modeled by the Watts–Strogatz model (1998), which also reproduces high clustering.
  • Clustering and community structure — nodes group into densely connected modules with sparser links between them.
  • Hierarchical structure, resilience, and growth — many networks grow by preferential attachment ("the rich get richer"), as in the Barabási–Albert model (1999), making them robust to random failures yet vulnerable to targeted attacks on hubs.

Examples

Canonical examples include the World Wide Web and Internet router graph, citation and collaboration networks, online social networks, metabolic and protein–protein interaction networks, neural networks of the brain, transportation and power networks, and ecological food webs. Network models have proved central to understanding epidemic spreading, where hub structure can eliminate the classic epidemic threshold.

History

The field builds on classical graph theory (Euler; Erdős and Rényi's random graphs) and sociological work on social networks, but crystallized around 1998–1999 when large datasets from the web and molecular biology revealed shared statistical patterns across domains, igniting "network science" as an organized discipline.
