The Timurid Empire was a Persianate Turco-Mongol empire founded by the conqueror Timur (Tamerlane) in 1370. At its greatest extent under Timur, it stretched from southeastern Turkey and Syria in the west to the borders of China in the east, encompassing Iran, Mesopotamia, Central Asia, and parts of the Indian subcontinent and the Caucasus. Its capitals, Samarkand and later Herat, became centres of a brilliant cultural flowering known as the Timurid Renaissance.

History

Timur, a member of the Barlas tribe from Transoxiana, rose to power in the 1360s and spent over three decades campaigning almost continuously. His armies sacked Delhi in 1398, defeated the Mamluks in Syria, and crushed the Ottoman sultan Bayezid I at the Battle of Ankara in 1402. He died in 1405 at Otrar while marching to invade Ming China.

After a succession struggle, Timur's son Shah Rukh consolidated the eastern empire from Herat, ruling until 1447. His son Ulugh Beg, ruler of Samarkand, was a distinguished astronomer who built a great observatory and produced a star catalogue. In the later 15th century, under Sultan Husayn Bayqara, Herat flourished as a hub of Persian literature, miniature painting (notably the master Bihzad), and architecture, while the empire gradually shrank. The Uzbeks under Muhammad Shaybani conquered Samarkand and Herat by 1507, ending the empire; one Timurid prince, Babur, went on to found the Mughal Empire in India in 1526.

Culture

The Timurids patronised Persian literature, historiography, calligraphy, and astronomy, and raised monumental architecture — the Gur-e Amir mausoleum and Bibi-Khanym mosque in Samarkand, and the great complexes of Herat — decorated with turquoise tilework that became emblematic of Central Asian Islamic art. Chagatai Turkic also emerged as a literary language under the poet Ali-Shir Nava'i.
