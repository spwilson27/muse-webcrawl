Dedekind domain

In abstract algebra, a Dedekind domain is an integral domain in which every non-zero proper ideal factors uniquely into a product of prime ideals. The concept, named after Richard Dedekind, generalises the fundamental theorem of arithmetic from the integers to a broader class of rings central to algebraic number theory.

Definition

A Dedekind domain can be characterised as an integral domain that is Noetherian, of Krull dimension one (every non-zero prime ideal is maximal), and integrally closed in its field of fractions. Equivalently, it is a Noetherian domain in which the localisation at every maximal ideal is a discrete valuation ring or a field.

Examples

Every principal ideal domain is a Dedekind domain; in particular the ring of integers ℤ and any field's polynomial ring are Dedekind. The most important examples are the rings of integers of number fields, such as the Gaussian integers ℤ[i]. Regular coordinate rings of smooth algebraic curves also form Dedekind domains.

Ideal factorisation

Although elements of a Dedekind domain need not factor uniquely into irreducibles — the ring ℤ[√−5] is the classic counterexample — ideals always do. The failure of unique element factorisation is measured by the ideal class group; a Dedekind domain is a unique factorisation domain if and only if its class group is trivial, equivalently if and only if it is a principal ideal domain.
