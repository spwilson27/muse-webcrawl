Naïve art

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Naïve art is any form of visual art that is created by a person who lacks the formal education and training that a professional artist undergoes in anatomy, art history, technique, perspective, and ways of seeing. It is characterised by a childlike simplicity of execution, a fresh, unsophisticated vision, bright colours, and often a strikingly inventive approach to composition despite (or because of) "incorrect" perspective and scale.

History

The style was championed in the early 20th century when modern artists celebrated the work of the French customs officer Henri Rousseau, whose jungle scenes and portraits Picasso and others admired. The German collector Wilhelm Uhde promoted Rousseau and other "sacred heart" painters, and later the Croatian school around Ivan Generalić and the Hlebine painters, the Serbian and Haitian schools, and the American folk painter Grandma Moses brought international fame to the genre.

Characteristics

Naïve works often show flattened space, pattern-like detail, disregard for vanishing points, and an intensity of colour and narrative. The term is looser than "folk art" or "outsider art": some naïve painters are fully aware of academic conventions and knowingly set them aside. Museums devoted to the style exist in Paris, Zagreb, and elsewhere. See also Henri Rousseau and Outsider art.
