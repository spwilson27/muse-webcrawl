Island arc

An island arc forms where oceanic crust subducts beneath other oceanic crust.

An island arc is a type of archipelago formed by plate tectonics as one oceanic tectonic plate subducts under another and produces magma — a long chain of active volcanoes with intense seismic activity found along convergent tectonic plate boundaries.

Formation

As the descending slab warms, volatiles — principally water — are driven off and lower the melting point of the overlying mantle wedge, generating magma that rises to form volcanoes roughly 100 km above the slab. The volcanic islands emerge in an arc parallel to the ocean trench that marks the plate boundary, typically separated from it by a forearc basin, and bounded on the other side by a deep back-arc basin.

Examples

Modern island arcs include the Aleutian Islands, the Japanese archipelago, the Philippines, the Mariana Islands, the Lesser Antilles, the South Sandwich Islands, Tonga and Vanuatu. Ancient island arcs preserved on land are important evidence used by geologists to reconstruct former ocean basins.
