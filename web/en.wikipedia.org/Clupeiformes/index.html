The Clupeiformes are an order of ray-finned fishes that includes the herrings, shads, sardines, anchovies, and menhadens. Members of the order are typically small, silvery, schooling fishes that feed on plankton, and many species are of enormous importance to commercial fisheries and marine food webs.

Characteristics

Clupeiform fishes generally have a streamlined body covered in silvery cycloid scales that are easily shed, a single dorsal fin, and a deeply forked tail. A distinctive feature of the group is the connection between the swim bladder and the inner ear, which gives them exceptionally acute hearing; some can detect the high-frequency sounds produced by echolocating predators such as dolphins. Most species lack a lateral line on the body, and many filter plankton through long, fine gill rakers.

Families and distribution

The order contains several families, including the Clupeidae (herrings, sardines, and shads), the Engraulidae (anchovies), and the Pristigasteridae. They are found worldwide in coastal waters and the open ocean, and a number of species live in fresh water or migrate between the sea and rivers. The Peruvian anchoveta, a member of this order, has at times been the most heavily harvested fish species in the world.

Ecology

By converting plankton into fish biomass, clupeiforms form a crucial link between primary producers and larger predators, including tuna, seabirds, and marine mammals. Fluctuations in their populations, driven by climate patterns such as El Niño, can reshape entire coastal ecosystems.
