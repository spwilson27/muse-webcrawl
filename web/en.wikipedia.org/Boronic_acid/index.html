Boronic acid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A boronic acid is an alkyl- or aryl-substituted boric acid containing a carbon–boron bond, belonging to the larger class of organoboranes. Boronic acids act as Lewis acids: the boron atom has an empty p orbital and a trigonal planar geometry in the neutral acid, readily accepting a hydroxide ion to form a tetrahedral boronate anion. They generally have the formula R–B(OH)2.

Suzuki coupling

Boronic acids are best known as the coupling partners in the Suzuki–Miyaura reaction, a palladium-catalyzed cross-coupling with aryl or vinyl halides that forms carbon–carbon bonds. The reaction's mild conditions, the stability and low toxicity of boronic acids, and their tolerance of many functional groups have made it one of the most widely used reactions in pharmaceutical and materials synthesis; Akira Suzuki shared the 2010 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the work.

Other applications

Boronic acids bind reversibly to diols, including sugars, which has been exploited in sensors for glucose, in carbohydrate recognition, and in dynamic covalent chemistry. They also inhibit certain enzymes: the proteasome inhibitor bortezomib, used to treat multiple myeloma, contains a boronic acid warhead, as do the β-lactamase inhibitor vaborbactam and the antifungal tavaborole.

Synthesis and handling

Boronic acids are commonly prepared by reacting borate esters with Grignard or organolithium reagents, by hydroboration routes, or by palladium-catalyzed borylation of aryl halides (the Miyaura borylation). On heating, boronic acids dehydrate to cyclic trimers called boroxines.
