Kraków is the second-largest and one of the oldest cities in Poland, situated on the Vistula River in the Lesser Poland region. The traditional capital of the Kingdom of Poland and the country's capital until 1596, it remains Poland's foremost centre of culture, academia, and tourism, with a remarkably preserved medieval old town centred on the largest medieval town square in Europe.

History

Legend attributes Kraków's founding to the mythical ruler Krakus, said to have slain the Wawel Dragon. A major trading settlement by the 10th century, it became the seat of Polish kings, crowned and buried in Wawel Cathedral. Devastated by the Mongol invasion of 1241, the city was rebuilt on a grid plan around the Main Market Square (Rynek Główny), with the Cloth Hall (Sukiennice) and St Mary's Basilica — from whose tower the hejnał bugle call is still played hourly. Casimir III the Great founded the Jagiellonian University in 1364, one of Europe's oldest, where Nicolaus Copernicus later studied.

The royal court moved to Warsaw in 1596, and Kraków passed through the partitions of Poland to Austrian rule. In the Second World War it became the capital of the Nazi General Government under Hans Frank; its Jewish community of Kazimierz was destroyed in the Holocaust, a history partly told through Oskar Schindler's factory. The communist-era steel district of Nowa Huta, built after 1949, later became a centre of Solidarity resistance. Karol Wojtyła, Archbishop of Kraków, became Pope John Paul II in 1978.

Culture and sights

Kraków's historic centre was among the first sites inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1978. Highlights include Wawel Castle and Cathedral, the Barbican and Florian Gate, Kazimierz's synagogues, and hundreds of churches and museums. The city hosts major festivals and was European Capital of Culture in 2000; nearby lie the Wieliczka Salt Mine and the Auschwitz-Birkenau memorial.
