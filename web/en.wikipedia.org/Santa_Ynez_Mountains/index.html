The Santa Ynez Mountains are a portion of the Transverse Ranges on the California coast, running roughly east–west for about 100 kilometres (60 mi) through Santa Barbara County and into Ventura County. Unusually for North American ranges, they trend east–west because of the bend in the San Andreas Fault system. The mountains form the dramatic backdrop to the city of Santa Barbara and shelter the Santa Ynez Valley and its wine country on their northern side.

Geology and geography

The range is composed mainly of sedimentary rocks — sandstone, shale and conglomerate — deposited in a marine basin and uplifted rapidly over the past several million years. Its southern face rises steeply from the coastal plain, dissected by canyons carrying creeks toward the Pacific. Much of the range lies within Los Padres National Forest, including the San Rafael and Dick Smith wilderness areas to the northeast.

Climate, ecology and fire

The mountains support chaparral, oak woodland and coastal sage scrub, with riparian corridors of sycamore and oak. The range is known for the "sundowner" winds — hot, dry downslope winds that descend toward Santa Barbara, usually in the late afternoon or evening, and which have contributed to destructive wildfires such as the Painted Cave Fire of 1990 and the Thomas Fire of 2017, among the largest in California history.

History

The Chumash people inhabited the region for millennia and left painted caves in the range, including Painted Cave State Historic Park. Cold Spring Tavern, on San Marcos Pass, was a stagecoach stop on the route over the mountains. The pass roads, Gibraltar Reservoir and Lake Cachuma remain important to the region's water supply and recreation.
