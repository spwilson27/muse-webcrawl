Endowment effect

The endowment effect is a finding in behavioral economics and psychology that people tend to assign more value to things merely because they own them. In other words, people are more willing to retain an object they own than to acquire the same object when they do not own it. The effect is closely related to loss aversion and to the status quo bias.

Overview

The endowment effect was first described by economist Richard Thaler, who connected it with prospect theory, developed by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky. Under prospect theory, losses loom larger than equivalent gains; giving up an owned good is experienced as a loss, while acquiring it is experienced as a gain, so owners demand more to part with an item than buyers are willing to pay for it.

A classic experiment by Kahneman, Jack Knetsch, and Thaler (1990) gave students coffee mugs and then allowed trading. The median selling price demanded by owners was roughly twice the median buying price offered by non-owners, even though the mugs had been assigned randomly and minutes earlier. Far fewer trades occurred than standard economic theory predicts, suggesting that ownership itself creates value.

Explanations

Several mechanisms have been proposed. Loss aversion holds that parting with a good is coded as a loss relative to the reference point of current ownership. Psychological ownership and mere exposure may also play a role, as may reluctance to trade in situations of uncertainty about value. Critics have argued that some demonstrations of the effect reflect experimental artifacts, misunderstanding of instructions, or attachment formed during evaluation rather than ownership per se, and the size of the effect varies with the good, the market experience of participants, and the elicitation procedure.

Applications

The endowment effect has implications for markets, negotiations, environmental policy, and the law. It helps explain why willingness to accept compensation for losing an amenity often far exceeds willingness to pay to obtain it, a gap that complicates cost-benefit analysis. In marketing, free trials and money-back guarantees exploit the effect: once consumers feel they own a product, they value it more highly.
