Seven deadly sins

The seven deadly sins, also known as the capital vices or cardinal sins.

The seven deadly sins, also known as the capital vices or cardinal sins, are a grouping and classification of vices within Christian teachings. Although they are not directly mentioned in the Bible, behaviours or habits classified under this categorisation are used to fester other immoralities. According to the standard list, they are pride, greed, wrath, envy, lust, gluttony and sloth, which are contrary to the seven capital virtues.

History

The concept originated with the fourth-century monk Evagrius Ponticus, who listed eight evil thoughts (logismoi) that monks had to guard against. His list was later translated and adapted by John Cassian, and in 590 CE Pope Gregory I revised the list to form the more familiar seven: vainglory was merged into pride, and envy was added. The seven deadly sins were widely popularised in medieval theology and literature, most famously in Dante Alighieri's Divine Comedy, where they structure the terraces of Purgatory.

The sins

Pride is considered the original and most serious of the seven, the source of the others. Greed is an excessive desire for wealth or possessions. Wrath is uncontrolled anger and hatred. Envy is resentful covetousness of another's traits, status, abilities or rewards. Lust is an intense or uncontrolled desire, usually sexual. Gluttony is excessive consumption, especially of food and drink. Sloth is spiritual or emotional apathy and laziness, a failure to act or to make use of one's talents.

Capital virtues

Each deadly sin has a corresponding virtue: humility counters pride, charity counters greed, patience counters wrath, kindness counters envy, chastity counters lust, temperance counters gluttony, and diligence counters sloth.
