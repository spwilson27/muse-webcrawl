Revenue Act of 1913

The Revenue Act of 1913, also known as the Underwood Tariff or the Underwood–Simmons Act (ch. 16, 38 Stat. 114), re-imposed the federal income tax in the United States and substantially lowered tariff rates. The act was sponsored by Representative Oscar Underwood, passed by the 63rd United States Congress, and signed into law by President Woodrow Wilson on October 3, 1913.

Background

The act formed part of President Wilson's "New Freedom" programme, which also included the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 and antitrust legislation. Ratification of the Sixteenth Amendment in February 1913 had removed the constitutional obstacle erected by the Supreme Court's decision in Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co. (1895), allowing Congress to levy an income tax without apportioning it among the states by population.

Provisions

Income tax

The act imposed a federal income tax of 1 percent on incomes above $3,000 for individuals ($4,000 for married couples), with a graduated surtax rising to a maximum of 6 percent on incomes over $500,000. Because of the high exemption, fewer than 2 percent of households paid any income tax in the first year. A 1 percent tax was also applied to corporate net income.

Tariffs

The act substantially reduced average tariff rates, from about 40 percent to about 26 percent, and placed many items, including wool, steel and agricultural products, on the free list. It was the first significant downward revision of protective tariffs since before the Civil War.

Legacy

The income tax provisions of the Revenue Act of 1913 established the foundation of the modern United States federal income tax system, administered by the Bureau of Internal Revenue. Although rates rose sharply during the First World War, the structure of a progressive tax on personal income above an exemption survived.
