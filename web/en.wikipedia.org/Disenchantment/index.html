Disenchantment

In social science, disenchantment (German: Entzauberung) is the cultural rationalization and devaluation of religion and mysticism apparent in modern society. The term was borrowed by Max Weber from Friedrich Schiller to describe the character of a modernized, bureaucratic, secularized Western society, where scientific understanding is more highly valued than belief, and where processes are oriented toward rational goals rather than traditional or charismatic authority.

Weber's account

Weber introduced the concept in his 1917 lecture "Science as a Vocation", arguing that the increasing intellectualization and rationalization of the world means "there are no mysterious incalculable forces that come into play… one can, in principle, master all things by calculation". For Weber this loss of enchantment was bound up with the rise of capitalism, bureaucracy, and the Protestant ethic, and it carried costs: a world rendered calculable could also feel meaningless.

Criticism and legacy

Later thinkers debated whether modernity is truly disenchanted. Critics note the persistence and reinvention of religion, magic, and myth in consumer culture, politics, and technology, speaking of "re-enchantment"; others, following Weber, link disenchantment to alienation and the "iron cage" of rationalized life. The concept remains central to sociology of religion and theories of secularization.
