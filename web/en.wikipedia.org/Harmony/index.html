Harmony

In music, harmony is the concept of combining different sounds together in order to create new, distinct musical ideas. The study of harmony involves describing the construction of chords (groups of three or more notes), chord progressions (sequences of chords), and the principles of counterpoint and voice leading that govern them. Harmony is the "vertical" aspect of music, as distinguished from melody, its "horizontal" aspect.

Historical development

In the Middle Ages, harmony was understood mainly through intervals and the modes of Gregorian chant; the systematic treatment of simultaneous sonorities grew with organum and early polyphony. Jean-Philippe Rameau's Treatise on Harmony (1722) founded modern harmonic theory by explaining chords as built in thirds above a fundamental bass. In the common practice period (roughly 1600–1900), harmony became tonal: chords were organized around a tonic, and progressions were understood functionally, with dominant chords resolving to the tonic to create tension and release.

Consonance and dissonance

Combinations of tones are traditionally classified as consonant (stable, restful — such as the perfect fifth and major third) or dissonant (unstable, requiring resolution — such as the minor second and tritone). These categories reflect both the physics of the harmonic series, in which simple frequency ratios sound smooth, and cultural learning; the treatment of dissonance relaxed steadily from Renaissance counterpoint through the emancipation of dissonance in twentieth-century music.

Types of harmony

Harmony may be diatonic (using only the notes of the governing scale) or chromatic (introducing outside notes); it includes practices such as modal harmony, quartal harmony (chords built in fourths, common in jazz and in composers such as Debussy), and the extended tertian chords of jazz. Analyses of harmony in Western music often use Roman numeral analysis to label chords by their scale degree and function.
