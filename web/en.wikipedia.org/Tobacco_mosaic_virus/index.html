Tobacco mosaic virus

Tobacco mosaic virus
Group: Group IV ((+)ssRNA)
Family: Virgaviridae
Genus: Tobamovirus

Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) is a single-stranded RNA virus of the genus Tobamovirus that infects a wide range of plants, especially tobacco and other members of the family Solanaceae. It causes a characteristic mottled, mosaic-like discoloration of leaves and stunted growth. TMV was the first virus ever identified, in work by Martinus Beijerinck in 1898 building on experiments by Dmitri Ivanovsky, and it became a foundational model organism of virology.

Structure

The virion is a rigid rod about 300 nm long and 18 nm in diameter, made of 2,130 identical coat-protein subunits arranged helically around a single molecule of positive-sense RNA about 6,400 bases long. Wendell Stanley crystallised the virus in 1935, showing that an infectious agent could be crystallised like a chemical, and Rosalind Franklin later determined key features of its structure by X-ray diffraction.

Transmission and control

TMV is exceptionally stable and spreads mechanically — on tools, hands, and clothing — rather than by insect vectors, and it can persist in dried plant material and tobacco products for years. Control relies on sanitation, resistant cultivars, and removal of infected plants, as no cure exists for infected crops.
