Forest law

Forest law was a body of law in medieval England, introduced by the Norman kings after the conquest of 1066, governing land designated as royal forest. Contrary to the modern sense, a medieval "forest" was a legal status rather than necessarily wooded ground: any area could be afforested—placed under forest law—including moorland, heath, and farmland. The law reserved hunting of game (the "venison") and protection of its cover (the "vert") to the king, and sharply restricted the rights of those who lived within the forest.

Royal forests were hunting preserves governed by forest law.

Administration and offences

Forests were administered by foresters, verderers, and itinerant forest courts, including the severe forest eyre. Offences included hunting deer, clearing woodland (assarting), and enclosing land (purpresture), punishable by heavy fines and, in the earliest Norman period, by blinding or death. William the Conqueror and his successors afforested vast areas—the New Forest in Hampshire being the most famous—and by the late 12th century perhaps a third of England lay under forest law.

Reform and decline

Resentment of forest law contributed to the baronial revolt against King John; Magna Carta (1215) addressed some grievances, and the Charter of the Forest (1217), issued under Henry III, reduced penalties (abolishing death and mutilation for taking venison), ordered disafforestation of forests created by John and Richard, and restored common rights. Forest law decayed in later centuries, though remnants survived into the modern era in the New Forest and the Forest of Dean.
