The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) was a court-like restorative justice body assembled in South Africa in 1996 after the end of apartheid. Authorized by the Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act 34 of 1995, and chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu, witnesses who were identified as victims of gross human rights violations were invited to give statements about their experiences, and some were selected for public hearings. Perpetrators of violence could also give testimony and request amnesty from both civil and criminal prosecution.

Committees and work

The TRC operated through three committees. The Human Rights Violations Committee investigated abuses between 1960 and 1994, taking some 21,000 victim statements. The Amnesty Committee considered applications from individuals who made full disclosure of acts associated with a political objective; of more than 7,000 applications, roughly 850 were granted. The Reparations and Rehabilitation Committee recommended measures for victims, though payment of the recommended reparations was limited and delayed, a persistent source of criticism. Public hearings, broadcast widely, became a defining national ritual of the transition.

Report and legacy

The commission's five-volume final report was handed to President Nelson Mandela on 29 October 1998. It held the apartheid state primarily responsible for gross violations, while also finding the African National Congress and other liberation movements accountable for abuses, including in their exile camps — findings the ANC briefly sought to block in court. The TRC became an influential global model of transitional justice, inspiring truth commissions elsewhere, though scholars debate whether truth-telling delivered reconciliation or justice, and note that most perpetrators never applied for amnesty and few prosecutions followed.
