Self-awareness

Self-awareness is the capacity to recognise oneself as an individual separate from the environment and from other individuals — to be the object of one's own attention. It ranges from a basic bodily sense of self to reflective awareness of one's own thoughts, feelings, traits and mental states, and it underpins introspection, self-evaluation and much of social cognition.

Development

Developmental psychologists trace self-awareness through stages in infancy: newborns distinguish self-produced from external touch, and by about 15–24 months most children pass the mirror test — recognising a mark placed on their own face in a mirror as their own. Gordon Gallup Jr. introduced the mirror test for animals in 1970; great apes, dolphins, elephants and magpies are among the species reported to pass, though the test's meaning remains debated. Subsequent development brings self-conscious emotions such as pride and embarrassment, an autobiographical sense of self, and awareness of how one appears to others.

Psychology and neuroscience

Objective self-awareness theory (Duval and Wicklund, 1972) holds that attention directed at the self prompts comparison with standards, producing either behaviour change or discomfort. Excessive self-focused attention features in social anxiety, depression and rumination. Neural correlates implicate midline cortical structures, including the medial prefrontal cortex and posterior cingulate, and the insula for interoceptive awareness. Disorders of self-awareness range from anosognosia — unawareness of one's own disability after brain injury — to the depersonalisation of certain psychiatric conditions.

Philosophy

Philosophers from Descartes onward have treated self-awareness as foundational: the cogito makes one's own existence the one certainty. Later thinkers debated whether self-knowledge is direct or inferential, and contemporary philosophy of mind distinguishes pre-reflective from reflective self-consciousness in analysing subjective experience.
