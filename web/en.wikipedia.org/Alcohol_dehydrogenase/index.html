Alcohol dehydrogenase

Alcohol dehydrogenases (ADH) (EC 1.1.1.1) are a family of dehydrogenase enzymes occurring in many organisms that facilitate the interconversion of alcohols with aldehydes or ketones, using the reduction of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) to NADH. In humans, alcohol dehydrogenase in the stomach and liver performs the first step of ethanol metabolism, oxidising ethanol to acetaldehyde, which is then converted to acetate by aldehyde dehydrogenase.

In humans

Human ADHs are dimeric zinc-containing enzymes organised into several classes encoded by genes on chromosome 4 (ADH1A, ADH1B, ADH1C, ADH4, ADH5, ADH6, ADH7). Genetic variants of ADH1B and ALDH2 influence alcohol metabolism and flushing responses and are associated with differing risks of alcohol dependence across populations.

Role in other organisms

In yeast and many bacteria, alcohol dehydrogenase runs in the reverse physiological direction, producing ethanol during fermentation: pyruvate is decarboxylated to acetaldehyde, which ADH reduces to ethanol, regenerating NAD+. Fruit flies use their Adh enzyme to detoxify alcohols in fermenting fruit.

Clinical relevance

Because ADH also metabolises other alcohols, competitive inhibition of ADH by fomepizole (or ethanol itself) is used as an antidote in methanol and ethylene glycol poisoning, preventing formation of their toxic metabolites.
