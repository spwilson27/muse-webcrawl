Seaside resort

A classic British seaside resort promenade.

A seaside resort is a town located on the coast that is a popular place for day-trippers and holidaymakers in the summer. Typically, a British seaside resort features a beach, a promenade, amusement arcades, piers, and fish and chip shops. The phenomenon arose in the 18th century and reached its height during the Victorian era and the first half of the 20th century.

Origins

Sea bathing was promoted for its health benefits in the early 1700s, when physicians recommended immersion in cold seawater. Scarborough, in Yorkshire, claims to be Britain's first seaside resort, with visitors drawn by its spa waters and, from the 1730s, its bathing machines. Brighton became fashionable after visits by the Prince Regent, later George IV, who built the Royal Pavilion there.

Victorian boom

The spread of the railways from the 1840s brought coastal towns within reach of industrial cities, and the Bank Holidays Act of 1871 gave workers paid leisure. Resorts such as Blackpool, Southend-on-Sea, Margate and Llandudno grew explosively, developing the standard pattern of pier, winter gardens, tower, illuminations and rows of guest houses.

Decline and revival

From the 1960s, cheap package holidays to the Mediterranean drew visitors abroad, and many resorts suffered decline, derelict attractions and seasonal unemployment. In the 21st century some towns have revived through arts-led regeneration, heritage funding and domestic "staycations", while others continue to struggle.

Elsewhere

Comparable resorts developed worldwide: Atlantic City and Coney Island in the United States, Ostend in Belgium, the French Riviera, Scheveningen in the Netherlands, and the Baltic and Black Sea coasts of Europe.
