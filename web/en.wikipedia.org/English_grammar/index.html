English grammar

English grammar is the set of structural rules of the English language. This includes the structure of words, phrases, clauses, sentences, and whole texts. English is a relatively analytic language: it relies heavily on word order and auxiliary verbs rather than on inflection. Compared with its ancestor Old English, Modern English has lost most grammatical gender and case endings.

Word classes

Traditional grammar recognizes eight parts of speech: nouns, pronouns, adjectives, verbs, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions and interjections. Modern analyses add categories such as determinatives. Nouns inflect for number and a genitive case; verbs distinguish tense, aspect and mood largely through auxiliaries such as have, be and modal verbs.

Syntax

The unmarked word order of English declarative clauses is subject–verb–object (SVO). Questions are formed by subject–auxiliary inversion, and negation normally requires the auxiliary do. Clauses combine through coordination and subordination, and relative clauses typically follow the noun they modify.

Morphology

English inflection is sparse: regular verbs have four forms (for example walk, walks, walked, walking), and pronouns preserve remnants of case (I/me, he/him). Derivation by affixes such as -ness and un-, compounding and conversion are highly productive ways of forming new words.

History of description

The first grammar of English, William Bullokar's Pamphlet for Grammar, appeared in 1586. Influential later works include Lindley Murray's English Grammar (1795) and modern reference grammars such as The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language (2002).
