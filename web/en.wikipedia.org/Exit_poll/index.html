Exit poll

An exit poll is a poll of voters taken immediately after they have exited the polling stations. A similar poll conducted before actual voters have voted is called an entrance poll. Pollsters – usually private companies working for newspapers or broadcasters – conduct exit polls to gain an early indication of how an election turned out, as in many elections the actual result may take hours or even days to count.

History

One of the first known exit polls was a straw poll taken in Delaware in 1792. Modern exit polling was developed by Warren Mitofsky, who conducted the first major exit poll for CBS News in a 1967 Kentucky gubernatorial election. In the United States, national exit polls have been run jointly by media consortia (now the National Election Pool).

Purpose

Exit polls not only project winners but also collect demographic data on voters and their reasons for voting, allowing analysis of why the electorate voted as it did. They are also used to check the official results and detect possible irregularities.

Criticism and errors

Exit polls have sometimes diverged noticeably from results — for example, the 2000 and 2004 US elections, the 2014 Indian general election, and the Brexit referendum in the UK in 2016 — because of nonresponse bias, differential refusal, early/absentee voters being missed, and sampling variation. Several countries restrict publication of exit polls before voting ends.
