Forestry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Forestry is the science and craft of creating, managing, planting, using, conserving, and repairing forests and woodlands for associated resources for human and environmental benefits. Forestry is practiced in plantations and natural stands. The science of forestry has elements that belong to the biological, physical, social, political, and managerial sciences.

History

The preindustrial practice of forestry developed over centuries in many cultures, with forest reserves set aside for hunting and timber in medieval Europe and regulated cutting in Imperial China and Japan. Modern scientific forestry emerged in 18th- and 19th-century Germany, where foresters such as Heinrich Cotta developed systematic approaches to sustained-yield management, and was exported to British India and the United States, where the United States Forest Service was founded in 1905 under Gifford Pinchot.

Practice

Foresters plan and carry out forest management for purposes including timber extraction, wildlife habitat, water quality, recreation, and carbon sequestration. Key practices include silviculture (the growing and cultivation of trees), forest inventory and mensuration, reforestation after harvesting or disturbance, thinning of overcrowded stands, and prescribed burning.

Forest management plans typically balance ecological, economic, and social objectives over rotations that may last from a few years for fast-growing plantations to more than a century for some hardwood forests.

Forest products and ecology

Forests supply timber, pulpwood for paper, fuelwood, and non-timber products such as resins, fruits, and medicinal plants. Forests also store large quantities of carbon, regulate water cycles, and shelter a majority of terrestrial biodiversity, which has made deforestation and forest degradation central issues in environmental policy.
