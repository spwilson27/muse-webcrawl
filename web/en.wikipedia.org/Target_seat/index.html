Target seat

In politics, a target seat is a constituency that a political party designates as a priority for winning at an upcoming election, typically because the incumbent majority is small enough that the seat is considered realistically winnable with a concentrated campaign effort. Parties draw up target lists, ranked by the swing required, and direct disproportionate resources — candidate selection attention, activist deployment, direct mail, and leader visits — to those seats.

Strategy

Targeting is central to campaigning under first-past-the-post systems such as those of the United Kingdom and Canada, where national vote share matters less than winning marginal constituencies. A typical strategy divides seats into defensive targets (held but vulnerable) and offensive targets (held by opponents but winnable), with "super-marginals" requiring less than a 1–2% swing at the top of the list. Resources are usually allocated down the list until the party judges the marginal return no longer justifies further spending.

Effects

Heavy targeting can produce localized swings well above the national trend, and targeted seats receive measurably more campaign contact than safe seats. Critics argue that targeting neglects voters in safe constituencies and entrenches the marginal-seat bias of electoral politics. In list and proportional systems the concept is weaker, though parties may still target regions or specific list positions.
