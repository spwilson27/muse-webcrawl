Non-Desarguesian plane

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A non-Desarguesian plane is a projective plane in which Desargues's theorem does not hold. Desargues's theorem — that two triangles perspective from a point are perspective from a line — is automatically true in projective spaces of three or more dimensions and in any plane that can be coordinatized by a division ring. Its failure in some abstract projective planes shows that the incidence axioms of plane geometry alone are too weak to prove the theorem, a fact central to David Hilbert's programme of founding geometry on axioms.

Background

Every projective plane can be coordinatized by a ternary ring or related algebraic structure, and the algebraic properties of that structure correspond to geometric configuration theorems. Planes over division rings are exactly the Desarguesian planes. To obtain non-Desarguesian examples one must therefore use weaker coordinate structures. Hilbert gave the first construction, and the subject grew into the study of translation planes, nearfield planes and other classes.

Examples

The best-known example is the Moulton plane (1902), obtained by "bending" lines of negative slope as they cross the y-axis of the ordinary Euclidean plane, then completing to a projective plane; Desargues's theorem visibly fails for suitable configurations. Other families include the Hughes planes, Hall planes (constructed from nearfields), André planes, and planes coordinatized by quasifields and semifields. The smallest finite non-Desarguesian planes have order 9; all planes of order up to 9 except order 9 itself are classical, and the four planes of order 9 include three non-Desarguesian examples.
