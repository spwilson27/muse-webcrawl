North Cascades National Park

North Cascades National Park
Location: Washington, United States
Area: 504,781 acres (2,042.8 km²)
Established: October 2, 1968
Visitors: about 40,000 (recent years)

North Cascades National Park is an American national park in the state of Washington. The park forms part of the North Cascades National Park Service Complex, which also includes the Ross Lake and Lake Chelan National Recreation Areas. It is noted for its rugged alpine terrain, dense forests, and more than 300 glaciers, the largest concentration in the contiguous United States.

Geography

The park is divided into northern and southern units by the Skagit River and Ross Lake. Peaks such as Mount Shuksan, Mount Triumph, and Eldorado Peak rise steeply from forested valleys. Colonial, Pyramid, and Boston glaciers are among the hundreds of glaciers that mantle the range; their number and area have declined noticeably with climate warming.

Ecology

The park spans a dramatic west-to-east moisture gradient, from temperate rainforest on the western slopes to dry ponderosa pine forest in the east. It protects grizzly bears, gray wolves, wolverines, lynx, and more than 200 bird species, and contains some of the most biologically diverse plant communities in North America.

Human history and recreation

Indigenous peoples have lived in the region for at least 8,000–10,000 years. Miners, trappers, and homesteaders arrived in the nineteenth century, and hydroelectric dams on the Skagit predate the park. Established on October 2, 1968, the park is one of the least visited national parks, largely because few roads penetrate it; State Route 20, the North Cascades Highway, is its principal access. The Pacific Crest Trail crosses the park's eastern side.
