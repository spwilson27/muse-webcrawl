Tidal locking

Tidal locking is the phenomenon by which the rotational period of an astronomical body matches its orbital period around a partner, so that the same hemisphere always faces the body it is orbiting. The best-known example is Earth's Moon, whose far side remained unseen until photographed by the Soviet Luna 3 probe in 1959.

Mechanism

The gravity of the primary raises tides in the satellite. If the satellite rotates faster than it orbits, the tidal bulges are carried ahead of the line between the bodies, and the gravitational pull of the primary on the offset bulges creates a torque that brakes the rotation. If it rotates more slowly, the torque acts in the opposite direction. Either way, rotation evolves towards synchrony. Because tidal forces fall off steeply with distance (roughly as the inverse cube), locking happens quickly for close satellites and slowly, if at all, for distant ones. In the Solar System, all the large moons close to their planets are tidally locked, and Pluto and its moon Charon are mutually locked, each keeping the same face towards the other.

Variations

Mercury was long assumed to be locked 1:1 to the Sun, but radar observations in 1965 showed it is instead in a 3:2 spin–orbit resonance, rotating three times for every two orbits — a stable alternative outcome for eccentric orbits. Similar resonance captures are possible for exoplanets. Tidally locked exoplanets in the habitable zones of red dwarfs are of special interest for climate studies, since one side experiences perpetual day and the other perpetual night, possibly with a temperate "terminator" region.
