In Greek mythology, Daedalus (Greek: Δαίδαλος, "cunningly wrought") was a skillful architect and craftsman, seen as a symbol of wisdom, knowledge and power. He is the father of Icarus, the uncle and teacher of Perdix, and possibly also the father of Iapyx.

Mythology

The Labyrinth

Summoned to Crete by King Minos, Daedalus built the Labyrinth at Knossos to imprison the Minotaur, the half-man, half-bull offspring of Queen Pasiphaë. He also gave Ariadne the ball of thread — the "clue" — by which Theseus found his way out after slaying the monster. To prevent Daedalus from revealing the Labyrinth's secrets, Minos imprisoned the craftsman and his son Icarus in a high tower.

The flight from Crete

Unable to escape by land or sea, Daedalus fashioned wings of feathers held together with wax and thread. He warned Icarus to fly neither too low, lest the sea dampen the wings, nor too high, lest the sun melt the wax. Icarus, exhilarated, flew too close to the sun; the wax melted and he fell into the sea, thereafter called the Icarian Sea. Daedalus flew on to Sicily, where he was received by King Cocalus. Minos, pursuing him, was killed in Sicily — in one account scalded in his bath by Cocalus's daughters, who had been taught by Daedalus.

Inventions

Ancient writers credited Daedalus with the invention of carpentry, the saw, the axe, the plumb-line and other tools, and with creating statues so lifelike they seemed to move. In Athenian tradition he was of the royal house of Erechtheus and fled Athens after killing his nephew and pupil Perdix (or Talos), whom he pushed from the Acropolis out of envy of the boy's inventions; Athena saved Perdix by turning him into a partridge.

Legacy

Daedalus has remained an emblem of the inventor and the ambivalence of human ingenuity. He appears in Ovid's Metamorphoses, Virgil's Aeneid, and Dante, and lends his name to Stephen Dedalus in the works of James Joyce.
