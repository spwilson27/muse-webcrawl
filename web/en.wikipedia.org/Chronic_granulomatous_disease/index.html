Chronic granulomatous disease

Chronic granulomatous disease (CGD), also known as Bridges–Good syndrome or Quie syndrome, is a diverse group of hereditary diseases in which certain cells of the immune system have difficulty forming the reactive oxygen compounds (most importantly the superoxide radical due to a defective phagocytic NADPH oxidase) used to kill certain ingested pathogens. This leads to the formation of granulomata in many organs.

Signs and symptoms

CGD affects about 1 in 200,000 people in the United States, with about 20 new cases each year. Affected individuals suffer recurrent, severe infections of the skin, lungs, liver, lymph nodes and bones, particularly with catalase-positive bacteria and fungi such as Staphylococcus aureus, Serratia marcescens, Burkholderia cepacia and Aspergillus species. Abscesses and granulomas can obstruct the gastrointestinal and urinary tracts, and inflammatory complications resembling inflammatory bowel disease are common.

Genetics

Most cases are inherited in an X-linked recessive pattern through mutations in the CYBB gene encoding the gp91phox subunit of NADPH oxidase; autosomal recessive forms arise from mutations in genes for the other oxidase subunits (p22phox, p47phox, p67phox and p40phox).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by tests of neutrophil oxidative burst, such as the dihydrorhodamine flow cytometry assay (the older nitroblue tetrazolium test is now rarely used), followed by genetic testing. Management includes lifelong prophylactic antibiotics and antifungals, interferon gamma therapy, and prompt aggressive treatment of infections. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation can be curative, and gene therapy is under investigation.
