Ringer's lactate

Ringer's lactate solution (RL), also known as sodium lactate solution and Hartmann's solution, is a mixture of sodium chloride, sodium lactate, potassium chloride and calcium chloride dissolved in water. It is an isotonic crystalloid fluid given by intravenous infusion to replace lost body fluid and restore circulating volume, and it is on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines.

A bag of Ringer's lactate for intravenous use.

Composition

One litre of Ringer's lactate contains approximately 130 mmol sodium, 4 mmol potassium, 1.5 mmol calcium, 109 mmol chloride and 28 mmol lactate, giving an osmolarity of about 273 mOsm/L. The lactate is metabolised by the liver to bicarbonate, giving the solution a mild alkalinising effect, which is why it is classed as a "balanced" crystalloid, more similar to plasma than normal saline.

Medical uses

Ringer's lactate is used in fluid resuscitation after blood loss due to trauma, surgery or burns, and in the treatment of severe dehydration, for example in cholera. Because it avoids the hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis associated with large volumes of saline, trials such as SMART (2018) have favoured balanced crystalloids in many critically ill patients. It is generally avoided as a diluent for blood transfusion (the calcium may promote clotting in stored blood) and used with care in severe liver failure, where lactate metabolism is impaired.

History

The solution descends from the physiological salt solution devised in the 1880s by British physiologist Sydney Ringer. In the 1930s American paediatrician Alexis Hartmann added sodium lactate to Ringer's solution to treat acidosis in children, producing the formulation still known as Hartmann's solution.
