Rembrandt lighting
TypePortrait lighting technique
Used inPhotography, cinematography, painting

Rembrandt lighting is a standard lighting technique used in studio portrait photography and cinematography, named after the Dutch painter Rembrandt van Rijn, whose portraits characteristically used this effect. The setup combines short lighting with a distinctive illuminated triangle on the subject's shadowed cheek, creating a moody, dimensional image with strong modelling of the face.

Technique

In a typical setup, the key light is placed about 45 degrees to one side of the subject and raised above eye level, angled downward. The subject's face is turned so that one side is lit while the other falls into shadow, except for a small inverted triangle of light that appears on the shadowed cheek, below the eye. The triangle should be no wider than the eye and no longer than the nose. A weaker fill light or reflector may be used to control the contrast ratio, but the shadow side must retain the triangular highlight for the effect to qualify as Rembrandt lighting.

Origins

Rembrandt's portraits and self-portraits frequently show sitters lit from a high window to one side, with half the face in shadow and a small highlight on the dark cheek. Film directors and photographers of the early twentieth century — notably in the work of director Cecil B. DeMille, who is credited with popularising the term in cinema — adopted and named the pattern. It became a staple of Hollywood glamour photography and film noir.

Effect and use

Rembrandt lighting is considered flattering yet dramatic: it sculpts the face, conceals skin imperfections on the shadow side, and conveys seriousness or introspection. It is widely used in character portraits, editorial photography and dramatic film scenes. Related patterns include loop lighting, split lighting and butterfly (Paramount) lighting, which differ in the position of the key light and the resulting shadow shapes.
