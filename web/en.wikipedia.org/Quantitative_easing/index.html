Quantitative easing

Quantitative easing (QE) is a monetary policy action where, in order to stimulate the economy, a central bank purchases predetermined amounts of government bonds or other financial assets, without reference to interest rates and in a non-competitive manner. This form of monetary policy is usually used when inflation is very low or negative, and when standard monetary policy has become ineffective.

Process

A central bank implements quantitative easing by creating money ex nihilo and using it to purchase financial assets, mainly government bonds and mortgage-backed securities, from commercial banks and other private institutions. The purchases increase the price of these assets and lower their yield, while simultaneously increasing the money supply and the excess reserves of the banking system. Unlike conventional operations, the central bank targets the quantity of assets to purchase rather than the price (interest rate).

History

The Bank of Japan is credited with implementing QE first, in the early 2000s, although economists debate whether its earlier policies qualify. The term was popularised later during the financial crisis of 2007–2008, when the US Federal Reserve, the Bank of England, and the European Central Bank launched large-scale asset purchase programmes. QE was used again on an enormous scale in response to the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020.

Effects and risks

Supporters argue QE lowers borrowing costs, supports asset prices, prevents deflation and stimulates lending. Critics point to risks including asset-price inflation, increased inequality, currency depreciation, misallocation of capital, and the difficulty of unwinding the programmes — a process known as quantitative tightening (QT) when central banks let holdings mature or sell assets.
