EBCDIC (Extended Binary Coded Decimal Interchange Code) is an eight-bit character encoding used mainly on IBM mainframe and midrange computer operating systems, including z/OS, OS/400 and VM. It was developed by IBM in 1963–64 and announced with the release of the System/360 line of mainframes.

History

EBCDIC descended from the punched-card codes used in earlier IBM equipment and from the six-bit BCD encoding used on IBM's 1400 series. Because it evolved from punched-card practice, the letters of the alphabet are not contiguous in EBCDIC: there are gaps between I and J and between R and S, which complicates sorting and range tests compared with ASCII.

Code pages

There is no single EBCDIC; numerous national variants, known as code pages, rearrange punctuation and national characters. Well-known variants include code page 037 (US/Canada), 500 (International) and 1047 (Latin-1 for Open Systems). Several EBCDIC characters, such as the cent sign and the "not" sign, have no simple ASCII equivalent, and conversion between EBCDIC and ASCII requires translation tables.

Usage

Although ASCII and Unicode dominate elsewhere, EBCDIC remains in daily use on IBM Z mainframes in banking, insurance and government systems, where vast archives of EBCDIC data persist. Modern IBM software routinely converts data between EBCDIC and ASCII-based encodings.
