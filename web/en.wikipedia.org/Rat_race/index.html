Rat race

The metaphor derives from rats striving pointlessly in laboratory mazes and wheels

A rat race is an endless, self-defeating or pointless pursuit. The phrase equates humans to rats attempting to earn a reward such as cheese, in vain. It may also refer to a competitive struggle to get ahead financially or routinely, and is commonly used to describe work life in modern urban societies as repetitive, exhausting and competitive, in which people are trapped in a cycle of employment and consumption with little time for rest or reflection.

Origin of the metaphor

The imagery draws on laboratory rats racing through mazes or running in wheels for food rewards. In its modern figurative sense the term gained currency in the mid-20th century. The underlying image is that, however fast the rats run, they remain within the maze; likewise, however hard the worker competes, the structure of the race yields no lasting escape or satisfaction.

Usage and criticism

The phrase is used critically of long working hours, commuting, consumer debt and status competition. It appears frequently in discussions of work–life balance, early retirement and financial independence movements, "downshifting", and critiques of materialism. Counter-movements—such as the FIRE movement (Financial Independence, Retire Early)—explicitly frame their goal as "escaping the rat race". Critics of the metaphor note that it can undervalue ordinary work and ambition, and that many people find meaning and community in the routines the phrase disparages.

In popular culture

The term is the title of the 1960 Broadway play The Rat Race and the 2001 comedy film Rat Race. It features in songs such as Bob Marley's "Rat Race" and in the board game of the same name satirising corporate life.
