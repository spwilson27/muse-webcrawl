Mid-air collision

TypeAviation accident
PreventionAir traffic control, TCAS, see-and-avoid

A mid-air collision is an accident in which two or more aircraft come into unplanned contact during flight. Owing to the relatively high speeds involved and the likelihood of subsequent impact with the ground or sea, mid-air collisions are usually catastrophic, with few if any survivors among those on board the aircraft involved.

Causes

Collisions most often result from failures of separation: pilot error, air traffic control error, communication failures, or aircraft straying from cleared routes or altitudes. Risk is greatest in uncontrolled airspace near busy general-aviation airports, where pilots rely on the "see-and-avoid" principle, and during military training operations.

Prevention and notable accidents

Modern prevention depends on layered defences: organised airspace and ATC separation, transponders, and the Traffic Collision Avoidance System (TCAS), which commands climb or descent manoeuvres when a conflict is detected. The 1956 Grand Canyon mid-air collision, in which two airliners collided killing all 128 people aboard, spurred the creation of the U.S. Federal Aviation Agency and a modern air traffic control system. The 2002 Überlingen mid-air collision, in which a passenger jet and a cargo aircraft collided over Germany, highlighted conflicts between TCAS instructions and controller instructions, leading to clarified procedures worldwide.
