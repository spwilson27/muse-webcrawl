First-wave feminism was a period of feminist activity, principally during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, that focused on legal issues, primarily on securing women's right to vote, along with property rights, access to education and employment, and reform of marriage and divorce law. The term "first wave" was coined retrospectively, after "second-wave feminism" came into use in the late 1960s.

Origins

Precursors include Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792). In the United States, the 1848 Seneca Falls Convention, organised by Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott, issued a Declaration of Sentiments demanding equality including the vote. In Britain, campaigns for married women's property rights produced the Married Women's Property Acts of 1870 and 1882, and John Stuart Mill presented a suffrage petition to Parliament in 1866 and published The Subjection of Women in 1869.

Suffrage campaigns

New Zealand became the first self-governing country to grant women the vote in 1893, followed by Australia, Finland and Norway. British suffragists split between the constitutional suffragists of Millicent Fawcett and the militant suffragettes of Emmeline and Christabel Pankhurst's WSPU, whose tactics included window-smashing, arson and hunger strikes answered by forcible feeding; Emily Davison died under the King's horse at the 1913 Derby. Britain enfranchised (some) women in 1918 and equalised the franchise in 1928. In the United States, the Nineteenth Amendment, ratified in 1920, prohibited denying the vote on account of sex.

Legacy and criticism

After the suffrage victories the movement fragmented and is often said to have ended, though organising continued on issues such as nationality rights, birth control and equal rights amendments. Historians note that the "wave" model can obscure the contributions of Black, Indigenous and working-class women, whose struggles did not always align with those of white middle-class campaigners.
