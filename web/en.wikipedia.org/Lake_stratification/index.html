Lake stratification

Lake stratification is the tendency of lakes to form separate and distinct thermal layers during warm and cold weather. Typically stratified lakes show three main layers: the epilimnion, the warm top layer; the metalimnion (or thermocline), the middle layer where temperature changes rapidly with depth; and the colder hypolimnion, extending to the lake bottom.

Mechanism

Water is densest at about 4 °C. In summer the sun warms surface water, which floats on colder, denser deep water; wind mixes only the upper layer. In autumn, surface cooling causes the layers to mix — the fall turnover. In winter, ice cover can produce inverse stratification, with 0 °C water just under the ice and 4 °C water at the bottom, followed by a spring turnover.

Mixing regimes

Lakes are classified by how often they mix: dimictic lakes mix twice a year; monomictic lakes once; polymictic lakes frequently; meromictic lakes rarely or never fully mix, because a chemically distinct deep layer (the monimolimnion) resists mixing.

Ecological significance

Stratification controls oxygen and nutrient distribution. The hypolimnion can become anoxic in summer in productive lakes, stressing fish and releasing phosphorus from sediments. Turnover redistributes nutrients and oxygen. Climate change is lengthening stratification in many lakes.
