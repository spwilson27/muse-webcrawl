An ephod (Hebrew: אֵפוֹד, ʾēp̄ōd) was a type of apron worn by the high priest of ancient Israel, described in the Book of Exodus as an elaborate garment of gold, blue, purple and scarlet material and fine linen, held together by a girdle of similar workmanship. Two onyx stones, engraved with the names of the twelve tribes of Israel, were set on its shoulder pieces.

Description

According to Exodus 28, the ephod was worn over the blue robe and beneath the breastplate, which was fastened to it with gold chains and contained the Urim and Thummim, objects associated with divination. The description is generally attributed by scholars to the Priestly source and to the ritual of the Tabernacle and later the Temple in Jerusalem.

Other uses in the Hebrew Bible

The word is used in several apparently different senses elsewhere in the Bible. Samuel is described as a boy ministering before the Lord "girded with a linen ephod", and David danced before the Ark wearing a linen ephod — suggesting a simpler garment of linen. In Judges, Gideon makes an ephod from gold taken as spoil, which becomes an object of illicit worship, and Micah's household shrine includes an ephod alongside a graven image, suggesting the term could also denote a cultic object or idol. Priests at Nob are said to be those "who wear the linen ephod".

Interpretation

The range of uses has led scholars to debate whether "ephod" originally denoted a garment, a covering for a divine image, or a container for lots used in divination. Rabbinic tradition treated the high priest's ephod as one of the eight priestly garments required for Temple service, whose absence invalidated the rites.
