Accountability

In ethics and governance, accountability is answerability: the acknowledgment and assumption of responsibility for actions, products, decisions, and policies, including the administration, governance, and implementation within the scope of a role or position, and encompassing the obligation to report, explain, and be answerable for resulting consequences. In leadership roles, accountability is often a reckoning for one's actions — accepting blame when things go wrong rather than shifting it to others.

Political accountability

Political accountability concerns the accountability of the government, civil servants, and politicians to the public and to legislative bodies such as a parliament. Elections are a central mechanism: voters can reward or remove officeholders. Recall elections, where voters can remove an elected official before the end of a term, are another form of political accountability found in some jurisdictions. Scholars distinguish vertical accountability (citizens over officials, e.g. through elections and a free press) from horizontal accountability (state institutions — courts, audit offices, ombudsmen — checking one another).

Organisational and administrative accountability

Within organisations, accountability involves clear definitions of who is responsible for decisions and outcomes, transparent reporting, and mechanisms for review, including audits, performance management, and codes of conduct. In public administration, accountability to legislatures, courts, and the public is a core principle of good governance, balanced against the need for administrative discretion.

Ethical and personal accountability

In ethics, accountability is connected to moral responsibility and blameworthiness: agents are accountable when they can be asked to justify their conduct and may appropriately face praise, blame, or sanction. Philosophical debates concern the conditions of accountability — freedom, knowledge, and control — and its relationship to forgiveness and punishment.
