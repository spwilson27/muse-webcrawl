Victory column

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A victory column, or triumphal column, is a type of monumental column erected to commemorate a victory in war, a battle, or a military campaign. The column typically stands on a pedestal and is crowned by a victory symbol, such as a statue of the goddess Victoria (in Greek, Nike), an eagle, or a figure of the victorious commander.

History

The form derives from ancient Rome. Celebrated examples include Trajan's Column (completed AD 113) and the Column of Marcus Aurelius in Rome, whose spiral reliefs narrate the emperors' campaigns; both survive today. Later Roman and Byzantine emperors erected similar monuments, such as the Column of Constantine in Constantinople.

The type was revived in the early modern and modern periods. Famous victory columns include Nelson's Column in London (1843), commemorating Admiral Horatio Nelson's death at Trafalgar; the July Column in Paris; the Berlin Victory Column (Siegessäule), designed by Heinrich Strack and inaugurated in 1873 to celebrate Prussian victories in the Danish, Austrian and Franco-Prussian wars; the Alexander Column in Saint Petersburg; and the Column of the Angel of Independence in Mexico City.

Design

Victory columns are usually built of stone or bronze-clad masonry, often decorated with reliefs of battles, captured weapons, or inscriptions recording the triumph. The Berlin column's shaft incorporates gilded cannon barrels captured in the wars it celebrates, and it is topped by an 8.3-metre gilded bronze statue of Victoria, nicknamed "Goldelse". Observation platforms inside many columns make them landmarks and viewpoints as well as memorials.
