Íslendingabók (Old Norse pronunciation: [ˈiːslɛndɪŋɡaˌboːk], Latin: Libellus Islandorum, "The Book of the Icelanders") is a short historical work written in the early 12th century by the Icelandic priest and scholar Ari Þorgilsson (Ari the Learned). It is the earliest known written history of Iceland and the oldest surviving prose narrative in Icelandic, covering the settlement of the island, the establishment of the Althing, the conversion to Christianity, and the history of Iceland's early bishops down to Ari's own time.

Content

The work traces the Norwegian settlement of Iceland beginning around 870, the founding of the national assembly, the Althing, in 930, and the legal adoption of Christianity in the year 999/1000, an event Ari describes in notable detail, including the role of the lawspeaker Þorgeir Þorkelsson, who is said to have lain under a cloak for a day and a night before proclaiming that all Icelanders should be Christian. Íslendingabók also recounts the discovery and settlement of Greenland, the consecration of Iceland's first bishops, and a chronological framework based on the succession of lawspeakers and bishops. Ari carefully names the oral informants on whom he relied, giving the work an unusually explicit source-critical character for its time.

Manuscripts and transmission

Ari's original text does not survive, nor does an earlier, longer version he mentions having written, which included genealogies and the lives of Norwegian kings. The surviving shorter version is preserved in two 17th-century paper manuscripts copied by Jón Erlendsson from a medieval exemplar, now lost, dating to around 1200. Despite its late transmission, the text is generally regarded as a faithful copy of Ari's work.

Significance

Íslendingabók is a foundational source for the early history of Iceland and for the settlement period more broadly. Its chronology of the settlement, sometimes called the Settlement Age dating, has been broadly corroborated by archaeology, including the dating of the widespread volcanic ash layer (the settlement tephra) to 871 ± 2. Together with the more detailed Landnámabók, it underpins modern knowledge of how Iceland was settled and organised as a commonwealth.
