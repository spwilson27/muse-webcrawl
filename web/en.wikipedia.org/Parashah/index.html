Parashah

A parashah (Hebrew: פָּרָשָׁה, "portion"; plural parashiyyot), formally a Parashat ha-Shavua ("portion of the week"), is a section of the Torah read publicly during Jewish religious services, most notably at Shabbat (Saturday) morning services. In common usage, "the parashah" refers to the weekly Torah portion.

The annual cycle

The Torah (Five Books of Moses) is divided into 54 parashiyyot, read in sequence over the course of a year, beginning and ending on the festival of Simchat Torah. The cycle starts with Bereshit (Genesis) in the autumn and proceeds through the books of Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy. Because the Jewish leap year has extra weeks, some portions are combined in non-leap years so the cycle finishes on schedule. On certain weeks, special readings (such as the Four Parashiyyot before Passover) replace or supplement the regular portion.

The reading

The weekly portion is divided into seven sections (aliyyot), each read by a person called up to the Torah; on festivals and other occasions the number varies. A related prophetic reading, the Haftarah, follows. In ancient Israel a triennial cycle, reading the Torah over three years, was customary; some modern congregations, especially in the Conservative and Reform movements, use a triennial division of each weekly portion.

Study

Studying the weekly parashah is a widespread practice; traditional commentaries by Rashi, Ibn Ezra, and Nachmanides accompany the text in printed editions (the Mikraot Gedolot), and countless modern sermons and essays are keyed to the week's portion.
