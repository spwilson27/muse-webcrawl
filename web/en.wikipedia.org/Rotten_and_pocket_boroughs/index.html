Rotten and pocket boroughs

A rotten borough was a parliamentary borough in England, and later the United Kingdom, that had a very small electorate yet still returned two Members of Parliament — the same representation as large and growing towns. A pocket borough was one whose representation was effectively controlled ("in the pocket of") a wealthy patron or landowner, who could nominate the MPs. Both were notorious features of the unreformed electoral system before the Reform Act 1832.

Causes

Borough boundaries and franchises had been fixed in the Middle Ages and were never adjusted for population change. Places that had once been prosperous market towns declined — sometimes to a handful of houses or, in the case of Dunwich in Suffolk, largely to the sea — while industrial cities such as Manchester, Birmingham, and Leeds had no parliamentary representation of their own at all before 1832. Where the electorate was tiny, bribery or a patron's influence could determine the result outright.

Notorious examples

The most infamous rotten borough was Old Sarum in Wiltshire, an abandoned hilltop settlement with a handful of voters that returned two MPs and was controlled by the Pitt family. Gatton in Surrey, with as few as seven voters, and Newtown on the Isle of Wight were also frequently cited. Pocket boroughs belonging to great aristocratic families were traded and inherited like property; seats could effectively be bought, and young politicians — including William Pitt the Younger, who entered Parliament for the pocket borough of Appleby — routinely began their careers in such seats.

Reform

Reformers had attacked the system for decades, but change came only with the Reform Act 1832, passed amid political crisis. The Act disfranchised 56 rotten boroughs entirely, reduced others, and redistributed seats to the counties and new industrial towns, while modestly widening the franchise. Later Reform Acts of 1867 and 1884–85 and the Ballot Act 1872 further reduced patronage and corruption.

Legacy

The phrase "rotten borough" survives as a general term for an unrepresentative or corrupt constituency. Scotland and Ireland had analogous systems of burghs and boroughs reformed by companion acts of 1832–1833.
