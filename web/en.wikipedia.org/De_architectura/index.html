De architectura

De architectura (On Architecture) is a treatise on architecture written by the Roman architect and military engineer Marcus Vitruvius Pollio and dedicated to his patron, the emperor Augustus. Written around 30–15 BC, it is the only treatise on architecture to survive from classical antiquity, and it profoundly influenced Renaissance and later architecture.

Content

The ten books cover city planning and the qualifications of the architect; building materials; temples and the classical orders; public buildings including theatres and baths; private houses; floors and plaster; water supply and aqueducts; astronomy, clocks, and sundials; and civil and military machines. Vitruvius famously held that a building must satisfy three conditions — firmitas (durability), utilitas (utility), and venustas (beauty) — the "Vitruvian triad."

Book III describes the proportions of the human body as a model for architectural harmony, the text Leonardo da Vinci illustrated in his drawing Vitruvian Man (c. 1490).

Legacy

Rediscovered in the early fifteenth century — a complete manuscript was famously identified by Poggio Bracciolini in 1414 — the treatise underpinned the theory of Alberti, Serlio, and Palladio, and remains a primary source for Roman building practice.
