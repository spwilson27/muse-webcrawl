Quenelle

A quenelle is a mixture of creamed fish or meat, sometimes combined with breadcrumbs, with a light egg binding, formed into an egg-like (oval) shape and then cooked, typically by poaching. In classical French cuisine the most celebrated version is the quenelle de brochet, made from pike, a speciality of Lyon and the Nantua region, traditionally served with sauce Nantua, a crayfish-flavoured béchamel.


Quenelles de brochet, sauce Nantua

Preparation

The base is a panade of flour, butter, milk and egg yolk, mixed with finely sieved raw fish or meat (the mousseline forcemeat), then enriched with cream and egg white and seasoned. The mixture is shaped between two spoons into smooth ovals — a technique that has given the name "quenelle" more generally to any food, such as ice cream or sorbet, shaped this way with spoons — and gently poached in stock or salted water, during which the quenelles swell. Lyonnaise bouchons serve them gratined in sauce, a dish also associated with the cuisine of the Bugey.

Other uses of the word

In France the word "quenelle" became widely known outside the kitchen for a gesture popularised by the comedian Dieudonné, in which one arm is pointed downward while the other hand touches its shoulder; the gesture has been the subject of public controversy and legal proceedings over its interpretation. In cooking, the term is also used as a verb: to quenelle is to form a mixture into the characteristic three-sided oval using one or two spoons.
