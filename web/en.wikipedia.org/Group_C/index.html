Group C

Group C was a category of motorsport, introduced by the FIA in 1982 for sports car racing, along with Group A for touring cars and Group B for grand touring cars. Group C prototypes headlined the World Sportscar Championship throughout the 1980s and are widely regarded as among the finest sports racing cars ever built.

Regulations

Unlike earlier categories, Group C was primarily a fuel-consumption formula: cars were limited by the amount of fuel they could use over a race distance rather than by engine capacity, encouraging manufacturers to develop efficient turbocharged engines. A second category, initially Group C Junior and later C2, applied stricter fuel limits for smaller teams.

Manufacturers and cars

Porsche dominated the early years with the 956 and 962C. Later competition came from Jaguar (XJR series), Mercedes-Benz with Sauber, Nissan, Toyota, Mazda — whose rotary-engined 787B won the 1991 24 Hours of Le Mans — and Peugeot, whose 905 won Le Mans in 1992 and 1993 under revised 3.5-litre rules.

Decline

Rising costs and controversial rule changes led manufacturers to withdraw; the World Sportscar Championship was cancelled after 1992 and Group C faded by 1994. Many cars continue to race in historic Group C series.
