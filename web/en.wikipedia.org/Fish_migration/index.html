Fish migration

Fish migration is the regular, large-scale movement of fish from one part of a water body to another, typically on a seasonal or life-cycle basis. Many types of fish migrate on a regular basis, on time scales ranging from daily to yearly, and over distances from a few metres to thousands of kilometres. Migration usually serves feeding or breeding purposes; in some cases the reason is unknown. Fish migrations are ecologically central, moving nutrients between ecosystems and supporting major fisheries and predators.

Classification

The classical terminology, formalised by George Sprague Myers in 1949, distinguishes:

  • Diadromous fish, which migrate between salt water and fresh water. Subcategories include:
    • Anadromous fish, which live mainly in the sea and migrate into fresh water to spawn — for example salmon, shad, and lampreys.
    • Catadromous fish, which live in fresh water and migrate to the sea to spawn — for example freshwater eels, which travel to the Sargasso Sea.
    • Amphidromous fish, which move between fresh and salt water at a particular stage of the life cycle but not for breeding.
  • Potamodromous fish, migrating wholly within fresh water.
  • Oceanodromous fish, migrating wholly within the sea, such as tunas, herring, and capelin.

Daily vertical migrations are also widespread: many species rise toward the surface at night to feed and descend to deep, dark water by day, the largest migration by biomass on Earth.

Notable migrations

Pacific and Atlantic salmon runs are among the most famous: adults navigate from ocean feeding grounds back to their natal streams, leaping rapids and waterfalls, spawn, and (for Pacific species) die, feeding bears, eagles, and forests in the process. The sardine run off South Africa's east coast, involving billions of fish followed by sharks, dolphins, and birds, is one of the largest marine migrations. Tunas undertake transoceanic migrations tracked by satellite tagging.

Navigation and threats

Migratory fish navigate using olfactory imprinting, celestial and geomagnetic cues, currents, and temperature gradients. Migrations are threatened by dams and weirs that block passage (mitigated by fish ladders and bypasses), overfishing on migration routes, habitat loss, and climate-driven shifts in temperature and currents. The removal of obsolete dams has restored some historically important runs.
