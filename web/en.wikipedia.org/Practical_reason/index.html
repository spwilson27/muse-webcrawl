Practical reason

Practical reason is the faculty of reason concerned with action: it involves deliberating about what one ought to do, choosing means to ends, and forming intentions. It is contrasted with theoretical reason, which is concerned with what is the case—with truth and knowledge. In philosophy, the study of practical reason overlaps with ethics, decision theory, and the philosophy of action.

History

Aristotle distinguished theoretical wisdom (sophia) from practical wisdom (phronesis), the virtue of deliberating well about what is good and advantageous for living well, and developed the account of the practical syllogism. In the medieval period, practical reason was tied to natural law theory. David Hume famously held that reason is, and ought only to be, "the slave of the passions", arguing that reason alone cannot motivate action—a thesis known as Humeanism.

Immanuel Kant gave practical reason primacy in moral philosophy. In the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785) and the Critique of Practical Reason (1788), he argued that reason itself can be practical and can determine the will through the categorical imperative, a moral law known a priori. Kant's account underlies later debates between Kantian constructivists, moral realists, and expressivists.

Contemporary debates

Central questions in contemporary philosophy of practical reason include whether reasons for action are grounded in agents' desires (internalism) or exist independently of them (externalism); the nature of instrumental rationality and whether it is governed by substantive norms; how intentions structure planning and coordination over time, as analyzed by Michael Bratman; and whether there are genuinely "moral" reasons that are both categorical and overriding. Practical reasoning is also modelled formally in decision theory, game theory, and artificial intelligence planning.
