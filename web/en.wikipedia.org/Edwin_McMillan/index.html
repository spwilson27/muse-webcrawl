Edwin McMillan

Edwin Mattison McMillan (September 18, 1907 – September 7, 1991) was an American physicist credited with being the first to produce a transuranium element, neptunium. For this and his co-discovery of plutonium he shared the 1951 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Glenn Seaborg.

Early work and transuranium elements

McMillan studied at Caltech and Princeton (PhD 1933) before joining Ernest Lawrence's Radiation Laboratory at Berkeley. Working on the products of neutron bombardment of uranium in 1939–40, he identified a new element with atomic number 93 — neptunium — formed when uranium-238 captures a neutron. With Philip Abelson he established its chemistry. His colleague Glenn Seaborg's team then isolated element 94, plutonium, from the decay of neptunium — discoveries foundational to nuclear weapons and nuclear energy.

The synchrotron

During the Second World War McMillan worked on radar, sonar, and briefly the atomic bomb project. In 1945 he independently conceived the principle of phase stability, also discovered by Vladimir Veksler in the USSR, which made possible the synchrotron — a particle accelerator in which particles are kept in step with the accelerating field as they gain energy. The principle underlies essentially all large circular accelerators, including the LHC.

Later career

McMillan directed the Lawrence Radiation Laboratory from 1958 to 1973, overseeing its growth into a leading centre of physics. He received the Atoms for Peace Award in 1963 and the National Medal of Science in 1990.
