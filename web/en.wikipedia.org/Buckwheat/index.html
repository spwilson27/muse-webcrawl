Buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum) is a flowering plant in the knotweed family Polygonaceae cultivated for its grain-like seeds and as a cover crop. Despite its name, buckwheat is not closely related to wheat; it is not a cereal nor a grass, but is related to sorrel, knotweed, and rhubarb. It is classified as a pseudocereal because its seeds are used in a similar culinary role to true cereals.

Cultivation and use

Buckwheat is a short-season crop that does well on low-fertility or acidic soils, though the soil must be well drained. It has been cultivated for thousands of years; domestication is thought to have occurred in southwestern China, from where it spread across Asia and into Europe. In many cultures buckwheat flour is used for noodles, pancakes, and porridges. Japanese soba noodles, French galettes, and Eastern European kasha are among its best-known foods.

Nutrition

Buckwheat groats are rich in protein, dietary fiber, and several minerals including manganese and magnesium. The protein is of high quality, containing all essential amino acids, and the grain is naturally gluten-free, making it suitable for people with coeliac disease.

Ecology

The plant's flowers are fragrant and attract bees; buckwheat honey is a prized dark, strongly flavored variety. Because it grows quickly and smothers weeds, buckwheat is also grown as a cover crop and green manure.
