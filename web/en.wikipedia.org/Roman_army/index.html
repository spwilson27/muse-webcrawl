The Roman army was the armed force deployed by the Romans throughout the duration of ancient Rome, from the Roman Kingdom (to c. 500 BC) through the Republic (500–31 BC) and into the Empire (31 BC – AD 395/1453). It is widely regarded as one of the most effective military forces in history, distinguished by its organisation, engineering, discipline and logistics.

History

The early army was a citizen militia fighting as hoplites. After the Gallic sack of Rome (390 BC) it reorganised into the manipular legion of hastati, principes and triarii. The reforms of Gaius Marius (c. 107 BC) opened service to the propertyless, standardised equipment and made the army professional. Augustus created a standing army of some 28 legions plus auxiliary units, with fixed terms of service and retirement bonuses, and the Praetorian Guard as the emperor's bodyguard.

Organisation

The legion, of roughly 5,000 heavy infantry, was divided into ten cohorts, each of centuries commanded by centurions. Non-citizen auxilia provided cavalry, archers and light troops, earning citizenship on discharge. Legionaries famously built roads, bridges and fortified camps, and engineering skill was as much a weapon as the gladius and pilum.

Late antiquity

From the third century the army was progressively restructured into frontier troops (limitanei) and mobile field armies (comitatenses), a process associated with Diocletian and Constantine. The western army disintegrated in the fifth century, while the eastern (Byzantine) army continued for a thousand years.
