Hypostatic union

The hypostatic union (from the Greek ὑπόστασις hypostasis, "substance" or "person") is a term in Christian theology used to describe the union of Christ's humanity and divinity in one person (hypostasis). According to the doctrine, Jesus Christ is one person who possesses two complete natures — fully divine and fully human — united "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation".

History

The doctrine was defined at the Council of Chalcedon in 451 AD, whose Definition confessed Christ "in two natures" in one person and rejected both Nestorianism (which divided Christ into two persons) and Eutychian monophysitism (which blended the natures into one). The term hypostasis had been developed by the Cappadocian Fathers in Trinitarian theology, where God is one essence (ousia) in three hypostases. Chalcedonian churches — Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and most Protestant traditions — accept the definition; Oriental Orthodox churches rejected Chalcedon and hold a miaphysite formula of "one united nature".

Related concepts

The union grounds doctrines such as the communicatio idiomatum (the predication of the properties of either nature of the one person, e.g. "God suffered") and the title Theotokos ("God-bearer") for Mary, affirmed at the Council of Ephesus in 431. Later disputes over Christ's wills (monothelitism) were settled at the Third Council of Constantinople (680–681), which affirmed two natural wills.
