Umayyad conquest of Hispania

The Umayyad conquest of Hispania, also known as the Muslim conquest of Spain, was the invasion of the Iberian Peninsula by the Umayyad Caliphate between 711 and 718, which destroyed the Visigothic Kingdom and established the Muslim territory of al-Andalus. Beginning with the victory of Tariq ibn Ziyad at the Battle of Guadalete, most of the peninsula fell under Muslim control within about seven years.

Background

By 710 the Visigothic kingdom was weakened by a disputed royal succession: Roderic had seized the throne against the partisans of Witiza's family, and counts such as Julian of Ceuta, according to tradition, facilitated the Muslim crossing. The governor of Umayyad Ifriqiya, Musa ibn Nusayr, first sent a raiding force under Tarif ibn Malik in 710.

The conquest

In spring 711, Tariq ibn Ziyad, Musa's Berber client and governor of Tangier, crossed the strait with about 7,000 men, landing near the rock that still bears his name (Jabal Tariq — Gibraltar). On 19 July 711 his army routed and killed King Roderic at the Battle of Guadalete. Tariq advanced through Écija and Córdoba to Toledo, the Visigothic capital. Musa followed in 712 with a larger, mainly Arab army, taking Seville, Mérida (after a siege) and other cities; the two commanders met at Toledo and then subdued the Ebro valley and the northeast. By about 718, apart from small Christian holdouts in the northern mountains of Asturias and the Pyrenees, the peninsula was in Muslim hands.

Aftermath

The conquered territory became the province of al-Andalus, ruled first by governors appointed from Damascus or Kairouan, then, after the Abbasid revolution of 750, by the independent Umayyad emirate founded by Abd al-Rahman I. Treaties such as the Pact of Theodemir (713) allowed some local Christian rulers to retain authority in return for tribute. Christian tradition later framed the long struggle to retake the peninsula as the Reconquista, beginning symbolically with the Battle of Covadonga (c. 718–722).
