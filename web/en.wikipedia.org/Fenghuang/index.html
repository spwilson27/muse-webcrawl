Fenghuang

A fenghuang in traditional Chinese art.

The fenghuang (Chinese: 鳳凰) is a mythological bird found in Sinosphere mythology that reigns over all other birds. In the male–female pairing of its original name, feng denotes the male and huang the female, but the creature came to be regarded as female and paired with the male Chinese dragon as a symbol of the empress alongside the emperor. It is often called the "Chinese phoenix", though it differs from the Western phoenix in not being reborn from ashes.

Appearance and symbolism

Classical descriptions make the fenghuang a composite creature: the beak of a rooster, the face of a swallow, the forehead of a fowl, the neck of a snake, the breast of a goose, the back of a tortoise, the hindquarters of a stag and the tail of a fish, with plumage in the five fundamental colours — black, white, red, blue and yellow. Its appearance was an omen of peace and prosperity, said to occur only in times of good government; Confucius lamented that the fenghuang did not appear, signalling a disordered age.

Cultural role

The fenghuang is one of the Four Auspicious Beasts alongside the dragon, qilin and tortoise, and its imagery adorns palaces, wedding garments and ornaments, where it signifies virtue, grace and the union of yin and yang. Cognate birds appear throughout East Asia — as the Japanese hōō and Korean bonghwang — and the motif remains a popular emblem in modern Chinese culture.
