Expectorant

An expectorant is a medication that helps bring up mucus and other material from the lungs, bronchi, and trachea by thinning secretions or increasing their volume, making coughs more productive. Expectorants are common ingredients in over-the-counter cough and cold preparations, and are sometimes loosely grouped with mucolytics, which break down the chemical structure of mucus itself.

Common agents

The best-known expectorant is guaifenesin, the only expectorant recognized as safe and effective in the United States Food and Drug Administration's OTC monograph for cough/cold products; it is sold under brand names such as Robitussin and Mucinex and is thought to work by increasing the volume and reducing the viscosity of airway secretions. Older preparations used agents such as ipecacuanha, ammonium chloride, potassium iodide, and terpin hydrate, most of which have fallen out of favor. Drinking adequate fluids is often recommended alongside any expectorant.

Evidence

Clinical evidence for the effectiveness of over-the-counter expectorants is limited and mixed; systematic reviews have generally found insufficient high-quality evidence that they outperform placebo for acute cough, although guaifenesin has shown some benefit in studies of respiratory infections and chronic bronchitis. Adverse effects are usually mild, mainly nausea or gastrointestinal discomfort.
