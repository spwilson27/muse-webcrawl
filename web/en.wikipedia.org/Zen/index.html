Zen (Japanese: 禅; Chinese: 禪, Chán) is a school of Mahayana Buddhism that originated in China during the Tang dynasty, where it developed as Chan through the interaction of Indian Buddhism — especially meditation (dhyāna, the root word behind both Chán and Zen) — with Taoist thought. It spread to Vietnam, Korea and, by the 12th century, Japan, where as Zen it became deeply influential.

Teaching

Zen emphasises direct experience of one's true nature over scriptural study, describing itself as "a special transmission outside the scriptures, not founded upon words and letters; directly pointing to the human mind — seeing into one's nature and becoming a Buddha." Practice centres on seated meditation (zazen): in the Sōtō school as "just sitting" (shikantaza), and in the Rinzai school together with koans — paradoxical questions or stories, such as "What is the sound of one hand?", used under a teacher's guidance to exhaust conceptual thinking and provoke awakening (kenshō, satori).

History

Tradition traces Chan to Bodhidharma, the semi-legendary monk said to have brought the teaching from India to Shaolin in the 5th–6th century. The school took shape with masters such as Huineng, the Sixth Patriarch, whose Platform Sutra championed sudden enlightenment. In Japan, Eisai founded the Rinzai line and Dōgen the Sōtō line in the 12th–13th centuries. Zen aesthetics shaped Japanese arts — ink painting, calligraphy, tea ceremony, garden design and martial arts.

Zen in the West

Writers such as D. T. Suzuki and Alan Watts introduced Zen to Western audiences in the 20th century, influencing the Beat generation, humanistic psychology and, later, the mindfulness movement — sometimes in forms quite detached from Zen's monastic roots.
