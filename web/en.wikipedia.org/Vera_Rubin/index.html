Vera Rubin

Born23 July 1928, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania
Died25 December 2016, Princeton, New Jersey
FieldAstronomy

Vera Florence Cooper Rubin (1928–2016) was an American astronomer whose measurements of galaxy rotation curves provided some of the strongest evidence for the existence of dark matter. Working at the Carnegie Institution of Washington, she showed that stars at the edges of spiral galaxies orbit far faster than the visible mass can explain.

Early life and education

Rubin studied astronomy at Vassar College, graduating in 1948 as the only astronomy major in her class. Denied entry to Princeton's graduate programme, which did not admit women until 1975, she earned a master's degree at Cornell and a PhD at Georgetown University in 1954, supervised by George Gamow.

Rotation curves and dark matter

In the 1970s, working with spectrograph designer Kent Ford, Rubin measured the rotation curves of dozens of spiral galaxies, including the Andromeda Galaxy. Contrary to expectations from Keplerian dynamics, orbital speeds stayed roughly constant with distance from the galactic centre, implying vast halos of unseen mass. Her results transformed the dark matter hypothesis, first proposed by Fritz Zwicky, into a central problem of cosmology.

Legacy

Rubin received the National Medal of Science in 1993 and campaigned throughout her career for women's access to astronomy. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile, whose Legacy Survey of Space and Time began in the 2020s, is named in her honour.
