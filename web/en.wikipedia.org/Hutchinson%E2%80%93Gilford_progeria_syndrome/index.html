Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome

Medical summary

Also known as: Progeria

Gene: LMNA (lamin A)

Cause: Usually a new (de novo) dominant mutation

Average lifespan: About 14–15 years

Prevalence: Roughly 1 in 20 million births

Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS), commonly called progeria, is an extremely rare genetic disorder in which symptoms resembling aspects of premature ageing appear at a very early age. It is named after Jonathan Hutchinson and Hastings Gilford, who described the condition in the late 19th century. Children with progeria generally appear normal at birth but begin to show signs within the first two years of life.

Signs and symptoms

Characteristic features include severe growth failure, loss of body fat and hair (alopecia), aged-looking skin, stiff joints, a small face with a beaked nose and prominent eyes, and delayed tooth formation. Intelligence is typically unaffected. The most serious complication is severe atherosclerosis, which leads to heart attacks and strokes in childhood and adolescence.

Cause

Progeria is caused by a mutation in the LMNA gene, which normally produces the structural protein lamin A. The mutation activates a cryptic splice site, producing an abnormal protein called progerin that lacks a section normally removed during processing. Progerin accumulates in cell nuclei, distorting their structure and function. The condition is almost always the result of a new spontaneous mutation rather than inheritance.

Treatment

In 2020 the United States FDA approved lonafarnib, a farnesyltransferase inhibitor originally developed for cancer, as the first treatment for progeria; studies indicated it extends lifespan by an average of several years. Supportive care includes low-dose aspirin, physical therapy, and management of cardiovascular disease.
