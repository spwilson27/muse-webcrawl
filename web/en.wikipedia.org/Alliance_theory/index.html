Alliance theory

Field: Anthropology of kinship
Key figure: Claude Lévi-Strauss
Key work: The Elementary Structures of Kinship (1949)

Alliance theory, also known as the generalised exchange theory, is a structuralist method of studying kinship relations. It was pioneered by Claude Lévi-Strauss in his 1949 work The Elementary Structures of Kinship. Alliance theory holds that the universal incest taboo is not primarily a prohibition but a positive rule of reciprocity: by forbidding marriage within one's own group, it obliges groups to exchange spouses with one another, thereby creating networks of social alliance.

Principles

Whereas descent theory, associated with British anthropologists such as A. R. Radcliffe-Brown, explained social organisation through unilineal descent groups, alliance theory focuses on marriage exchanges between groups. Lévi-Strauss distinguished "elementary structures", in which the spouse category is positively specified (for example, cross-cousin marriage), from "complex structures" governed only by negative prohibitions. In systems of generalised exchange, groups give women to one partner group and receive them from another, forming long cycles of reciprocity that integrate the society as a whole.

Influence and criticism

Alliance theory profoundly influenced structural anthropology and feminist anthropology: Gayle Rubin drew on it in her essay "The Traffic in Women" (1975). Critics have objected that it treats women as passive objects of exchange between men and that its formal models fit some ethnographic cases poorly.
