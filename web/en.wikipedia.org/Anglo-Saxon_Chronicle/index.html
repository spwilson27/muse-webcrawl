The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle is a collection of annals in Old English chronicling the history of the Anglo-Saxons. The original manuscript of the Chronicle was created late in the 9th century, probably in Wessex, during the reign of Alfred the Great. Copies were distributed to monasteries across England and were independently updated; one version, the Peterborough Chronicle, continued to be maintained until 1154, by which time its language had shifted into early Middle English. It is one of the most important sources for the history of early medieval England.

Origins and manuscripts

The Chronicle begins with an entry for 60 BC (the invasion of Julius Caesar) drawn from earlier sources such as Bede, regnal lists and Easter tables, but becomes substantially original from the later 9th century onward. Nine manuscripts or fragments survive, conventionally lettered A–I, associated with Winchester, Abingdon, Worcester, Peterborough and Canterbury. Because the copies were maintained separately, the versions differ in content and bias — for example in their treatment of the earls of the 11th century and of the Norman Conquest.

Contents and character

Most annals are terse — a king's death, a battle, a comet, a famine — but some entries expand into notable pieces of writing, including the Old English poems The Battle of Brunanburh (937) and The Battle of Maldon-era entries, and vivid narrative passages on the reign of King Stephen, whose Peterborough continuator described the anarchy of the 1130s–40s in famous terms: men said openly that "Christ and his saints slept".

Historical value

The Chronicle is the principal narrative source for Anglo-Saxon England, though historians treat it critically: dates can be displaced by a few years in the early annals, and entries reflect the politics of the monasteries that kept them. Together with Bede's Ecclesiastical History it underpins the modern understanding of the period from the Anglo-Saxon settlements to the Norman Conquest and beyond.
