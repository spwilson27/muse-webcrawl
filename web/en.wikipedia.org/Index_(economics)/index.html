Index (economics)

In economics and finance, an index (plural: indices or indexes) is a statistical measure of change in a representative group of individual data points, typically expressed as a percentage change from a base value normalized to 100 in a base period. Index numbers allow diverse quantities — prices, wages, production, or share values — to be compared over time on a common scale.

Price indices

The best-known examples are price indices. The consumer price index (CPI) tracks the cost of a basket of goods and services purchased by households and is the most widely used measure of inflation; the producer price index (PPI) measures wholesale prices. Two classical constructions are the Laspeyres index, which uses base-period quantities as weights, and the Paasche index, which uses current-period quantities; the Fisher "ideal" index is the geometric mean of the two and is used, in chained form, in measures such as the U.S. personal consumption expenditures price index.

Stock market indices

A stock market index measures the value of a portfolio of shares, such as the S&P 500, the Dow Jones Industrial Average, or the FTSE 100. Indices are computed by price weighting or market-capitalization weighting, and underlie index funds and index futures.

Other indices

Composite indices summarize complex phenomena in a single number, as in the Human Development Index, purchasing managers' indices, and indices of economic freedom. Constructing an index involves choices of components, weighting, and aggregation, which can significantly affect results.
