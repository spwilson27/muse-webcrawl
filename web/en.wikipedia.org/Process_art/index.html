Process art

Process art is an artistic movement, primarily of the 1960s and 1970s, in which the process of creating the work — gathering, sorting, dropping, pouring, and the behaviour of materials over time — is as important as, or more important than, the finished product. It is also called processism and relates to postminimalism and Arte Povera.

Characteristics

Process artists engaged with ephemeral and unconventional materials such as felt, latex, lead, ice, fat, earth, and industrial scraps, letting natural forces — gravity, melting, drying, decay — determine the final form. Works often deliberately resist monumentality and permanence, challenging the idea of the artwork as a stable, saleable object.

Artists

Robert Morris's scattered felt pieces and his 1968 essay "Anti Form" were foundational. Other practitioners include Richard Serra, whose "splash pieces" of molten lead were hurled against gallery walls; Eva Hesse, who used latex and fibreglass in precarious, sagging sculptures; Bruce Nauman; Lynda Benglis; and Barry Le Va, known for his "distributions" of scattered materials.

Legacy

Process art's emphasis on temporality and material behaviour influenced installation art, Land art, and performance art, and remains a reference point in contemporary sculpture.
