Southern blot


Schematic of the Southern blot method

A Southern blot is a method used in molecular biology for the detection of a specific DNA sequence in DNA samples. It combines electrophoresis of DNA fragments, transfer of the separated fragments to a filter membrane, and subsequent fragment detection by probe hybridization.

The method is named after the British biologist Edwin Southern, who first published it in 1975. Other blotting methods that use similar principles have taken names that play on this eponym, including the northern blot (for RNA), the western blot (for proteins), and the eastern blot.

Method

Genomic DNA is first digested with restriction enzymes to produce fragments of varying length. The fragments are separated by size using agarose gel electrophoresis, then denatured in the gel with an alkaline solution so that the double-stranded DNA becomes single-stranded. The DNA is transferred from the gel to a nitrocellulose or nylon membrane by capillary action or vacuum blotting, and immobilized on the membrane by baking or ultraviolet crosslinking. The membrane is then exposed to a labelled hybridization probe — a single-stranded DNA fragment with a sequence complementary to the sequence of interest — so that the probe binds only to its target. Unbound probe is washed away, and the pattern of hybridization is detected, historically by autoradiography when radioactive probes were used.

Applications

Southern blotting is used to determine whether a particular gene is present in a genome, to measure gene copy number, and to detect large-scale rearrangements, deletions, and insertions. It has been applied to DNA fingerprinting in forensics and paternity testing, to the diagnosis of genetic disorders such as Fragile X syndrome, and to confirm the presence of transgenes in genetically modified organisms. Although quantitative PCR and DNA sequencing have largely superseded it for many purposes, Southern blotting remains a reference method for detecting large structural changes.
