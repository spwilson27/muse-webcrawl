Louis IX (25 April 1214 – 25 August 1270), commonly revered as Saint Louis, was King of France from 1226 until his death in 1270. He is widely considered the model of the medieval Christian monarch: a crusader who led the Seventh and Eighth Crusades, a far-reaching reformer of royal justice and administration, and the only French king to be canonised. His reign is remembered as a golden age in which France achieved great economic and artistic prosperity, exemplified by the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris.

Saint Louis, the canonised king of France

Early life and regency

Louis succeeded his father Louis VIII at the age of twelve; his mother, Blanche of Castile, governed as regent and defeated baronial revolts against the child king. Louis married Margaret of Provence in 1234 and assumed personal rule as he came of age, defeating Henry III of England at Taillebourg and Saintes in 1242 and consolidating Capetian authority in the south and west.

Reforms

Louis curbed private warfare and trial by combat, promoted appeals to royal courts, and sent out royal investigators (enquêteurs) to redress abuses by officials. His ordinances reformed coinage, and his reputation for personal justice — famously dispensing judgement beneath an oak at Vincennes — made him the arbiter of Europe; he mediated between Henry III and his barons in the Mise of Amiens (1264). The Treaty of Paris (1259) settled outstanding claims with England.

Crusades and death

Taking a vow during a grave illness in 1244, Louis led the Seventh Crusade to Egypt (1248–1254): after capturing Damietta, the crusader army was destroyed at Mansurah and Louis himself captured at Fariskur in 1250, then ransomed. He spent four years strengthening the Crusader states before returning to France. In 1270 he launched the Eighth Crusade against Tunis, where he died — probably of dysentery — on 25 August 1270.

Legacy

Canonised in 1297, Saint Louis became the patron of numerous institutions; his relic collection, housed in the Sainte-Chapelle (consecrated 1248), included the Crown of Thorns, purchased in 1239. The city of St. Louis, Missouri, and many churches bear his name. His life is known in vivid detail from Jean de Joinville's Life of Saint Louis.
