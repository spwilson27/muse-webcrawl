McMahon–Hussein Correspondence

The McMahon–Hussein Correspondence is a series of letters exchanged during World War I between Hussein bin Ali, Sharif of Mecca, and Lieutenant Colonel Sir Henry McMahon, British High Commissioner in Egypt. During their correspondence from July 1915 to February 1916, the British government promised Hussein control of Arab lands in exchange for the Arabs rebelling against the Ottoman Empire, an arrangement that led to the Arab Revolt of 1916.

Contents

Ten letters survive. Hussein demanded British recognition of an independent Arab state spanning the Arabian Peninsula and the Levant, including Palestine. McMahon's reply of 24 October 1915 accepted most of the demands but excluded "the districts of Mersina and Alexandretta and portions of Syria lying to the west of the districts of Damascus, Homs, Hama and Aleppo", on the grounds that they could not be said to be purely Arab — an ambiguity that became the heart of the later dispute over whether Palestine had been promised to the Arabs.

Conflict with other pledges

The correspondence conflicted with two other British commitments: the secret Sykes–Picot Agreement of 1916, which divided the region into British and French spheres, and the Balfour Declaration of 1917, which promised a "national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine. The letters were not officially published by Britain until 1939, and the controversy over their meaning has been central to the history of the Arab–Israeli conflict.
