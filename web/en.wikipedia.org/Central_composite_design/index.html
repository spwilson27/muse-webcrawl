Central composite design

In statistics, a central composite design is an experimental design, useful in response surface methodology, for building a second-order (quadratic) model for the response variable without needing to use a complete three-level factorial experiment. It was introduced by George E. P. Box and K. B. Wilson in 1951.

Structure

The design consists of three groups of experimental points:

Factorial points — a two-level factorial (or fractional factorial) design in the factors, with levels coded as −1 and +1. Axial (star) points — two points on the axis of each factor, at a distance α from the design center and zero on all other factors. Center points — replicated runs at the center of the design, used to estimate pure error and check for curvature.

Choice of α

The value of α determines properties of the design. A design is rotatable when the variance of the predicted response depends only on the distance from the center; for a rotatable central composite design, α is the fourth root of the number of factorial points. When α = 1, the star points lie on the faces of the factorial cube, giving a face-centered design, which requires only three levels of each factor and is popular when the axial points of a rotatable design would lie outside safe operating limits.

Application

After a first-order model from a screening experiment suggests the optimum is near, a central composite design can be run sequentially: the factorial and center points are often already available, and the axial points are added to complete the design. The fitted quadratic model is then used to locate a stationary point and characterize the response surface. The design is one of the most widely used classes of response surface designs in industrial experimentation, alongside the Box–Behnken design.
