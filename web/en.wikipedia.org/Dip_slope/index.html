Dip slope

A dip slope is a geological landform: a slope whose surface is parallel to the bedding planes (strata) of the underlying rock, descending in the same direction and at roughly the same angle as the dip of the strata. Dip slopes form one side of a cuesta or hogback ridge, opposite the steep scarp slope (escarpment) that cuts across the bedding.

Diagram of a cuesta: the gentle dip slope and the steep scarp slope.

Formation

Dip slopes develop where tilted or gently folded sedimentary strata of differing resistance are eroded. A hard, resistant layer (such as limestone or sandstone) protects the rocks beneath; erosion breaches it along a steep scarp face, while the gently inclined upper surface of the hard layer forms the dip slope behind it. Classic examples include the dip slopes of the Chiltern Hills and the North and South Downs in England, which slope gently away from their chalk escarpments.

Characteristics

Because the slope follows the bedding, dip slopes tend to be long and uniform. They can be susceptible to landslides where bedding-plane weaknesses are lubricated by groundwater, and in civil engineering, cuts made parallel to a dip slope can destabilise the strata.
