Aubade

An aubade is a morning love song (as opposed to a serenade, intended for performance in the evening), or a song or poem about lovers separating at dawn. The term derives from the Old French aube, meaning dawn. The aubade gained particular popularity in the 19th century, and was also a distinct genre of poetry in the courtly love tradition of the troubadours, where it was known in Occitan as the alba.

The aubade is a song of dawn and parting lovers.

The medieval alba

In the troubadour alba, lovers who have spent the night together lament the coming of dawn, announced by a watchman (gaita) who warns them of the risk of discovery by the jealous husband. A secular counterpart was written by medieval German Minnesänger as the Tagelied. Famous English examples include the dawn parting in Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde and the dialogue after the wedding night in Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet, where Juliet insists the bird they hear is the nightingale, not the lark.

Later poetry

John Donne's "The Sun Rising" is a celebrated variation that rebukes the sun for disturbing the lovers. In the 20th century, Philip Larkin's poem "Aubade" (1977) inverted the tradition: its speaker wakes before dawn to contemplate mortality and the fear of death.

In music

Composers have used "aubade" as a title for morning pieces and dawn serenades, including works by Lalo, Poulenc and others; in ballet and orchestral music an aubade is typically a short, bright concert piece evoking morning.
