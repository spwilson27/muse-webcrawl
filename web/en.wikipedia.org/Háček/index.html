Háček

Mark: ˇ (caron)
Unicode: U+02C7; combining U+030C
Used in: Czech, Slovak, Slovene, Croatian, Lithuanian, pinyin

A háček (from Czech háček, "little hook"), also called a caron, is a diacritical mark ◌̌ placed over letters to modify their sound. It typically indicates palatalisation or a postalveolar pronunciation, as in Czech č /tʃ/, š /ʃ/, ž /ʒ/, ř, ň, ď, ť, and ľ.

History

The mark was introduced in the early 15th century in the orthographic reforms attributed to Jan Hus, whose De orthographia bohemica (c. 1406) replaced digraphs with single dotted letters; the dot over consonants later developed into the háček. It spread to Slovak, Croatian (in Ljudevit Gaj's Latin alphabet), Slovene, Serbian Latin, Lithuanian, and Latvian-related scholarly transliteration.

Use

In Czech and Slovak it marks postalveolar fricatives and affricates and palatal consonants. In Slovene and Croatian it marks č, š, ž. Lithuanian uses č, š, ž. In Mandarin pinyin the caron marks the third (falling-rising) tone, as in hǎo. In the International Phonetic Alphabet a subscript caron denotes voicing.

Typography

With lowercase d, l, and t in Czech and Slovak typefaces, the háček often takes an apostrophe-like reduced form (ď, ľ, ť). The name "caron" is of uncertain origin, possibly a portmanteau of "caret" and "macron".
