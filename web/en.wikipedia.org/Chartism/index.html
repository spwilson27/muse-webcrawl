Chartism

Chartism was a working-class movement for political reform in the United Kingdom that erupted in 1838 and continued as a mass movement until the late 1850s. It took its name from the People's Charter of 1838 and gained support among artisans and industrial workers embittered by economic depression, the hated New Poor Law of 1834, and their exclusion from the franchise, which the Reform Act 1832 had extended only to the middle classes. Chartism is often regarded as the first mass working-class political movement in history.

The Six Points

The People's Charter, drafted by the London cabinet-maker William Lovett and the radical MP Francis Place, demanded:

  • A vote for every man over 21, of sound mind, and not undergoing punishment for a crime;
  • The secret ballot;
  • No property qualification for Members of Parliament;
  • Payment of MPs;
  • Equal electoral districts; and
  • Annual parliaments.

Course of the movement

Chartism surged in three waves coinciding with economic slumps. The first national petition (1839) gathered about 1.28 million signatures; its rejection by Parliament was followed by the Newport Rising (November 1839), an armed march on Newport, Wales, led by John Frost, which troops suppressed with loss of life, and by widespread arrests. A second petition in 1842, with over 3 million signatures, accompanied the "Plug Plot" general strikes. Led in its later phase by the charismatic Irish-born Feargus O'Connor and his Northern Star newspaper, the movement revived during the European revolutionary year 1848: a third petition — claimed at nearly 6 million signatures (actually under 2 million, with many forged) — was to be presented after a mass meeting on Kennington Common on 10 April 1848. The government mobilised troops and special constables; O'Connor agreed to disperse the crowd, and the petition was carried to Parliament in cabs and rejected.

Decline and legacy

Splintered between "moral force" and "physical force" strategies, and helped into decline by mid-Victorian prosperity, Chartism faded after 1848, though Ernest Jones sustained a radical rump into the 1850s. Its demands, dismissed as absurd in 1838, were nearly all eventually enacted: manhood suffrage (1918), the secret ballot (1872), payment of MPs (1911), abolition of the property qualification (1858), and substantially equal districts (from 1885). Only annual parliaments were never adopted. Chartism shaped British labour politics, fed organisers into trade unions, Chartist emigration streams, and later socialist movements.
