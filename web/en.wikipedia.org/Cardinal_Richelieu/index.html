Armand Jean du Plessis, Duke of Richelieu (1585–1642), commonly known as Cardinal Richelieu, was a French statesman and clergyman who served as chief minister to King Louis XIII from 1624 until his death. He is widely regarded as the architect of the French absolute monarchy and of France's emergence as the dominant power in Europe.

Rise

Nominated bishop of Luçon at 21, Richelieu entered national politics through the patronage of the queen mother, Marie de' Medici, becoming a secretary of state in 1616. Exiled during her fall from favor, he negotiated her reconciliation with the king, was made a cardinal in 1622, and entered the royal council in 1624, soon dominating it.

Domestic policy

Richelieu's program had three aims: break the political and military power of the Huguenots, humble the great nobility, and exalt the crown. After the Siege of La Rochelle (1627–28), the Peace of Alès (1629) stripped the Huguenots of their fortified places while confirming their religious rights. Noble conspiracies — those of Chalais, Montmorency, and Cinq-Mars — were answered with executions. Intendants extended royal administration into the provinces, and taxation to fund war rose steeply, provoking revolts such as the Croquants and the Nu-pieds.

Foreign policy

Guided by raison d'état, the Catholic cardinal subsidized Protestant powers — Sweden under Gustavus Adolphus and the Dutch — against the Habsburgs, and from 1635 brought France openly into the Thirty Years' War against Spain and the Emperor. His diplomacy prepared the settlement completed under his successor Mazarin at Westphalia (1648) and the Pyrenees (1659).

Culture and legacy

Richelieu founded the Académie française (1635), rebuilt the Sorbonne, and patronized the arts. Alexandre Dumas's The Three Musketeers fixed his popular image as a scheming villain, but historians generally credit him with laying the foundations of the modern French state.
