Cantata

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cantata (Italian for "sung", from cantare, "to sing") is a vocal composition with an instrumental accompaniment, typically in several movements, often involving a choir. The term originated in the early 17th century, in contrast to the instrumental sonata, a piece to be played.

Baroque cantata

The cantata flourished in the Baroque era in both secular and sacred forms. In Italy, composers such as Alessandro Scarlatti wrote hundreds of secular chamber cantatas for solo voice. In Germany, the sacred cantata became central to Lutheran church music, reaching its peak in the works of Johann Sebastian Bach, who composed several cycles of church cantatas for the Sundays and feast days of the liturgical year — around 200 of his cantatas survive.

Later usage

In later centuries the term was applied more loosely to choral works of varied scale, including pieces by Beethoven, Berlioz, and Britten. In the 20th century the cantata often took on a commemorative or ceremonial character.
