The Articles of Confederation and Perpetual Union was an agreement among the 13 states of the United States of America that served as its first constitution. It was approved by the Second Continental Congress in November 1777 and ratified by the states in 1781. It established a confederation of sovereign states and a weak central government, the Congress of the Confederation.

Provisions

The Articles retained state sovereignty and independence, granting Congress limited powers: conducting foreign affairs, declaring war, and requisitioning funds and troops from the states. Congress could not levy taxes directly, regulate interstate commerce, or compel state compliance. Amendments required unanimous consent.

Weaknesses and replacement

Chronic funding shortages, commercial disputes among states, and unrest such as Shays' Rebellion exposed the system's weaknesses. In 1787 the Philadelphia Convention drafted the current United States Constitution, which replaced the Articles in 1789 with a stronger federal government.

Achievements

Despite its flaws, the Confederation Congress conducted the war to a successful conclusion, negotiated the Treaty of Paris (1783), and passed the Northwest Ordinance of 1787, which organized western territories and banned slavery there.
