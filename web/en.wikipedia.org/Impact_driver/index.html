Impact driver

An impact driver is a power tool designed to drive screws, bolts, and other fasteners by delivering strong, sudden rotational and downward force — rotational impacts — in addition to continuous rotation. It is distinguished from an impact wrench, which is typically larger and uses a square drive for sockets, whereas impact drivers use a 1/4-inch hexagonal quick-change chuck for screwdriver bits.

Mechanism

Inside the tool, a motor spins a spring-loaded hammer and anvil mechanism. As resistance builds, the hammer disengages, is spun rapidly by the motor, and then strikes the anvil tangentially, delivering a burst of torque. This cycle repeats many times per second (commonly measured in impacts per minute, IPM), producing far more peak torque than a conventional drill/driver of similar size while transferring little reaction torque to the user's wrist.

Uses

Impact drivers are widely used in construction, carpentry, and assembly work for driving long screws and lag bolts into wood, metal, and masonry (with suitable bits), tasks that would stall or strain a standard drill. Cordless models powered by lithium-ion batteries (commonly 12 V, 18 V, or 20 V Max platforms) dominate the market.

Comparison with drills

Compared with a drill/driver, an impact driver is smaller, lighter, and produces higher torque without kickback, but it lacks a precision clutch and is much louder in operation. Drills remain better suited to drilling clean holes and to delicate fastening tasks.
