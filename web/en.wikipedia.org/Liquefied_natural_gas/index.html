Liquefied natural gas (LNG) is natural gas that has been cooled to approximately −162 °C, at which it condenses into a liquid occupying about 1/600th of its gaseous volume. Liquefaction makes it economical to transport gas by ship between continents where pipelines are impractical, and LNG has grown into a globally traded commodity central to energy security in Asia and Europe.

Production and transport

At liquefaction plants ("trains"), raw gas is purified of water, carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and heavier hydrocarbons, then refrigerated through cascades of propane, ethylene, and methane refrigerants. The LNG is stored in insulated tanks and loaded onto LNG carriers — most using membrane containment or spherical Moss tanks — for voyages of days to weeks. At the destination, regasification terminals warm the LNG back into gas for pipeline distribution; floating storage and regasification units (FSRUs) allow rapid deployment, as used extensively in Europe after 2022.

History and market

The first commercial LNG shipment crossed the Atlantic in 1959 aboard the converted Methane Pioneer; regular trade began from Algeria to Britain and France in 1964–65. Qatar, Australia, and the United States became the dominant exporters in the 2010s–2020s, the US leveraging shale gas and Gulf Coast terminals. Spot and short-term trading has grown at the expense of traditional long-term, oil-indexed contracts.

Safety and environment

LNG is not stored under high pressure and is not explosive as a liquid; hazards arise if vapour clouds ignite, and spills vaporize rapidly without polluting soil or water, though they can cause cryogenic burns and brittle fracture of steel. Lifecycle emissions benefit from gas burning cleaner than coal, offset partly by methane leakage in the supply chain and the energy cost of liquefaction.
