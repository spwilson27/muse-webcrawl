The Panama scandals (French: scandale de Panama) were a corruption affair in the French Third Republic arising from the collapse of Ferdinand de Lesseps's Panama Canal Company in 1889. The liquidation was the largest bankruptcy of the 19th century, ruining hundreds of thousands of small French investors, and subsequent revelations that company funds had been used to bribe politicians and journalists made it one of the defining scandals of the era.

Contemporary press coverage of the Panama trials

Background

Flush with his triumph at the Suez Canal, Ferdinand de Lesseps founded the Compagnie Universelle du Canal Interocéanique de Panama in 1879 to cut a sea-level canal through the Isthmus of Panama. Tropical disease, landslides and the engineering difficulty of a sea-level cut caused costs to explode; to keep funds flowing, the company raised repeated share and bond issues, and secretly paid newspapers for favourable coverage and bribed deputies to authorise a lottery loan. In February 1889 the company was declared bankrupt, with losses of roughly 1.8 billion francs.

The scandal breaks

In 1892 the press — notably Édouard Drumont's antisemitic paper La Libre Parole — exposed the bribery network run through intermediaries including the financier Baron Jacques de Reinach (who died by suicide in November 1892) and Cornelius Herz, who fled to England. A parliamentary inquiry revealed that around a hundred deputies and senators had taken money. In the 1893 fraud trial, Ferdinand de Lesseps, his son Charles, and the engineer Gustave Eiffel were convicted; the elderly de Lesseps did not serve his sentence, and several convictions were later overturned on technicalities. A second trial convicted Charles de Lesseps and a former minister of public works.

Consequences

The affair discredited the political class, discredited several newspapers, and fuelled the antisemitic and anti-parliamentary movements of the 1890s, forming a backdrop to the Dreyfus affair. The canal itself was eventually completed by the United States, which bought the company's assets in 1904 and opened the Panama Canal in 1914.
