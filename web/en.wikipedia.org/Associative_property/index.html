Associative property

FieldMathematics (algebra)
Statement(a · b) · c = a · (b · c)

In mathematics, the associative property is a property of some binary operations stating that rearranging the parentheses in an expression will not change the result. Formally, an operation · is associative if (a · b) · c = a · (b · c) for all a, b, and c.

Examples

Addition and multiplication of real numbers are associative: (2 + 3) + 4 = 2 + (3 + 4) = 9, and (2 × 3) × 4 = 2 × (3 × 4) = 24. String concatenation, function composition, and matrix multiplication are also associative, which is why such expressions can be written without parentheses. By contrast, subtraction, division, and exponentiation are not associative: (5 − 3) − 1 ≠ 5 − (3 − 1).

Algebraic structures

Associativity is a defining axiom of groups and monoids, and of the addition and multiplication in rings and fields. Because associative operations do not require parentheses, chains of them are notationally unambiguous, a convenience relied upon throughout algebra. A weaker related notion, power associativity, applies to operations such as the cross product contexts and octonion multiplication.
