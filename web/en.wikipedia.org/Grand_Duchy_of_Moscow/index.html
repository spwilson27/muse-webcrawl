Grand Duchy of Moscow

The Grand Duchy of Moscow (Russian: Великое княжество Московское), also known as Muscovy, was a medieval Russian principality that existed from 1263 to 1547. Centered on the city of Moscow, it grew from a small appanage of Vladimir-Suzdal into the dominant state of northeastern Rus', and eventually became the core of the Tsardom of Russia.

Origins

The principality was founded in the 13th century when Daniel, the youngest son of Alexander Nevsky, inherited Moscow around 1263. Under Daniel and his son Yuri, the principality began to expand, annexing Kolomna and Mozhaysk. Daniel's descendants benefited from Moscow's position at the crossroads of trading routes and from their role as collectors of tribute for the Golden Horde.

Rise to power

Under Ivan I "Kalita" (the Moneybag, r. 1325–1340), Moscow secured the title of Grand Prince of Vladimir and became the seat of the Russian Orthodox metropolitan. Dmitry Donskoy won a major victory over the Mongols at the Battle of Kulikovo in 1380, greatly enhancing Moscow's prestige.

Unification of Rus'

During the 15th and early 16th centuries, Moscow absorbed its rivals: Novgorod was annexed in 1478, Tver in 1485, and Pskov, Smolensk and Ryazan followed. In 1480 the Great Stand on the Ugra River marked the end of Mongol dominion over Rus'. Ivan III adopted the title "Sovereign of all Rus'" and married Sophia Palaiologina, niece of the last Byzantine emperor.

From duchy to tsardom

Ivan IV "the Terrible" was crowned Tsar of All Russia in 1547, formally transforming the Grand Duchy into the Tsardom of Russia, although the name Muscovy continued to be used in Western Europe for some time afterward.
