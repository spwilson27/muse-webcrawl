The Crab Nebula (catalogue designations M1, NGC 1952, Taurus A) is a supernova remnant and pulsar wind nebula in the constellation of Taurus. The common name comes from a drawing by William Parsons, 3rd Earl of Rosse, in 1842 (or 1844) using a 36-inch telescope, in which it resembled a crab. The nebula was discovered by English astronomer John Bevis in 1731 and independently by Charles Messier in 1758.

Corresponding to the bright supernova recorded by Chinese astronomers in AD 1054 (“SN 1054”), the nebula is one of the best-studied objects in astronomy. Located about 6,500 light-years from Earth, it has a diameter of about 11 light-years and is expanding at roughly 1,500 kilometres per second.

The Crab Pulsar

At the centre of the nebula lies the Crab Pulsar, a neutron star about 28–30 kilometres in diameter spinning 30.2 times per second. It emits pulses of radiation from gamma rays to radio waves and is one of the brightest persistent gamma-ray sources in the sky. The pulsar was discovered in 1968 and its identification with the remnant provided key evidence linking pulsars to supernovae.

Structure and composition

The nebula consists of an outer shell of expanding filaments of ionised gas, rich in helium and hydrogen, surrounding a central blue region of synchrotron radiation produced by relativistic electrons spiralling in the pulsar's magnetic field. Its total mass is estimated at about 4.6 solar masses.

Transits by solar system objects

The Crab Nebula's torus lies close to the ecliptic; the Moon and planets occasionally transit it. In 2003 the occultation of the nebula by Saturn's moon Titan was used to study Titan's atmosphere.
