Generalplan Ost (German for "Master Plan for the East") was a secret plan developed by Nazi Germany during the Second World War for the ethnic cleansing, extermination, and colonization of Eastern Europe. It envisaged the removal — by deportation, starvation, and mass murder — of tens of millions of Slavs and other peoples from territories conquered from Poland and the Soviet Union, and their replacement with German settlers.

Development

Versions of the plan were prepared in 1941 and 1942, principally by the Reich Security Main Office and by academics such as the agronomist Konrad Meyer, working for Reich Commissioner Heinrich Himmler in his capacity as Reich Commissioner for the Consolidation of German Nationhood. The plan formed part of the broader ideology of Lebensraum ("living space").

Scope

The various drafts proposed that the majority of the native populations of Poland, Ukraine, Belarus, Russia, and the Baltic states be eliminated over a period of some 25–30 years, with estimates of those to be removed ranging from 31 million to over 50 million people. Populations judged capable of "Germanization" would be assimilated; the rest were to be deported to Siberia, enslaved, or killed. The Hunger Plan, which caused the deaths of millions of Soviet citizens during the war, is often regarded as a related component.

Implementation and legacy

The full plan was never implemented because Germany lost the war, but elements were put into practice in occupied Poland and elsewhere, including mass expulsions and the settlement of ethnic Germans. Knowledge of the plan derives mainly from surviving memoranda, notably the April 1942 critique by Erhard Wetzel, since most copies of the plan itself were destroyed. It is cited by historians as evidence of the genocidal aims of the Nazi regime in the East.
