Amniocentesis is a medical procedure used primarily in the prenatal diagnosis of genetic conditions. A small sample of amniotic fluid — the fluid surrounding the fetus, which contains fetal cells and chemicals — is removed from the amniotic sac through a fine needle passed through the mother's abdomen under ultrasound guidance, and analysed in the laboratory.

Amniocentesis: a needle guided by ultrasound draws a sample of amniotic fluid.

Indications

Amniocentesis is usually offered between 15 and 20 weeks of pregnancy when screening tests or history suggest an increased chance of a chromosomal or genetic condition — for example a high-risk first-trimester screening result, advanced maternal age, a previous affected pregnancy, or a family history of an inherited disorder. It can diagnose Down syndrome (trisomy 21) and other trisomies, single-gene disorders such as cystic fibrosis where the familial mutation is known, and — via alpha-fetoprotein levels in the fluid — open neural tube defects. Later in pregnancy it has been used to assess fetal lung maturity, infection and haemolytic disease.

Procedure

After ultrasound locates the fetus, placenta and a pocket of fluid, the clinician cleans the abdomen and inserts a thin needle into the amniotic sac, withdrawing about 20 millilitres of fluid; the fetal cells in the sample are cultured or analysed directly by techniques such as karyotyping, fluorescence in situ hybridisation, chromosomal microarray and DNA sequencing. Results for rapid tests arrive within days; full karyotypes take one to two weeks. The procedure takes a few minutes and is usually done without anaesthesia, though local anaesthetic may be used.

Risks and alternatives

The main risk is procedure-related miscarriage, historically quoted at about 1 in 200 but estimated in modern studies at closer to 1 in 300–900. Other risks include infection, leakage of amniotic fluid, cramping and, for Rh-negative mothers, sensitisation — prevented with anti-D immunoglobulin. Alternatives include chorionic villus sampling (performed earlier, at 10–13 weeks) and non-invasive prenatal testing of cell-free fetal DNA in maternal blood, a screening rather than diagnostic test.

History

Amniotic fluid sampling was described in the late 19th century and was used in the 1950s to manage Rh disease. In 1966 researchers cultured fetal cells from the fluid and determined their karyotype, and by the 1970s amniocentesis had become an established method of prenatal diagnosis, greatly refined by real-time ultrasound guidance.
