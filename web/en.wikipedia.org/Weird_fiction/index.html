Weird fiction

Weird fiction is a subgenre of speculative fiction originating in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. It blends elements of horror, fantasy and science fiction, and is characterized by an atmosphere of dread at realities beyond human comprehension. The term is closely associated with the American pulp magazine Weird Tales (founded 1923) and with H. P. Lovecraft, who wrote essays defining the genre as requiring "a certain atmosphere of breathless and unexplainable dread of outer, unknown forces."

History

Precursors include Edgar Allan Poe, Algernon Blackwood, Lord Dunsany, Arthur Machen, M. R. James and Ambrose Bierce. In the pulp era, Lovecraft, Clark Ashton Smith and Robert E. Howard expanded the "weird tale," creating cosmic horror in which humanity is insignificant before ancient alien entities such as Cthulhu.

New Weird

Since the 1990s a "New Weird" movement — authors such as China Miéville, Jeff VanderMeer, M. John Harrison and Thomas Ligotti — has revived the mode, mixing it with slipstream and literary fiction. VanderMeer's Annihilation and Miéville's Perdido Street Station are prominent examples.
