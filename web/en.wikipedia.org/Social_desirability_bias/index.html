Social desirability bias

Social desirability bias is a type of response bias in research, especially in self-report surveys and questionnaires, in which respondents answer questions in a manner that will be viewed favorably by others. It can take the form of over-reporting "good" behavior or under-reporting "bad" or undesirable behavior, and it tends to inflate results on sensitive topics such as drug use, income, voting, and personal hygiene.

Measurement

The best-known instrument for detecting the bias is the Marlowe–Crowne Social Desirability Scale, introduced in 1960, a true–false questionnaire of culturally approved but improbable behaviors. Paulhus's Balanced Inventory of Desirable Responding distinguishes two components: impression management (deliberate tailoring of answers to an audience) and self-deceptive enhancement (an honestly held but inflated self-view).

Effects on research

The bias is a persistent problem in survey methodology. It is implicated in discrepancies between reported and actual voter turnout, in underestimates of prejudice, and in health research where patients overstate adherence to treatment. Mode of administration matters: respondents report more undesirable behavior in self-administered and computerized surveys than in face-to-face interviews.

Mitigation

Researchers reduce the bias by guaranteeing anonymity, using indirect questioning, randomized response techniques, the "bogus pipeline" procedure, and validating self-reports against behavioral or archival measures.
