Black Panther Party

Black Panther Party members, late 1960s.

The Black Panther Party (originally the Black Panther Party for Self-Defense) was a Marxist-Leninist and black power political organization founded by college students Bobby Seale and Huey P. Newton in October 1966 in Oakland, California. The party was active in the United States between 1966 and 1982, with chapters in many major American cities and international chapters in the United Kingdom and Algeria.

Founding and program

The Panthers were founded in response to police brutality against African American communities. Their founding document, the Ten-Point Program, demanded full employment, decent housing, exemption from the military draft, and an end to police brutality. Members initially gained attention for openly carrying firearms while observing police patrols, which was then legal under California law.

Survival programs

Beyond political activism, the party organized community "survival programs," most famously the Free Breakfast for Children program, which fed tens of thousands of children in cities across the country, as well as free health clinics, sickle-cell anemia testing, food distribution, and liberation schools. Historians credit these programs with influencing later federal school breakfast initiatives.

Government response and decline

The Federal Bureau of Investigation, under J. Edgar Hoover's COINTELPRO program, targeted the party with surveillance, infiltration, disinformation, and raids; Hoover described the Panthers as "the greatest threat to the internal security of the country" in 1969. A police raid in Chicago in December 1969 killed leaders Fred Hampton and Mark Clark. Internal divisions, criminal charges against members, and Newton's increasingly erratic leadership contributed to decline; the party formally dissolved in 1982.

Legacy

The Black Panther Party remains an influential symbol of Black empowerment and community organizing, and scholarly reassessment has emphasized its community service work and its role in shaping American politics and culture.
