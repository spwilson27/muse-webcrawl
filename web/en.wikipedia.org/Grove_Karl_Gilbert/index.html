Grove Karl Gilbert

Grove Karl Gilbert (May 6, 1843 – May 1, 1918), often known as G. K. Gilbert, was an American geologist regarded as one of the founders of modern geomorphology. Working for the United States Geological Survey from its founding in 1879, he made fundamental contributions to the study of erosion, river processes, lacustrine geology, impact craters and isostasy, and produced classic monographs on the Henry Mountains of Utah and on Pleistocene Lake Bonneville.

Career

Gilbert studied at the University of Rochester and served in the Civil War before joining the Wheeler Survey and then John Wesley Powell's surveys of the American West. His Report on the Geology of the Henry Mountains (1877) introduced the concept of the laccolith and described landscape as a balance between rock resistance and erosion. His monograph on Lake Bonneville (1890) documented the shorelines of the vanished Pleistocene lake and the crustal rebound following its desiccation, an early demonstration of isostasy.

Later work

Gilbert studied Meteor Crater in Arizona, initially attributing it to volcanism but helping frame the impact-crater debate; he also proposed that lunar craters were impact features. After the 1906 San Francisco earthquake he photographed and analysed the surface rupture of the San Andreas Fault. His 1914 study of hydraulic-mining debris in the Sierra Nevada remains a landmark in fluvial sediment transport. He twice served as president of the Geological Society of America, and the GSA's G. K. Gilbert Award for geomorphology is named in his honour.
