Materialism (philosophy)

In philosophy, materialism is the doctrine that all things are composed of material and that all phenomena, including consciousness, result from material interactions. It is a form of monism, contrasting with idealism, dualism and pluralism, and is closely related to the modern view of physicalism, which holds that everything supervenes on the physical.

History

Materialist ideas appear in ancient Greek atomism of Democritus and Epicurus, in Lucretius' poem De rerum natura, and in ancient Indian and Chinese thought. In the Enlightenment, figures such as La Mettrie (L'Homme Machine, 1747), Diderot and d'Holbach defended mechanistic materialism. Marx and Engels developed dialectical and historical materialism, applying materialist analysis to society and history. In the 20th century, philosophers such as J.J.C. Smart and David Armstrong argued for mind–brain identity theory, while eliminative materialists such as Paul and Patricia Churchland hold that folk-psychological concepts may be discarded by mature neuroscience.

Arguments and criticism

Supporters cite the causal closure of the physical world and the success of the physical sciences. Critics raise the hard problem of consciousness, qualia, intentionality and abstract objects such as numbers as difficulties for materialist accounts. Hempel's dilemma questions how "material" should be defined — by current physics, which is incomplete, or future physics, which is unknown.
