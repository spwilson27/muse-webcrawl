Indian Civil Rights Act

Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968
EnactedApril 11, 1968
Public lawPub. L. 90-284
Codified25 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1303
Signed byPresident Lyndon B. Johnson

The Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968 (ICRA) is a United States federal law that applies portions of the Bill of Rights to American Indian tribal governments. Enacted as Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1968, it imposed most of the restrictions on government contained in the First, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, and Eighth Amendments, as well as the equal protection and due process clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment, upon the governments of federally recognized tribes.

Background

Before 1968, the U.S. Supreme Court had held in cases such as Talton v. Mayes (1896) that the Bill of Rights did not apply to tribal governments, because tribes were separate sovereigns whose powers pre-dated the U.S. Constitution. Congressional hearings in the 1960s, led by Senator Sam Ervin, examined complaints about tribal government abuses and produced the ICRA as a compromise between protecting individual rights and preserving tribal sovereignty.

Provisions

The Act guarantees, among other rights, freedom of speech, press, and religion; protection against unreasonable search and seizure; protection against double jeopardy and self-incrimination; speedy and public trials; and equal protection under tribal law. Notably, it did not impose the Establishment Clause on tribes, nor did it require a right to appointed counsel in the same manner as federal law, and it originally limited tribal criminal sentences to six months' imprisonment and a $500 fine. The Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 later allowed enhanced sentencing of up to three years per offence under certain conditions.

Enforcement

In Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez (1978), the Supreme Court held that the ICRA created no federal cause of action against tribes except for habeas corpus petitions challenging detention, and that tribal sovereign immunity barred most suits. As a result, the statute is primarily enforced in tribal courts.
