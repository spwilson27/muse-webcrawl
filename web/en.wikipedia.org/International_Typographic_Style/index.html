International Typographic Style

The International Typographic Style, also known as the Swiss Style, is a graphic design style that emerged in Russia, the Netherlands and Germany in the 1920s and was further developed by designers in Switzerland after 1945. It emphasises cleanliness, readability and objectivity, and favours asymmetrical layouts, grids, sans-serif typefaces such as Akzidenz-Grotesk and Helvetica, flush-left ragged-right text, and photography instead of illustration or drawing.

History

The style grew out of earlier movements including De Stijl, Bauhaus and Jan Tschichold's "New Typography" of the 1920s. In postwar Switzerland, designers such as Max Bill, Ernst Keller, Josef Müller-Brockmann, Armin Hofmann and Emil Ruder — centred on schools in Zürich and Basel — systematised the approach. Müller-Brockmann's book Grid Systems in Graphic Design (1981) codified the mathematical grid that became the style's signature device.

Characteristics and influence

Swiss Style design uses a modular grid to organise content; type is set in a limited range of sizes with clear hierarchy; lowercase letterforms, generous white space and a restrained colour palette — often black, white and a single accent such as red — are typical. The style's belief that design should be socially useful and the content should speak for itself made it the basis of corporate identity systems worldwide from the 1950s to the 1970s, influential in the United States through figures and firms such as Paul Rand, the Container Corporation of America, and the New York City Transit Authority Graphics Standards Manual by Massimo Vignelli and Bob Noorda (1970). Its principles continue to shape modernist and minimalist web and interface design.

Criticism

Later designers criticised the style as formulaic and cold; the postmodern design of the 1970s and 1980s, including Wolfgang Weingart's rule-breaking work at Basel, deliberately subverted its conventions, though the Swiss Style's emphasis on clarity remains a cornerstone of design education.
