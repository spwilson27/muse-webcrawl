Antidepressant

Antidepressants are a class of medications used to treat major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders, chronic pain, and other conditions. They aim to correct chemical imbalances of neurotransmitters in the brain that are believed to be responsible for changes in mood and behavior.

Classes

The main classes include selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as fluoxetine, sertraline, and escitalopram; serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) such as venlafaxine and duloxetine; tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) such as amitriptyline; monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) such as phenelzine; and atypical agents such as bupropion and mirtazapine. The first antidepressants, iproniazid and imipramine, were introduced in the 1950s.

Efficacy and use

Antidepressants are among the most commonly prescribed medications in many countries. Meta-analyses generally find them more effective than placebo for moderate to severe depression, and guidelines recommend them, often combined with psychotherapy. Onset of therapeutic effect typically takes two to six weeks.

Side effects and discontinuation

Side effects vary by class and include nausea, sexual dysfunction, weight change, and insomnia. Abrupt discontinuation, especially of SSRIs and SNRIs, can cause a discontinuation syndrome. Concerns about a possible increase in suicidal thinking in adolescents led regulators to add boxed warnings, a topic that remains debated.
