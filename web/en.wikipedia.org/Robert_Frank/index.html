Robert Frank (November 9, 1924 – September 9, 2019) was a Swiss-American photographer and documentary filmmaker. His most notable work, the 1958 book The Americans, radically changed the language of photography with its grainy, spontaneous, and often melancholy images of postwar American life, and is widely regarded as one of the most influential photography books of the twentieth century.

The Americans

Born in Zürich, Frank emigrated to the United States in 1947 and worked as a fashion and commercial photographer in New York. With a Guggenheim Fellowship in 1955, he travelled some 10,000 miles across the country by car, taking roughly 27,000 photographs, of which 83 were selected for the book. First published in France in 1958 and in the United States in 1959 with an introduction by Jack Kerouac, the book's pictures of diners, jukeboxes, funerals, rodeos, and segregated Southern lunch counters portrayed an America marked by loneliness, racial division, and quiet despair. Initially criticized for its bleak tone and loose technique, it was later canonized as a masterpiece of street photography.

Film work

From the late 1950s Frank increasingly turned to film, beginning with Pull My Daisy (1959), co-directed with Alfred Leslie and narrated by Kerouac. His experimental autobiographical films include Me and My Brother (1968) and the Rolling Stones documentary Cocksucker Blues (1972), which was suppressed by the band. He divided his later life between New York and Mabou, Nova Scotia.
