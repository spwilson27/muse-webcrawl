Phospholipid

Phospholipids are a class of lipids whose molecule has a hydrophilic "head" containing a phosphate group and two hydrophobic "tails" derived from fatty acids, joined by a glycerol molecule (in glycerophospholipids) or by sphingosine (in sphingophospholipids). They are the major component of all cell membranes, in which they form lipid bilayers because of their amphiphilic character: in water, phospholipids spontaneously assemble so that their hydrophobic tails are shielded from water while their hydrophilic heads face it.

Structure

The most common phospholipids are glycerophospholipids, built on a glycerol backbone. Two of glycerol's hydroxyl groups are esterified to fatty acids — typically one saturated and one unsaturated — forming the hydrophobic tails, while the third is linked through a phosphate group to a polar head group. Variation in the head group defines the major classes: phosphatidylcholine (the most abundant phospholipid in animal membranes), phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylinositol, and phosphatidylglycerol. Sphingomyelin, the principal sphingophospholipid, is a major component of the myelin sheath of nerve fibres. The length and degree of unsaturation of the fatty-acid tails influence membrane fluidity.

Biological functions

Phospholipid bilayers form the structural basis of the plasma membrane and of intracellular membranes, creating selective barriers between aqueous compartments. Membrane phospholipids are asymmetrically distributed between the two leaflets — phosphatidylserine, for example, is normally confined to the inner leaflet, and its appearance on the cell surface is a signal for apoptosis and blood coagulation. Certain phospholipids also act as signalling molecules or their precursors: phosphatidylinositol is phosphorylated to produce second messengers such as IP3 and diacylglycerol, and phospholipid hydrolysis releases arachidonic acid, the precursor of prostaglandins and other eicosanoids. In the lungs, the phospholipid dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine is the main component of pulmonary surfactant, which reduces surface tension in the alveoli.

Amphiphilic character and self-assembly

Because they possess both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions, phospholipids are amphiphiles. In aqueous solution they form bilayers, micelles, or liposomes depending on their geometry and concentration. This self-assembly is exploited pharmaceutically in liposomal drug-delivery systems, in which drugs are encapsulated within phospholipid vesicles.

Industrial and food uses

Commercial phospholipid mixtures, notably lecithin (rich in phosphatidylcholine), are extracted mainly from soybean oil and egg yolk and are widely used as emulsifiers in foods such as chocolate, margarine, and baked goods, as well as in cosmetics and pharmaceuticals. Phospholipids were first identified in 1847 by the French chemist Théodore Gobley, who isolated lecithin from egg yolk.
