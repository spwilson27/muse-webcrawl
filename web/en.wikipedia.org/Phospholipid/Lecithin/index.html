Lecithin (from the Greek lekithos, "egg yolk") is a generic term for a group of fatty substances occurring in animal and plant tissues, composed of mixtures of phospholipids — primarily phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, and phosphatidylinositol — together with triglycerides and other compounds. Commercially, lecithin is obtained mainly from soybean oil, egg yolk, and sunflower seeds.

History

French chemist Théodore Gobley isolated the orange-red substance from egg yolk in 1845 and named it lecithin in 1850; he later showed it also occurred in blood, bile, and brain tissue. Industrial production from soybeans began in the early 20th century as a by-product of soybean oil refining.

Properties and uses

Lecithin is an effective emulsifier and lubricant: its phospholipid molecules have both water-loving and fat-loving ends, allowing it to stabilize mixtures of oil and water. As food additive E322 it is used in chocolate, margarine, baked goods, and cooking sprays, and it is sold as a dietary supplement, being a source of choline. It is also used in pharmaceuticals (including intravenous fat emulsions and lung surfactant preparations), cosmetics, paints, and animal feed.

Biology

In the body, phosphatidylcholine is a major component of cell membranes and of bile, where it helps solubilize cholesterol. The body can synthesize it, and dietary choline is an essential nutrient.
