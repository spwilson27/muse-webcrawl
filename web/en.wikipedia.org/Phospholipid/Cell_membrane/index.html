The cell membrane (also known as the plasma membrane or cytoplasmic membrane) is a biological membrane that separates and protects the interior of a cell from the outside environment. It consists of a lipid bilayer — two layers of phospholipids — with embedded protein molecules, and controls the movement of substances in and out of cells and organelles.

Structure

Phospholipids arrange themselves with their hydrophilic heads facing outward and their hydrophobic tails inward, forming a barrier permeable to small nonpolar molecules but not to ions and most polar molecules. Membrane proteins float within or span the bilayer as described by the fluid mosaic model: integral proteins act as channels, pumps, carriers, and receptors, while peripheral proteins attach to one face. Cholesterol modulates fluidity, and carbohydrate chains on the outer surface (the glycocalyx) mediate cell recognition.

Transport

Substances cross the membrane by passive diffusion, facilitated diffusion through channels and carriers, active transport powered by ATP (such as the sodium–potassium pump), and bulk transport by endocytosis and exocytosis. This selective permeability allows the cell to maintain internal conditions — ion concentrations, pH, and volume — very different from its surroundings.

Other functions

Beyond transport, the cell membrane hosts receptors for signalling molecules, enzymes, and adhesion molecules that anchor cells to each other and to the extracellular matrix; in plants, fungi, and bacteria it is supported externally by a cell wall.
