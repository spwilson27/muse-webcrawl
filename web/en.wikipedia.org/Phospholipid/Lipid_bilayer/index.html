Lipid bilayer

Lipid bilayer
CompositionTwo layers of amphiphilic lipids, mainly phospholipids
ThicknessAbout 5 nm

PropertiesSelf-assembling, fluid, selectively permeable, self-sealing

The lipid bilayer (or phospholipid bilayer) is a thin polar membrane made of two layers of lipid molecules. These membranes are flat sheets that form a continuous barrier around all cells. The cell membranes of almost all organisms and many viruses are made of a lipid bilayer, as are the nuclear membrane and the membranes of organelles such as the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus.

Structure

The bilayer forms because its constituent phospholipids are amphiphilic: each molecule has a hydrophilic phosphate "head" and two hydrophobic fatty acid "tails." In water, the molecules spontaneously arrange into a double sheet with the tails shielded inside and the heads facing the aqueous environments on either side. The structure is held together by non-covalent interactions, so it is fluid — lipids diffuse rapidly within each leaflet — and self-sealing, since exposing hydrophobic tails to water is energetically unfavourable.

Permeability and proteins

A pure bilayer is permeable to gases and small uncharged molecules (water, oxygen, carbon dioxide, urea) but effectively impermeable to ions and large polar molecules. Cells therefore embed proteins in the bilayer — channels, pumps, carriers, and receptors — to control traffic and signalling, as described by the fluid mosaic model of the cell membrane.

Asymmetry and rafts

The two leaflets differ in composition: phosphatidylserine and phosphatidylethanolamine are concentrated in the inner (cytosolic) leaflet, while phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin dominate the outer leaflet. Cholesterol-rich regions called lipid rafts are thought to organize signalling proteins.
