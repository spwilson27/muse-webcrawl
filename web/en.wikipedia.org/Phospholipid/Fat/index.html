Fat

A fat (triglyceride) beside a phospholipid: one fatty-acid tail traded for a phosphate head.

In nutrition science, fats are triglycerides — glycerol esterified to three fatty acids — used by organisms for energy storage. The phospholipid is fat's close structural cousin: it too is built on glycerol with fatty-acid tails, but the third position carries a phosphate group instead of a third fatty acid. That single substitution converts a purely hydrophobic energy store into an amphiphilic molecule able to form membranes, emulsions, and carriers for fat itself.

Fatty acids shared

Both lipids draw on the same fatty-acid pool. Saturated acids (palmitic, stearic) make fats solid and pack tightly in phospholipid tails; unsaturated acids (oleic, linoleic, arachidonic) keep cooking oils liquid and membranes fluid — one tail of a typical phospholipid is usually unsaturated, its kink helping maintain bilayer fluidity.

Metabolic traffic

Dietary fat cannot travel unaided in blood. Intestinal cells reassemble absorbed fatty acids into triglycerides and pack them into chylomicrons whose outer shell is a phospholipid monolayer studded with cholesterol and proteins. Phospholipid emulsification (see Emulsion) is likewise essential in the gut, where bile phosphatidylcholine helps form the micelles that carry fats to the intestinal wall.
