Cholesterol

Cholesterol is the principal sterol of all animals, distributed in body tissues, especially the brain and spinal cord, and in animal fats and oils. It is a structural component of cell membranes, where it modulates fluidity, and a precursor for the biosynthesis of steroid hormones, bile salts, and vitamin D. Cholesterol is not present in most plants, which contain related phytosterols instead.

Physiology

Because cholesterol is insoluble in blood, it is transported within lipoproteins — particles with a surface of phospholipid, free cholesterol, and apolipoproteins surrounding a core of cholesteryl esters and triglycerides. Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) carries cholesterol to tissues, while high-density lipoprotein (HDL) returns it to the liver. In popular terms these are often called "bad" and "good" cholesterol. The liver is the main site of cholesterol synthesis; dietary cholesterol comes only from animal products.

Function

In membranes, cholesterol's small hydroxyl head and rigid steroid ring system interact with phospholipid molecules, buffering membrane fluidity against temperature changes. Cholesterol is also concentrated in lipid rafts and is required for the formation of caveolae. Chemically it is the substrate for all steroid hormones produced by the adrenal cortex and gonads.

Clinical significance

Elevated blood cholesterol, particularly LDL cholesterol, is a major risk factor for atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease. François Poulletier de la Salle first identified cholesterol in solid form in gallstones in 1769; its structure was worked out in the early 20th century. Statins, which inhibit the enzyme HMG-CoA reductase, are the most common cholesterol-lowering drugs.
