Emulsion

An emulsion is a mixture of two or more liquids that are normally immiscible (unmixable or unblendable) owing to liquid–liquid phase separation. Emulsions are part of a more general class of two-phase systems of matter called colloids. Common emulsions include milk, mayonnaise, vinaigrette, and many cosmetics and pharmaceutical creams.

Types

In an oil-in-water emulsion, droplets of oil (the dispersed phase) are suspended in water (the continuous phase); in a water-in-oil emulsion the arrangement is reversed. Milk is an oil-in-water emulsion of butterfat globules stabilized by a membrane of phospholipids and proteins. Multiple emulsions, such as water-in-oil-in-water, also exist.

Emulsifiers

Emulsions are inherently unstable and tend to separate over time through creaming, coalescence, and flocculation. Emulsifiers (surfactants) stabilize them by adsorbing at the interface and lowering surface tension. Lecithin — a mixture rich in phospholipids from egg yolk or soy — is a classic food emulsifier, as are mustard in vinaigrettes and mono- and diglycerides in processed foods. In digestion, bile salts emulsify dietary fat, and pancreatic lipase then hydrolyzes the triglycerides.

Properties and appearance

Emulsions scatter light and typically appear milky or opaque when droplet sizes are comparable to the wavelength of light; very fine microemulsions can be transparent and are thermodynamically stable. The word "emulsion" comes from the Latin emulgere, "to milk out".

Applications

Emulsions are widespread in foods (milk, butter, dressings), pharmaceuticals (creams, intravenous fat emulsions), cosmetics, paints, asphalt, and photographic film, whose light-sensitive layer is historically called an emulsion.
