Liposome

A liposome: phospholipid self-assembled into a hollow sphere with a water core.

A liposome is a spherical vesicle composed of one or more phospholipid bilayers enclosing an aqueous interior. When phospholipids are dispersed in water they spontaneously self-assemble into these closed shells, because a bilayer edge would expose hydrophobic tails; sealing into a sphere eliminates the edge. Liposomes were described by Alec Bangham in the 1960s and have become central to drug delivery and membrane research.

Formation and types

Sonication or extrusion of phospholipid dispersions yields small unilamellar vesicles (20–100 nm); gentler hydration produces large and multilamellar vesicles. Their properties depend on the phospholipid chosen — chain length and saturation set the phase-transition temperature and leakiness — and on added cholesterol, which stiffens the bilayer.

Drug delivery

Liposomes can carry water-soluble drugs in their core and fat-soluble drugs in their membrane. PEGylated ("stealth") liposomes evade the immune system and accumulate in tumours; approved products include the anticancer formulation Doxil (liposomal doxorubicin) and the antifungal AmBisome. Lipid nanoparticles, close relatives built from ionizable lipids and phospholipids, deliver the mRNA of modern vaccines. In cosmetics and foods, the same emulsifying versatility (see Emulsion) carries flavours and vitamins.
