Phospholipid

Phospholipids self-assemble into bilayers, the basis of cell membranes.

Phospholipids are a class of lipids whose molecule has a hydrophilic "head" containing a phosphate group and two hydrophobic "tails" derived from fatty acids, joined by an alcohol residue (usually glycerol). They are a major component of all cell membranes, in which they form lipid bilayers because of their amphiphilic character: the hydrophilic heads face outward toward the water on both sides, and the hydrophobic tails point inward, away from the water.

Structure and types

Glycerophospholipids, the most common type, are built on a glycerol backbone with two fatty acid chains and a phosphate group bearing a small polar molecule such as choline (phosphatidylcholine, or lecithin), ethanolamine (phosphatidylethanolamine), serine (phosphatidylserine) or inositol (phosphatidylinositol). Sphingomyelin is the principal phospholipid built on a sphingosine backbone and is abundant in the myelin sheaths of nerve cells.

Biological functions

Beyond their structural role in membranes, phospholipids participate in cell signalling: phosphatidylinositol phosphates (such as PIP2) are cleaved to release the second messengers IP3 and diacylglycerol, and phosphatidylserine exposed on the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane signals apoptosis. In the lungs, phospholipids (chiefly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine) form pulmonary surfactant, reducing surface tension in the alveoli. Phospholipids also emulsify fats in digestion and form the surface layer of lipoproteins.

Sources and synthesis

Cells synthesize phospholipids mainly in the endoplasmic reticulum, via the Kennedy pathway and related routes. In food technology, mixtures of phospholipids — marketed as lecithin, extracted mainly from soybeans and egg yolk — are widely used as emulsifiers in chocolate, margarine and baked goods.
