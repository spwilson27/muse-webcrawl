Liposome

A liposome is a small artificial vesicle, spherical in shape, having at least one lipid bilayer. Due to their hydrophobicity and/or hydrophilicity, biocompatibility, particle size, and other properties, liposomes can be used as drug delivery vehicles for administration of pharmaceutical drugs and nutrients, such as lipid nanoparticles in vaccines. Liposomes can be prepared by disrupting biological membranes (such as by sonication) or by hydrating dried phospholipids, which spontaneously self-assemble into bilayers enclosing an aqueous core.

Structure and composition

Liposomes are most often composed of phospholipids, especially phosphatidylcholine, often mixed with cholesterol, which stiffens the bilayer and reduces its permeability. They are classified by size and by the number of bilayers: small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs), large unilamellar vesicles (LUVs), and multilamellar vesicles (MLVs). Hydrophilic cargo can be carried in the aqueous interior, while hydrophobic drugs dissolve in the bilayer.

History

Liposomes were first described by the British haematologist Alec Bangham in 1961 (published 1964), who observed that phospholipids in water formed closed bilayer structures. Gregory Gregoriadis pioneered their use as drug carriers in the 1970s.

Medical applications

Liposomal formulations can reduce toxicity and improve targeting of drugs. Notable approved products include Doxil (liposomal doxorubicin), AmBisome (liposomal amphotericin B), and Onpattro, the first siRNA therapy, delivered in lipid nanoparticles. Lipid nanoparticles closely related to liposomes deliver the mRNA in the Pfizer–BioNTech and Moderna COVID-19 vaccines. "Stealth" liposomes coated with polyethylene glycol (PEG) circulate longer by evading the immune system.

Other uses

Liposomes are used in cosmetics, as model systems for studying cell membranes, and in research on the origin of life, since simple fatty-acid vesicles are plausible precursors of the first cells.
