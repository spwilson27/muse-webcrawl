Emulsion

Oil droplets in water, each stabilized by a monolayer of phospholipid emulsifier.

An emulsion is a mixture of two immiscible liquids, one dispersed as fine droplets in the other. Phospholipids are among nature's most important emulsifiers: their amphiphilic structure — water-loving phosphate head, water-fearing fatty tails — lets them coat oil droplets with a protective monolayer, heads facing the water and tails the oil, preventing the droplets from coalescing.

Lecithin

The phospholipid mixture called lecithin, rich in phosphatidylcholine and extracted from egg yolk and soybeans, is the classic food emulsifier. It stabilizes mayonnaise, chocolate, and margarine, and is labelled as additive E322. Within the body, phospholipids perform the same service in blood, coating the lipids of chylomicrons and other lipoprotein particles so that fats can travel through aqueous plasma (see Fat).

Stability

Emulsions are thermodynamically unstable and tend over time to cream, flocculate, or coalesce. Phospholipid films slow these processes by electrostatic and steric repulsion between droplets and by lowering interfacial tension. In pharmacy and cosmetics, phospholipid emulsions deliver drugs and moisturizers; in the laboratory, related self-assembly produces liposomes.
