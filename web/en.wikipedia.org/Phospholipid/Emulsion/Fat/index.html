Fat

In nutrition, biology, and chemistry, fat usually means any ester of fatty acids, or a mixture of such compounds, most commonly those that occur in living beings or in food. The term often refers specifically to triglycerides (triple esters of glycerol), the main constituents of vegetable oils and of fatty tissue in animals; or, even more narrowly, to triglycerides that are solid or semisolid at room temperature, thus excluding oils.

Role in living organisms

Fats are one of the three main macronutrient groups, along with carbohydrates and proteins. They are a major source of energy — yielding about 9 kilocalories per gram — and serve as energy stores, thermal insulation, and protective cushioning for organs. Fatty tissues also store fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, and K) and provide essential fatty acids that the body cannot synthesize, such as linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid.

Chemical structure

A fat molecule consists of a glycerol backbone bonded to three fatty acid chains. The properties of the fat depend on the lengths and saturation of those chains. Saturated fats have no carbon–carbon double bonds and tend to be solid at room temperature; unsaturated fats contain one (monounsaturated) or more (polyunsaturated) double bonds and are more often liquid oils. Industrial partial hydrogenation can convert oils into semisolid fats and may produce trans fats.

Digestion and metabolism

In digestion, bile salts emulsify fat globules into fine droplets, greatly increasing their surface area so that pancreatic lipase can hydrolyze triglycerides into fatty acids and monoglycerides. These are absorbed by the intestine, reassembled into triglycerides, and transported in the blood within lipoproteins called chylomicrons.

Health

Dietary guidance generally recommends limiting saturated and trans fats because of their association with cardiovascular disease, while unsaturated fats are considered more favorable. Fats are nonetheless essential to a healthy diet.
