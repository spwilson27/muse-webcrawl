Liposome

A liposome is a small artificial vesicle, spherical in shape, having at least one lipid bilayer. Due to their hydrophobicity and/or hydrophilicity, biocompatibility, particle size and many other properties, liposomes can be used as drug delivery vehicles for administration of pharmaceutical drugs and nutrients, such as lipid nanoparticles in mRNA vaccines, and DNA transfection. Liposomes can be prepared by disrupting biological membranes (such as by sonication).

Liposomes are most often composed of phospholipids, especially phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine, but may also include other lipids, such as those found in egg and plant phosphatidylcholine such as soy phosphatidylcholine. A liposome design may employ surface ligands for attaching to desired targets, such as healthy or unhealthy tissue.

Discovery

The word liposome comes from two Greek words: lipo ("fat") and soma ("body"). The structure was discovered in 1961 by British haematologist Alec Douglas Bangham, who was studying phospholipids and blood clotting at the Babraham Institute in Cambridge. The discovery was published in 1964, and liposomes were later described as a model system for the study of biological membranes.

Structure

Liposomes are typically, but not by definition, composed of a bilayer of phospholipids, which have a hydrophilic phosphate head attached to hydrophobic fatty acid tails. When dispersed in water, the phospholipids spontaneously arrange so that the hydrophobic tails are shielded from the aqueous environment while the hydrophilic heads face the water, forming a closed spherical vesicle with an aqueous core.

Liposomes are classified by size and number of bilayers: small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs, 20–100 nm), large unilamellar vesicles (LUVs, greater than 100 nm), giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs, greater than 1 μm), and multilamellar vesicles (MLVs), which contain several concentric bilayers.

Applications

Liposomes are used as carriers in drug delivery because they can encapsulate both hydrophilic drugs (in the aqueous core) and hydrophobic drugs (in the bilayer). Clinically approved liposomal formulations include Doxil (liposomal doxorubicin) and AmBisome (liposomal amphotericin B). Lipid nanoparticles, a related technology, were used to deliver the mRNA in several COVID-19 vaccines.
