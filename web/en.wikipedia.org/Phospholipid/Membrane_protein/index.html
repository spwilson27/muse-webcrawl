Membrane Protein

Proteins floating in and spanning the phospholipid sea of the membrane.

Membrane proteins are proteins embedded in or attached to the phospholipid bilayer of a cell membrane. In the fluid mosaic model of Singer and Nicolson (1972), they drift within the two-dimensional phospholipid fluid, and together the lipids and proteins make the membrane a selective, communicative interface. Membrane proteins constitute roughly a third of the proteome and the majority of drug targets.

Types

Integral (intrinsic) proteins span or embed in the bilayer, anchored by hydrophobic stretches that match the phospholipid tails; they include channels, transporters, pumps, and receptors such as G-protein-coupled receptors. Peripheral proteins attach to the surface through lipid anchors or interactions with phospholipid head groups — phosphatidylserine and the phosphoinositides, for example, recruit signalling proteins to the membrane's inner face.

Lipid–protein interplay

The bilayer is no passive solvent: its thickness, curvature, and specific phospholipids regulate protein activity. Annular phospholipids coat each integral protein, while lipids such as PIP2 act as cofactors for ion channels, and cholesterol-rich rafts cluster receptors. Conversely, proteins reshape the membrane — bending it into vesicles, tubules, and structures like liposomes in vitro.
