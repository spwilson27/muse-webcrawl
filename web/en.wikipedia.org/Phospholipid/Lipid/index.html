In biology, a lipid is a biomolecule that is soluble in nonpolar solvents. Lipids include fats, oils, waxes, certain vitamins (A, D, E, and K), hormones, and most of the non-protein components of cell membranes. Unlike proteins, nucleic acids, and carbohydrates, lipids are defined by their solubility rather than by a common chemical structure.

Categories

The LIPID MAPS classification divides lipids into eight categories: fatty acids; glycerolipids (including triglycerides, the main storage fat); glycerophospholipids (such as phosphatidylcholine); sphingolipids; sterol lipids (such as cholesterol); prenol lipids; saccharolipids; and polyketides.

Biological functions

Lipids serve three principal biological roles. As energy stores, triglycerides in adipose tissue yield more than twice the energy per gram of carbohydrates or protein. As structural components, phospholipids and cholesterol form the lipid bilayers of all cell membranes. As signalling molecules, lipids include steroid hormones, prostaglandins, and lipid second messengers such as diacylglycerol.

Metabolism

Dietary lipids are emulsified by bile salts, hydrolysed by pancreatic lipases, absorbed in the small intestine, and transported in the blood as lipoproteins (chylomicrons, VLDL, LDL, HDL). Fatty acids are oxidized for energy or synthesized and stored; cholesterol balance is central to cardiovascular health.
