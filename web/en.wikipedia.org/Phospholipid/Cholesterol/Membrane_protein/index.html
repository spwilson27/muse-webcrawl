Membrane protein

Membrane proteins are common types of proteins that are part of, or interact with, biological membranes. Membrane proteins fall into several broad categories depending on their location: integral membrane proteins are permanently anchored or part of the membrane, while peripheral membrane proteins are only temporarily attached to the lipid bilayer or to other integral proteins. The integral proteins span the membrane (transmembrane proteins) or are anchored on one side (integral monotopic proteins).

Functions

Membrane proteins perform most of the specific tasks of a membrane. They include receptors that transduce signals (such as G protein-coupled receptors), channels and transporters that move ions and molecules across the membrane (such as ion channels and the sodium–potassium pump), enzymes, cell-adhesion molecules, and proteins of the electron transport chain. Because of these roles, membrane proteins are the targets of over half of all modern medicinal drugs.

Abundance and structure

Roughly 20–30% of the genes in known genomes encode membrane proteins. Compared with soluble proteins, their structures are harder to determine because they require detergents or membrane-like environments; nevertheless, techniques such as X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy have yielded many structures, beginning with the photosynthetic reaction center in 1985. Transmembrane segments are typically alpha helices of hydrophobic amino acids, or, in outer membranes of bacteria, beta barrels.

Interaction with lipids

Membrane proteins are surrounded by annular lipids whose properties are influenced by the protein, and specific lipids such as cholesterol can modulate protein activity by altering membrane thickness, fluidity, and lateral organization into domains such as lipid rafts.
