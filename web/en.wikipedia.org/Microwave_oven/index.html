Microwave oven

A microwave oven (commonly referred to as a microwave) is an electric oven that heats and cooks food by exposing it to electromagnetic radiation in the microwave frequency range, usually 2.45 gigahertz. Microwaves excite polar molecules — chiefly water — in food, producing heat through dielectric heating.

History

The heating effect of microwaves was noticed by Raytheon engineer Percy Spencer in 1945, when a candy bar in his pocket melted near a radar magnetron. Raytheon patented the process and in 1947 introduced the Radarange, a refrigerator-sized commercial oven. The countertop home model followed in 1967 from Amana. By the late 1970s microwave ovens were among the most common kitchen appliances in developed countries.

How it works

A magnetron generates microwaves that are guided into the cooking cavity, where a turntable or mode stirrer distributes them evenly. Microwaves penetrate food a few centimetres deep and heat it by rotating polar molecules; heat then conducts inward. Metal reflects microwaves and can cause arcing, while glass, ceramic and most plastics are transparent to them. Superheating of liquids and uneven heating (cold spots) are known hazards and quirks of the technology.

Uses and safety

Microwaves are used to reheat, defrost and cook food rapidly, and in industrial food processing. Modern ovens include inverter power control, sensor cooking and combination convection models. Regulatory limits ensure leakage is far below harmful levels; microwave radiation is non-ionizing and does not make food radioactive.
