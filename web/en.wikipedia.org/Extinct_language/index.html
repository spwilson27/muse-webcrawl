Extinct language

An extinct language is a language that no longer has any first-language speakers, especially if it has no living descendants. By contrast, a dead language is one that is no longer the native language of any community even though it may still be used, as in liturgical or scholarly contexts — Latin, for example, is often called a dead language but is not extinct in this strict sense.

Language death

Languages become extinct through language death, when speakers shift to another language, often under political, economic, or social pressure. Of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken today, a large proportion are endangered; linguists estimate that a language dies every few weeks, and many predict that up to half of the world's languages will vanish by 2100.

Examples

Well-known extinct languages include Sumerian, Akkadian, Ancient Egyptian (whose descendant Coptic survives only liturgically), Etruscan, Gothic, Old Prussian, and Tasmanian languages. Some languages, such as Cornish and Manx, were declared extinct but have been revived by enthusiasts; Hebrew is the most famous case of a language restored to everyday vernacular use.

Revival and documentation

Language documentation — recording grammars, dictionaries, and texts — preserves knowledge of dying languages, and language revitalization movements seek to restore intergenerational transmission. UNESCO maintains an Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger.
