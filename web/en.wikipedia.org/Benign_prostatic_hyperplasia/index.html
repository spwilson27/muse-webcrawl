Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), also called prostate enlargement, is a non-cancerous increase in the size of the prostate gland. It is extremely common in ageing men: histological BPH is present in roughly half of men in their fifties and in the great majority by their eighties. Growth of the gland around the urethra can obstruct urine flow from the bladder.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms are grouped as lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS): storage symptoms such as frequency, urgency and nocturia (waking at night to urinate), and voiding symptoms such as a weak or intermittent stream, straining, hesitancy and a feeling of incomplete emptying. Complications can include urinary retention, recurrent infections, bladder stones and, rarely, kidney damage. Symptom severity is commonly measured with the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS).

Causes and diagnosis

BPH arises from proliferation of stromal and epithelial cells in the transition zone of the prostate, driven by androgens — particularly dihydrotestosterone (DHT) — and by ageing itself. Diagnosis rests on history, digital rectal examination, urinalysis and often a prostate-specific antigen (PSA) blood test; ultrasound and flow-rate studies may be used. BPH is distinct from prostate cancer, though the two can coexist.

Treatment

Mild symptoms are managed with watchful waiting and lifestyle changes. Medications include alpha blockers (such as tamsulosin), which relax smooth muscle and act quickly, and 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors (such as finasteride), which shrink the gland over months. For severe or refractory cases, procedures include transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP), the long-standing standard operation, and newer minimally invasive options such as laser enucleation, water-vapour therapy and prostatic urethral lift.
