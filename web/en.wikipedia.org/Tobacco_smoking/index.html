Tobacco smoking

A cigarette, the most common form in which tobacco is smoked.

Tobacco smoking is the practice of burning tobacco and inhaling the smoke that is produced, most commonly in the form of cigarettes, cigars, or pipes. The smoke contains thousands of chemical compounds, including the stimulant alkaloid nicotine, which is highly addictive. Tobacco smoking is one of the leading preventable causes of death and disease worldwide, and is a major risk factor for lung cancer, cardiovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and many other conditions.

According to the World Health Organization, tobacco kills more than eight million people each year, including roughly 1.3 million non-smokers exposed to second-hand smoke. Around 1.3 billion people worldwide use tobacco, the great majority of them in low- and middle-income countries.

History

Smoking tobacco originated among the indigenous peoples of the Americas, where the plant Nicotiana had been cultivated and used in religious and medicinal rituals for centuries, possibly for millennia. Following the European colonisation of the Americas in the late 15th and 16th centuries, tobacco was introduced to Europe and Asia and rapidly became a valuable trade commodity. Smoking spread worldwide through trade, colonisation, and later mass production.

The invention of cigarette-rolling machines in the 1880s transformed tobacco consumption by making cigarettes cheap and abundant. Smoking became widely fashionable in the first half of the 20th century, promoted by advertising and distributed free to soldiers in both world wars. Consumption peaked in many Western countries in the mid-20th century, even as epidemiological evidence of its dangers accumulated.

Health effects

Cancer

Tobacco smoke contains at least 70 known carcinogens, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and nitrosamines. Smoking is the leading cause of lung cancer, accounting for the large majority of cases, and also increases the risk of cancers of the mouth, throat, larynx, oesophagus, bladder, pancreas, kidney, cervix, stomach, liver, and colon, among others.

Cardiovascular and respiratory disease

Smoking damages blood vessels, raises blood pressure, and increases the risk of coronary heart disease, stroke, and peripheral arterial disease. It is the primary cause of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), including chronic bronchitis and emphysema, and worsens asthma and respiratory infections.

Addiction and other effects

Nicotine acts on the brain's reward system, producing dependence comparable to that of other addictive drugs. Smoking during pregnancy increases the risk of miscarriage, premature birth, low birth weight, and sudden infant death syndrome. Second-hand smoke exposure increases the risk of heart disease and lung cancer in non-smokers and of respiratory illness in children.

Quitting smoking at any age produces substantial health benefits: the risk of heart disease falls rapidly within a few years, the risk of lung cancer declines progressively, and former smokers who quit before middle age avoid most of the excess mortality associated with smoking.

Forms of smoking

  • Cigarettes — finely cut tobacco rolled in paper; by far the most common form globally.
  • Cigars — rolled bundles of fermented tobacco wrapped in tobacco leaf.
  • Pipes — tobacco burned in a bowl and drawn through a stem.
  • Water pipes (hookah, shisha) — smoke passed through water before inhalation; common in the Middle East and South Asia.
  • Bidis and kreteks — hand-rolled products common in South and Southeast Asia; kreteks contain cloves.

Regulation and control

Scientific evidence linking smoking to lung cancer became compelling in the 1950s, notably through the work of Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill, and the 1964 report of the United States Surgeon General marked a turning point in public policy. Governments have since adopted taxation, advertising bans, graphic health warnings, smoke-free laws in public places, and restrictions on sales. The WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control, adopted in 2003, was the first treaty negotiated under WHO auspices and has been ratified by most countries. Smoking rates have fallen substantially in many high-income countries, though they remain high in parts of Eastern Europe and Asia and are rising in some regions.

Society and culture

Smoking has long carried social and symbolic meanings — sophistication, rebellion, relaxation, and social bonding — reinforced by its portrayal in film and advertising. As health evidence accumulated and smoke-free norms spread, smoking has become increasingly stigmatised in many societies. The rise of electronic cigarettes in the 21st century has introduced new debates about harm reduction, youth uptake, and regulation.
