Time of flight

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Diagram of a time-of-flight measurement, showing a pulse travelling from emitter to detector]

Time of flight (ToF) is the measurement of the time taken by an object, particle or wave (acoustic, electromagnetic or other) to travel a distance through a medium. The measurement can be used directly to determine the distance travelled (if the speed is known), the speed of the object (if the distance is known), or properties of the medium itself.

Principles

The basic relation is d = c·t for one-way travel, or d = c·t/2 for a reflected pulse that travels out and back. Because light travels at about 3×10⁸ m/s, measuring distances of metres requires timing resolution of nanoseconds; a timing error of one nanosecond corresponds to roughly 15 cm of range error in a two-way measurement.

Applications

Rangefinding and imaging

Time-of-flight cameras illuminate a scene with modulated light or short pulses and measure the phase shift or delay of the returning signal at each pixel, producing a depth map. The same principle underlies lidar, radar and ultrasonic distance sensors used in vehicles, robotics and surveying.

Mass spectrometry

In time-of-flight mass spectrometry (TOF-MS), ions are accelerated through a known potential and drift down a field-free tube; lighter ions arrive at the detector sooner than heavier ones, so the arrival-time spectrum yields the mass-to-charge ratios of the sample. Reflectrons — ion mirrors — correct for the spread in initial kinetic energies and greatly improve resolution.

Particle physics and medicine

Particle detectors use ToF to identify particles by combining measured velocity with momentum. In positron emission tomography, time-of-flight information about the two annihilation photons localises the emission point along the line of response and improves image quality.
