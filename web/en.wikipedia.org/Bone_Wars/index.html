Bone Wars

Period1877–1892
ParticipantsEdward Drinker Cope; Othniel Charles Marsh
FieldPalaeontology
Principal sitesComo Bluff, Wyoming; Garden Park, Colorado; Morrison Formation

The Bone Wars, also known as the Great Dinosaur Rush, was a period of intense and ruthlessly competitive fossil hunting and discovery during the Gilded Age of American history, marked by a heated rivalry between Edward Drinker Cope of the Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia and Othniel Charles Marsh of Yale University.

Rivalry

Formerly friends, Cope and Marsh fell out in the late 1860s. Their competition played out in the fossil beds of the American West, where each employed field crews, bribery, spies, and allegedly even dynamite and rockfalls to keep the other from promising sites. In print they raced to name new species and attacked each other's errors — most famously over Cope's reconstruction of Elasmosaurus with the head placed on the tail.

Discoveries

Between them, Cope and Marsh described over 130 new dinosaur species, including Triceratops, Stegosaurus, Allosaurus, Diplodocus, and Apatosaurus (Brontosaurus), transforming public and scientific understanding of prehistoric life.

Legacy

The feud ruined both men financially and professionally, but their collections — now held by institutions such as Yale's Peabody Museum and the Smithsonian — founded American vertebrate palaeontology. The Bone Wars remain the most famous scientific rivalry of the 19th century.
