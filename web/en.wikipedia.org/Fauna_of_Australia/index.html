Fauna of Australia

The fauna of Australia is highly distinctive, with very high levels of endemism: about 83% of mammals, 89% of reptiles, 90% of fish and insects and 93% of amphibians are found nowhere else.

The fauna of Australia consists of a huge variety of animals; some 46% of birds, 69% of mammals, 94% of frogs and 93% of reptiles that inhabit the continent are endemic to it. This high level of endemism results from Australia's long period of geographic isolation after separating from the Gondwanan supercontinent, its tectonic stability, and the effects of a unique pattern of climate change during the Cenozoic.

Mammals

Australia is famous for its marsupials — kangaroos, koalas, wombats, possums and the Tasmanian devil — which evolved in isolation after the continent split from Gondwana. It is also home to the only living monotremes, the platypus and echidnas, egg-laying mammals. Placental mammals include bats, rodents, and marine mammals such as dugongs; the dingo was introduced by humans several thousand years ago.

Birds

Australia has around 800 bird species, including the flightless emu and cassowary, many parrots and cockatoos, kookaburras, and lyrebirds noted for mimicry.

Reptiles and amphibians

The continent hosts the saltwater crocodile, the world's largest reptile, numerous venomous snakes, monitors such as the perentie, and unique frogs adapted to arid conditions.

Invertebrates and marine life

Australian waters support the Great Barrier Reef ecosystem, while the land invertebrate fauna includes funnel-web spiders, redback spiders and vast numbers of endemic insects and earthworms.

Conservation

Since European settlement in 1788, Australia has suffered the world's highest rate of mammal extinctions, driven by habitat loss and introduced predators such as foxes and feral cats. Many species are now protected under national and state conservation law.
