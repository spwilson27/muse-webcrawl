Strait of Dover

The Strait of Dover (French: Pas de Calais) is the narrowest part of the English Channel, separating Great Britain from continental Europe. At its narrowest point, between South Foreland near Dover and Cap Gris Nez near Calais, it is only about 33 kilometres (20 miles) wide, and on clear days the opposite coast is plainly visible.

Geology

The strait was formed by erosion: most geologists believe a chalk ridge once joined Britain to France, impounding a huge proglacial lake fed by the Rhine and Thames. Catastrophic megaflood events — one perhaps 450,000 years ago and another about 160,000 years ago — breached the ridge and scoured the channel floor, whose streamlined bedrock features were mapped by sonar in the 2000s.

Shipping

The strait is among the busiest shipping lanes in the world, with hundreds of vessels passing through daily between the North Sea and the Atlantic. It was the site of the world's first international traffic separation scheme, adopted in 1967, under which east- and west-bound ships use separated lanes monitored by the Channel Navigation Information Service. The Channel Tunnel, opened in 1994, passes beneath the strait.

Swimming

The strait is the classic course for Channel swimming: Captain Matthew Webb made the first unassisted crossing in 1875, and Dover remains the starting point for most attempts to swim to France.
