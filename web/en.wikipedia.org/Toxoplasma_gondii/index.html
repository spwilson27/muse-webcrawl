Toxoplasma gondii

Toxoplasma gondii is an obligate intracellular parasitic protozoan (an apicomplexan) that causes the disease toxoplasmosis. It is one of the most common parasites in the world: a large fraction of the human population — estimates range from roughly a third to over half in some regions — carries a chronic, usually asymptomatic infection. Members of the cat family (Felidae) are its only definitive hosts, in which it can reproduce sexually.

Life cycle

The parasite has three infectious stages: tachyzoites, which multiply rapidly during acute infection; bradyzoites, which persist slowly in tissue cysts, especially in muscle and brain; and sporozoites within oocysts shed in cat feces, which become infectious after maturing in the environment. Warm-blooded animals, including humans, become infected by ingesting oocysts from contaminated soil, water, or food, by eating undercooked meat containing tissue cysts, or congenitally from an infected mother.

Effects on hosts

In immunocompetent people, infection is usually mild or silent, though it can cause flu-like symptoms and, rarely, eye disease. In people with weakened immune systems, reactivation can cause life-threatening encephalitis. Congenital infection can cause miscarriage or serious neurological and ocular damage. Research has also examined associations between latent infection and behavioral changes in rodents — infected rats and mice famously lose their aversion to cat odors — and possible statistical links to human behavioral and psychiatric traits, though these remain under study.

History

T. gondii was first described in 1908 by Nicolle and Manceaux in the gundi, a North African rodent, from which it takes its species name. Its full life cycle, including the role of cats, was worked out in the 1960s and 1970s.
