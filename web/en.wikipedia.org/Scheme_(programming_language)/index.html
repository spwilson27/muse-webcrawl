Scheme (programming language)

Scheme is a programming language that supports multiple paradigms, including functional programming and imperative programming, and is one of the two main dialects of Lisp, the other being Common Lisp. Unlike Common Lisp, Scheme follows a minimalist design philosophy, specifying a small standard core with powerful tools for language extension.

Scheme was created during the 1970s at the MIT AI Lab by Guy L. Steele and Gerald Jay Sussman, and introduced via a series of memos now known as the Lambda Papers. It was the first dialect of Lisp to choose lexical scoping and to require implementations to support tail-call optimization.

History

Scheme began as an attempt to understand Carl Hewitt's actor model. Steele and Sussman wrote a tiny Lisp interpreter in MacLisp and then added mechanisms for creating actors; the result, which dropped the actor-specific constructs, became Scheme. The language has been standardized in the series of Revisedn Report on the Algorithmic Language Scheme documents, most notably R5RS (1998), R6RS (2007), and R7RS (2013).

Design

Scheme is distinctive for its minimalism: the standard core is small enough to be described in around fifty pages. Key features include first-class procedures, proper tail recursion, first-class continuations (via call/cc), lexical scoping, and a powerful hygienic macro system that lets programmers extend the language's syntax.

(define (factorial n)
  (if (= n 0)
      1
      (* n (factorial (- n 1)))))

(factorial 5) ; => 120

Implementations

There are many implementations of Scheme, including MIT/GNU Scheme, Chicken Scheme, Racket (which began as a Scheme implementation), Guile (the GNU project's extension language), Gambit, and Chez Scheme. Scheme is widely used in education; the influential textbook Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs uses Scheme throughout.
