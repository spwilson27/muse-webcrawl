Rudder

A rudder is a primary control surface used to steer a ship, boat, submarine or aircraft. On watercraft, the rudder is a flat, hinged blade mounted at the stern; turning it deflects the flow of water, generating a force that swings the stern and changes the vessel's heading. The helmsman moves the rudder through a wheel or tiller connected by cables, hydraulics or a steering engine.

History

Early vessels were steered with steering oars mounted on the quarter. The hinged, centreline sternpost rudder appeared in China by the Han dynasty and in Europe by the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, greatly improving control of larger ships and enabling the great age of oceanic sailing. A popular misconception holds that the word "starboard" derives from "steerboard", the side on which the steering oar was hung — this etymology is in fact correct.

Types

Common forms include the spade rudder (mounted only on its stock), the semi-balanced rudder partly supported by a skeg, and rudders hung on the transom of small boats. Specialised designs such as the Becker rudder add a hinged flap to increase lift at high deflection, while azimuth thrusters and waterjets can replace rudders entirely. Twin rudders are fitted to wide-sterned sailing yachts to keep a blade immersed when heeled.

Aircraft rudders

On aircraft the rudder is the hinged rear portion of the vertical stabiliser. It controls yaw — rotation about the vertical axis — and is used mainly to coordinate turns and counteract adverse yaw and engine failures, rather than to turn the aircraft, which is achieved by banking with the ailerons. Pilots operate it with foot pedals.
