Acid–base homeostasis

Acid–base homeostasis is the homeostatic regulation of the pH of the body's extracellular fluid (ECF). The proper balance between the acids and bases (i.e. the pH) in the ECF is crucial for the normal physiology of the body and for cellular metabolism. The pH of the intracellular fluid and the extracellular fluid need to be maintained at a constant level; in humans, arterial blood pH normally lies between 7.35 and 7.45.

Mechanisms

Three lines of defence maintain acid–base balance:

  • Chemical buffer systems act within seconds. The principal extracellular buffer is the bicarbonate/carbonic acid system (CO2 + H2O ↔ H2CO3 ↔ H+ + HCO3−); phosphate and protein buffers, especially haemoglobin, contribute intracellularly.
  • Respiratory regulation: the lungs adjust ventilation in response to chemoreceptor feedback, exhaling or retaining CO2 and thereby shifting pH within minutes.
  • Renal regulation: the kidneys excrete hydrogen ions, reabsorb and generate bicarbonate, and excrete ammonium and titratable acid over hours to days, providing the most powerful long-term control.

Disorders

A fall in blood pH below 7.35 is acidosis and a rise above 7.45 is alkalosis; each may be respiratory (driven by CO2) or metabolic (driven by bicarbonate and other acids/bases), and the body compensates through the other system. Examples include respiratory acidosis in hypoventilation, metabolic acidosis in diabetic ketoacidosis or renal failure, and metabolic alkalosis from prolonged vomiting. Assessment uses arterial blood gas analysis together with electrolytes and the anion gap.
