Matignon Agreements

The Matignon Agreements (French: Accords de Matignon) were signed in the Hôtel de Matignon by the French government, employers and trade unions on 7 June 1936. They were concluded during a wave of strikes and factory occupations that followed the election victory of the Popular Front under Léon Blum, and granted French workers major concessions: the right to organize, collective bargaining, a 40-hour working week, paid holidays, and substantial wage increases of 7 to 15 percent.

Background

After the Popular Front won the legislative elections of May 1936, a spontaneous strike movement spread across France, with workers occupying factories. Blum's government, wishing to end the strikes without repression, brought the Confédération générale du patronat français (CGPF) and the Confédération générale du travail (CGT) together at Matignon, the prime minister's residence.

Terms

The agreements recognized trade union freedom in the workplace, instituted worker delegates, and established collective contracts as the framework for labour relations. Employers agreed to general wage rises. Legislation followed quickly: the 40-hour week and two weeks' paid annual leave were enacted in June 1936.

Later usage

The name "Matignon Agreements" was also used for the 1988 accords on New Caledonia signed under Michel Rocard, which set a path toward a referendum on independence, and for the 1936 Franco-Syrian context in popular usage. In French political vocabulary, "Matignon" by extension refers to major social negotiations at the seat of government.
