S&P 500

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Standard and Poor's 500, or simply the S&P 500, is a stock market index tracking the stock performance of 500 of the largest companies listed on stock exchanges in the United States. It is one of the most commonly followed equity indices and is widely regarded as a gauge of the U.S. stock market and economy. The index is maintained by S&P Dow Jones Indices and is weighted by free-float-adjusted market capitalization, so larger companies exert proportionally greater influence on its value.

Selection and calculation

Constituents are chosen by an index committee according to criteria including market capitalization, liquidity, public float, sector representation, and financial viability; unlike the Dow Jones Industrial Average, the S&P 500 is not simply the 500 largest companies. The index level is computed by dividing the aggregate free-float market value of the constituents by a proprietary divisor that is adjusted for corporate actions. A price-return version is quoted in the news, while a total-return version reinvests dividends.

Investing

The S&P 500 is the benchmark for a large share of professionally managed U.S. equity assets and the basis of widely held index funds and ETFs, such as the SPDR S&P 500 ETF (SPY), launched in 1993 as the first U.S. ETF, and the Vanguard 500 Index Fund, the first index mutual fund for individual investors. Index futures and options on the S&P 500 are among the most traded derivatives contracts in the world.

History

Standard & Poor's introduced its first daily index in 1923; the 500-stock composite dates to 1957, with historical values reconstructed back to 1926. Over long horizons the index's nominal total return has averaged roughly 10% per year, punctuated by severe declines in 1929–32, 1973–74, 2000–02, 2007–09, and 2020.
