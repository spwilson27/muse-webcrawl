Gulab jamun

Gulab jamun is a sweet confectionery, originating in the Indian subcontinent, made mainly from milk solids — traditionally khoya (reduced milk) — that are kneaded into a dough, shaped into small balls, deep-fried at low temperature, and soaked in a light sugar syrup flavoured with green cardamom, rose water, saffron, or kewra water. It is one of the most popular desserts in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Nepal, and is widely served at festivals, weddings, and celebrations, often warm and accompanied by vanilla ice cream.

Etymology and history

The name combines gulab, from Persian gulāb (rose water, referring to the scented syrup), and jamun, the Hindi-Urdu name for the Java plum (Syzygium cumini), whose dark colour and size the fried balls resemble. Food historians generally trace the dish to medieval India, where it developed under Persian and Central Asian culinary influence during the Mughal period; it is related to the Arab sweet luqmat al-qadi and the Turkish tulumba, which travelled east and were adapted to local milk-based ingredients.

Preparation and variants

Traditional gulab jamun uses khoya with a little flour and leavening; modern home and commercial versions often use milk powder instead. The balls must be fried slowly so they cook through without hardening, then rested in warm syrup so they absorb it and become soft and spongy. Notable regional variants include kala jamun, fried longer to a darker, almost black colour; pantua of Bengal, made with chhena; and stuffed versions filled with nuts. In southern India, similar sweets are prepared with sweet potato. Commercial mixes and ready-made syrup-soaked tins have made the sweet a global Indian-restaurant staple.
