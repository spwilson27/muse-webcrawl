Circuit breaker

A circuit breaker is an electrical safety device designed to protect an electrical circuit from damage caused by overcurrent, typically resulting from an overload or short circuit. Unlike a fuse, which operates once and must be replaced, a circuit breaker can be reset — manually or automatically — to resume normal operation.

Operation

When a fault current flows, internal mechanisms separate the breaker's contacts and interrupt the circuit. Common tripping mechanisms include a bimetallic strip that bends when heated by sustained overload, and an electromagnet that acts instantly on short-circuit currents. High-voltage breakers must also quench the arc that forms when contacts part, using air, oil, vacuum or SF6 gas as the interrupting medium.

Types

Types range from miniature circuit breakers (MCBs) protecting household wiring, through residual-current devices that guard against electric shock, to large air, vacuum and gas-insulated breakers used in power transmission at hundreds of kilovolts. Standard ratings and breaking capacities are defined by IEC and national standards.

History

An early circuit breaker was described by Thomas Edison in an 1879 patent application, and modern designs were developed in the early twentieth century by companies such as Brown, Boveri & Cie.
