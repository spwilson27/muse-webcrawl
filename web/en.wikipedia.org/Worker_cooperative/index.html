Worker cooperative

A worker cooperative is a cooperative owned and self-managed by its workers, who share in profits and govern the enterprise on the basis of one member, one vote. Worker cooperatives exist in most economic sectors, from manufacturing and retail to professional services.

History

The model descends from the Rochdale Pioneers (1844) and nineteenth-century craft associations. The Mondragon Corporation in Spain's Basque Country, founded in 1956, grew into one of the world's largest federations of worker cooperatives. Italy's Emilia-Romagna region and Argentina's recovered factories (empresas recuperadas) are other notable concentrations.

Governance

Members elect boards and managers, set pay ratios, and decide how surpluses are divided between wages, reserves, and community funds. Supporters argue the form raises productivity and job stability; critics point to difficulties raising external capital and scaling democratic governance.

Legal status

Many countries provide specific cooperative statutes; in the United States, worker cooperatives incorporate under state cooperative laws or as LLCs and corporations with cooperative bylaws.
