Weyl curvature hypothesis

The hypothesis constrains the universe's initial singularity to have vanishing Weyl curvature

The Weyl curvature hypothesis is a conjecture in cosmology proposed by the British mathematical physicist Roger Penrose in 1979, in an essay on the origin of the second law of thermodynamics. It proposes an explanation for why the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state, which is required for the second law of thermodynamics to hold throughout cosmic history.

Background

The entropy of the observable universe has been increasing since the Big Bang, in accordance with the second law of thermodynamics. Extrapolating backwards, the universe must have started in a state of extremely low entropy — an extraordinarily special, finely tuned initial condition. Yet the matter and radiation of the early universe were in thermal equilibrium, which would naively seem to be a state of maximum entropy.

Penrose's resolution appeals to gravity. For a gravitating system, a smooth, uniform distribution is actually a state of very low entropy, because gravity tends to make matter clump: the maximum-entropy state of a self-gravitating system is a black hole, with entropy given by the Bekenstein–Hawking formula. The low initial entropy is therefore a statement about the geometry of spacetime itself.

Statement

Spacetime curvature can be divided into two parts: the Ricci curvature, which is directly determined by matter and energy via Einstein's field equations, and the Weyl curvature, which describes the free gravitational field — tidal distortions and gravitational radiation. The hypothesis states that at the initial singularity of the universe, the Weyl curvature tensor was zero (or strongly suppressed), whereas near final singularities, such as those inside black holes, the Weyl curvature grows without bound.

Formally, Penrose proposed a quantity built from the ratio of the (squared) Weyl and Ricci curvature scalars that remains finite at initial singularities but diverges at final ones, serving as a measure of gravitational entropy.

Consequences

The hypothesis is central to Penrose's conformal cyclic cosmology, in which the remote future of one cosmic aeon is conformally identified with the Big Bang of the next, a transition possible only if the Weyl curvature vanishes at the crossover. The hypothesis remains unproven and is regarded as a constraint that a future theory of quantum gravity might explain.
