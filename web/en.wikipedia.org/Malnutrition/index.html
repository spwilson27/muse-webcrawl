Malnutrition

Malnutrition
TypesUndernutrition (stunting, wasting, underweight); micronutrient deficiency; overnutrition (overweight, obesity)
CausesInadequate or excessive nutrient intake; poor nutrient absorption; poverty, famine, illness
Key deficienciesVitamin A, iron, iodine, zinc, folate
At-risk groupsChildren under five, pregnant women, elderly, chronically ill
Global burden~148 million children stunted; ~45 million wasted (early 2020s estimates)

Malnutrition occurs when the intake of nutrients or energy is too high, too low, or poorly balanced, causing measurable adverse effects on body composition, function, or clinical outcome. In common usage it usually refers to undernutrition, which includes stunting (low height for age), wasting (low weight for height), underweight (low weight for age), and deficiencies of essential vitamins and minerals. Malnutrition also encompasses overnutrition — overweight and obesity — so that many countries now face a "double burden" of both forms simultaneously.

Causes

The immediate causes are inadequate dietary intake and disease; underlying causes include household food insecurity, poor sanitation and water, inadequate maternal and child care, and poverty. Famine, war, displacement, and economic shocks cause acute crises, while chronic deprivation quietly produces stunting in about one in five of the world's children. In hospitals and in old age, malnutrition often results from illness, immobility, or difficulty eating.

Effects

Undernutrition in the first 1,000 days of life impairs brain development and physical growth irreversibly and weakens immunity: malnourished children are far more likely to die of common infections such as diarrhoea, pneumonia, and measles, and undernutrition underlies roughly 45% of deaths of children under five. Severe acute malnutrition takes two classic forms — marasmus (severe wasting) and kwashiorkor (with oedema). Micronutrient deficiencies cause specific diseases: vitamin A deficiency blinds children, iron deficiency causes anaemia, iodine deficiency impairs cognition, and folate deficiency causes neural tube defects.

Treatment and prevention

Severe acute malnutrition is treated with therapeutic milks and ready-to-use therapeutic foods (such as the peanut-based paste Plumpy'Nut) under WHO protocols, with careful management of refeeding to avoid refeeding syndrome. Prevention includes breastfeeding promotion, dietary diversification, food fortification (iodised salt, fortified flour), vitamin A supplementation, clean water, and social protection programmes; the UN Sustainable Development Goal 2 ("Zero Hunger") targets ending all forms of malnutrition by 2030.
