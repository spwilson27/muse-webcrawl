Match cut

A match cut is a film edit in which two successive shots are joined by a strong correspondence in composition, shape, action, or subject matter, so that elements of the first shot visually "match" elements of the second. Unlike a standard continuity cut, which preserves the flow of action within a scene, a match cut often bridges large gaps of time or space, using the visual rhyme to suggest a thematic or narrative connection.

A graphic match pairs similar shapes across a cut to connect two scenes.

Types

  • Graphic match — similar shapes, colours, or compositions are aligned across the cut.
  • Match on action — an action begun in one shot is completed in the next, often from a different angle; a staple of continuity editing.
  • Eyeline match — a shot of a character looking is followed by a shot of what they see, from a matching viewpoint.

Famous examples

The most celebrated match cut in cinema is in Stanley Kubrick's 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968), where a bone thrown into the air by a prehistoric ape cuts to an orbiting space station of similar shape and orientation, compressing millions of years of evolution into a single edit. In David Lean's Lawrence of Arabia (1962), Lawrence blows out a match, and the cut lands on a desert sunrise — a match on the image of flame and light. Alfred Hitchcock and many other directors used match cuts to link motifs and foreshadow events.

Function

Match cuts can create metaphors, imply causality or parallel between two scenes, or elide enormous amounts of time elegantly. Because the eye follows the matched element, they can also make a transition feel seamless even when the subject changes completely, and are a standard tool in montage sequences, trailers, and advertising.
