Henrik Ibsen

Henrik Ibsen

Born: 20 March 1828, Skien, Norway

Died: 23 May 1906 (aged 78), Christiania (Oslo), Norway

Occupation: Playwright, theatre director, poet

Notable works: Peer Gynt, A Doll's House, Ghosts, Hedda Gabler

Ibsen in later life

Henrik Johan Ibsen (20 March 1828 – 23 May 1906) was a Norwegian playwright and theatre director. As the founder of modernism in theatre, he is often referred to as "the father of realism" and is one of the most influential playwrights of all time; his plays are among the most frequently performed in the world after Shakespeare's.

Life

Ibsen was born in Skien to a merchant family whose bankruptcy in his youth left a lasting mark. Apprenticed to an apothecary in Grimstad, he wrote his first plays in verse, then worked as a theatre director in Bergen and Christiania. From 1864 he lived 27 years in self-imposed exile, chiefly in Rome, Dresden and Munich, where he wrote his major works. He returned to Norway in 1891 and died in Christiania (now Oslo) in 1906.

Works

His early verse dramas include Brand (1866) and Peer Gynt (1867), later set to music by Edvard Grieg. His sequence of twelve realistic prose dramas, beginning with The Pillars of Society (1877) and including A Doll's House (1879), Ghosts (1881), An Enemy of the People (1882), The Wild Duck (1884), Hedda Gabler (1890) and The Master Builder (1892), revolutionised European theatre with their psychological depth and unsparing treatment of marriage, hypocrisy, heredity and social convention. His final play, When We Dead Awaken (1899), he called his "dramatic epilogue."

Influence

Ibsen profoundly influenced playwrights from Chekhov and Shaw — who championed him in The Quintessence of Ibsenism — to the modern stage generally. The final door-slam of A Doll's House became a symbol of female emancipation, and Ibsen festivals and the Ibsen Award keep his name central to world drama.
